Overview

What it is
The longest and most important tale of the Ulster Cycle of early Irish literature: Queen Medb of Connacht leads the armies of four of the five provinces of Ireland into Ulster to carry off the great Brown Bull of Cúailnge, and the seventeen-year-old Cú Chulainn holds them off alone
Language and date
Old and Middle Irish. The earliest surviving version was put together in the eleventh century from material whose linguistic core goes back to the eighth century, perhaps earlier
Form
Mainly prose, with passages of verse: rhetorical rosc (obscure, alliterative, unrhymed 'runs') and later syllabic poems, especially dialogues and laments
Recension I
Lebor na hUidre ('The Book of the Dun Cow', Royal Irish Academy, written at Clonmacnoise around 1100, incomplete) and the Yellow Book of Lecan (late fourteenth century); a compilation of older, partly contradictory versions
Recension II
The Book of Leinster (Trinity College Dublin, written around 1160): a smoother, more unified literary reworking in an ornate Middle Irish style
Recension III
Fragments in later manuscripts; the Stowe version (Royal Irish Academy) is a late medieval text close to Recension II, edited by O'Rahilly in 1961
Setting
A legendary Ireland of the 'heroic age', dated by medieval historians to the time of Christ: Cruachain in Connacht, Emain Macha in Ulster, and the fords and plains of Mag Muirthemne and the Cooley peninsula (Co. Louth)
Quoted from
Mary A. Hutton, The Táin: An Irish Epic Told in English Verse (Dublin, 1907): a free blank-verse retelling, not a translation of any one manuscript, with Hutton's own phonetic spellings (Cucullin, Maev, Al-yill, Faerdeeah)
Major translations
Thomas Kinsella (1969), Cecile O'Rahilly's scholarly editions of both recensions (1967, 1976), Ciaran Carson (2007)

Context

The Táin Bó Cúailnge ('The Driving-off of the Cattle of Cúailnge', usually called The Cattle Raid of Cooley) is the central tale of the Ulster Cycle, a group of some eighty stories about King Conchobar mac Nessa of Ulster, his court at Emain Macha (Navan Fort, near Armagh) and his warriors, above all Cú Chulainn. It is the nearest thing early Ireland produced to a national epic, and the only Irish saga long enough to bear comparison with Beowulf or the Iliad. Many shorter tales, the remscéla or 'fore-tales', were composed to explain its background: how the Ulstermen came to suffer their debility, why Fergus is in exile in Connacht, how the two bulls began as rival swineherds (Thurneysen 1921) (Ní Bhrolcháin 2009).

The tale survives in three main versions. Recension I is found in Lebor na hUidre, written at Clonmacnoise around 1100, and more fully in the Yellow Book of Lecan. It is a compilation in which a redactor has set older and newer material side by side, sometimes with the note that 'other versions' tell a story differently; its language is mostly Old Irish of the eighth and ninth centuries modernised in places. Recension II, in the Book of Leinster (around 1160), is a deliberate literary rewriting that smooths out contradictions and adds ornate, alliterative description. A later version, the Stowe text, stands close to Recension II. Cecile O'Rahilly's editions with translations of the Book of Leinster text (1967) and of Recension I (1976) are the standard scholarly texts, and she also edited the Stowe version (1961) (O'Rahilly 1967) (O'Rahilly 1976) (O'Rahilly 1961).

Like most early Irish narrative, the Táin is written mainly in prose. At moments of high feeling or prophecy it turns to verse: the dense, alliterative rhetorical form called rosc or retoiric, often so archaic that even medieval scribes found it hard, and in later layers to rhymed syllabic stanzas, as in the dialogue and lament of the fight with Ferdiad. The verse passages are dialogue, prophecy, boast and lament rather than narration. Students reading an English verse version such as Mary Hutton's should remember that it gives the whole saga a uniformly poetic surface that the Irish does not have (Tymoczko 1999).

Mary A. Hutton published her retelling in 1907, when the Táin had become a text of the Irish Literary Revival. It is not a translation of a single manuscript. Hutton worked from both recensions and from related tales, filled gaps, reordered episodes and turned the whole into blank verse, adding long passages of her own, and she spelled the names phonetically so that English readers could say them: Cucullin for Cú Chulainn, Maev for Medb, Al-yill for Ailill, Faerdeeah for Ferdiad, More-reega for the Morrígan. Her frame, 'The Finding of the Táin' and 'The Writing of the Táin', comes from the medieval tale of how the Táin was recovered, and it ends with Saint Ciarán writing the story on the hide of his dun cow, an allusion to Lebor na hUidre itself. This guide refers to her sections as 'Hutton, Book IV' and so on, and names the corresponding episodes of the Irish where the match is certain (Hutton 1924).

The Táin mattered intensely to the Irish Revival. Standish O'Grady's histories, Lady Gregory's Cuchulain of Muirthemne (1902), Yeats's Cuchulain plays and poems, and Patrick Pearse's school at St Enda's, where the boy-hero's motto was painted on the wall, turned Cú Chulainn into a model of self-sacrifice for the nation. In 1935 Oliver Sheppard's bronze of the dying Cú Chulainn was placed in the General Post Office in Dublin as the memorial of the Easter Rising. Later the saga was claimed by Ulster loyalists too, as the defender of Ulster against the rest of Ireland. Thomas Kinsella's translation of 1969, with brush drawings by Louis le Brocquy, and Ciaran Carson's of 2007 brought the Irish text, with all its harshness and comedy, back to a wide readership (Gregory 1902) (Kinsella 1970) (Carson 2007) (Kiberd 1995).

Plot overview

In bed at Cruachain, Queen Medb and her husband Ailill compare their wealth. They are equal in everything except that Ailill owns the white-horned bull Finnbennach, which was born in Medb's herd but would not stay under a woman's rule. Medb learns that the only equal bull in Ireland is the Donn Cúailnge, the Brown Bull of Cúailnge in Ulster. When its owner refuses to lend it, she summons the armies of Connacht, Leinster and Munster, with the Ulster exiles led by Fergus mac Róich, to take it by force.

The invasion comes when the Ulstermen are struck down by their periodic debility, the curse laid on them by the goddess Macha. Only the seventeen-year-old Cú Chulainn, who is exempt, stands against the host. He harasses it, kills its warriors in hundreds, and forces Medb to agree to send him one champion a day at a ford, while the army may advance only while each fight lasts. In camp Fergus and others tell the stories of his boyhood deeds; the Morrígan tempts and attacks him; his divine father Lug heals him; the boy-troop of Ulster is massacred, and in the ríastrad, his battle frenzy, Cú Chulainn slaughters the host on Mag Muirthemne.

The longest single combat is with Ferdiad, his foster-brother and fellow pupil of the warrior-woman Scáthach. Medb's bribes and threats force Ferdiad to the ford; after three days of fighting Cú Chulainn kills him with the terrible gae bolga and laments over him. Meanwhile the Donn Cúailnge has been found and driven towards Connacht.

At last Cú Chulainn's mortal father Sualtam rouses the Ulstermen, who rise from their debility and meet the Connacht army at Gáirech and Ilgáirech. Fergus, about to strike Conchobar, turns aside and cuts the tops off three hills; Cú Chulainn rises from his wounds, and Fergus keeps a promise to yield to him, taking his men from the field. The armies of Ireland flee, and Medb, guarded by Cú Chulainn's generosity, returns home. The two bulls fight across all Ireland; the Donn kills Finnbennach, scatters his remains over the country, goes home to Cúailnge and dies. Ailill and Medb make peace with Ulster for seven years.

Part by part

Part 1: The Finding of the Táin: Senchán, the Saints and the Ghost of Fergus

The Finding of the Táin

Summary

The aged chief poet Shen'-cawn (Senchán Torpéist), charged by the swineherd-saint Mar-vawn' (Marbán), asks the poets of Ireland whether any of them can recite the Táin Bó Cúailnge whole. None can: they know only fragments, and the book in which it was written has been carried off to 'Latin lands'. The poets search Ireland and then Scotland for a year without success.

On their return Senchán's kinsman, Saint Caillín, takes them to Marbán, who tells them that no one among the living or the dead knows the whole tale except Fergus mac Róich. The saints of Ireland, Colum Cille, the two Ciaráns, Brendan and the two Finnians among them, gather at Fergus's grave and fast for three days. Senchán sings a poem to the gravestone; a mist closes round them, and Fergus rises in his old splendour, takes the seat they courteously give him, and teaches them the Táin from beginning to end.

Analysis

Hutton opens with a medieval story about the Táin rather than with the Táin itself. The tale of its recovery (Do Fallsigud Tána Bó Cuailnge, 'On the Finding of the Táin') survives in the Book of Leinster and elsewhere, and it reflects a real situation: the medieval literati knew that their great saga existed in broken and competing versions. The claim that the book had gone abroad 'in exchange for' a Latin text, Isidore's Etymologies in one version, shows the learned class measuring native tradition against the Latin learning of the Church (O'Rahilly 1967) (Ó Cathasaigh 2014).

The frame makes a pagan witness speak to Christian saints. Fergus, who took part in the events, is the only authority the poets can trust, but he can be reached only by prayer and fasting. The story thus gives the Church the role of custodian of the native past, a point central to the argument that the sagas are learned monastic literature rather than raw survivals of pagan oral poetry (McCone 1990). Hutton's list of saints, with their gentle animal companions and golden moons of holiness, is her own idealising expansion.

Part 2: The Pillow Talk and the Two Bulls

Book I

Summary

At sunset Ailill and Medb look out over their rich land from the mound of Cruachain. In bed that night Ailill remarks that it is good to be the wife of a wealthy man, and Medb replies that she was richer than he when they married. She recounts her descent from the high-king Eochaid Feidlech, her suitors, and the three conditions she set for a husband: that he be without meanness, jealousy or fear. Ailill, she says, lives on a woman's wealth.

Their possessions are brought out and counted: vessels, jewels, clothes, flocks, herds and horses. They are equal in everything except that Ailill owns the white-horned bull Finnbennach, which was calved in Medb's herd but went over to the king's because it would not be ruled by a woman. The herald Mac Roth tells Medb that the only equal bull is the Donn Cúailnge, owned by Dáire mac Fiachna in Ulster, and a druid recounts the earlier lives of the two bulls as rival swineherds who passed through many shapes.

Medb tells her daughter Findabair why she hates Ulster and its king Conchobar, who was once her husband. She sends Mac Roth with nine men to borrow the Donn for a year, offering rich rewards. Dáire agrees, but at the feast that night one of the messengers boasts that the bull would have been taken by force anyway. Dáire withdraws his consent, and Medb swears that the Donn will come to Cruachain.

Analysis

The pillow talk is found only in Recension II and Hutton follows it; Recension I begins instead with the mustering of the armies at Cruachain. The scene is comic and pointed at once. Under Irish law the partner who brought more property to a marriage had the greater say, and the quarrel about who is richer is a quarrel about authority (Kelly 1988) (O'Rahilly 1967). Medb's three conditions, a husband without meanness, jealousy or fear, are a portrait of the ideal king, and they show that she sees herself as the one who chooses kings.

Cattle were the principal measure of wealth in early Ireland, and a bull was the pride of a herd (Kelly 1997). Yet the two bulls are no ordinary animals: the druid's account of their transformations (told in the fore-tale De Chophur in Dá Muccida, 'The Quarrel of the Two Swineherds') makes them reborn rivals whose feud is older than the war it causes. Hutton, following the medieval tradition, gives Medb a long autobiography and a personal grudge against Conchobar that the oldest version lacks; the effect is to make her a wronged woman as well as an ambitious queen.

The raid begins in a broken promise of hospitality. Dáire is willing to lend the bull until a drunken messenger boasts; the war arises from words spoken at a feast, a recurring pattern in early Irish saga.

Part 3: The Hosting Summoned; Leborcham's Tale of Deirdre and the Sons of Uisliu

Book II

Summary

Messengers summon Medb's seven sons, the Maines, and the kings and chiefs of the Four Fifths of Ireland to Cruachain. While the hosts gather, the poetess Lowercam (Leborcham) is asked to tell a story at the feast, and Medb chooses the tale of the sons of Uisliu, hoping to stir Fergus against Conchobar.

Leborcham tells how the druid Cathbad prophesied over the unborn Derdriu (Hutton's Daerdra) that she would be beautiful and would bring ruin on Ulster. Conchobar has her reared in seclusion to be his wife. Seeing a raven drink calf's blood on the snow, she longs for a man with hair like the raven, cheeks like blood and skin like snow, and she claims Noísiu, son of Uisliu, who flees with her and his brothers to Scotland. Conchobar sends Fergus to promise them safety; Fergus is held back by a feast he is bound by geis to attend, and the sons of Uisliu are killed at Emain Macha by Eógan mac Durthacht. Fergus takes vengeance, burns Emain and goes into exile in Connacht with three thousand men. Derdriu, after a year of grief with Conchobar, dashes her head against a rock.

Fergus leaves the feast in tears. Afterwards Medb finds him on the mound and urges him to lead the host against the king who dishonoured his guarantee, reminding him that she has loved him and borne him sons.

Analysis

Here Hutton departs most clearly from the medieval Táin, which refers to the exile of Fergus without telling the story. She inserts a full retelling of Longes mac nUislenn ('The Exile of the Sons of Uisliu'), one of the fore-tales, so that the reader learns why the greatest of the Ulster heroes is fighting for Connacht. The Deirdre story was the best known of all Irish tales during the Revival, retold by Yeats, Synge and AE, and its inclusion shows how Hutton read the Táin through the literature of her own day (Hutton 1924) (Gregory 1902).

The tale is a lesson in broken guarantees. Fergus's honour depends on the safety of those he protects, and Conchobar's treachery makes him an exile with a grievance. Derdriu's accusation, that he sold his honour for an ale-feast, shows how the obligations of geis and hospitality can trap a hero. The divided loyalty of Fergus, who loves Ulster but marches against it, becomes one of the main threads of the Táin.

Part 4: The Muster at Cruachain, the Curse of Macha and Fedelm's Prophecy

Book III

Summary

The hosts arrive on Mag Aí: Medb's seven sons, all called Maine because a druid told her that Conchobar would be killed by Maine; the seven sons of Mágach; the men of Munster and Leinster; Fráech with his fairy-gifted company; and Cormac Conloingeas, Conchobar's exiled son, with three bands. The army waits fifteen days for good omens.

Uneasy, Medb consults her druid, who explains why Ulster will be helpless: the curse of Macha. The wife of Crunniuc, a woman from the otherworld, was forced by the king of Ulster to race his horses while she was near her time. She won, gave birth to twins at the finish, and cursed the men of Ulster to suffer the weakness of a woman in labour whenever they were in greatest need. Only women, boys and one future hero, Cú Chulainn, are exempt.

As Medb drives away she meets a young woman standing on the shaft of a chariot, the poetess Fedelm from the fairy mound of Cruachain. Asked how she sees the host, Fedelm answers again and again, 'I see them red', and in a final vision describes a young warrior with the shape of a dragon in battle, whose fury distorts him: Cú Chulainn.

Analysis

The curse of Macha (Noínden Ulad, 'the debility of the Ulstermen') is told in its own short tale and only alluded to in the Táin; Hutton places the story in the mouth of a druid. It is the device that makes the plot possible: a whole province is disabled, and a boy must defend it. Macha is one of several figures linked with sovereignty and the land, and the curse binds the warrior aristocracy of Ulster to a woman's suffering they refused to respect (Ó Cathasaigh 2014) (McCone 1990).

Fedelm's prophecy is a rosc in Recension I, one of the archaic verse passages, and its refrain is among the most memorable lines of the saga. Medb tries to explain away each warning, and the scene sets her confidence against fate. It is also the first portrait of Cú Chulainn, seen from the enemy side as something monstrous before he appears as a boy.

Part 5: The March to Ulster, the Withe and the Forked Pole

Book IV

Summary

The army sets out from Cruachain, south-east across Connacht. Medb fears the three thousand Leinstermen, the Gaileóin, who are better soldiers than all the rest and might turn against her; she wants them killed, but Fergus protects them and they are dispersed among the other troops. Fergus, who is guiding the army, leads it on long detours to give Ulster time, and secretly warns his foster-son. When Medb reproaches him he says he is keeping clear of a certain 'Slaughter-hound' of Mag Muirthemne.

At the Ulster border the host finds a hoop twisted from an oak sapling, cut and made with one hand, standing on one foot and seeing with one eye, and marked with an ogham inscription that forbids them to pass until one of them makes a hoop in the same way. The army goes round through the forest and camps in heavy snow. The next day the two scouts Err and Innel and their charioteers are found beheaded, their heads set on the prongs of a forked branch driven into a ford with one cast, which is named Áth Gabla. Fergus breaks seventeen chariots pulling it out, and finally frees it one-handed.

Fráech's three harpers play the strains of sorrow, laughter and sleep. Ailill asks who could have done these deeds; Fergus rules out the great warriors of Ulster one by one and names Cú Chulainn, his foster-son, praising him at length. Medb replies that he has only one body. Fergus begins to tell of the boy's youth.

Analysis

The episodes at the border show Cú Chulainn's method before we see him. He fights through signs, conditions and bargains as much as through strength: the ogham hoop imposes a geis on the army, and the forked branch is both a threat and a riddle. The one-handed, one-eyed, one-footed posture is ritual, and has been compared with the stance of figures who work magic in other Irish tales (Ó Cathasaigh 2014). The episode also shows the Táin's interest in place names. Many incidents end with a dindshenchas, an account of how a ford or hill received its name.

Fergus's position is now plain. He is the guide of the Connacht army and secretly works against it, and the narrative enjoys his double dealing. Medb's suspicion of the Leinstermen, whom she would kill because they are too good, is a first glimpse of the ruthlessness that critics have read either as political realism or as hostile caricature (O'Rahilly 1967).

Part 6: The Boyhood Deeds of Cú Chulainn

Book V

Summary

In camp the Ulster exiles tell the macgnímrada, the boyhood deeds. Fergus tells how the five-year-old Sétanta, son of Súaltam and Deichtire, left his home at Dún Delga for Emain Macha, playing with toy javelin, shield, hurley and ball on the way. He ran onto the playing field without asking the protection of the boy-troop, fought off all of them, and was taken under Conchobar's protection.

Cormac Conloingeas tells how, a year later, the boy went late to a feast given by the smith Culann and was attacked by the smith's huge hound, which he killed by dashing it against a pillar stone. When Culann lamented the loss of his guardian, the boy offered to take its place until a whelp could be reared, and the druid Cathbad named him Cú Chulainn, 'the Hound of Culann'.

Fíacha mac Fir Febe, an Ulster exile, tells how at seven the boy overheard Cathbad say that whoever took arms that day would be famous but short-lived, and demanded arms from Conchobar, breaking every set until he was given the king's own. He drove to the border, killed the three sons of Nechta Scéne, who had slain many Ulstermen, and returned with their heads on his chariot, a flock of white birds tethered above it and a wild stag running behind. Conchobar recognised his sister's son, and the boy was plunged into three vats of cold water until his battle-heat was cooled.

Analysis

The boyhood deeds form a self-contained section of the Táin, told in retrospect as stories in the enemy camp. The device lets the narrative stop while the army is held at the border, and it lets Fergus and Cormac, Ulstermen in exile, express their pride in the hero they are fighting. Each tale ends with the same point: why wonder that such a boy, now seventeen, should hold off an army? (O'Rahilly 1976)

The episodes follow a pattern found in heroic biographies across many cultures: precocious strength, a new name, a choice of short life and lasting fame, and a first raid beyond the frontier (Ó Cathasaigh 2014). The choice at seven, 'one day and one night' of life if only his deeds are remembered, became the motto of Pearse's school. The cooling in the vats (in the Irish preceded by the women of Emain coming out naked to shame the boy, an episode Hutton leaves out) shows the community's need to tame the force it depends on: Cú Chulainn's heat is necessary in war and dangerous at home (Dooley 2006).

Part 7: Ferdiad's Tale, I: The Wooing of Emer

Book VI

Summary

In the royal tent at Áth Gabla the young warrior Ferdiad of the Fir Domnann, who trained with Cú Chulainn under Scáthach, offers to tell Ailill and Medb how the hero was trained in arms. He begins at Emain Macha with a description of Conchobar's hall, the Cráebrúad or Red Branch, and of its warriors. At fifteen Cú Chulainn is loved by the women of Ulster, and the men, anxious that he should leave a son, look for a wife for him. He has already chosen Emer, daughter of Forgall Manach, who has the six gifts of a woman: beauty, voice, sweet speech, needlework, wisdom and chastity.

Cú Chulainn drives to her father's house with Láeg and the horses Líath Macha and Dub Sainglenn. He and Emer talk in riddles that her companions cannot follow; he boasts of his fosterage by the wise men of Ulster and the favour of the god Lug, and she sets him impossible conditions, among them that he must kill a hundred men at every ford. As they drive north he explains the riddles to Láeg, with the stories of the places he named.

Analysis

Books VI and VII are Hutton's longest addition to the Táin. She makes Ferdiad tell the tale Tochmarc Emire ('The Wooing of Emer'), a separate saga that survives in several versions, so that the reader knows the bond between the two foster-brothers before they meet at the ford. The device copies the boyhood deeds of Book V, in which the exiles tell stories in the enemy camp, and it gives Ferdiad a voice and a history that the Táin itself barely sketches (Hutton 1924) (Thurneysen 1921).

The riddling dialogue is a display of learning. Its obscure metaphors, place lore and allusions to gods resemble the style of the rosc, and they present the hero as a master of words as well as arms, as the Irish poets themselves wished to be seen. The conditions Emer sets anticipate the slaughter at the fords in the Táin.

Part 8: Ferdiad's Tale, II: Training with Scáthach and the Gae Bolga

Book VII

Summary

Forgall, disguised as a foreign envoy, persuades Cú Chulainn to seek training abroad, hoping he will not return. Cú Chulainn and Emer pledge themselves to each other, and he sails with Conall Cernach and Lóegaire. A false vision sends his companions home, and he goes on alone, guided by a beast and by a youth who gives him a wheel and an apple to lead him across the Plain of Ill Luck and a perilous glen, to the island fortress of the warrior-woman Scáthach. There he finds Ferdiad and other pupils from Ireland.

He crosses the Bridge of the Cliff at the third attempt with his 'salmon-leap', and on the advice of Scáthach's daughter Úathach forces Scáthach, with his sword at her breast, to grant him three wishes: full training, marriage to her daughter and prophecy of his future. He learns the feats, fights in Scáthach's war against Aífe, and Scáthach lays a geis on her pupils never to fight each other. She gives him alone the gae bolga and sings of what awaits him: the Táin, the great slaughter, and a short life of lasting fame. On the way home he rescues a princess from the Fomorians. Ferdiad ends his story with an oath that he would rather kill himself than kill Cú Chulainn.

Analysis

The training with Scáthach completes the hero's education, and it is the shared past that makes the later fight so painful. Scáthach, a woman who teaches war in a land beyond the sea, is one of several powerful female figures in the Ulster Cycle; her prophecy, which in the Irish is an archaic rosc, here becomes a song in short rhyming lines (Ó Cathasaigh 2014).

The episode is full of tests of a kind familiar from international folk tale, the dangerous bridge, the helpful guide, the magical gifts, and the gae bolga is its prize. Ferdiad's oath at the end is irony of the sharpest kind: the reader already knows from the Táin that he will meet Cú Chulainn at the ford (Hutton 1924).

Part 9: The Early Exploits on the Border; the Morrígan

Book VIII

Summary

The hosts move on. Cú Chulainn fells an oak across their path with an ogham geis, and thirty chariots are wrecked leaping it. Fráech is sent against him and drowned as they wrestle in a river; women in green carry his body into the fairy mound. Cú Chulainn kills men in sixes and in threes, and with sling-stones kills Medb's pet dog, bird and squirrel. He shapes a holly shaft so smooth that a fly could not settle on it, frightens the charioteer he is helping by revealing who he is, and kills his master Orlám, Ailill's son.

A red woman with a one-legged horse appears to him; she offers him her love and help, and when he refuses she threatens to hinder him in his fights as an eel, a wolf and a heifer. Only when she becomes a black bird does he recognise the Morrígan. She warns the Donn Cúailnge, which moves away from the raiders. Ailill steals Fergus's sword while Fergus lies with Medb, and Fergus fits a wooden sword to his sheath. Cú Chulainn's sling kills a hundred men each night, and he refuses Medb's first offers of terms.

Analysis

These short episodes, many of them ending with the name of a place, form the core of the Táin's first half in both recensions (O'Rahilly 1976). They show Cú Chulainn's mixture of courtesy and terror, and the comedy that runs through the saga: a warrior who smooths sticks for an enemy's charioteer and then kills his master.

The Morrígan, 'phantom queen' or 'great queen', is a war goddess whose appearances link sex, prophecy and death. Her offer and threat set up the fight with Lóch in Book X. The stealing of Fergus's sword while he lies with Medb is a sly comic episode that has a serious end: Fergus will fight with a wooden sword until the last battle (Ó Cathasaigh 2014).

Part 10: The Compact of Single Combat; Fergus and Etarcomol

Book IX

Summary

The army camps for three days, losing a hundred men a night to Cú Chulainn's sling. Medb sends her herald Mac Roth to offer terms, and when the hero refuses to see the women and cattle of Ulster become slaves of Connacht, Fergus brings his own condition: one man a day is to fight him at a ford, and while the fight lasts the army may march; when the man is dead it must halt. Medb accepts, reasoning that it is better to lose one man a day than a hundred each night.

Fergus goes to Cú Chulainn and the two greet each other warmly. A young Connacht warrior, Etarcomol, follows Fergus out of curiosity and insults the hero. Cú Chulainn spares him three times, cutting the sod from under his feet, shaving his head and slicing his clothes, and at last cuts him in three. Fergus, whose guarantee of safety has been violated, drags the body back to Medb's camp.

Analysis

The compact turns war into a series of formal duels and so gives the middle of the Táin its shape: a long succession of fords and champions. The ford is the boundary between territories, and in Irish law and story it is the natural place of combat (Kelly 1988). The bargain also shows the importance of guarantees: Fergus binds both sides, and Etarcomol's death, which breaks his protection, is felt as a personal injury.

Cú Chulainn's three warning strokes are a display of control that makes the final stroke deliberate. The young man is killed not by the hero's fury but by his own arrogance. The episode is a small model of the many in which the Connacht champions, tempted or shamed by Medb, walk to their deaths.

Part 11: The Combats at the Fords: Nad Crantail, Fer Báeth and Lóch

Book X

Summary

A long series of champions fails. Nad Crantail attacks with spits of holly and Cú Chulainn stands on each one as it flies; next day Nad Crantail is killed. Cú Chulainn, mocked as beardless, puts on a false beard of grass so that grown men will fight him. His fellow pupil Fer Báeth, tempted by Medb with Findabair, is killed by a holly spike thrown backwards over the hero's shoulder. Láréne, brother of his friend Lugaid, is crushed and spared, but crippled for life.

Medb then brings Lóch mac Mo Femis, protected by a skin of horn. As they fight in the ford the Morrígan attacks Cú Chulainn as an eel, a wolf and a red heifer, as she had threatened; he breaks her ribs, an eye and a leg, and kills Lóch with the gae bolga, granting his request to fall facing east, away from the men of Ireland. Later he meets an old woman milking a cow with three teats, blesses her three times for three drinks, and so heals the Morrígan's three injuries. Meanwhile Medb has found the Donn Cúailnge and driven it away, and the exhausted hero raises a terrible cry from his camp.

Analysis

The single combats repeat a pattern with small variations, and the repetition itself is meaningful: each opponent is lured by gifts, flattery or the promise of Medb's daughter, and each dies. Critics have seen in Medb's repeated offer of Findabair to one man after another the saga's most damaging picture of her, and in Cú Chulainn's reluctance to kill his friends a growing weariness (Dooley 2006) (O'Rahilly 1967).

The fight with Lóch combines the human and divine levels of the Táin. The Morrígan's attack and her later healing show the uneasy relation between the hero and the powers of the otherworld: he wounds her, refuses her and, without knowing it, cures her. The Donn's capture means that Medb has won her object halfway through the story, and what remains is a question of whether she can bring it home.

Part 12: Lug's Healing, the Boy-Troop and the Great Slaughter; Fergus Yields

Book XI

Summary

As Cú Chulainn sits wounded and weary, a tall, shining warrior crosses the Connacht camp unseen by its men. He is Lug son of Ethliu, Cú Chulainn's father from the otherworld, who sings him into a sleep of three days and nights and heals his wounds. While he sleeps the boy-troop of Emain Macha, a hundred and fifty boys led by Follomain son of Conchobar, attacks the host three times and is destroyed.

When Cú Chulainn wakes and hears of it, he arms himself and his chariot, and the ríastrad comes on him: his body turns inside its skin, one eye sinks into his head and the other bulges out on his cheek, sparks rise over his head, his hair stiffens like the branches of a thorn, and a column of dark blood rises from his head like the mast of a ship. He drives round the host and makes the great slaughter of Mag Muirthemne, killing men past counting. Next morning he shows himself in his beauty, and the women climb on the men's shields to look at him.

Fergus is now sent to fight him. At the ford Cú Chulainn agrees to retreat before his foster-father, on condition that Fergus will retreat before him in the final battle. Then Calatín and his twenty-seven sons attack with poisoned weapons and are killed with the help of Fíacha, an Ulster exile.

Analysis

This book contains the most famous image of the Táin, the ríastrad or 'distortion', which Kinsella rendered as the 'warp-spasm' and so gave English a new word. The description is grotesque and exact, and it treats heroic rage as a physical transformation, something that happens to the hero rather than something he chooses. Whether such passages are archaic survivals or the work of learned writers enjoying hyperbole is part of the debate about the saga's origins (Kinsella 1970) (McCone 1990).

The episode also brings together the divine and human sides of the hero. Lug, the god of many skills, appears as his father and heals him, and the boys' deaths give the slaughter the character of revenge. Fergus's retreat before his foster-son, with his promise to be repaid in the final battle, is the hinge on which the end of the Táin turns.

Part 13: The Fight with Ferdiad

Book XII

Summary

Medb holds a council and her chiefs agree that only one man can match Cú Chulainn: Ferdiad son of Damán of the Fir Domnann, his foster-brother, trained with him by Scáthach, and protected by a skin of horn that no weapon can pierce. Ferdiad will not fight his friend, so Medb sends satirists who threaten to raise blisters of shame on his face. At a feast she offers him wealth and her daughter Findabair, and when he still hesitates tells him, falsely, that Cú Chulainn has belittled him. He swears to fight.

Fergus warns Cú Chulainn, and Ferdiad's charioteer recalls the kindnesses of the past. At the ford Cú Chulainn appeals to their friendship and Ferdiad answers with taunts. They fight for three days, first with missiles, then with spears, then with heavy swords; each night they embrace, send each other healing herbs and food, and their horses and charioteers share a camp.

On the fourth day they fight in the ford. Ferdiad wounds Cú Chulainn, and Láeg taunts his master to rouse his fury. Cú Chulainn calls for the gae bolga, which Láeg sends to him down the stream, and casts it with his foot; it enters Ferdiad's body and he dies. Cú Chulainn carries him north across the ford, so that he will lie on the Ulster side, and laments over him. The ford is named Áth Fir Diad, Ardee.

Analysis

The combat with Ferdiad is the emotional centre of the Táin and its most elaborate episode, especially in Recension II, where much of it is in verse dialogue and lament (O'Rahilly 1967). It is the last of the single combats and the one in which the code that governs them becomes tragic. Both men are bound by honour: Ferdiad by the fear of satire, which could destroy a man's standing, and by his oath; Cú Chulainn by his duty to Ulster. The courtesies of the nights, embraces, healing herbs and shared food, sharpen the cruelty of the days.

The gae bolga, the barbed spear that only Scáthach taught to Cú Chulainn, decides the fight unfairly, and the narrative does not hide it. Cú Chulainn's lament, with its refrain of shared training and its bitter judgement on Medb's false promise, is a model for later Irish elegy. Hutton keeps Ferdiad's fatalism, 'What must be, must be', which gives the episode the shape of tragedy rather than a victory (Kinsella 1970) (Tymoczko 1999).

Part 14: Súaltam's Warning and the Rising of Ulster

Book XIII

Summary

Ulster friends carry the wounded Cú Chulainn to the streams of Mag Muirthemne, into which the Túatha Dé Danann throw healing herbs. The armies of Ireland move on, and Medb sets Mac Roth to watch for the Ulstermen. Small bands come out of the debility to fight: old Iliach crushes his enemies with stones and his bare arms before Dóche kills him; the poet Amairgin pelts the host with stones until Cú Roí answers him and a truce is made. The young Rochad mac Faithemain is lured with an offer of Findabair; the kings to whom she has also been promised quarrel and fight, Fergus makes peace, and Findabair dies of shame.

Cú Chulainn's mortal father Súaltam finds him covered in wounds, and the son sends him to Emain Macha. Three times Súaltam cries out that men are being killed, women carried off and cattle driven, but no one may answer him: the Ulstermen may not speak before their king, nor the king before his druids, and the druid Cathbad only rebukes him. Turning in anger, Súaltam falls on the rim of his own shield, which cuts off his head; the head, carried back on his horse, repeats the cry. Conchobar rises and sends Findchad to summon the men of Ulster, naming a long roll of chiefs. Conchobar and Celtchar rescue captive women at Áth Féne, and Celtchar, Cormac and Dubthach chant prophecies of the battle to come.

Analysis

The waking of Ulster is told in a scene of dark comedy that also makes a serious point. The Ulstermen's custom that no one speaks before the king, and the king not before his druids, turns order into paralysis; it takes a decapitated head to break it. Súaltam, a humble figure beside the divine father Lug, has his one great moment in dying (O'Rahilly 1976). The episode of Findabair, offered to many men and finally destroyed by the offers, makes Medb's use of her daughter a source of open scandal.

Hutton also keeps a passage the medieval text calls one of the chief marvels of the Táin, the death of Iliach, which shows that the elderly and the weak of Ulster can still fight. The long roll of summoned warriors in the Irish is a catalogue in the epic manner, like the catalogue of ships in the Iliad.

Part 15: Mac Roth's Survey of the Ulster Host

Book XIV

Summary

Ailill decides to stand and fight in Meath and sends Mac Roth to watch the plain. Mac Roth twice reports strange signs, a roar like an earthquake and then a grey mist with lights and shapes in it, and Fergus interprets them: the Ulstermen are cutting a road through the forest, and the mist is the breath of men and horses, the lights their eyes and weapons. Fergus warns Medb that no army on earth can withstand the Ulstermen once roused.

Next day Mac Roth describes, band by band, the companies that arrive on the hill of Slane in Meath, and for each one Fergus names the leader and his deeds: Conchobar himself, the sage Sencha, Láegaire Búadach, Amairgin, Celtchar, Munremar, Furbaide, the boy Erc of Tara, and many more. The last band, grieving and leaderless, are the men of Mag Muirthemne, without their lord Cú Chulainn. A cry is heard, and Fergus explains that it is Cú Chulainn struggling to rise from his sickbed. That night the Morrígan cries between the two armies, prophesying slaughter.

Analysis

The survey (often called 'the Array of the Host' or 'the Muster of the Men of Ulster') is a set piece of description in which a watcher describes and an expert identifies. The technique resembles the teichoskopia of Iliad 3, where Helen names the Greek heroes for Priam, though there is no reason to think of influence. The descriptions are among the most ornate passages in the Book of Leinster version, with long runs of epithets, and they offer a gallery of clothes, weapons and hair-styles that encouraged Jackson's reading of the saga as a picture of Iron Age society (Jackson 1964) (O'Rahilly 1967).

Fergus again speaks for Ulster from the Connacht camp. His interpretations turn the enemy's ignorance into fear, and the closing image of the men of Mag Muirthemne without their young lord reminds the audience that the coming battle cannot be decided without Cú Chulainn.

Part 16: The Battle of Gáirech and the Fight of the Bulls

Book XV

Summary

Hearing the Morrígan's cries, Cú Chulainn sends Láeg to rouse Ulster. The Ulstermen rise, and the armies meet at Gáirech and Ilgáirech. Medb goads Fergus with Conchobar's wrongs, and Ailill gives him back his own sword, the Caladbolg. Fergus drives the Ulstermen back three times and strikes Conchobar's shield, which roars. Conchobar answers him; Cormac begs him to spare Ulster, and Fergus turns aside and cuts the tops off three hills, the Máela of Meath.

Cú Chulainn hears the shield and breaks his bonds. He reminds Fergus of his promise, made earlier in the Táin, to yield to him when asked; Fergus withdraws with his men, the Leinster and Munster troops follow, and the Connacht army is left to fight alone. Medb, who has sent the Donn Cúailnge ahead to Cruachain, is overtaken by Cú Chulainn but spared, because he does not kill women. At her request he guards the retreat, and cuts the tops from three hills of his own. Fergus comments that it is what happens to a herd led by a mare.

At Cruachain the Donn Cúailnge meets Finnbennach. The bulls fight all night across Ireland; in the morning the Donn passes Cruachain with the remains of Finnbennach on his horns, scattering them over the land, so that places such as Athlone, 'the ford of the loin', are named after them. He returns to Cúailnge, kills many of the women and children who had come out to mourn him, and dies there, his heart broken.

Analysis

The battle is not won by force. It is decided by a series of personal bonds: Fergus's love for Ulster and his kinship with Conchobar, Cormac's plea, and above all the promise made to Cú Chulainn. The Táin repeatedly makes honour and obligation more powerful than armies (Ó Cathasaigh 2014). Fergus cutting the hilltops is a typical gesture of the saga, violence displaced onto the landscape and remembered in place names.

Fergus's remark about the herd led by a mare is the most quoted judgement on Medb, and it has been read as the moral of the whole saga, a warning against government by women, and as the bitter word of a man who has lost the battle and his own honour (Dooley 2006). The fight of the bulls returns the story to its beginning: the cause of the war destroys itself, the dismembered Finnbennach names the places of Ireland, and the Donn dies at home. The ending is a dindshenchas on the largest scale, mapping the country through the remains of the war's prize.

Part 17: The Writing of the Táin

The Writing of the Táin

Summary

Fergus finishes his tale and returns to his grave; the mist lifts and the saints see only the flagstone and the stream. They disperse, and Senchán goes with Saint Ciarán towards Ciarán's foundation. On the way they pass Cruachain and the burial ground where Ailill and Medb lie in their mound, with their seven sons and the last pagan high-king, Dathí.

They come to the place where Clonmacnoise will one day stand, then only a few wattled cells and an oak church without a single grave. Ciarán takes the hide of the dun cow that fed him as a student, makes it into a book, and writes in it the Táin as Fergus told it, so that it will be known in later days.

Analysis

Hutton's closing frame joins two traditions. The famous manuscript Lebor na hUidre, 'The Book of the Dun Cow', written at Clonmacnoise around 1100, contains the oldest copy of the Táin; medieval legend held that it was made from the hide of Saint Ciarán's cow. Hutton turns that legend into the end of her story and so gives the saga a single, sacred origin (Hutton 1924) (O'Rahilly 1976).

Historically the opposite is true: the Táin we have was compiled from several versions, and the scribes themselves sometimes signal disagreement. Recension II even ends with a colophon in which one scribe blesses those who memorise the tale faithfully, and another, writing in Latin, declares that he does not believe it, since some things in it are the tricks of demons, some poetic inventions and some meant for the pleasure of fools (O'Rahilly 1967). Hutton's version leaves out that sceptical voice.

Characters

Ulster

Cú Chulainn
Hutton's Cucullin. Born Sétanta, son of Deichtire, sister of Conchobar; his mortal father is Súaltam and his divine father the god Lug. He takes the name 'Hound of Culann' after killing the smith's dog, chooses at seven a short life with lasting fame, and is trained in arms by Scáthach. At seventeen, the only man of Ulster free of the debility, he holds the border alone. He is courteous, beautiful, witty and generous, but in battle he undergoes the ríastrad, a monstrous distortion of body and mind.
Conchobar mac Nessa
Hutton's Conor. King of Ulster at Emain Macha and Cú Chulainn's uncle and foster-father. A wise and generous ruler in most tales, he is also the treacherous king who killed the sons of Uisliu and drove Fergus into exile. He lies under the debility for most of the Táin and leads Ulster at Gáirech.
Láeg mac Riangabra
Cú Chulainn's charioteer and constant companion, who watches, reports, tends the horses Líath Macha and Dub Sainglenn, sends his master the gae bolga at the ford, and goads him into fury when he falters against Ferdiad.
Súaltam
Hutton's Sooaltim. Cú Chulainn's mortal father, a modest figure who wakes Ulster with his cry 'men are slain, women carried off, cattle driven' and dies on the rim of his own shield, his head continuing the warning.
Emer
Daughter of Forgall Manach, wooed by Cú Chulainn in riddles and later his wife. She has a large part only in Hutton's inserted Wooing of Emer (Books VI–VII).
Conall Cernach, Lóegaire Búadach, Celtchar and the Ulster heroes
The great warriors of the Red Branch, idle under the curse for most of the Táin. Mac Roth's survey in Book XIV describes them company by company as they arrive for the final battle.
Cathbad and Sencha
Cathbad is the druid of Ulster, who names Cú Chulainn and foretells his short life; Sencha is the wise judge and peacemaker of Ulster, who could calm a quarrel with a word.

Connacht and its allies

Medb
Hutton's Maev. Queen of Connacht and daughter of the high-king, the moving force of the raid. She demands a husband without meanness, jealousy or fear, has lovers including Fergus, offers her daughter to one champion after another, and leads the armies herself. She may be read as a political queen, as a humanised goddess of sovereignty whose union makes kings, or as a figure of misrule shaped by clerical misogyny.
Ailill mac Máta
Hutton's Al-yill. Medb's husband and king of Connacht, owner of the bull Finnbennach. Shrewd, jealous and often ironic, he tolerates Medb's affair with Fergus but steals Fergus's sword while the lovers are together.
Fergus mac Róich
Former king of Ulster, exiled after Conchobar violated his guarantee to the sons of Uisliu. He guides the Connacht army while secretly working against it, tells the story of his foster-son's boyhood, fights with a wooden sword, and in the final battle keeps his promise to retreat before Cú Chulainn. In the frame he is the ghost who dictates the Táin.
Ferdiad mac Damáin
Hutton's Faerdeeah. Cú Chulainn's foster-brother and fellow pupil under Scáthach, protected by a skin of horn. Shamed by Medb's satirists and tempted by her daughter, he fights his friend for three days and is killed by the gae bolga. In Hutton he also narrates the hero's training (Books VI–VII).
Findabair
Daughter of Ailill and Medb, promised to champion after champion as the price of fighting Cú Chulainn. When the deception is exposed she dies of shame.
Cormac Conloingeas
Son of Conchobar, who went into exile with Fergus. He tells part of the boyhood deeds, and at Gáirech persuades Fergus to spare Ulster.
Mac Roth
Medb's chief herald, who carries her offer to Dáire, negotiates with Cú Chulainn and describes the Ulster host in the great survey of Book XIV.
Fráech, Etarcomol, Nad Crantail, Fer Báeth and Lóch
A few of the many champions who meet Cú Chulainn at the fords and die there; Lóch, armoured with horn, is killed with the gae bolga while the Morrígan attacks the hero.

The supernatural

The Morrígan
Hutton's More-reega, the 'great queen' or 'phantom queen', a goddess of war, prophecy and death. She offers Cú Chulainn her love, attacks him as eel, wolf and heifer when he refuses, is healed by his blessing, warns the Donn Cúailnge, and cries prophecies of slaughter over the armies.
Lug
Hutton's Loo, son of Ethliu, the god of many arts and Cú Chulainn's father from the otherworld. He heals his son in a three-day sleep while the boy-troop dies.
Macha
The otherworld woman forced to race the king's horses while pregnant; dying in childbirth at the finish, she curses the men of Ulster with weakness in their hour of need. Emain Macha is named after her.
Fedelm
The prophetess from the fairy mound who tells Medb, again and again, 'I see them red', and gives the first description of Cú Chulainn.
Scáthach
The warrior-woman beyond the sea who trains Cú Chulainn and Ferdiad, gives the gae bolga to Cú Chulainn alone and prophesies his career.
The Donn Cúailnge and Finnbennach
The two great bulls, once rival swineherds of otherworld kings who passed through many shapes. The Brown Bull of Cúailnge is the object of the raid; the White-horned Bull of Ailill's herd dies in their final fight.

Others

Senchán Torpéist
Hutton's Shen'-cawn, chief poet of Ireland in the seventh century, who recovers the lost Táin from the ghost of Fergus.
Saint Ciarán
Founder of Clonmacnoise. In Hutton's closing frame he writes the Táin on the hide of his dun cow, the legendary origin of Lebor na hUidre.

Themes

Heroism and its excesses

Cú Chulainn embodies the heroic ideal of the saga: courage, skill, loyalty to Ulster, courtesy to enemies and the choice of fame over long life. Yet the Táin shows the cost of that ideal. His fury must be cooled in vats before he can re-enter society, his feats are as grotesque as they are admirable, and his greatest victory, over Ferdiad, leaves him broken with grief.

Recent critics read the saga as an exploration rather than a celebration of heroism. Ann Dooley's study treats the Táin as a text in which heroic roles are tested, played and sometimes mocked, and argues that its comedy and grotesquerie are part of its meaning (Dooley 2006) (Ó Cathasaigh 2014).

Sovereignty, kingship and the land

The war begins in a dispute over who rules a marriage and ends with the landscape of Ireland named from the remains of a bull. Medb's three conditions for a husband describe the virtues of a king; Fergus, a deposed king, fights against the king who deposed him; and Conchobar's authority is both necessary and compromised.

Many scholars connect Medb, whose name may mean 'the intoxicating one', with the goddess of sovereignty who in Irish tradition marries the rightful king. Read in that light, her many husbands and lovers reflect a mythical role rather than personal vice (Ó Cathasaigh 2014) (McCone 1990).

Honour, oaths and obligation

Nearly every turn of the plot is determined by a promise. Fergus's broken guarantee to the sons of Uisliu causes his exile; his guarantee to Etarcomol is violated; his promise to retreat decides the final battle. Ferdiad is forced to fight by fear of satire, which could destroy a man's honour, and by an oath made in drink.

The geis, a personal prohibition or obligation that a hero must not break, works in the same way. The Táin repeatedly sets one obligation against another, and its tragedies arise when honour demands two incompatible things (Kelly 1988).

Women, men and power

The Táin is full of powerful women: Medb, the Morrígan, Macha, Scáthach, Fedelm. The curse that disables Ulster is the curse of a woman humiliated by men, and it inflicts on them the pains of labour. At the same time the saga's best-known verdict, Fergus's remark about a herd led by a mare, condemns female leadership. How to weigh these elements is one of the main questions of modern criticism (Dooley 2006).

Friendship and fosterage

Fosterage, the practice of raising children in another family, created bonds as strong as kinship in early Ireland (Kelly 1988). The Táin is a tale of foster-relationships: Fergus and Cú Chulainn, Cú Chulainn and Ferdiad, Cú Chulainn and the whole court of Ulster. The war is painful precisely because it sets foster-kin against each other, and the fight at the ford is the most extreme case.

Landscape and naming

Many episodes end by explaining a place name: Áth Gabla, Áth Fir Diad, the Máela of Meath, Athlone. This lore, dindshenchas, was a recognised branch of learning, and the Táin can be read as a map of the route from Cruachain to Cúailnge written in blood. The final journey of the Donn Cúailnge spreads the war's prize across the whole country.

Motifs

The ford

Almost every combat takes place at a ford, the boundary between territories and a place where crossing can be refused. Fords give their names to the dead: Áth Gabla, Áth Fir Diad, Áth Lúain (Athlone).

Prophecy

Fedelm, Cathbad, Scáthach and the Morrígan foretell what will happen, and the characters act in full knowledge of doom. Prophecy is often given in the archaic verse of the rosc.

Satire and shame

The threat of satire, which could raise blisters on the face and destroy a man's honour, is a weapon Medb uses to force champions to fight.

Stories told in camp

The boyhood deeds (and, in Hutton, the Wooing of Emer and the training with Scáthach) are told by characters in the enemy tents. The device fills the long pauses of the march and lets enemies praise the hero.

Transformation

The bulls pass through many forms, the Morrígan becomes bird, eel, wolf, heifer and old woman, and Cú Chulainn's body is transformed in his battle fury.

Symbols

The Donn Cúailnge

The object of the raid, a bull so large that fifty boys could play on his back. He stands for the wealth and the dignity of Ulster, and his end, killing his own people and dying at home, suggests the self-destruction that the war has brought.

The gae bolga

The barbed spear that only Cú Chulainn can use, the gift of Scáthach. It decides the two most important combats and represents a power beyond fair fighting.

The ríastrad

The distortion of the hero's body is the visible sign of his fury, a force that protects Ulster but has to be cooled before he can return to society.

Ogham and the withe

The inscribed hoop and branch with which Cú Chulainn halts the army show writing as a form of power, and recall that this oral tradition survives only because it was written.

Critical debates

A window on the Iron Age?

In 1964 Kenneth Jackson argued that the Ulster Cycle preserves a genuine picture of Irish society in the centuries before Christianity, an aristocracy of chariot warriors who took heads as trophies, feasted in great halls and settled disputes by single combat. He compared the details with the accounts of the Gauls by Classical writers such as Posidonius and concluded that the tales give a 'window on the Iron Age' (Jackson 1964).

Later scholars have been more sceptical. N. B. Aitchison argued that the heroic image is a product of the period when the tales were written, and J. P. Mallory has compared the material culture of the sagas with archaeology and found that much of it, such as the swords, brooches and large halls, belongs to the early Christian period rather than the Iron Age (Aitchison 1987) (Mallory 1992) (Mallory 2013).

Pagan survival or monastic literature?

The older view, which went back to the nineteenth century, treated the sagas as relics of pagan oral tradition, written down by monks who barely understood them. Kim McCone's Pagan Past and Christian Present (1990) argued instead that early Irish narrative is a learned literature created in monastic schools, whose authors knew the Bible and Latin learning and used native tradition for their own purposes, and that it should be read as consciously crafted literature (McCone 1990).

The debate is not simply either/or. Many scholars accept that the tales were composed by Christian literati while drawing on older oral tradition. Tomás Ó Cathasaigh's essays show how much of the saga's meaning can be found in its literary structure, whatever the sources of its material (Ó Cathasaigh 2014).

Oral tradition and the written text

The story of how the Táin was recovered from Fergus's ghost suggests that the medieval literati themselves believed the tale had once existed whole in oral form. Recension I, with its doublets and its notes that 'other versions' tell a story differently, looks like a compilation of written sources rather than the transcript of a performance, and Recension II is clearly a literary rewriting (O'Rahilly 1976) (O'Rahilly 1967).

Thurneysen's analysis of the strata of Recension I remains the starting point, and the essays in Ulidia and Aspects of the Táin discuss how the various layers came together (Thurneysen 1921) (Mallory 1994) (Mallory 1992).

Medb: queen, goddess or caricature?

Medb has been read in at least three ways. As a historical or literary queen, she is a ruler who demands equality with her husband and leads her own armies. As a goddess of sovereignty humanised by the tradition, she is the figure through whose union kings gain the right to rule, which would explain her many husbands. As a creation of clerical writers, she is a caricature of female rule, ambitious, promiscuous and destructive, and Fergus's verdict on her is the moral of the story (Ó Cathasaigh 2014) (McCone 1990).

Dooley and other recent critics argue that the saga is more ambivalent than any one of these readings allows, and that Medb's portrayal changes between the recensions, Recension II making her both grander and more blameworthy (Dooley 2006).

The nature of Cú Chulainn's heroism

For the Revival Cú Chulainn was an ideal of self-sacrifice: the lonely defender of his people, choosing fame over life. Medieval readers, however, could also see him as an excessive, even monstrous figure, and the saga juxtaposes his beauty with his distortion, his courtesy with his slaughter (Dooley 2006).

Critics now ask whether the Táin admires heroism without reserve or exposes its limits. The fight with Ferdiad, where heroic obligation destroys the closest of friendships, and the boy-troop's deaths, which provoke a slaughter past counting, are central to the argument (Ó Cathasaigh 2014) (Kinsella 1970).

The Táin and the Irish nation

Lady Gregory's Cuchulain of Muirthemne (1902), with a preface by Yeats, gave the saga a polished Hiberno-English form and made it a classic of the Revival. Pearse held Cú Chulainn up to his pupils as a model, and after 1916 the hero became a symbol of the Rising, commemorated in Sheppard's statue in the General Post Office. Yeats wrote about the danger of that identification in 'The Statues' (Gregory 1902) (Kiberd 1995).

Maria Tymoczko has shown how early translators shaped the Irish material for their purposes, softening or removing sexual and grotesque episodes and making the hero noble rather than strange. Hutton's version belongs to that tradition: it omits, for instance, the women of Emain who come out naked to meet the boy hero (Tymoczko 1999) (Hutton 1924).

Translating the Táin

Every English version makes choices about the mixture of prose and verse. Kinsella's translation of 1969, based mainly on Recension I with additions from the Book of Leinster, keeps prose for narrative and renders the rosc as dense, broken verse; it restored the saga's harshness and humour for a modern readership. Ciaran Carson's 2007 version brings an Ulster poet's ear to the text and also follows Recension I (Kinsella 1970) (Carson 2007).

Earlier versions show other priorities: the prose translations of Winifred Faraday and Joseph Dunn aimed at fidelity, Gregory and Hutton at literary dignity, and O'Rahilly's editions at scholarly accuracy (Faraday 1904) (Dunn 1914) (O'Rahilly 1967) (O'Rahilly 1976).

Questions for study

  1. Why does the Táin begin with a quarrel in bed about property?

    Think about Irish marriage law, Medb's three conditions and the link between wealth and authority.

  2. What does the curse of Macha contribute to the story, and what does it say about the men of Ulster?

    Consider who is exempt, what kind of weakness it is, and why it was imposed.

  3. Why are the boyhood deeds told by Fergus and other exiles in the Connacht camp?

    Think about the pause in the action, the speakers' loyalties and the refrain that ends each tale.

  4. How does the Táin present Cú Chulainn's battle-fury? Is it admirable, frightening or both?

    Look at the ríastrad, the vats of cold water and his appearance the morning after the slaughter.

  5. Why is the fight with Ferdiad the emotional climax of the saga?

    Consider the foster-bond, the courtesies of the nights, satire, the gae bolga and the lament.

  6. Is Medb a villain?

    Weigh the pillow talk, her use of Findabair, the sovereignty-goddess reading and Fergus's final verdict.

  7. What role do the supernatural figures play?

    Compare the Morrígan, Lug, Fedelm and Macha: do they help, hinder or simply foretell?

  8. How does Fergus's divided loyalty shape the plot?

    Trace his detours, his stories, the wooden sword and his promise to retreat.

  9. What is gained and lost when a prose saga with verse passages is retold entirely in blank verse, as by Hutton?

    Compare Hutton's style with what you know of the Irish mixture of prose and rosc, and think about her additions such as the Wooing of Emer.

  10. Why did the Táin matter to the Irish Revival, and what did the Revival change in it?

    Think about Gregory, Yeats, Pearse and the GPO statue, and about what early translators left out.
