Overview

Author
William Shakespeare (1564–1616), actor, shareholder and playwright of the Lord Chamberlain's Men
Genre
History play, mixing blank verse for the court and the rebels with prose for the tavern
Date
Written c. 1596–97; entered for publication in February 1598 and printed that year
Setting
England and Wales, 1402–1403: the court in London, the Boar's Head tavern in Eastcheap, the road at Gad's Hill, the rebels' bases, and the battlefield of Shrewsbury
Main source
Raphael Holinshed's Chronicles (1587), with Samuel Daniel's The Civil Wars (1595) and the anonymous play The Famous Victories of Henry V
Protagonists
Prince Hal, heir to the throne; Sir John Falstaff; Hotspur (Harry Percy); King Henry IV
Major conflict
The Percy family's rebellion against the king they helped to crown, and Hal's choice between the tavern and his father's court
Climax
The Battle of Shrewsbury (5.4), where Hal saves his father and kills Hotspur in single combat
Sequel
The Second Part of King Henry the Fourth, which ends with Hal crowned as Henry V and Falstaff rejected
Name controversy
Falstaff was first called Sir John Oldcastle; the name was changed after protests from Oldcastle's descendants, the Lords Cobham

Context

Henry IV, Part 1 belongs to Shakespeare's second group of English history plays, the 'second tetralogy': Richard II, the two parts of Henry IV and Henry V. Written around 1596–97, it follows directly from Richard II, in which Henry Bolingbroke deposed his cousin and took the crown. The guilt of that act, and the debts Henry owes to the nobles who helped him, drive the political plot of this play. Its sequel, Part 2, carries the story to Henry's death and Hal's accession; scholars still debate whether Shakespeare planned the two parts together or wrote the second after the first succeeded (Hunter 1954) (Jenkins 1956) (Hawkins 1982).

Shakespeare's main source was the second edition of Raphael Holinshed's Chronicles (1587), which he used for the rebellion of the Percys, the quarrel over the Scottish prisoners, the alliance with Glendower and Mortimer, and the battle of Shrewsbury (Holinshed 1587) (Bullough 1962). He changed history freely. In Holinshed, Hotspur was older than Henry IV himself; Shakespeare makes him Hal's age so that the two can be rivals. Holinshed does not say who killed Hotspur; Shakespeare gives the deed to Hal. The rescue of the King from Douglas comes from Samuel Daniel's poem The Civil Wars, and the legend of the wild prince who reforms comes from popular tradition and from the old play The Famous Victories of Henry V (Saccio 1977).

The comic plot is largely Shakespeare's own. Its central figure, Sir John Falstaff, was originally named Sir John Oldcastle after a real knight in the old play, a friend of Henry V who was executed in 1417 as a Lollard heretic and later honoured by Protestants as a martyr. Oldcastle's descendants, the Brooke family, Lords Cobham, objected, and the name was changed, probably after Shakespeare's first performances. Traces remain: Hal calls Falstaff 'my old lad of the castle' in 1.2, and the epilogue to Part 2 insists that 'Oldcastle died a martyr, and this is not the man' (Taylor 1985) (Taylor 1987) (Corbin 1991).

The play was one of the most popular of its age. It was reprinted in quarto again and again before the First Folio of 1623, and Falstaff became a celebrity whose name was quoted in letters and who, according to a later legend, Queen Elizabeth wished to see in love, which led to The Merry Wives of Windsor. Modern editions are based on the first quarto of 1598 (Bevington 1987).

Criticism has long turned on three questions. The first is Falstaff: is he a coward and a parasite, or the play's great voice of life and wit? The second is Hal: is his reformation a sincere redemption or a political performance? The third is the play's view of history: does it support the Tudor idea of order restored, or does it show authority as theatre and honour as an empty word (Tillyard 1944) (Greenblatt 1988) (Rackin 1990)?

Plot overview

King Henry IV, who took the crown from Richard II, longs for peace and a crusade but faces rebellion on his borders. His son and heir, Prince Hal, spends his time in the taverns of Eastcheap with Sir John Falstaff, a fat, old, witty knight, and his band of petty thieves. Meanwhile Harry Percy, known as Hotspur, the brave son of the Earl of Northumberland, defeats the Scots and then quarrels with the King over his prisoners. His uncle Worcester draws him into a plot with the Scottish Earl of Douglas, the Welsh leader Owen Glendower and Edmund Mortimer.

Hal agrees to a joke: Falstaff and his friends will rob travellers at Gad's Hill, and Hal and Poins, in disguise, will rob them in turn. Falstaff runs away and later tells a ridiculous story of fighting off dozens of attackers. When Hal is called to court, he and Falstaff act out his interview with his father in a play improvised in the tavern. In soliloquy, Hal has already told the audience that he will throw off his companions in time.

The rebels meet, divide England on a map and quarrel among themselves. The King rebukes Hal, who promises to redeem himself by defeating Hotspur. Falstaff takes command of a troop of ragged conscripts. At Shrewsbury, Northumberland and Glendower fail to arrive, and Worcester hides the King's offer of pardon from Hotspur. Before the battle, Falstaff decides that honour is only a word.

In the battle Hal saves his father from Douglas and kills Hotspur in single combat. Falstaff, who has played dead to escape Douglas, rises, stabs Hotspur's body and claims the kill, and Hal lets the lie stand. Worcester and Vernon are executed, Douglas is released, and the King divides his army to deal with the rebels who remain, which leads into Part 2.

Scene by scene

Scene 1: Act I, Scene 1: A king without peace

Act I, Scene 1 (the whole scene)

Summary

King Henry, worn out by civil strife, announces that England will at last unite in a crusade to the Holy Land. Westmoreland brings bad news: Edmund Mortimer has been defeated and captured by the Welsh rebel Owen Glendower. A second report is happier. Harry Percy, called Hotspur, son of the Earl of Northumberland, has routed the Scots under the Earl of Douglas at Holmedon and taken many noble prisoners.

The King envies Northumberland such a son and wishes that a fairy had swapped the babies in their cradles, for his own heir, Prince Harry, lives in riot. Yet Hotspur refuses to hand over his prisoners, and the King postpones the crusade and summons the Percys to court.

Analysis

The opening speech sets the play's political tone. Henry took the crown from Richard II, and his longed-for crusade is both a penance for that guilt and a policy for keeping restless nobles busy abroad. Each time it is announced it is postponed; in 2 Henry IV he will die in a room called Jerusalem. Critics who read the histories as a providential sequence see the civil wars of these plays as the price of Richard's deposition (Tillyard 1944); others stress how coolly the King calculates (Saccio 1977).

The scene also sets up the play's central comparison. Before we meet either young man, the father has paired Hal and Hotspur as rivals, one the model of honour and the other its stain. The rest of the drama tests whether that judgement is right.

Scene 2: Act I, Scene 2: Hal, Falstaff and the plan for Gad's Hill

Act I, Scene 2 (the whole scene)

Summary

In the prince's London lodging, Hal and Sir John Falstaff trade insults about time, drink, thieving and the gallows. Falstaff asks Hal not to hang thieves when he is king and calls himself one of the moon's gentlemen. Poins arrives with a plan: travellers carrying money will pass Gad's Hill early next morning, and the friends will rob them. Hal at first refuses, then agrees to a second scheme. He and Poins will let Falstaff and the others commit the robbery, then rob the robbers in disguise and enjoy the lies Falstaff tells afterwards.

Left alone, Hal speaks the soliloquy 'I know you all'. He will tolerate his companions for a while, he says, like the sun letting clouds hide it, so that his reformation will shine all the brighter when he casts them off.

Analysis

The prose exchange introduces Falstaff as a master of wit who turns every charge into a joke, and Hal as his equal in repartee. Falstaff's request that thieves be called 'Diana's foresters' gives the tavern world its own festive language, set against the timed and dutiful world of the court (Barber 1959).

The soliloquy is the most debated speech in the play. Some readers take it as Shakespeare's reassurance to an audience who knew that the wild prince became the hero of Agincourt; others hear a cold politician planning to use his friends (Greenblatt 1988). The imagery is that of money and debt: Hal will 'pay the debt' he never promised and win credit by the contrast. The closing phrase, 'Redeeming time', joins the language of salvation to that of the counting-house.

Scene 3: Act I, Scene 3: Hotspur's prisoners and the Percy plot

Act I, Scene 3 (the whole scene)

Summary

The King quarrels with the Percys. When Worcester reminds him that their family helped him to the throne, Henry sends him from the room. Hotspur explains that he refused to give up his prisoners because a perfumed courtier demanded them on the battlefield while he was faint from his wounds. The King answers that Hotspur still keeps them back in order to force him to ransom Mortimer, Hotspur's brother-in-law, whom Henry calls a traitor. He orders the prisoners sent at once and leaves.

Hotspur explodes. Northumberland and Worcester remind him that Mortimer was named heir by Richard II, which is why the King fears him. Worcester reveals a plot: the Scottish prisoners will be freed to win Douglas, the Archbishop of York will join them, and Mortimer and Glendower will rise in Wales.

Analysis

Hotspur's speech about the 'popinjay' shows him at his most attractive and most comic, a soldier who despises courtly manners. In his outburst about plucking 'bright honour from the pale-faced moon', honour is something to be seized and kept for oneself, 'without corrival'. Worcester and his father can hardly get a word in: his impatience is both his charm and his danger (Jenkins 1956).

The scene shows that the rebellion grows out of the deposition of Richard II. The Percys made Henry king and now feel cheated; they call him 'this vile politician, Bolingbroke'. Shakespeare follows Holinshed in confusing two Edmund Mortimers, the captured soldier and the young Earl of March who was Richard's heir (Bullough 1962).

Scene 4: Act II, Scene 1: Carriers at Rochester

Act II, Scene 1 (the whole scene)

Summary

Before dawn in an inn-yard at Rochester, two carriers grumble about fleas, a dead ostler and the price of oats as they load their horses for London. Gadshill, the thief who gives his name to the robbery, borrows a lantern and is refused. He learns from the inn's chamberlain, his informer, that a rich franklin and others will travel the road that morning.

Gadshill boasts that he keeps company with men of rank who will protect him, and that the robbers walk invisible.

Analysis

This short scene of ordinary working men is one of Shakespeare's most vivid pictures of common English life, and it widens the world of the play beyond court and tavern. The carriers have no part in the plot; they are there to make the kingdom felt as a real place with real victims (Rackin 1990).

Gadshill's claim that 'great oneyers' support him is a comic version of the play's larger theme. Robbery is protected by rank, and the heir to the throne is about to take part in it.

Scene 5: Act II, Scene 2: The robbery at Gad's Hill

Act II, Scene 2 (the whole scene)

Summary

On the road near Gad's Hill, Poins has hidden Falstaff's horse, and the fat knight complains bitterly of having to walk. The thieves put on their masks, rob the travellers and bind them, while Falstaff abuses the victims as young and fat. As the robbers sit down to share the money, Hal and Poins, in buckram suits, attack them. The others run at once, and Falstaff, after a blow or two, runs too, leaving the money behind.

Hal and Poins ride away laughing, picturing Falstaff sweating to death as he goes.

Analysis

The robbery is farce, but it matters. The heir to the throne takes part in a crime against his father's subjects, although he later returns the money. Hal never takes anything from the travellers; he robs only thieves, a distinction the play is careful to keep. Critics have compared this joke to the more serious deceptions of the battlefield, where the King too will appear in disguise (Greenblatt 1988).

Falstaff's cry that the victims 'hate us youth' is typical of his comedy, which delights in claims that are plainly untrue. He is old and fat and claims to be young; the joke is in the shamelessness.

Scene 6: Act II, Scene 3: Hotspur and Lady Percy

Act II, Scene 3 (the whole scene)

Summary

At Warkworth, Hotspur reads a letter from a nobleman who refuses to join the rebellion, calling the plan dangerous and its friends uncertain. Hotspur argues with the absent writer, insults him and decides to set out that night. His wife, Lady Percy, enters and asks why he has stopped eating and sleeping and talks of battles in his dreams.

Hotspur will not tell her his plans, teases her and promises only that she will follow him.

Analysis

The letter scene reveals Hotspur's mind in motion: he answers every objection with a burst of contempt, and the fine phrase about plucking 'this flower, safety' from 'this nettle, danger' sums up his courage and his refusal to calculate. The audience may notice that the letter-writer's doubts are sensible ones and will prove correct.

Lady Percy's speech is one of the play's few views of the cost of war from the household. Feminist critics see in her and in Lady Mortimer a domestic world that the history plays push to the edges, and Hotspur's teasing shows that his world of honour has little room for women (Howard 1997).

Scene 7: Act II, Scene 4: The tavern, the lies and the play extempore

Act II, Scene 4 (the whole scene)

Summary

At the Boar's Head in Eastcheap, Hal boasts of having learned the drawers' slang and plays a trick on Francis, a young waiter, calling 'Anon, anon' between Hal and Poins. Falstaff arrives, curses cowards, and tells how he fought off a growing number of men in buckram: two, then four, then seven, then eleven, and three more in Kendal green, though it was too dark to see. Hal reveals the truth. Falstaff answers that he knew them all along and ran from instinct, as a lion will not touch a true prince.

A messenger summons Hal to court: the rebellion has begun. Falstaff proposes a rehearsal of Hal's interview with his father. Falstaff plays the King and praises Falstaff; then they change roles, and Hal, as King, denounces Falstaff as a 'misleader of youth'. Falstaff, as Hal, pleads 'banish plump Jack, and banish all the world'; Hal answers 'I do, I will'. The Sheriff arrives in pursuit of the robbers, Hal protects Falstaff, and the scene ends with Hal reading Falstaff's tavern bill as he sleeps.

Analysis

The longest scene in the play is its comic centre and turns on acting. The buckram story is a performance that Falstaff knows will be exposed, and his escape through 'instinct' is the real joke. Since Maurice Morgann, critics have debated whether Falstaff is a coward or a man too clever to fight for nothing (Morgann 1777) (Wilson 1943).

The play extempore rehearses the end of 2 Henry IV, when Hal, newly crowned, rejects Falstaff. Hal's 'I do, I will' moves from the present tense of the game into the future, and many productions mark the change with a pause. Barber reads the scene as a saturnalian mock-king whose dismissal clears the way for real rule (Barber 1959); others find in it the moment the friendship becomes conditional (Auden 1962).

Scene 8: Act III, Scene 1: The rebels divide England

Act III, Scene 1 (the whole scene)

Summary

At Bangor, Hotspur, Worcester, Mortimer and Glendower meet to plan the rising. Glendower claims that the earth shook at his birth and that he can call spirits; Hotspur mocks him. They divide the kingdom on a map into three parts, and Hotspur objects that the river Trent cuts a piece out of his share. Glendower offers to change its course, then yields, at which Hotspur loses interest.

Mortimer's Welsh wife cannot speak English and he cannot speak Welsh; she sings to him in Welsh while Hotspur teases Lady Percy and refuses to let her sing.

Analysis

The rebels quarrel even before they fight. The map of a divided England, three parts for three leaders, shows what the rebellion would mean for the kingdom. Hotspur's mockery of Glendower is funny but tactless, and Mortimer and Worcester warn him that he is damaging the alliance (Jenkins 1956).

The Welsh song and the untranslated Welsh lines make this the play's most lyrical moment and its most foreign. Critics have read Wales as a space of feminine and magical power that the English history must master or exclude (Howard 1997) (Rackin 1990).

Scene 9: Act III, Scene 2: Father and son

Act III, Scene 2 (the whole scene)

Summary

The King sends his lords away and rebukes Hal privately. He compares the prince to Richard II, who cheapened himself by mixing with common people, and himself to the young Bolingbroke, who kept himself rare and won the crowd's wonder. He says Hotspur has more right to the throne than Hal, and fears that his son will fight against him.

Hal asks for pardon and promises to redeem himself on Percy's head: Hotspur has been gathering glory for him, which he will take at Shrewsbury. The King, moved, gives him a command. Blunt brings news that the rebels have gathered at Shrewsbury.

Analysis

The King's advice repeats the logic of Hal's soliloquy in 1.2. Both men understand power as a performance whose effect depends on rarity. The difference is that Hal has already planned what his father thinks he must be taught. New historicist critics read the scene as a lesson in the theatre of royal authority (Greenblatt 1988) (Kastan 1986).

Hal's promise uses the language of trade. Percy is his 'factor', an agent buying glory on his behalf, and Hal will call him to account. The image makes honour a commodity, which will be exchanged in the fight in 5.4.

Scene 10: Act III, Scene 3: Falstaff called to war

Act III, Scene 3 (the whole scene)

Summary

At the Boar's Head, Falstaff complains that he is wasting away and teases Bardolph about his red nose. He accuses the Hostess, Mistress Quickly, of letting his pocket be picked of a valuable ring; she answers that he owes her money and that Hal has called the ring copper. Hal enters and reveals that the pocket held only tavern bills and a bit of sugar-candy.

Hal tells Falstaff that the stolen money has been paid back and that he has a command of infantry. The prince leaves for war, and Falstaff calls for breakfast.

Analysis

The scene shows Hal moving out of the tavern. He has made peace with his father and repaid the robbery, and he brings Falstaff into the war. Falstaff's jokes about his own body and his debts continue, but they now sound against the background of the coming battle.

Falstaff's argument that he has more flesh than other men and so more frailty is a parody of Christian theology: since Adam fell in innocence, what hope is there for Jack Falstaff in a wicked age? The comedy borrows the language of sin and grace that runs through Hal's plans (Bloom 1998).

Scene 11: Act IV, Scene 1: The rebels at Shrewsbury

Act IV, Scene 1 (the whole scene)

Summary

In the rebel camp, Hotspur and Douglas praise each other. A letter brings bad news: Northumberland is sick and will not come. Hotspur turns this into good news, since victory without him will be greater. Vernon reports that the King's army is coming, and describes the prince armed and mounted, a vision of chivalry.

Hotspur interrupts the praise of his rival, longs to meet Hal in single combat, and hears that Glendower cannot come for another fourteen days.

Analysis

Vernon's description of Hal is the first sign that the rumours about the prince are wrong. The heavy armour, the leap into the saddle and the image of Mercury and Pegasus present him as a classical hero. Hotspur cannot bear to hear it.

The rebels' cause weakens before the battle. Northumberland's 'sickness' looks like caution, and Glendower's delay leaves the army short. Hotspur's optimism grows as his real chances shrink.

Scene 12: Act IV, Scene 2: Falstaff's recruits

Act IV, Scene 2 (the whole scene)

Summary

On a road near Coventry, Falstaff confesses to the audience that he has abused the King's licence to press soldiers. He conscripted men of property who paid him to be released, and filled their places with beggars, servants and released prisoners, so ragged that he will not march through Coventry with them.

Hal and Westmoreland pass and comment on the wretched troops. Falstaff answers that they are good enough to be killed.

Analysis

The scene is comic but bitter. Falstaff's corruption has real victims, and his phrase 'food for powder' (cannon fodder) is one of the sharpest comments on war in Shakespeare. It is hard to keep laughing once one knows that nearly all these men will die at Shrewsbury.

Critics divided over Falstaff's morality cite this scene on both sides. For some it proves him a parasite of the state; for others it tells the truth about what armies are made of, a truth the heroic speeches leave out (Wilson 1943) (Bloom 1998).

Scene 13: Act IV, Scene 3: The King's offer

Act IV, Scene 3 (the whole scene)

Summary

The rebel leaders argue about whether to attack at once. Sir Walter Blunt arrives from the King with an offer: if they name their grievances, they will be met and the rebels pardoned. Hotspur tells the story of how the Percys helped Bolingbroke when he returned from exile, claiming only his duchy, and how he then took the crown, killed Richard and treated his helpers with suspicion.

Hotspur says that Worcester will bring an answer in the morning.

Analysis

Hotspur's long account of Henry's rise is the rebels' version of history. It is partial but not false, and it reminds the audience that Henry is a usurper. The Percys' grievance is that they were used and then cast aside (Saccio 1977).

Blunt's offer is generous and probably sincere. The tragedy of the rebellion, as the next act shows, is that it will never reach Hotspur.

Scene 14: Act IV, Scene 4: The Archbishop of York

Act IV, Scene 4 (the whole scene)

Summary

In York, the Archbishop sends letters to his allies. He fears the outcome of the battle at Shrewsbury, since Northumberland, Glendower and Mortimer are absent and the King's army is strong.

He expects that the King, if he wins, will turn against him next, and prepares for that.

Analysis

This short scene is a bridge to the second part of the story. The Archbishop's rebellion will be the main plot of 2 Henry IV, so the scene shows that Shrewsbury will not end the civil war. Critics who think Shakespeare planned both parts at once cite such links; others argue that Part 2 was written only after Part 1 proved a success (Hunter 1954) (Jenkins 1956).

Scene 15: Act V, Scene 1: The parley and the catechism

Act V, Scene 1 (the whole scene)

Summary

Near Shrewsbury, Worcester comes to the King's camp. The King asks why he has rebelled; Worcester repeats the Percys' grievance. Hal praises Hotspur and offers to settle the quarrel in single combat. The King renews his offer of pardon and sends Worcester back.

Alone with Falstaff, Hal tells him he owes God a death. Falstaff, left alone, delivers his catechism on honour: honour cannot set a broken leg or heal a wound, it is only a word, air, and belongs to the dead. He will have none of it.

Analysis

Hal's offer of single combat shows his new public role and his generosity to his rival; it also shows his confidence. Falstaff's quip about the rebellion, 'Rebellion lay in his way, and he found it', mocks Worcester's claim that he never sought the quarrel.

The catechism, in the question-and-answer form of a church lesson, is the play's sharpest challenge to the values of Hotspur and the court. Falstaff empties honour of meaning just before men die for it. Some critics see him as a voice of life and good sense against a deadly code (Bloom 1998); others see the speech as a coward's excuse that the play exposes (Wilson 1943) (Tillyard 1944).

Scene 16: Act V, Scene 2: Worcester's deception

Act V, Scene 2 (the whole scene)

Summary

Worcester decides not to tell Hotspur of the King's offer, because he believes that the King will never trust the Percys again. Hotspur, he fears, might be forgiven as a hot-headed youth, but Worcester and Northumberland would pay. He tells Hotspur that the King is set on battle.

Vernon reports Hal's challenge, praising his modesty. Hotspur is scornful but excited. He hears that the enemy is coming and urges his men to fight.

Analysis

The scene turns the battle into a tragedy of misinformation. Worcester's suspicion is not foolish, for Henry has already broken faith once, but his silence costs thousands of lives. His decision mirrors the King's own politics of mistrust.

Hotspur's speech about the shortness of life is one of his finest. It joins his love of honour to a sense of mortality that the catechism had treated as a reason to run away.

Scene 17: Act V, Scene 3: Counterfeit kings

Act V, Scene 3 (the whole scene)

Summary

Douglas meets Sir Walter Blunt, who is dressed in the King's coat, and kills him, believing that he has killed the King; he has already killed Lord Stafford in the same disguise. Hotspur tells him the truth. Falstaff finds Blunt's body and jokes about 'grinning honour'.

Hal finds Falstaff idle and asks for his sword. Falstaff offers his pistol, which turns out to be a case holding a bottle of sack, and Hal throws it at him.

Analysis

The King's practice of dressing nobles in his armour multiplies false kings across the field. Douglas's frustration makes the question of who the real king is literal, and it recalls Hal's play extempore, where kingship was a matter of costume (Kastan 1986).

Falstaff's comment on Blunt's corpse repeats the catechism in visual form: honour is a dead man's grin. The bottle in the pistol case is the tavern still refusing to take the war seriously, and Hal's anger shows that he no longer finds it funny.

Scene 18: Act V, Scene 4: Hal and Hotspur

Act V, Scene 4 (the whole scene)

Summary

Douglas finds the real King and nearly kills him, but Hal arrives and drives the Scot away, proving his loyalty. Hotspur enters, and the two Harrys meet at last. While they fight, Douglas attacks Falstaff, who falls down as if dead. Hal kills Hotspur, whose last words are left unfinished, and speaks a generous epitaph over him, then sees Falstaff and says farewell to him too.

When Hal leaves, Falstaff gets up, justifies counterfeiting death, stabs Hotspur's body in the thigh and carries it off, claiming to have killed him. Hal agrees to support the lie.

Analysis

The single combat completes the play's design. Hal fulfils his vow and takes Hotspur's 'proud titles'; the rival doubles of 1.1 become one prince. Shakespeare invented the meeting: Holinshed reports that Hotspur was killed, no one knows by whom, and makes him much older than Hal (Bullough 1962) (Saccio 1977). The rescue of the King draws on Samuel Daniel's poem The Civil Wars.

Falstaff's resurrection is the play's great comic reply to its heroic climax. His argument that counterfeiting dying is the 'perfect image of life' turns the theme of counterfeits on its head, and his line about discretion and valour has become proverbial. Hal's willingness to 'gild' the lie may be generosity, or a sign that he can afford to give away the credit (Greenblatt 1988).

Scene 19: Act V, Scene 5: Victory and unfinished business

Act V, Scene 5 (the whole scene)

Summary

The King has won. He condemns Worcester and Vernon to death for not passing on his offer. Hal asks that Douglas, who was captured after falling from a hill, be set free without ransom, and gives the honour of releasing him to his brother John.

The King divides his forces: Prince John and Westmoreland will march against Northumberland and the Archbishop, while he and Hal go to Wales to fight Glendower and Mortimer. The rebellion is broken but not ended.

Analysis

The ending is a victory and a pause. The King's first line puts the official moral, but the scene ends with more war to come, which leads directly into 2 Henry IV. Critics who see the two parts as one design point to this open ending (Hunter 1954) (Hawkins 1982).

Hal's release of Douglas is chivalrous, and his gift of the honour to his brother shows that he can now give honour away as well as win it. He is no longer the prodigal of the tavern.

Characters

The court and the tavern

Prince Hal (Henry, Prince of Wales)
The King's eldest son and heir, later Henry V. He spends his days with Falstaff, trading wit and playing jokes, but tells the audience in his first soliloquy that he will throw off these companions at the right time. At Shrewsbury he saves his father's life and kills Hotspur. Whether he is a prodigal truly reformed or a politician who planned his own reform is the play's central question about him.

The tavern

Sir John Falstaff
A fat, old, impoverished knight, Hal's companion, thief, liar and wit. He turns every accusation into a joke and every danger into a reason to run away. His speech on honour and his fake death at Shrewsbury challenge the heroic values of the court. Originally named Sir John Oldcastle.
Poins
Hal's companion who devises the trick at Gad's Hill.
Bardolph, Peto and Gadshill
Falstaff's companions in robbery. Bardolph's red nose is a running joke.
Mistress Quickly
The Hostess of the Boar's Head, to whom Falstaff owes money.

The rebels

Hotspur (Henry Percy)
Son of the Earl of Northumberland, a brave, impetuous soldier who lives for honour. Funny, impatient and tactless, he mocks courtiers and Glendower alike and loves his wife in a rough, teasing way. He leads the rebellion and is killed by Hal at Shrewsbury.
Thomas Percy, Earl of Worcester
Hotspur's uncle and the brains of the rebellion. He hides the King's offer of pardon from Hotspur and is executed after the battle.
Henry Percy, Earl of Northumberland
Hotspur's father, who helped Henry to the throne. He falls sick, or claims to, and fails to join the battle.
Edmund Mortimer
Hotspur's brother-in-law, captured by Glendower and married to his daughter. Shakespeare follows Holinshed in merging him with the Earl of March, Richard II's heir.
Owen Glendower
The Welsh leader, proud, learned and believed to have magical powers. He quarrels with Hotspur and does not arrive at Shrewsbury in time.
Archibald, Earl of Douglas
The Scottish warrior defeated by Hotspur and then his ally. At Shrewsbury he kills several men dressed as the King, and is released by Hal after the battle.
Sir Richard Vernon
A rebel who describes Hal's transformation and who is executed with Worcester.
Lady Percy (Kate)
Hotspur's wife, who fears for him and is teased by him.
Lady Mortimer
Glendower's daughter and Mortimer's wife, who speaks and sings only in Welsh.

The court

King Henry IV
Henry Bolingbroke, who deposed Richard II. Anxious, guilty and politic, he longs for a crusade, distrusts the Percys, and fears that his son is unworthy. He uses doubles in his armour at Shrewsbury.
Sir Walter Blunt
A loyal knight who carries the King's offer to the rebels and is killed at Shrewsbury while dressed as the King.
Prince John of Lancaster and the Earl of Westmoreland
The King's younger son and a loyal nobleman, who will march against the Archbishop in Part 2.

Themes

Honour

The play sets three views of honour side by side. For Hotspur it is a prize to be seized, a personal possession that he will share with no one. For Falstaff it is 'a word', 'air', a painted shield at a funeral. For Hal it is something that can be won, transferred and even given away, as he takes Hotspur's honours in 5.4 and gives his brother the honour of freeing Douglas in 5.5.

The play does not choose one view. Hotspur's honour gets him killed, Falstaff's realism leads him to fraud, and Hal's practical honour looks like the most successful but also the most calculated (Bloom 1998) (Jenkins 1956).

Reformation and redemption

Hal's soliloquy promises a 'reformation' that will redeem time, and the play's language of redemption joins Christian ideas of salvation with financial ideas of paying debts. Hal's career follows the pattern of the prodigal son, and Falstaff himself invokes the parable when he describes his ragged soldiers in 4.2. The question is whether his change is a conversion or a planned performance (Greenblatt 1988) (Kris 1948).

Kingship and legitimacy

Henry IV took the crown by force, and the rebels' claim is that he owes them for it. The play shows a king who rules by policy, disguise and the management of appearances, and a prince who learns the same arts. Some critics read the plays as a lesson in the Tudor doctrine of order; others see them as revealing kingship to be a kind of theatre (Tillyard 1944) (Kastan 1986).

Fathers and sons

The play is full of fathers: the King, Northumberland, and Falstaff, Hal's substitute father in the tavern. The King wishes that Hotspur were his son; in the play extempore Falstaff plays the King and Hal plays his father in turn. Psychoanalytic critics have read Hal's struggle as a conflict between two fathers, the guilty king and the indulgent old knight (Kris 1948) (Auden 1962).

Counterfeits and acting

The play is full of performances and fakes: the disguises at Gad's Hill, the play extempore, the King's doubles at Shrewsbury, Falstaff's fake death. Falstaff's speech in 5.4 plays on the word 'counterfeit', and Hal's whole career is a performance. The play asks what is true and false in a world where kings, too, are actors (Greenblatt 1988) (Kastan 1986).

Time

From Hal's first question to Falstaff, which asks what he has to do with the time of day, to Hotspur's dying words that time itself 'must have a stop', the play keeps returning to time. The court is ruled by schedules and deadlines; the tavern lives outside them. Hal's promise to 'redeem time' makes him the master of both (Barber 1959).

Motifs

Robbery and debt

Robbery runs through the play, from Gad's Hill to Bolingbroke's 'theft' of the crown and Hal's taking of Hotspur's honours. Debt runs with it: Falstaff owes the Hostess, the King owes the Percys, and Hal promises to pay a debt he never made.

Doubles

The play pairs its characters: Hal and Hotspur, two Harrys; the King and Falstaff, two fathers; the court and the tavern. The King's doubles at Shrewsbury make the motif literal.

Prose and verse

Court and rebels speak mostly blank verse, the tavern prose. Hal moves between them, and his ability to speak both is a sign of his range.

Sun and moon

Hal compares himself to the sun hidden by clouds; Falstaff and his thieves serve the moon. Hotspur would pluck honour from the moon.

Play-acting

Hal and Falstaff play roles in 2.4, Glendower performs his magic, and the King's knights wear his coat. Acting is the play's way of thinking about power.

Symbols

Falstaff's body

Falstaff's enormous body stands for appetite, the flesh and the life the play's heroic world denies. The jokes about it are also jokes about mortality.

The map in 3.1

The map divided into three parts stands for the kingdom torn apart by rebellion, and the quarrel over the Trent shows how the rebels' greed will undo them.

The King's coats

The coats worn by Blunt and others make kingship a costume that any man can put on, and that may be the death of whoever wears it.

Sack

Falstaff's sweet Spanish wine is the symbol of the tavern; Hal's discovery of the tavern bill and the bottle in the pistol case show the gap between the tavern and the battlefield.

The crown

Though rarely seen, the crown is the play's hidden centre: Henry's guilt over winning it and Hal's right to inherit it shape every scene.

Critical debates

Is Falstaff a coward?

Maurice Morgann's essay of 1777 argued that Falstaff is not a coward but a man of courage and sense who refuses to die for nothing, and that the audience feels this even when the plot suggests the opposite. This started the modern criticism of character in Shakespeare (Morgann 1777).

J. Dover Wilson replied that Falstaff is the Vice of the morality plays, a tempter whom Hal must reject, while A. C. Bradley regretted the rejection as a betrayal of the play's spirit of freedom (Wilson 1943) (Bradley 1909). Harold Bloom has made Falstaff the supreme example of Shakespeare's 'invention of the human' (Bloom 1998).

Hal's soliloquy: sincerity or policy?

Older critics read 'I know you all' as Shakespeare's guarantee to his audience that the wild prince was always the hero of tradition. Later readers find it chilling, a sign that Hal uses his friends as a foil for his own glory.

Stephen Greenblatt's 'Invisible Bullets' reads the prince as a master of political theatre who exploits the tavern world while appearing to enjoy it, and argues that the play contains subversion only to strengthen authority (Greenblatt 1988). Psychoanalytic readers see the speech as a defence against guilt toward a usurping father (Kris 1948).

Holinshed and the uses of history

Comparing the play with Holinshed shows how Shakespeare shaped history for the stage. He made Hotspur young so that he could be Hal's rival, invented their duel, and gave the comic plot more space than any chronicle could justify (Bullough 1962) (Holinshed 1587).

Tillyard read the history plays as dramatising the 'Tudor myth' of order lost and restored; later critics have stressed Shakespeare's scepticism about providential history and his interest in how history is made by those who tell it (Tillyard 1944) (Rackin 1990) (Saccio 1977).

Oldcastle or Falstaff?

Falstaff was first called Oldcastle, and the change was forced on Shakespeare by the Cobham family. Gary Taylor argued that the play should be printed with the original name, as Shakespeare first wrote it, and the 1986 Oxford Complete Works did so (Taylor 1985) (Taylor 1987).

Others, including David Scott Kastan, argue that the play as it was printed and performed, with Falstaff, is the version that history and Shakespeare accepted, and that the change was part of the play's life in the theatre (Kastan 1999). The rival Oldcastle play written in reply shows the stakes of the quarrel for Protestant memory (Corbin 1991).

One play or two?

Did Shakespeare plan both parts of Henry IV from the start? Harold Jenkins argued that he changed his mind midway through Part 1, which is why Hal's reformation seems almost complete at Shrewsbury and must be staged again in Part 2 (Jenkins 1956).

G. K. Hunter compared the two parts to other Elizabethan two-part plays, which repeat the same pattern rather than continue it, and Sherman Hawkins argued for a single design across both (Hunter 1954) (Hawkins 1982).

Festive comedy and the tavern

C. L. Barber read the tavern scenes as a version of holiday misrule, in which a mock king rules for a time before order returns. On this view the rejection of Falstaff is the necessary end of the festival (Barber 1959).

W. H. Auden, by contrast, saw Falstaff as a figure of love outside the world of power, whose rejection condemns that world (Auden 1962).

Women, Wales and the nation

Feminist critics have noted how few women appear in the history plays and how they are placed at the margins of the nation: Lady Percy in the household, Lady Mortimer in Wales and in a language the English cannot understand (Howard 1997).

Phyllis Rackin argues that the history plays define English masculine history by excluding what is foreign and feminine, and that the Welsh scene in 3.1 is a place where that exclusion becomes visible (Rackin 1990).

Authority as theatre

New historicist critics see the play as a study of authority displayed. The King's lecture on keeping himself rare, his doubles at Shrewsbury and Hal's planned reform all treat power as a performance (Greenblatt 1988).

David Scott Kastan has argued that by showing kingship on stage the theatre demystifies it, making the monarch a role that actors can play (Kastan 1986).

Questions for study

  1. Is Hal's soliloquy in 1.2 sincere, calculating or both? How does it affect your view of the tavern scenes that follow?

    Look at the images of sun, debt and metal, and at how Hal treats Falstaff and Francis in 2.4.

  2. Compare the views of honour held by Hotspur, Falstaff and Hal.

    Set 1.3 and 5.2 beside the catechism in 5.1 and Hal's epitaph in 5.4.

  3. What does the play extempore in 2.4 reveal about Hal and Falstaff's relationship?

    Consider who plays whom, and the change of tense in 'I do, I will'.

  4. Is Falstaff a coward?

    Weigh the buckram story, the catechism and his fake death against his wit and the play's view of war.

  5. How does Shakespeare use the Gad's Hill robbery to comment on the political plot?

    Think about Bolingbroke's taking of the crown and Hal's taking of Hotspur's honours.

  6. How did Shakespeare change Holinshed, and why?

    Consider Hotspur's age, the single combat and the comic plot.

  7. What role do women play in this history play?

    Look at Lady Percy in 2.3 and the Welsh scene in 3.1.

  8. How do the rebels undermine their own cause?

    Consider the map in 3.1, Northumberland's sickness, Glendower's delay and Worcester's silence in 5.2.

  9. What difference does the original name 'Oldcastle' make to how we read Falstaff?

    Think of the historical Oldcastle as a Protestant martyr, and the jokes about Falstaff's religious language.

  10. How does the play treat counterfeits, from the disguises at Gad's Hill to the King's doubles at Shrewsbury?

    Read Falstaff's speech on counterfeiting in 5.4 alongside Douglas's hunt for the real king in 5.3 and 5.4.
