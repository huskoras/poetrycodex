Overview

Full title
Childe Harold's Pilgrimage: A Romaunt
Author
George Gordon Byron, sixth Baron Byron (1788–1824)
Published
Cantos I–II by John Murray, 10 March 1812; Canto III, 18 November 1816; Canto IV, 28 April 1818; the dedicatory poem 'To Ianthe' was added in the seventh edition (1813)
Written
Cantos I–II during Byron's travels of 1809–11; Canto III in Belgium and Switzerland in the summer of 1816; Canto IV in Venice and its neighbourhood in 1817
Form
Spenserian stanzas (eight iambic pentameters and a closing alexandrine, rhyming ababbcbcc), with lyrics in other measures set into Cantos I, II and III
Length
495 Spenserian stanzas: 93 in Canto I, 98 in Canto II, 118 in Canto III and 186 in Canto IV
Itinerary
England, Portugal, Spain, Malta, Albania, Greece (I–II); Waterloo, the Rhine, the Alps and Lake Geneva (III); Venice, Ferrara, Florence and Rome (IV)
Speaker
A first-person narrator-pilgrim who tells the story of the young nobleman Harold and gradually replaces him
Dedications
'To Ianthe' (Lady Charlotte Harley); Canto III opens and closes with Byron's daughter Ada; Canto IV is dedicated in prose to John Cam Hobhouse
Text in this archive
Project Gutenberg text; Byron's prose preface, the dedication of Canto IV and the notes are not included

Context

In July 1809 Byron, just of age and heavily in debt, sailed from Falmouth with his Cambridge friend John Cam Hobhouse. Because Napoleon controlled most of Europe, the usual Grand Tour through France was closed, and the two went instead to Lisbon, rode across Spain to Seville and Cadiz, and sailed by Gibraltar and Malta to Albania and Greece, reaching Constantinople in 1810. Byron began the poem at Ioannina in Albania in October 1809 and carried on with it in Athens and Smyrna. He returned to England in July 1811 to find, or soon to learn of, a run of deaths: his mother, his school friend John Wingfield, Charles Skinner Matthews, and the Cambridge chorister John Edleston, whom he mourned in Canto II (Marchand 1957) (MacCarthy 2002).

John Murray published the first two cantos as a handsome quarto on 10 March 1812, with long prose notes on the countries Byron had seen. The edition sold out within days, and Byron later recalled, in a remark preserved by his friend Thomas Moore, that he woke one morning to find himself famous. The poem made its author the most talked-about man in London society and established the commercial and personal 'Byron' whom readers would follow through the Turkish tales that followed (Christensen 1993) (Mole 2007).

Byron chose the stanza of Spenser's Faerie Queene, which James Thomson and James Beattie had revived in the eighteenth century, and dressed the opening cantos in mock-medieval diction: 'Childe' was the old title of a young man of noble birth not yet knighted, and the hero was called 'Childe Burun', an old form of the family name, in the manuscript. The archaisms ('whilome', 'hight', 'wight', 'fytte') are thick in the opening stanzas and thin out quickly, as the poem turns from a romance about a dissipated knight into a meditation on the places, wars and ruins the traveller sees (McGann 1980) (McGann 1968).

The later cantos belong to a different life. In April 1816, after the collapse of his marriage to Annabella Milbanke and a storm of scandal, Byron left England for good. He visited the field of Waterloo, went up the Rhine and spent the summer on Lake Geneva beside Percy and Mary Shelley, who carried the manuscript of Canto III back to England; it appeared in November 1816. Canto IV, written in and around Venice in 1817 after a visit to Rome, was published in April 1818 with Hobhouse's learned notes and a prose dedication to him, in which Byron admitted that he had given up trying to keep the pilgrim distinct from himself (Marchand 1957) (Franklin 2000).

The finished poem is therefore not a single design but a record of nine years: a young aristocrat's satirical travelogue of 1812 grows into the confession of an exile and finally into a long meditation on history, liberty and art. It shaped the idea of the 'Byronic hero', gave Romantic Philhellenism much of its language, and served nineteenth-century travellers as a guidebook in verse; for many European readers it was the poem that defined English Romanticism (Thorslev 1962) (Buzard 1993) (Bone 2004).

Plot overview

Canto I introduces Childe Harold, a young English lord sated with pleasure and loved by no one, who leaves his ancestral hall and sails away, singing a farewell 'Good Night' to his native land. He sees Lisbon and the beauties of Cintra, passes through Portugal, and crosses into Spain at the moment of the Peninsular War. The narrator takes over with addresses to Spain, scenes of the battles of Talavera and Albuera, the heroism of the Maid of Saragoza, the women of Cadiz and a bullfight in Seville. Harold remains bored and gloomy, and sings a lyric 'To Inez' about his weariness. The canto ends with an elegy for a dead friend.

Canto II moves to Greece. At Athens the narrator mourns the vanished greatness of the city and condemns Lord Elgin's removal of the Parthenon sculptures. Harold sails past Gibraltar and Malta, where he resists the charms of a lady called Florence, to Albania, where he visits Ali Pasha's court at Tepelene, is welcomed by Suliote warriors and hears their war-song. The canto turns into a lament for Greece under Ottoman rule, an appeal to the Greeks to free themselves, and finally a personal lament for a lost beloved.

Canto III begins with Byron's farewell to his infant daughter Ada and a new departure. Harold, now explicitly an exile, stands on the field of Waterloo; the narrator recreates the ball in Brussels on the eve of the battle and meditates on Napoleon. The pilgrim travels up the Rhine and into Switzerland, where on Lake Leman he seeks to lose himself in Nature, recalls Rousseau, witnesses a night storm over the Alps and the Jura, and visits Clarens, Lausanne and Ferney. The canto ends with a defiant declaration of independence from the world and a blessing on Ada.

Canto IV, in which Harold is barely mentioned, is spoken by the narrator from Venice to Rome. He reflects on the decline of Venice, Petrarch's tomb at Arquà, Tasso's imprisonment at Ferrara, the art and tombs of Florence, the battlefield of Trasimene and the falls of Velino. In Rome he meditates on ruin, tyranny and freedom, on love, on the Colosseum and the statue of the dying gladiator, on the Pantheon and St Peter's, and on the death of Princess Charlotte. From the Alban hills he looks at the sea, delivers his address to the Ocean, and bids the reader farewell.

Part by part

Part 1: Farewell and Portugal

To Ianthe; Canto I, stanzas 1–34

Summary

The poem opens with a dedication, 'To Ianthe', addressed to a girl of about eleven, Lady Charlotte Harley, whose beauty the poet says surpasses anything he saw in the East. Canto I itself begins by declining to call on the Greek Muse and then introduces, in mock-Spenserian language, a youth of Albion who has spent his days in riot, wine and loveless affairs. Childe Harold's lineage is noble but he has stained it. Sick at heart and 'with pleasure drugged', he resolves to leave his hall, his mother, his sister and his flatterers, none of whom, he thinks, truly love him.

At sunset on the ship he takes his harp and sings 'Good Night', a ballad in eight-line stanzas in which he comforts a weeping page and a pale yeoman who fear for those they leave behind, and declares that he himself has no one to mourn him. The ship reaches Lisbon, whose splendour from the river contrasts with the filth of its streets and the servility, as the narrator sees it, of its people. Cintra's mountains, convents and gardens draw a long passage of description, including the empty palace of William Beckford at Monserrate.

The narrator then attacks the Convention of Cintra (1808), the agreement by which British generals allowed a defeated French army to sail home with its arms and plunder; he imagines it as a grinning fiend in a fool's cap. Harold rides on past the palace-convent of Mafra and through the hills of the Alentejo to the frontier stream that divides Portugal from Spain.

Analysis

The first stanzas are a performance of literary antiquarianism. Byron borrows not only Spenser's stanza but his archaic vocabulary and the tone of a medieval romance about a knight who has gone astray. The joke is partly at his own expense, since readers soon identified the dissipated young lord with the author, and partly a way of keeping that identification at a distance. Once Harold sails, the archaisms fade, and the poem settles into the voice of a modern, opinionated traveller (McGann 1968) (Joseph 1964).

Harold is defined by negation: he is satiated, unloved, unable to weep, and incapable of being moved by the beauty he sees. This is the seed of the figure that later readers called the Byronic hero, though in 1812 he is still sketched lightly and almost satirically. The 'Good Night' lyric shows the method: the servants have real ties and real griefs, and Harold's boast that nobody will miss him is both proud and pathetic (Thorslev 1962).

From the start the travelogue is political. The narrator praises Portuguese landscape and despises Portuguese society, and his scorn for the Convention of Cintra is aimed at British politicians and generals rather than at the French. Byron writes as a young Whig sceptical of the war's management, and the mixture of picturesque description and topical satire would remain characteristic of the poem (Kelsall 1987).

Part 2: Spain, Talavera and the Maid of Saragoza

Canto I, stanzas 35–93, with the lyric 'To Inez'

Summary

The narrator hails 'lovely Spain', recalls its medieval wars against the Moors, and calls the Spanish to rise again. In a famous allegory War appears as a blood-haired Giant on the mountain, before whom France, Spain and England meet to make sacrifice at Talavera (July 1809). The narrator mocks the 'fools' of ambition who die there, and turns to Albuera (May 1811), a 'glorious field of grief'.

Harold rides on to Seville, where the narrator describes the peasantry's fear, the muleteer's song and the patriot leaders. He celebrates the Maid of Saragoza, Agustina, who took up a dead gunner's post during the French siege of her city in 1808, and the beauty of Spanish women in general. A digression addresses Mount Parnassus, which the poet is seeing while he writes. The scene moves to pleasure-loving Cadiz, where a Spanish Sunday is compared with a London one and a long account of a bullfight follows, with the narrator alternately excited and repelled by the cruelty of the sport.

Harold remains unmoved by beauty: love has left him nothing but bitterness. He sings 'To Inez', a lyric in short quatrains, confessing a weariness and gloom that he compares to the curse of the Wandering Jew. The narrator bids farewell to Cadiz, laments the continuing war, and ends with an elegy for a friend who died not in battle but of fever (John Wingfield), before closing the first 'fytte' of the pilgrimage.

Analysis

The war passages are the most vigorous in the canto and show Byron's lasting attitude to battle: admiration for the courage of individuals combined with contempt for the governments that spend them. The Giant of Talavera is a romantic personification in the manner of Spenser and the eighteenth-century ode, but the stanza that follows it turns grimly sardonic as three nations meet merely 'to feed the crow'. Glory is presented as a spectacle, and the narrator is aware that he enjoys it as one (Rutherford 1961).

The Maid of Saragoza allows the poem to imagine a heroism of the people rather than of kings and generals, and also to play with gender: the narrator insists that she is not an Amazon but a beautiful woman who became a soldier through necessity. The repeated contrast between Spanish peasants and their 'fallen Chivalry' anticipates the poem's later insistence that liberty depends on the people (Kelsall 1987).

The bullfight is a set piece in which the narrator records the crowd's excitement, the horses' suffering and the bull's death with almost cinematic detail, then judges the sport as a school of cruelty. Harold's 'To Inez', placed near the end, gives the clearest statement of his condition, a gloom with no cause he will name. The canto's last stanzas, mourning Wingfield, open a second, more personal register that will grow in Canto II (McGann 1968) (Stabler 2002).

Part 3: Athens, Albania and Ali Pasha

Canto II, stanzas 1–72, with the Suliote war-song 'Tambourgi! Tambourgi!'

Summary

Canto II begins on the Acropolis with an address to Athena, whose temple remains while her worship and her people's greatness have gone. The narrator invites the reader to look on 'a nation's sepulchre' and meditates on the passing of religions and the doubtful hope of an afterlife. Sitting on a fallen stone of the Parthenon, he denounces the Scottish nobleman Lord Elgin, 'the modern Pict', for stripping the temple of its sculptures and shipping them to Britain.

The narrative returns to Harold, who is sailing aboard a British warship past Gibraltar. Night at sea prompts reflections on solitude: being alone in nature is not solitude, but being alone in the crowds of cities is. At Malta ('Calypso's isles') Harold meets 'Florence', a married lady who is surprised to find him indifferent to her; the narrator hints that his coldness is chosen rather than natural.

Harold then lands in Albania, the land of Alexander and of Skanderbeg, and travels through Epirus by Zitza and Dodona to Tepelene, where Ali Pasha keeps his court. The narrator describes the Albanian, Tatar and Turkish soldiers in the courtyard during Ramadan, and Ali himself, gentle-faced and cruel. Later, driven ashore by a storm, Harold is welcomed by the Suliotes, and watches their night-fire dances and hears their war-song, given as a lyric in anapaestic couplets.

Analysis

The Athens stanzas join two great Romantic subjects, ruin and liberty. The narrator's melancholy over the Acropolis is not antiquarian but political: Athens has been reduced by 'the drear sceptre' of rulers who have never felt the glow of ancient culture, and modern Britons, through Elgin, have added a new desecration. Byron's attack was published four years before Parliament bought the sculptures for the British Museum in 1816, and it remains a founding text in the long argument about their return (St Clair 1967).

The passage on solitude in nature (2.25–26) is an early version of the Wordsworthian mood that will dominate Canto III, but here it is brief and conventional, closer to the eighteenth-century poetry of retirement. The Florence episode lets Byron ironise his hero's coldness, and his narrator's knowingness about women, in a tone that looks forward to Don Juan (McGann 1968).

Albania is the most exotic setting of the first two cantos, and Byron's description of Ali Pasha, half admiring and half horrified, set the pattern for his later Oriental tales. Byron himself visited Ali in October 1809. The Suliote song gives voice to a fierce, free mountain people and supplies an implicit contrast with the subject Greeks of the following stanzas (Beaton 2013) (Franklin 2000).

Part 4: The Lament for Greece

Canto II, stanzas 73–98

Summary

The narrator addresses 'Fair Greece', a 'sad relic' of past greatness, and asks who will lead her scattered children out of their long bondage. He recalls Thermopylae and Thrasybulus, who freed Athens from the Thirty Tyrants, and reproaches the modern Greeks for trembling under Turkish rule.

In the canto's most quoted stanza he tells them that 'hereditary bondsmen' must strike the blow themselves: neither France nor Russia will free them for their own sake. The narrator describes the gaiety of the carnival at Constantinople before Lent and contrasts the merriment of the Greeks with the sorrow a true son of Greece must feel.

He then turns to the land itself: its temples are crumbling into the soil, but its skies, mountains, and battlefields such as Marathon remain, 'haunted, holy ground'. In the last stanzas the voice becomes private. The poet mourns a beloved who has died while he was away (the 'Thyrza' of other poems, usually identified with John Edleston), and closes the canto alone, bereaved and old before his time.

Analysis

This section made Byron the poet of Greek liberty. Its rhetoric is double: it praises the ancient Greeks and despises the modern ones for submitting, but it also places the responsibility for freedom on the oppressed themselves rather than on foreign powers. Philhellenes across Europe quoted the 'hereditary bondsmen' stanza during the War of Independence that began in 1821, and Byron himself died in Greece in 1824 while serving the cause (St Clair 1972) (Beaton 2013).

The landscape stanzas develop one of the poem's central ideas: human works decay, but nature and 'well-recorded worth', the memory of great deeds, survive. Marathon, a bare plain, is more enduring than the Parthenon. The idea that the land itself carries history, so that the traveller treads 'haunted, holy ground', is part of what made the poem a guide for later visitors (Buzard 1993).

The closing elegy gives the cantos of 1812 a private ending. Grief for the dead beloved, for friends and for a mother breaks into the travel poem and identifies the narrator with the sorrowing Harold far more closely than the opening stanzas had allowed. Critics have seen here the first sign of the merging of poet and persona that becomes explicit in Cantos III and IV (McGann 1968) (Marchand 1965).

Part 5: Ada, Waterloo and the Rhine

Canto III, stanzas 1–55, with the lyric 'The castled crag of Drachenfels'

Summary

Canto III opens with the poet at sea, asking whether his daughter Ada, whom he has left behind, resembles her mother, and declaring that he departs he knows not where. He resumes the theme of his 'youth's summer', the wandering outlaw Harold, though his own heart and harp may have lost a string. Poetry, he says, is a means of creating a more intense being and of escaping himself. He then retells Harold's history: the hero tried to live among men again, found that he could not, and now wanders forth once more, 'self-exiled', with less hope but also less gloom.

Harold stands on the field of Waterloo, the 'place of skulls' where Napoleon's eagle fell. The narrator questions whether the victory has freed Europe or merely restored other despots. In the most famous passage of the poem he recreates the Duchess of Richmond's ball in Brussels on the night before the battle, the first sound of cannon, the hurried farewells, the march through the forest, and the dead heaped together by the next evening. He honours his cousin Frederick Howard, killed in the battle, and reflects on the long survival of grief.

The narrator then meditates on Napoleon, 'the greatest, nor the worst of men', whose restless spirit could not endure quiet and whose ambition consumed him. Harold continues up the Rhine past ruined castles, and the narrator contrasts the river's beauty with its history of feudal and modern wars. Harold's heart has not lost all love: he sends lilies to a beloved woman (Byron's half-sister Augusta) with a lyric about the crag of Drachenfels.

Analysis

Canto III is the work of a different poet from the young traveller of 1812. The archaism has almost gone, and the stanza has become a vehicle for long, enjambed, intensely personal reflection. The opening apostrophe to Ada and the account of a poetry that lets the writer 'live / A being more intense' place the author's own suffering at the centre, and the narrator now speaks of Harold as a version of himself (McGann 1968) (Gleckner 1967).

The Waterloo stanzas are a lesson in pacing. The ball is described in brilliant, lilting verse, and the interruption of the cannon is staged through questions, denials and exclamations, so that the reader hears the sound before the dancers admit it. Byron's politics complicate the patriotic subject: the narrator refuses to celebrate the battle as a triumph of liberty, and his mourning extends to all the dead, 'friend, foe', in one burial (Stabler 2002).

The portrait of Napoleon is the key to the canto's psychology. Napoleon is condemned for ambition but recognised as a kindred spirit, one of those 'quick bosoms' for whom quiet is hell. Many readers have taken the stanzas as an oblique self-portrait, in which Byron analyses the same restless 'fever at the core' that drives Harold and himself (Thorslev 1962) (Rutherford 1961).

Part 6: Lake Leman, Rousseau and Clarens

Canto III, stanzas 56–118

Summary

Passing the monument of the French general Marceau at Coblenz and the shattered fortress of Ehrenbreitstein, Harold bids farewell to the Rhine and enters the Alps. At Morat the narrator recalls the bones of the Burgundians killed by the Swiss, and at Avenches the story of Julia Alpinula. On Lake Leman (Lake Geneva) he seeks solitude, declaring that he lives not in himself but becomes part of the landscape around him, and that mountains, waves and skies are part of his soul.

He recalls Rousseau, born at Geneva, 'the apostle of affliction', whose eloquence made passion beautiful and whose writings, he suggests, helped to kindle the French Revolution. A calm night on the lake, with the stars as 'the poetry of heaven', gives way to a tremendous storm in the mountains, which the narrator greets with exultation. He wishes he could compress his whole being into one word, lightning.

In the morning he visits Clarens, the setting of Rousseau's novel Julie, and praises it as a place consecrated by love. He then turns to Lausanne and Ferney, homes of Gibbon and Voltaire, 'gigantic minds' whose sceptical boldness he respects. The canto closes with the poet's defiance of the world ('I have not loved the world, nor the world me'), a hope that goodness is not merely a name, and a final blessing on his absent daughter.

Analysis

The Lake Leman stanzas are the most Wordsworthian passage Byron ever wrote. They borrow the language of union with nature found in 'Tintern Abbey', and the summer of 1816 is usually given the credit: Shelley, Byron later said, had dosed him with Wordsworth's poetry. Yet the mood differs from Wordsworth's. Nature is valued above all as a refuge from mankind, and the self that dissolves into the landscape remains proud, wounded and restless (McGann 1968) (McGann 2002).

The storm stanzas show the other side of Byron's nature. Where Wordsworth found calm, Byron finds a sublime that mirrors his own passions, and he asks to be 'a portion of the tempest'. The narrator's longing to turn his whole being into one word of lightning expresses both the ambition and the frustration of Romantic self-expression (Gleckner 1967).

Rousseau, Voltaire and Gibbon are honoured as great, dangerous intellects who suffered for their boldness, and the portrait of Rousseau, self-tormenting and eloquent, is again a mirror for the poet. The close of the canto restates the exile's defiance, but its last word goes to Ada, so that the canto ends, as it began, with the family the poet has lost (Marchand 1965) (Franklin 2000).

Part 7: Venice, Arquà and Florence

Canto IV, stanzas 1–77

Summary

Canto IV opens with the narrator standing on the Bridge of Sighs in Venice between the Doge's palace and the prison. He evokes the city's former empire, now fallen under Austria, and her decaying palaces, and he defends the reality of 'the beings of the mind', the imagined figures of literature, against the disappointments of experience. He thinks of his own name, which he does not ask his country to remember, and admits that he has reaped what he planted.

Leaving Venice, the narrator describes a sunset on the Brenta, then visits Petrarch's tomb at Arquà in the Euganean hills and the court of Ferrara, where Tasso was confined as a madman. He addresses Italy, whose 'fatal gift of beauty' has drawn invaders, recalls a letter of the Roman Servius Sulpicius on the ruined cities of Greece, and comes to Florence.

In the Uffizi he contemplates the Venus de' Medici as a revelation of ideal beauty. In Santa Croce he names the dead buried there, Michelangelo, Alfieri, Galileo and Machiavelli, and regrets that Florence lacks the bones of Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio. He prefers nature to galleries, and moves on to the battlefield of Trasimene, the clear stream of Clitumnus, the waterfall of Velino at Terni, and Mount Soracte, where he takes leave of Horace, whom he was forced to study at school.

Analysis

Canto IV has almost no narrative. Its unity is that of a journey and of a mind. In the prose dedication Byron told Hobhouse that he had given up trying to separate the pilgrim from himself, and the canto is spoken throughout by a first-person 'I' who is plainly Byron, living in Venice, an exile with a famous name and a ruined marriage (McGann 1968) (Joseph 1964).

Venice and Italy are treated as female figures of fallen glory, admired and pitied for their beauty and their subjection. The narrator's politics are those of Italian nationalism in its early form: Italy has been made a prey by foreign powers, Venice is under Austria, and the poem's great Italians, Petrarch, Tasso and Dante, appear as patriots and victims as much as poets (Stabler 2002) (Kelsall 1987).

The canto's long meditations on art, especially on the Venus de' Medici, set the claim of the ideal, 'what Mind can make', against the ruin of history. The narrator's preference for nature over galleries prepares for the poem's climax at the sea, while the Trasimene and Velino stanzas show his favourite double vision of beauty and slaughter (Gleckner 1967) (McGann 1980).

Part 8: Rome, the Colosseum and the Ocean

Canto IV, stanzas 78–186

Summary

The narrator hails Rome, 'my country! city of the soul', the 'Niobe of nations' who sits childless among her ruins. He surveys the destruction wrought by Goths, Christians, time, war, flood and fire, and reflects on Sulla, on Cromwell, and above all on Napoleon, a 'bastard Caesar' who ruined himself. Disillusioned with the French Revolution, which 'got drunk with blood', he nevertheless insists that the banner of freedom still flies against the wind.

He visits the tomb of Cecilia Metella and wonders about the woman buried there, recalls Cicero, the Palatine, the Forum and Rienzi, the 'last of Romans', and comes to the grotto of Egeria, the nymph who counselled King Numa, which prompts a bleak meditation on love as an ideal that the mind creates and life never fulfils. In the moonlit Colosseum he calls on Time and Nemesis and pronounces his 'curse' on his enemies, which is forgiveness. He describes the statue of the dying gladiator, imagining the man's thoughts of his children by the Danube.

Further meditations follow on the Pantheon, St Peter's, the Laocoön and the Apollo Belvedere. The narrator asks where Harold is and answers that he has faded away. He mourns the death of Princess Charlotte in childbirth in 1817, then looks from the Alban hills over Lake Nemi to the sea. In a sequence of stanzas he addresses the Ocean, unchanged since creation, untouched by human ruin and beloved by him since boyhood, and finally bids farewell to the reader.

Analysis

Rome gives the poem its largest subject: the whole of human history understood as a cycle of greatness, tyranny and decay. The narrator's grief for the city absorbs his private woes ('What are our woes and sufferance?'), but his political conclusion is stubbornly hopeful: freedom has been betrayed by Napoleon and by the Restoration, yet its seed lasts. Byron would soon join the Carbonari in Ravenna (Kelsall 1987) (Beaton 2013).

The Colosseum stanzas combine personal and historical judgment. The narrator's 'curse' of forgiveness is a barely veiled reply to his wife and to English society, while the Dying Gladiator, 'butchered to make a Roman holiday', turns the ruin into an image of imperial cruelty and of barbarian vengeance to come. The statue, now known to represent a dying Gaul, became one of the most visited objects in Rome partly because of these lines (Buzard 1993) (Stabler 2002).

The dismissal of Harold (4.164) completes the long merger of persona and poet, and the Ocean apostrophe (4.178–184) provides the closing vision. The sea is the one thing that history cannot touch: empires rise and fall on its shores, fleets are wrecked on it, but 'Time writes no wrinkle' on its brow. The poet who has wandered among ruins finally claims the Ocean as his true parent and playmate, and the poem ends with his farewell rather than his hero's (McGann 1968) (Marchand 1965).

Characters

Harold and the narrator

Childe Harold
A young English nobleman of ancient but tarnished family, worn out by pleasure, unloved and unable to love, who leaves home to wander abroad. In Cantos I and II he is a lightly sketched, half-satirical figure who watches rather than acts; in Canto III he returns as a self-exiled outcast whose past is hinted at but never explained; in Canto IV he is mentioned only to be dismissed. Readers from 1812 onwards identified him with Byron, though Byron at first denied it.
The narrator (the Pilgrim)
The voice that tells Harold's story and comments on everything he sees. At first an urbane, opinionated guide, he grows more personal with each canto, mourning his own dead, addressing his daughter, and in Canto IV speaking openly as the poet himself. Much of the poem's interest lies in how this voice gradually absorbs the hero.
Ianthe
The dedicatee of the poem from 1813, Lady Charlotte Harley, daughter of the Earl and Countess of Oxford, then about eleven. The poet praises her innocent beauty and asks her to remember him.
Harold's page and yeoman
The two servants addressed in the 'Good Night' lyric of Canto I. The boy weeps for his parents and the yeoman fears for his wife and children; their simple ties throw Harold's loneliness into relief.
Inez
The Spanish woman to whom Harold sings in Canto I. She is never described; the lyric is a confession of the gloom that keeps him from responding to her.
Florence
A lady Harold meets at Malta in Canto II, based on Mrs Constance Spencer Smith, with whom Byron had a flirtation. She is surprised that Harold remains unmoved by her.
Ada
Byron's infant daughter by Annabella Milbanke, born in December 1815, whom he never saw again after the separation. She is addressed at the beginning and end of Canto III. She later became the mathematician Ada Lovelace.
The lost beloved
The dead person mourned at the end of Canto II, usually identified with John Edleston, a Cambridge chorister Byron loved, who died in 1811 and was addressed elsewhere as 'Thyrza'.

Historical figures

Napoleon Bonaparte
The dominant historical presence of Cantos III and IV. At Waterloo the narrator describes his fall; later he is 'the greatest, nor the worst of men', a restless spirit destroyed by ambition, and in Rome a 'bastard Caesar' who betrayed the cause of freedom. Byron's attitude mixes admiration, kinship and condemnation.
Ali Pasha
The Albanian ruler of Ioannina, nominally an Ottoman governor, whom Byron visited at Tepelene in 1809. The poem presents him as a gentle-looking old man whose face hides great cruelty.
Lord Elgin
Thomas Bruce, seventh Earl of Elgin, British ambassador to Constantinople, who removed much of the Parthenon's sculpture between 1801 and 1812. Byron attacks him as 'the modern Pict' and the 'dull spoiler' of Athens.
The Maid of Saragoza
Agustina Zaragoza (Agustina de Aragón), who fought in the defence of Saragossa against the French in 1808. Byron saw her in Seville and makes her the symbol of Spanish popular resistance.
Frederick Howard
Byron's cousin, killed at Waterloo. The narrator honours him partly in atonement for having wronged his father, the Earl of Carlisle, in an earlier satire.
Rousseau
Jean-Jacques Rousseau, born in Geneva, whose novel Julie made Clarens famous. The narrator describes him as a self-tormenting genius whose eloquence helped to inspire the French Revolution.
Voltaire and Gibbon
The sceptical writers who lived at Ferney and Lausanne, praised in Canto III as 'gigantic minds' of fire and of deep thought.
Petrarch, Tasso and Dante
The great Italian poets of Canto IV: Petrarch at his tomb in Arquà, Tasso as the persecuted prisoner of Ferrara, Dante as the exile whose bones Florence never recovered. They embody poetry as national memory and the poet as victim.
Princess Charlotte
Daughter of the Prince Regent and heir to the British throne, who died in childbirth in November 1817. Her death is mourned in Canto IV as the loss of a nation's hope.
John Cam Hobhouse
Byron's companion on the travels of 1809–10 and in Italy in 1817, to whom Canto IV is dedicated. He supplied the historical notes to the fourth canto.

Places and others

The Dying Gladiator
The ancient marble figure in the Capitoline Museums, now identified as a dying Gaul. Byron imagines him as a Dacian captive thinking of his family as he dies, and turns him into a figure of imperial cruelty.
The Ocean
The sea addressed at the end of Canto IV, the poem's last great presence: unchanging, indifferent to human power, and claimed by the poet as a parent and companion of his youth.
Rome
The 'city of the soul' and 'Niobe of nations', whose ruins dominate the second half of Canto IV and stand for the whole of human history.
Greece
Addressed as a fallen woman and a sacred land in Canto II, Greece is the centre of the poem's politics of liberty and its sense of a glorious past.

Themes

The self in exile

The poem's hero leaves home because he is weary of it, and its narrator becomes, by Canto III, an exile who cannot return. Travel is never simply pleasure: it is flight from the self and from society, and a search for a place where the self might be at rest. The narrator says as much in Canto III, where poetry and wandering are both ways of escaping 'the weary dream' of private grief.

The theme grows more serious as the poem proceeds. In 1812 Harold's alienation is half a pose; in 1816 and 1818 it is the author's real situation after the scandal of his separation. The poem's lasting achievement was to make this restless, self-divided consciousness the subject of a long poem and to persuade readers across Europe that it was representative of their age (Thorslev 1962) (McGann 1968).

Liberty and tyranny

Every canto contains a politics of freedom. Spain resists the French; Greece lies under the Ottomans and is despoiled by Elgin; Waterloo raises the question whether the victory over Napoleon has freed Europe or restored the old monarchies; Italy is held by Austria. The narrator consistently sides with peoples against governments, and with the memory of ancient republics against modern empires.

The poem's politics are those of an aristocratic Whig who admired republican Rome and distrusted the Tory government of his day, but its rhetoric reached much further. Its appeal to the 'hereditary bondsmen' of Greece and its image of Freedom's banner streaming against the wind were taken up by liberal and national movements throughout the nineteenth century (Kelsall 1987) (Beaton 2013).

Ruin and history

The poem is crowded with ruins: Athens, castles on the Rhine, the field of Waterloo, the decaying palaces of Venice, the Colosseum. They are occasions for melancholy reflection on the passing of power, but also for judgment, since the narrator usually finds the cause of ruin in human ambition and tyranny. History appears as a cycle in which nations rise, conquer and fall, and the same lessons are never learned.

Against this cycle the poem sets a few things that endure: the memory of heroic deeds, the works of genius, nature, and finally the Ocean, on which 'Time writes no wrinkle' (Gleckner 1967) (Stabler 2002).

Nature as refuge and mirror

Nature in the first cantos is chiefly scenery, though even there the narrator prefers mountains and sea to cities. In Canto III it becomes a spiritual resource: the poet seeks to lose himself in the lake and mountains and to mingle with the universe. The storm in the Alps and the Ocean in Canto IV show another kind of nature, sublime, violent and indifferent to man, which the poet loves because it reflects his own passions and transcends human corruption (McGann 2002).

Poetry, imagination and fame

The narrator repeatedly reflects on what poetry does. In Canto III it allows the poet to create a more intense being; in Canto IV the 'beings of the mind' are more real and lasting than the disappointments of life. The poem is also concerned with literary fame, from Petrarch's tomb and Tasso's prison to the poet's own name, which he refuses to beg his country to honour. Its ending, in which the poet extinguishes his 'midnight lamp', asks whether anything of the writer survives in the reader's memory (McGann 1968) (Mole 2007).

Love, loss and the family

Beneath the public themes runs a private history of loss: the friends who died in 1811, the beloved mourned at the end of Canto II, the separated wife, the daughter Ada and the sister Augusta. Love in the poem is usually lost, forbidden or idealised; Canto IV calls it a creation of the mind that no living person can satisfy. Yet the poem's most tender moments are its addresses to the absent child, and its curse on enemies is, at least in words, a curse of forgiveness (MacCarthy 2002) (Marchand 1965).

Motifs

Farewells

The poem is built on leave-takings: Harold's 'Good Night' to England, the farewell to Ada, to the Rhine, to Horace and finally to the reader in the last stanza. Each departure renews the sense of a life always in motion and never at home.

Pilgrimage

Harold's journey is a secular pilgrimage. Its shrines are battlefields, tombs of poets and ruined cities rather than holy places, and its goal, Rome, is approached as a 'city of the soul'. The figure of the pilgrim with 'sandal-shoon and scallop shell' recurs at the end.

Apostrophe

The narrator constantly turns to address absent or inanimate things: Spain, Athens, Greece, Ada, Napoleon, the Rhine, Lake Leman, Italy, Rome, Time, the Ocean. The device creates the poem's rhetorical intensity and makes the landscape a series of listeners for the poet's voice.

The battlefield

Talavera, Albuera, Marathon, Waterloo, Morat and Trasimene are each visited and remembered. The battlefield becomes the place where the poem's admiration for courage meets its contempt for the powers that waste lives.

Tombs and graves

The poem visits the graves of the great (Petrarch, Michelangelo, Galileo, Cecilia Metella) and laments the unburied or forgotten dead, from the sailors of the Ocean apostrophe to the poet's own friends.

Symbols

The Ocean

The sea stands for what is untouched by time and human ruin. It frames the whole poem, from Harold's first voyage to the final apostrophe, and becomes a symbol of the freedom and permanence the poet cannot find on land.

The Colosseum

The great amphitheatre stands for Rome's grandeur and cruelty at once, and for the endurance of ruin. In the moonlight it becomes the setting for the poet's appeal to Time and Nemesis.

The storm

The thunderstorm over Lake Leman symbolises the passionate energy of the poet's spirit, which seeks to become 'a portion of the tempest' rather than to find calm.

The eagle

Napoleon's imperial eagle falls at Waterloo and reappears in Rome, where the narrator imagines a man who would have tamed his eagles like a falcon. It symbolises the ambition that soars and destroys.

Critical debates

The Byronic hero and self-dramatisation

Harold is the prototype of the 'Byronic hero': proud, melancholy, guilty of unnamed sins, cut off from humanity and fascinating to it. Peter Thorslev's classic study placed him in a family of Romantic types, among them the Hero of Sensibility, the Gothic villain, the Noble Outlaw and the Satanic and Promethean rebel, and showed how Byron combined them. Harold in Canto I is a jaded Hero of Sensibility; in Canto III he shares the darker energy of the outlaws of Byron's tales (Thorslev 1962).

Critics have long debated whether this is self-dramatisation. Early reviewers, and Byron's Victorian detractors, saw the poem as an exhibition of the author's personality; later critics have argued that it is a consciously constructed role that Byron both inhabited and examined. The question is entangled with Byron's celebrity: Christensen and Mole show how the poem's intimacy with its readers was produced by and for a new commercial print culture (Christensen 1993) (Mole 2007) (McDayter 2009).

Narrator and persona

Byron's original preface insisted that Harold was a fictitious character, and the dedication to Canto IV admitted that the distinction had become impossible to maintain. Jerome McGann's Fiery Dust made this movement the centre of the poem: Childe Harold is the story of a poet discovering, through the act of writing, that his subject is himself, so that the merging of narrator and hero is the poem's real plot rather than a failure of design (McGann 1968).

Others have described the relation differently. M. K. Joseph treated Harold as a mask that Byron used and then discarded, while Andrew Rutherford regarded the first two cantos as artistically uncertain, divided between satire and sentiment, and found the later cantos more powerful but sometimes overwrought. McGann's later edition, by printing the manuscript variants, made it possible to trace how the poem changed in composition (Joseph 1964) (Rutherford 1961) (McGann 1980).

The politics of the poem

The poem's politics have been read in very different ways. For many nineteenth-century readers it was a liberal manifesto, a poem of liberty addressed to oppressed peoples. Malcolm Kelsall argued instead that Byron's politics were those of an aristocratic Whig, rooted in a classical republicanism that admired heroic freedom but distrusted democracy, and that the poem's laments for lost liberty are as much elegies for an aristocratic ideal as calls to revolution (Kelsall 1987).

Recent work has stressed the poem's historical precision: its responses to the Convention of Cintra, to Waterloo and the Congress of Vienna, to the Austrian occupation of Italy. Jane Stabler reads its digressions and notes as a way of engaging with history, while Roderick Beaton traces how the political attitudes of the Greek canto developed into Byron's direct involvement in the Greek Revolution (Stabler 2002) (Beaton 2013).

Philhellenism and the Elgin Marbles

Canto II helped to create European Philhellenism, the enthusiasm for the liberation of modern Greece in the name of ancient Greece. William St Clair's history of the Philhellenes shows how influential Byron's lines were, and also how far the idealised Greece of the poets differed from the realities of the war of the 1820s (St Clair 1972).

The attack on Elgin raises a related question. St Clair's study of Elgin documents the removal of the sculptures and the public debate that followed, in which Byron's poem and his satire The Curse of Minerva were major contributions. Readers have asked whether Byron's anger was aesthetic, patriotic or political; the poem frames the removal as one more act of foreign domination over an enslaved people (St Clair 1967) (Beaton 2013).

Wordsworthian nature in Canto III

The nature stanzas of Canto III have been called Byron's most Wordsworthian verse. Byron told Thomas Medwin that Shelley had made him read Wordsworth during the summer of 1816, and Wordsworth himself is reported to have complained that the canto borrowed his manner. Critics have debated whether Byron sincerely adopted a philosophy of nature or merely its vocabulary, and whether his mysticism of the lake is undercut by the proud misanthropy that accompanies it (McGann 1968) (Marchand 1965).

The question is sharpened by Byron's later mockery of Wordsworth in Don Juan and elsewhere. McGann has argued that Byron's Romanticism differs from Wordsworth's because it never stops being conscious of its audience and its own rhetoric; Gleckner reads the nature passages as one more failed attempt to escape a fallen world (McGann 2002) (Gleckner 1967).

Travel writing and the Grand Tour

Childe Harold belongs to the tradition of travel writing, and especially to the Grand Tour, though the war forced Byron's first journey off the traditional route. James Buzard has shown how the poem became, paradoxically, a guide for the tourists it seemed to despise: Murray's handbooks for travellers quoted Byron's stanzas at the sites they describe, and later visitors approached Waterloo, Lake Geneva or the Colosseum through his lines (Buzard 1993).

This raises questions about the poem's stance. The narrator often distinguishes himself from ordinary travellers and claims a deeper, more solitary vision, yet the poem also supplies information, historical notes and picturesque description. Critics have linked this tension to the commercial success of the poem and to Byron's self-image as an aristocratic traveller (Christensen 1993) (Franklin 2000).

Reception and 'Byronism'

The poem made Byron famous overnight, and its hero was soon imitated in poetry, fiction, fashion and behaviour. Contemporary reviews, gathered by Andrew Rutherford, show admiration for its energy alongside alarm at its scepticism and gloom. The figure of the brooding, exiled genius spread through European literature, from Pushkin and Lermontov to Heine and Berlioz (Rutherford 1970) (Bone 2004).

Victorian responses were divided. Andrew Elfenbein has shown how writers such as Carlyle, Tennyson and the Brontës defined themselves for or against Byron, and how 'Byronism' became a cultural style to be adopted, criticised or outgrown. Modern studies of celebrity have reopened the question of how much of the poem's power came from readers' belief that it revealed the real Byron (Elfenbein 1995) (McDayter 2009) (Mole 2007).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. How does the mock-medieval style of the opening stanzas shape our first impression of Harold?

    Consider the archaisms, the Spenserian stanza, the title 'Childe' and the tone of the first stanzas; ask whether they make Harold heroic, comic or both.

  2. Trace the relationship between Harold and the narrator across the four cantos. When does the distinction begin to break down?

    Compare the elegies at the end of Cantos I and II, the opening of Canto III, and the dismissal of Harold in 4.164.

  3. What is Byron's attitude to war in the poem?

    Look at Talavera (1.39–41), the Maid of Saragoza, Waterloo (3.21–28) and Trasimene (4.62–63); distinguish between individual courage and the purposes of states.

  4. How does Canto II connect the ruins of ancient Greece with the condition of modern Greeks?

    Think about the Elgin stanzas, the 'hereditary bondsmen' stanza and the claim that the land is 'haunted, holy ground'.

  5. In what ways is the portrait of Napoleon in Canto III also a portrait of the poet?

    Read 3.36–45 with 3.12–16 and the account of 'quick bosoms' for whom quiet is hell.

  6. How Wordsworthian is the nature poetry of Canto III?

    Compare 3.72–75 with the storm stanzas (3.92–97); consider whether nature brings peace or excitement, and whether the poet loses himself or asserts himself.

  7. What role do great writers and artists play in Canto IV?

    Think of Petrarch, Tasso, Dante, the Venus de' Medici and the poet's own concern with his name and fame.

  8. Why does the poem end with the Ocean rather than with Rome?

    Consider what the Ocean represents in contrast to the ruins of history, and how the final stanzas return to the beginning of the pilgrimage.

  9. How do private grief and public history interact in the poem?

    Consider the dead friends of 1811, Ada, Frederick Howard, the 'curse' of forgiveness and Princess Charlotte.

  10. Why do you think the poem became a guidebook for nineteenth-century travellers?

    Think about its itinerary, its descriptions of famous sites, its historical reflections and the personality of its narrator.
