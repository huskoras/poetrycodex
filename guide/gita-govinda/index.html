Gita Govinda

Overview

Poet
Jayadeva, a Sanskrit poet of eastern India; he names himself in nearly every song, calls his birthplace Kindubilva and his wife Padmavati, and is traditionally placed at the court of King Lakshmana Sena of Bengal
Date
Late twelfth century; Lakshmana Sena reigned from about 1178 to about 1206, and verses attributed to Jayadeva appear in Shridharadasa's Bengal anthology of about 1205
Language and form
Sanskrit; a cycle of twenty-four songs (prabandhas, usually of eight couplets and so also called ashtapadis), each marked with a raga and a tala, set within narrative and descriptive verses in classical metres
Structure
Twelve sargas ('cantos'), each named after an epithet of Krishna that sums up his state of mind in it
Subject
The love of Krishna and Radha: his dalliance with the cowherd women in spring, her jealous withdrawal, their separation, the messages carried by Radha's friend, reconciliation and union
Setting
The forests of Vrindavana on the banks of the Yamuna (Arnold's 'Jumna'), in spring and through one long night to dawn
Speakers
Radha, Krishna and Radha's friend, with the poet's own voice in the closing couplet of each song; short narrative verses link the songs
This translation
Sir Edwin Arnold, The Indian Song of Songs (London, 1875), a free verse paraphrase rather than a close translation
What Arnold leaves out
The opening verses of the first sarga, parts of the tenth and eleventh, and the whole of the twelfth, in which the lovers' union is described and Radha asks Krishna to adorn her again
Afterlife
Sung daily in the temple of Jagannatha at Puri, cherished by Chaitanya and the Bengal Vaishnavas, danced in Odissi, painted in Rajput and Pahari courts, and the first Sanskrit lyric widely read in Europe after William Jones's version of 1792

Context

The Gita Govinda ('Govinda in Song') was composed in the late twelfth century in eastern India. Its author, Jayadeva, tells us a little about himself in the signature verses that close his songs: he names his birthplace as Kindubilva and his wife as Padmavati, and one verse of the first sarga places him among four other poets, Umapatidhara, Sharana, Govardhana and Dhoyi, who are associated with the court of Lakshmana Sena, the last great Sena king of Bengal. On that evidence most scholars set the poem in Bengal in the years around 1180 to 1200, shortly before the Turkish conquest of the region. A rival tradition claims Jayadeva for Orissa, where a village called Kenduli also exists and where the poem was sung in the temple of Jagannatha at Puri from at least the fifteenth century; the question has never been settled (Miller 1977) (De 1961).

Jayadeva wrote for a court culture steeped in kavya, the refined Sanskrit poetry of love, landscape and praise collected in anthologies such as Vidyakara's Treasury, and his verses use its stock of images: the cool south wind from the Malaya hills, the koil's cry in the mango groves, the bee and the lotus, the god of love Kama with his five flower-arrows (Ingalls 1965). What is new is the form. Between the narrative verses in classical metres he set twenty-four songs, each labelled with a melodic mode (raga) and a rhythmic cycle (tala), each with a refrain, and each closing with a couplet in which Jayadeva names himself and offers the song to its hearers. The poem is therefore a work to be performed as well as read, close to the regional song traditions that grew alongside it (Miller 1977) (Siegel 2009).

Its subject is the love of Krishna and Radha. Krishna, worshipped as an incarnation or as the fullest form of the god Vishnu, was by Jayadeva's time the hero of a large body of stories about his youth among the cowherds of Vrindavana, told most fully in the tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana, where the cowherd women, the gopis, leave their homes to dance with him by night. Radha is not named in the Bhagavata; she appears earlier in scattered verses of Prakrit and Sanskrit poetry, but it is the Gita Govinda that makes her the single beloved at the centre of Krishna's story, the equal and sometimes the mistress of the god (Hawley 1982) (Archer 1957).

The poem's importance for Indian religion is hard to overstate. It became a foundational text for the devotional movements known as bhakti, in which God is approached through loving emotion rather than ritual or knowledge, and especially for the Vaishnavas of Bengal who followed Chaitanya (1486–1533), for whom the love of Radha and Krishna was the highest model of the soul's relation to God (De 1961) (Dimock 1966) (Hardy 1983). It was set to music and dance across India, copied and illustrated in hundreds of manuscripts, and imitated by later poets in Sanskrit and the vernaculars (Randhawa 1963).

For English readers the poem has had a second history. Sir William Jones, the founder of the Asiatic Society in Calcutta, published a prose version in Asiatick Researches in 1792, with an essay that read it as a mystical allegory comparable to Persian Sufi poetry and to the Song of Songs; the German Romantics read it through him, and Christian Lassen edited the Sanskrit text with a Latin translation in 1836 (Jones 1792) (Lassen 1836) (Schwab 1984). Edwin Arnold (1832–1904), who had taught at the Deccan College in Poona before becoming a journalist in London, made one of the first English verse renderings in 1875 and called it The Indian Song of Songs. His version, the one quoted in this guide, is a Victorian poem in its own right: musical, inventive, and much freer, and much more moralising, than its title page suggests (Arnold 1875) (Wright 1957).

Plot overview

After a hymn in praise of Vishnu's ten incarnations, the poem opens in spring in the forest of Vrindavana. Radha, searching for Krishna, is shown by her friend how he dances and makes love with the cowherd women. Wounded, Radha withdraws to a bower, remembers his love for her, and sends her friend to him. Krishna, meanwhile, grows tired of the dancers, thinks of Radha, and searches for her in vain; he sits by the Yamuna in remorse.

Radha's friend becomes the go-between. She tells Krishna how Radha is wasting with love and tells Radha how Krishna waits for her by the river, urging each to go to the other. Radha is too weak to go; Krishna is urged to come. As the moon rises and he does not appear, Radha imagines him in another woman's arms and pours out her jealousy and despair through the night.

At dawn Krishna arrives, bearing the marks of another night's love, and Radha angrily sends him away. Her friend persuades her to give up her proud anger. Krishna returns and pleads for forgiveness in a song of praise and self-abasement; Radha relents. In the evening her companions lead her, dressed as a bride, to the bower where Krishna waits, and she enters.

In Jayadeva's Sanskrit a twelfth sarga follows, describing the lovers' night together and Radha's request that Krishna rearrange her hair, ornaments and make-up, after which the poet closes with verses on his art. Arnold omits that sarga and ends his version as Radha crosses the threshold of the bower, with a blessing in the name of Hari and a last stanza of praise for the poem.

Part by part

Part 1: Hymn to Vishnu

Sanskrit sarga 1, songs 1–2

Summary

The poem begins with praise. In twelve quatrains and a closing litany Arnold renders Jayadeva's first song, a hymn to the god Keshava (Vishnu, of whom Krishna is a form) in his ten incarnations or avatars: the Fish who saved the Veda from the flood, the Tortoise who bore the earth, the Boar who raised it on his tusk, the Man-Lion who tore the demon king, the Dwarf who tricked King Bali with three strides, Parashurama with his axe, Rama the slayer of ten-headed Ravana, Balarama the ploughman, the Buddha who ended blood sacrifice, and Kalki, the rider with the sword who will come at the end of the age.

A second hymn, in a different metre, follows. It praises the god who lies on the breast of Lakshmi (Arnold's 'Kumla'), whose jewels are the planets and whose crest-stone is the sun, slayer of the demons Madhu and Naraka, rider of the bird Garuda. It ends with a prayer that the hearer may be blessed by the god whose chest still bears the yellow pollen and the mark left by Lakshmi's embrace, and with Jayadeva's claim that his story hides secrets for 'wise ears'.

Analysis

Opening a love poem with a hymn to the ten avatars tells the audience who Krishna is before he appears as a playful cowherd. In the Sanskrit each stanza ends with the refrain 'Keshava, who took the form of...: victory, Hari, Lord of the world!', and Krishna himself is not one of the ten forms listed; the god who takes all ten shapes is the one the poem will show in love. Balarama, Krishna's brother, stands in his place in the list (Miller 1977).

The second hymn joins majesty and eroticism without strain. The lord of the universe is praised through the marks of his consort's embrace, and the prayer for blessing is made in the name of that embrace. This fusion of the sacred and the sensual is the poem's central gesture, and it is announced here, before the story begins (Siegel 1978).

Arnold's rendering keeps the litany and its exclamations ('Jai! Hari, jai!') but turns the Sanskrit refrain into the Victorian 'Hail, Keshav, hail!', and he adds his own emphasis on mystery and hidden meaning. The labels 'the Music MALAVA and the Mode RUPAKA' come from the manuscripts: the first names the raga, the second the tala or rhythmic cycle, which Arnold, following Jones, calls a 'mode'. The Sanskrit sarga actually begins before the hymns, with a verse in which Nanda sends Radha to take Krishna home through the stormy dusk, and with verses on the poet and his peers; Arnold leaves these out.

Part 2: Sarga 1: The Sports of Krishna

Sanskrit sarga 1, songs 3–4

Summary

Radha waits in the spring woods for Krishna, and one of her companions sings of where he is: dancing with the cowherd girls among the flowering trees, while the south wind blows from the Malaya hills, bees fill the blossoms and the koil calls. Each stanza ends on the same thought: in spring no one can bear to be alone.

The friend then points Krishna out among the leaves and sings a second song describing the women one by one: one draws his head to her breast, one gazes into his eyes, one kisses his cheek, one tugs at his cloth to lead him into the mango shade, one dances the circle-dance (the rasa) before him. Krishna gives back glance for glance and kiss for kiss.

Between and after the songs Arnold inserts passages in which Jayadeva explains that this earthly love conceals a heavenly meaning, and that Krishna, though lost in 'phantasies', feels a pang of longing for something dearer. The section ends with a blessing in the name of the god who laughed when Radha slipped among the dancers, kissed him, and left him silent.

Analysis

The first spring song, with its refrain on the season 'when none will live alone', is the poem's great set piece of landscape. The flowering trees and their insects are not decoration: every image is erotic, and the season itself seems to compel desire. In the Sanskrit the refrain says simply that Hari plays here in the sweet spring, dancing with young women, a time cruel to those who are separated (Miller 1977).

In the second song the friend's description is designed to wound Radha. The gopis are individualised by gesture alone, and the scene works through what Indian theorists call the rasa of love in separation (vipralambha shringara), the emotion felt by a lover who watches the beloved enjoy another. Radha's later anger, longing and forgiveness grow from this scene (Siegel 1978).

Arnold's additions are telling. In the Sanskrit the dancers are real cowherd women; in Arnold they become 'Visions of the earth', a 'shadowed earthly love', and Krishna 'Letting pass life's greater chance'. Arnold is following the allegorical reading inherited from William Jones, in which Krishna is the soul distracted by the senses and Radha is divine love; he makes the meaning explicit in a way Jayadeva's poem, with its simultaneous delight in the dance and in the god, does not (Jones 1792) (Wright 1957).

Part 3: Sarga 2: The Penitence of Krishna

Sanskrit sarga 2, songs 5–6

Summary

Hurt by what she has seen, Radha withdraws to a bower and sings of Krishna: her dancer, her delight, whose fault she sees clearly and whom she cannot stop loving. She resolves to be patient and to draw him back. She then sends her companion to him with a message: let him come, forgiven, to the joys she has kept for him, though she will scold him first.

In the closing part of the section Radha imagines Krishna already growing weary of the dancers. Arnold gives Krishna a song of farewell to the 'beautiful Shadows' of the wood, in which he says the vision of Radha has spoiled all other pleasures.

Analysis

The two Sanskrit songs of this sarga are Radha's: in the first she remembers Krishna as he plays among the women, in the second she recalls their own past lovemaking in a secret thicket and longs for it. Radha's voice is complex from the start. She sees Krishna's betrayal, delights in his beauty, and desires him all at once; her love is not innocent waiting but a mature passion that includes anger and memory (Miller 1977).

Arnold has rearranged and largely rewritten the sarga. He gives Radha a self-portrait of saintly patience ('My soul for tenderness, not blame, was made') and a message to Krishna, and he invents Krishna's farewell to the 'delicate phantoms', which turns the gopis into illusions and Krishna into a soul waking from a dream. The title 'The Penitence of Krishna' belongs to this reading; the Sanskrit title of the sarga calls him 'carefree Keshava' (Siegel 2009).

Arnold's Radha already speaks of 'the realms divine' and 'Time's great gate'. Where Jayadeva leaves his audience to feel the theological meaning inside the erotic story, Arnold has Radha herself state it, so that she becomes less a woman in love than a figure of the heavenly soul waiting for the earthly one.

Part 4: Sarga 3: Krishna Troubled

Sanskrit sarga 3, song 7

Summary

Krishna enthrones Radha in his heart, leaves the other women, and searches the forest for her. Failing to find her, he sits among the reeds by the Yamuna and sings of his shame: she left because she saw him with the others, and he could not stop her. He longs for her day and night and begs forgiveness.

In a second movement he turns on Kama, the god of love, and protests that he is not Shiva, Kama's ancient enemy, that the god should shoot at him: the lotus garland on his neck is not a snake, the blue on his throat is not poison, the dust on his chest is sandal, not ashes. Radha's brows and glances are arrows enough. The section ends with Jayadeva's prayer that hearers may learn the wisdom beneath the scene.

Analysis

The god is now the lover in torment. The emotional pattern of the poem is that separation, viraha, intensifies love, and Krishna's song of remorse is the counterpart of Radha's songs in the second sarga. The Sanskrit title, 'bewildered Madhusudana', stresses confusion rather than penitence, and the scene is one of the poem's boldest reversals: the supreme god as a helpless, abject lover (Siegel 1978) (Kinsley 1979).

The address to Kama is a witty set piece of the kind Sanskrit court poets loved. Shiva burned Kama to ashes with his third eye; Krishna lists the attributes that might make Kama mistake him for Shiva (the serpent, the poisoned throat, the ash) and explains each away. The joke reminds the audience that this suffering lover is himself a great god, and lets the poem speak of love's torment in the language of myth (Miller 1977).

Here Arnold keeps the signature in recognisable form: 'Jayadev of Kinduvilva' is the poet's own self-naming. The address to Kama is also close to the Sanskrit in substance, though Arnold expands it into a wooing speech to Radha herself.

Part 5: Sarga 4: Krishna Cheered

Sanskrit sarga 4, songs 8–9

Summary

Radha's friend comes to Krishna among the reeds and tells him that Radha suffers as much as he does. Sandal paste, moonlight and the south wind, the usual cures and pleasures of lovers, only torment her. She weaves a protective armour of lotus leaves for him, prepares a bed of flowers, paints his image in her tears and prays that he will grow tired of his other loves.

A second song repeats a single appeal at the head of every couplet, 'Krishna, till thou come unto her', listing the signs of her love-sickness: her necklace is too heavy, her eyes droop, her bed is empty, she hides her face in her hands. The section ends with Jayadeva's prayer to Hari, who once lifted the mountain to shelter the herds.

Analysis

The friend (the sakhi) is the hinge of the poem's dramatic structure. She carries messages between lovers who cannot or will not meet, and her descriptions are always persuasive: she tells each lover how the other suffers in order to bring them together. Her role draws on the confidante of Sanskrit love poetry and drama, and Vaishnava readers would later find in her a model for the devotee who serves the divine couple (Dimock 1966) (Siegel 1978).

The catalogue of love-sickness follows conventional signs found in Sanskrit poetics: fever, pallor, aversion to cooling things, sleeplessness, absorption in the beloved's image. Jayadeva's skill lies in the intensity of the repetition rather than in novelty of detail (Miller 1977).

The closing prayer recalls Krishna lifting Mount Govardhana to protect the cowherds from the storm sent by Indra. Arnold adds, characteristically, that the god's lips took 'Earthly stamp and woodland sign' from the gopis' kisses, an apology for divine love that the Sanskrit does not need to make.

Part 6: Sarga 5: The Longings of Krishna

Sanskrit sarga 5, songs 10–11

Summary

Krishna sends the friend back to Radha. She describes him lying by the river, fevered by the moonlight, deaf to the bees, his bed now hard rock instead of flowers, repeating Radha's name like a spell. In a second song she urges Radha to go to him in the night: take off the anklet bells that might betray her, draw her braids over her face against the moonlight, and come swift as lightning, while he rearranges the bed of flowers twenty times at every rustle of a leaf.

A third movement in shorter lines describes the joy of reunion that awaits her, the end of parting in a union 'beyond Time's shadowy portal'. The section closes with an invocation of Krishna as the slayer of Kansa and the lord of the three worlds.

Analysis

The second song of this sarga is one of the most famous in the poem. Its Sanskrit refrain tells Radha that Krishna waits in the grove by the Yamuna, where the wind is gentle. It is an abhisara song: in Sanskrit love poetry the abhisarika is the woman who goes out secretly at night to meet her lover, and the details of muffled bells and dark clothing belong to that convention (Miller 1977) (Archer 1957).

The scene of Krishna fussing over the bed of flowers is touching and slightly comic. The god waits like any anxious lover, and the poem asks its audience to find his divinity in exactly this vulnerability, a theme that later bhakti theologians would develop (Kinsley 1979) (Hardy 1983).

Arnold's short-lined final movement turns the lovers' meeting into an image of the soul's eternal union with God, 'In the strong certainty of joys immortal'. It shows how far he takes the hint of Jayadeva's devotional signatures towards a Christian Platonist vision of heaven.

Part 7: Sarga 6: Krishna Made Bolder

Sanskrit sarga 6, song 12

Summary

Radha has the will to go but not the strength, and the friend returns to Krishna to tell him that he must come to her instead: the lotus does not seek the bee. Radha sits in the bower wearing his emblems, asking ten times whether he is coming, swooning, crying 'Now he cometh', and opening her arms to the empty air when a leaf falls.

The friend asks why he lingers in a place 'haunted by serpents' and does not go to Nanda's house, and Jayadeva prays that the message may guide all who hear it.

Analysis

This is the shortest sarga and one of the most dramatic in its quiet way: the friend reverses the poem's direction. Instead of the woman going to the lover, the god must go to the woman. The Sanskrit title calls Krishna 'bold Vaikuntha', and the bee-and-lotus image makes the reversal a matter of nature (Siegel 2009).

Radha's waiting is painted through hallucination: she sees Krishna where he is not and speaks to the falling leaf. In the vocabulary of Sanskrit poetics she is a vasakasajja, the heroine who has prepared herself and her chamber for a lover who has not yet come. The scene prepares for the dark seventh sarga (Miller 1977).

Arnold's line 'For all so strong in Heaven itself / Is Love' and his habit of calling Radha an 'angel' make her a spirit in paradise rather than a woman in a forest bower; the allegory is now complete in his version.

Part 8: Sarga 7: Krishna Supposed False

Sanskrit sarga 7, songs 13–16

Summary

The moon rises over Vrindavana and Krishna does not come. Radha sings in despair, with the refrain 'Yami hê kam sharanam!' ('Where can I go for refuge?'). When she sees the friend returning alone with downcast eyes, she imagines Krishna with another woman and describes her rival in jealous detail.

Through the rest of the night she sings a series of songs: Krishna paints the tilaka on the other woman's brow, ties flowers in her hair, hangs jewels on her breasts; let him enjoy her, since such love feeds on shadows; let the south wind and the god of love kill Radha, or let the Yamuna, sister of Yama the god of death, drown her. The section ends with an exhortation to meditate on Govinda by the sacred river.

Analysis

The seventh sarga is the longest in Arnold and the emotional low point of the poem. In Sanskrit poetics Radha here is the vipralabdha, the heroine deceived by a lover who has failed to keep a tryst, and her imaginings of Krishna with a rival are among the most vivid and sensual passages in the work. Her jealousy, called mana in the poem, will need to be overcome before reconciliation (Miller 1977) (Siegel 1978).

The refrain 'Yami he kam iha sharanam' carries a double weight. It is a cry of abandoned love, and it uses the language of seeking refuge (sharana), a central term of devotional religion. The jealous woman's despair and the devotee's helplessness are spoken in the same words (Hardy 1983).

Arnold leaves the Sanskrit refrain untranslated in the song, and his phrase 'love that feeds on shadow' again assigns the rival to the world of illusion. The appeal to the Yamuna as Yama's sister is close to Jayadeva's mythology; the closing image of gods leaning from heaven to guide Krishna 'to his sides' (presumably a misprint for 'skies') is Arnold's own.

Part 9: Sarga 8: The Rebuking of Krishna

Sanskrit sarga 8, song 17

Summary

At dawn Krishna comes at last, kneeling and wordless with fear and joy. Radha sees the signs of another night: his eyes are red from wakefulness, his lips are dark with the eye-black of another woman, his chest bears the marks of kisses and her saffron paste. She tells him to go back to the woman he has chosen, before her own resolve weakens.

Arnold ends the sarga with a light scene of his own: Radha, even as she sends him away, draws his dark robe over her face as a veil, the friend laughs, and Krishna understands that he is to stay.

Analysis

The Sanskrit song has the refrain 'yahi madhava yahi keshava', 'Go, Madhava! Go, Keshava!', followed by a demand that he stop his lying words. Radha here is the khandita, the offended heroine who sees the marks of a rival on her lover; the description of those marks is precise and unashamed, in the manner of court poetry (Miller 1977).

The scene is the poem's sharpest test of the devotional reading. The god is caught out like an unfaithful husband and rebuked by a woman, and devotional commentators have had to explain why such a scene should be holy. Bengal Vaishnava theology found an answer in the idea that the lovers' quarrels and Radha's jealous pride intensify a love which is itself the highest religious emotion (Dimock 1966) (Hawley 1982).

Arnold softens the accusation with forensic metaphors ('thy Judge / Had best not see thee') and with the comic ending, which mutes Radha's anger and moves the reconciliation forward. His Radha protests more than she accuses.

Part 10: Sarga 9: The End of Krishna's Trial

Sanskrit sarga 9, song 18

Summary

Radha's anger has not quite passed. Her friend sings to her in a song with the refrain 'Mâ kooroo mânini mânamayè', urging her not to keep up her proud resentment: Krishna has learned his lesson, the spring wind has brought him back, he sits lonely in the wood. Let her lift her veil and look at him.

The section closes with Arnold's praise of the 'rare voice' of Krishna, whose flute once called the deer-eyed maidens into the shade.

Analysis

The refrain transliterates the Sanskrit 'ma kuru manini manam aye', roughly 'do not, proud lady, nurse your pride'. Mana is a technical term of Indian love poetry for the jealous indignation of a woman wronged by her lover; overcoming it is a classic stage in the drama of love. The friend's song is also a lesson in surrender, and devotional readers took it so (Siegel 1978) (Miller 1977).

Arnold's title, 'The End of Krishna's Trial', makes the episode a test of Krishna's penitence. In the Sanskrit it is Radha's pride that must be dissolved; the moral weight falls on her as much as on him, which complicates any simple allegory of the erring soul and the waiting divine love.

The closing hymn to Krishna's voice and flute, 'Krishna's, Hari's--one in two', recalls the flute that summons the gopis in the Bhagavata Purana. Arnold uses it to bind the earthly lover and the god into one figure (Kinsley 1979).

Part 11: Sarga 10: Krishna in Paradise

Sanskrit sarga 10, song 19 (in part)

Summary

Radha begins to relent and smiles while her friend pleads, and Krishna comes near and sings his song of appeasement. Let her speak even once, so that he may hear her voice and see her teeth like pearls in a red shell; he is like the bird that pipes to the moon. She has been his blood, breath and being. If she is still angry, let her wound him with her glance, bind him in her arms and kill him.

He praises her features through a series of flower similes, each a weapon of the god of love, asks forgiveness, and in a final song dares to take her hand 'with a bridegroom's touch'. Jayadeva closes: so they met and were made one.

Analysis

Krishna's song is one of the most daring in the poem. In the Sanskrit he offers himself to Radha's anger and invites her to punish him with her nails and teeth in the play of love. Its most famous verse, in which the god asks Radha to place her foot on his head, gave rise to the legend that Jayadeva hesitated to write it and that Krishna himself completed the line. Arnold has nothing that clearly corresponds to it, unless it is the lover's rising 'Up from thy feet' near the end (Miller 1977) (Siegel 2009).

This is the theological heart of the poem for many devotional readers: the supreme god humbles himself before the beloved, and the human soul, figured in Radha, is raised above the god by love. Bhakti theologians saw in the scene the power of love to bind even God (Hardy 1983) (Dimock 1966).

Arnold's 'Fetter me! gyve me! lock me in the gaol / Of thy delicious arms' keeps the conceit of love's bondage but drops its physical violence. His note says he omitted parts of the sarga; the bridegroom's song that closes it frames the reunion as a marriage, which suits his title, The Indian Song of Songs.

Part 12: Sarga 11: The Union of Radha and Krishna

Sanskrit sarga 11, songs 20–22 (in part)

Summary

Peace is made. Krishna goes ahead to the bower, and Radha's companions dress her in golden wedding clothes and lead her after him at twilight, singing that she should follow: the night is her bridesmaid, the stars her marriage lamps, and every part of her beauty is now his to receive. They tell her to loosen her girdle so that its bells announce her.

At the entrance she pauses, shy of her own joy, and her companions sing a last song urging her to enter the house of love and give Krishna the nectar (amrit) of her lips. She enters; her shame departs, and his life rises to meet her like the ocean rising to the moon. Arnold ends with a blessing in the name of Hari and a stanza in praise of Jayadeva's 'Hymn of Love'.

Analysis

In the Sanskrit this sarga moves from the friend's urging to Radha's own approach and her sight of Krishna at the bower, a sight described with great sensuous richness. Radha is now the abhisarika, going to the tryst, and the poem's long night of separation ends in union. The twelfth sarga that follows, with its description of their lovemaking and its final scene of Radha asking Krishna to adorn her again, completes the arc: the woman who was waiting becomes the woman who commands (Miller 1977) (Siegel 2009).

Arnold's decision to end at the threshold changes the poem. By omitting the twelfth sarga he removes the consummation and also Radha's last, confident commands, and he replaces them with a wedding procession, a word Jayadeva does not use for the meeting in the bower. The Victorian proprieties of 1875 are part of the reason, but so is his allegorical design: the soul arriving at the gate of heaven is a fitting end for a 'Song of Songs' (Arnold 1875) (Wright 1957).

The final stanza, with its claim that the poem teaches skill in song, worship and love, renders in its way the closing verses of the Sanskrit, in which Jayadeva praises his poem's music, devotion and erotic art. Arnold moves that self-praise from the end of the twelfth sarga to the end of his eleventh.

Characters

The lovers

Krishna (Hari, Keshava, Govinda, Madhava)
The cowherd god of Vrindavana, worshipped as Vishnu or as the supreme God. The poem calls him by many names, and each sarga's title pairs one of them with his state of mind: joyful, carefree, bewildered, tender, longing, bold, and so on. He is in turn the playful lover of many women, the remorseful lover of one, and the humble suppliant at Radha's feet; the poem never lets its audience forget that this lover is also the lord of the ten avatars.
Radha
A cowherd woman and Krishna's chosen beloved. Barely mentioned in earlier literature, she is the centre of the Gita Govinda, which gives her its richest songs: jealousy, longing, despair, anger, forgiveness and command. For later Bengal Vaishnavas she became the divine consort and the perfect devotee. Arnold calls her an 'angel' and makes her a figure of heavenly love waiting for the erring soul.

Go-between

Radha's friend (the sakhi)
Radha's confidante, who carries messages between the lovers, describes each to the other and urges them to meet. She speaks many of the songs and is the main agent of the plot. In later devotion the role of the friend, who serves the lovers' union without seeking Krishna for herself, became a model for the worshipper.

The cowherd women

The gopis
The cowherd women of Vrindavana who dance and play with Krishna in the spring forest of the first sarga, and one of whom Radha imagines as her rival in the seventh. In the Sanskrit they are vivid and real; Arnold makes them 'Shadows', 'phantoms' and 'Visions of the earth', the distractions of sense.

The poet

Jayadeva
The poet speaks in his own name at the end of almost every song, offering it to its hearers and praying that it may delight and purify them. These signatures make the poem a devotional performance as well as a story. Arnold renders them as rhymed trochaic interludes in which 'Jayadev' explains the hidden meaning.

Divinities

Kama (Madana, the 'Bodiless Lord')
The god of love, armed with a sugar-cane bow and five flower-arrows. Burned to ashes by Shiva, he lives on bodiless. Krishna protests to him in the third sarga, and Radha begs him to kill her in the seventh.
Vishnu and Lakshmi
Vishnu, praised in the opening hymn through his ten avatars, is the god of whom Krishna is a form; his consort Lakshmi (Arnold's 'Kumla', from Kamala) appears in the second hymn as the goddess whose embrace marks his chest. Radha's love is implicitly mapped onto Lakshmi's.

Place

The Yamuna (Jumna)
The river of Vrindavana, by whose banks Krishna sits waiting and in whose waters Radha in despair wishes to drown. In myth the river is a goddess, sister of Yama, the god of death.

Themes

Sacred and erotic love

The poem refuses to separate religious and sexual feeling. Its hymns praise the god through the marks of his consort's embrace; its love songs end in prayers. Jayadeva's own verse near the beginning of the first sarga addresses those whose hearts delight in remembering Hari and who are curious about the arts of love, as if these were one audience (Siegel 1978).

Readers have divided on how to take this. Some see the eroticism as a code for spiritual truths; others see the poem as a celebration of human love sanctified by the divine. Most modern scholars stress that Jayadeva's tradition did not need to choose, since in it the experience of love (shringara) could itself be a way of knowing God (Miller 1977) (Siegel 1978).

Separation and longing (viraha)

Almost the whole poem is spent apart. The lovers meet only at the end; before that they see each other once in anger. Separation intensifies love, and the songs of longing, with their fevers, sleeplessness and hallucinations, are the poem's emotional substance.

In bhakti religion the pain of separation from God became a central devotional emotion, and the Gita Govinda gave it its classic images. Friedhelm Hardy traced this 'viraha-bhakti' back to early Tamil devotional poetry; Jayadeva's poem carried it into Sanskrit and across northern India (Hardy 1983).

Jealousy, pride and forgiveness

Radha is not a passive beloved. She watches, judges, sulks, rebukes and finally forgives. Her mana, the proud anger of the wronged woman, has to be overcome by her friend's persuasion and Krishna's pleading before union is possible.

The poem treats the quarrel as part of love, not its enemy. Jayadeva's sympathy is with Radha's anger as much as with Krishna's penitence, and the god must humble himself before her (Hawley 1982).

Divine play (lila)

Krishna's dalliance in the spring forest is lila, play: action freely done for delight, not need. The idea that God's relation to the world, and to the soul, is a kind of play gave theologians a way to see the love story as both trivial and infinitely serious (Kinsley 1979).

The poem's lightness of tone, its jokes against Kama and its comic bed-making scene belong to this playfulness. Arnold, more earnest, tends to turn play into error and repentance.

Nature as the language of love

Every natural detail in the poem expresses desire: the south wind, the mango blossoms, the bees, the koil's call, the moon. Spring and moonlight are the lovers' companions, and in separation they turn into torments.

This language draws on the long tradition of Sanskrit court poetry, in which landscapes have fixed emotional meanings; Jayadeva's achievement is to set it to music and fuse it with a devotional story (Ingalls 1965) (Miller 1977).

Motifs

The signature couplets

Each Sanskrit song ends with a couplet naming Jayadeva and offering the song to its hearers, the device later called bhanita. These signatures frame the love story as a sacred offering. Arnold expands them into trochaic interludes that explain the poem's hidden meaning (Miller 1977).

The refrain

Every song is built around a refrain, sung after each couplet. Arnold sometimes keeps it in Sanskrit ('Yami hê kam sharanam!', 'Mâ kooroo mânini mânamayè'), sometimes renders it as a repeated English phrase ('Krishna, till thou come unto her'), and sometimes drops it.

Messages and go-betweens

The plot is carried by the friend's journeys between Radha and Krishna, and by descriptions of each lover to the other. Much of what the audience sees is reported, imagined or persuasive speech.

Ornament and make-up

Necklaces, girdles, anklet bells, sandal paste, saffron, tilaka and eye-black recur as signs of love: too heavy to wear in separation, telltale on Krishna's body after infidelity, bells to be muffled or loosened on the way to the tryst.

Kama's arrows

The god of love's five flower-arrows appear in the spring landscape, in Krishna's complaint in the third sarga and in his praise of Radha's features in the tenth: love as a wound inflicted by beauty.

Symbols

The spring forest of Vrindavana

The place of Krishna's play and of desire itself; for devotional readers an earthly image of the divine realm.

The bower (kunja)

The secret thicket where the lovers meet, prepared with flowers and waited in; its threshold is where Arnold's version ends.

The moon

The lamp of lovers and the torment of the separated, rising over Vrindavana in the seventh sarga; Krishna compares himself to the chakora bird that lives on moonlight.

The bee and the lotus

The roving lover and the waiting beloved; in the sixth sarga the friend insists that the bee must find the flower.

The Yamuna

The river by which Krishna waits and in which Radha wishes to drown: the boundary of love and death, and a sacred river in its own right.

Critical debates

Sacred allegory or erotic poem?

From William Jones onwards, Western readers asked whether the Gita Govinda is a mystical allegory, in which Krishna is the human soul and Radha divine love or wisdom, or a sensual love poem with a religious veneer. Jones, comparing it with Sufi poetry and the Song of Songs, read it allegorically, and Arnold followed him (Jones 1792) (Arnold 1875).

Lee Siegel's study argued that the poem holds sacred and profane love together without reducing either to the other, and that Indian commentators read it in both registers at once. Barbara Stoler Miller likewise stressed that the erotic mood (shringara rasa) was itself a way to religious experience in Jayadeva's culture, so that the question 'sacred or profane?' is posed in Western rather than Indian terms (Siegel 1978) (Miller 1977).

Where was Jayadeva from?

Jayadeva calls himself a native of Kindubilva. A village called Kenduli in Birbhum, West Bengal, holds a yearly fair in his honour, and the verse naming poets of Lakshmana Sena's court supports a Bengali setting. Orissan tradition identifies another Kenduli near Puri, points to the poem's early and continuous use in the Jagannatha temple and to inscriptions, and claims Jayadeva as an Oriya poet.

Sushil Kumar De and Miller set out the Bengali case; the Orissan case has strong regional support, and the dispute remains open, partly because much of the evidence is late tradition (De 1961) (Miller 1977).

Radha before and after Jayadeva

Radha is absent from the Bhagavata Purana, the principal text of Krishna's youth, and appears only occasionally in earlier poetry. Did Jayadeva draw on a living cult of Radha, perhaps in eastern India, or did his poem largely create her religious importance? The essays edited by John Stratton Hawley and Donna Marie Wulff survey the evidence and the later theological elevation of Radha as Krishna's divine consort and energy (Hawley 1982).

W. G. Archer's survey of the Krishna stories in poetry and painting shows how quickly, after Jayadeva, Radha became the central heroine of the cowherd story across India (Archer 1957).

Song, drama or kavya?

Lassen subtitled his edition 'drama lyricum', a lyrical drama, and others have compared the poem to pastoral drama or to the cantata. The poem has speaking characters and a plot but no stage directions; it has the polished verses of court kavya but its songs carry raga and tala markings for performance (Lassen 1836).

Miller and Siegel treat it as a unique fusion of classical Sanskrit poetry with regional song forms, designed for performance in temple or court, and later Indian tradition has staged it in dance and music as well as reading it (Miller 1977) (Siegel 2009).

Bhakti, theology and the Sahajiyas

The poem was adopted by Chaitanya and by the Gaudiya Vaishnava theologians of Bengal, who built on it an elaborate doctrine of devotional emotion in which the love of Radha for Krishna is the supreme religious state. Its place in that tradition shaped how it was read for centuries (De 1961).

The Vaishnava Sahajiya movement went further, treating the love of Radha and Krishna as a model for ritual practice between human partners. Edward C. Dimock's study shows how the erotic mysticism latent in the Gita Govinda was taken up in radically different ways, and Friedhelm Hardy's work on viraha-bhakti places the poem in a longer history of devotional longing reaching back to South India (Dimock 1966) (Hardy 1983).

The Song of Songs comparison

Jones first compared the Gita Govinda to the Song of Songs, and Arnold's title made the comparison its frame. Both are sequences of love lyrics in dialogue, rich in garden and landscape imagery, which religious traditions have read as allegories of divine love: in Jewish and Christian exegesis, of God and Israel or Christ and the Church or soul (Jones 1792) (Pope 1977).

The comparison illuminates and distorts. Like the Song of Songs, the Gita Govinda resists purely allegorical reading; but unlike the biblical poem it names its divine lover openly, is framed by hymns and devotional signatures, and was composed as a sacred poem from the start. Marvin Pope's commentary on the Song of Songs shows how variously its own erotic and allegorical readings have been argued, which suggests that the comparison works better as a question than as an equation (Pope 1977) (Siegel 1978).

Arnold, Orientalism and translation

Arnold's version belongs to the Victorian discovery of Indian literature, which Raymond Schwab described as an 'Oriental Renaissance' and which later critics, after Edward Said, have examined for its colonial assumptions. Arnold's preface and notes present the poem as a spiritual allegory and adapt it to Victorian taste (Schwab 1984) (Said 1978) (Trautmann 1997).

Brooks Wright's biography shows Arnold as a popular interpreter of Asian religions whose Light of Asia (1879) made the Buddha a figure of Victorian moral idealism. The same tendencies shape The Indian Song of Songs: the gopis become phantoms, the lovers' reunion becomes a wedding, and the twelfth sarga is left out. Comparison with Miller's or Siegel's close translations makes Arnold's choices visible (Wright 1957) (Miller 1977) (Siegel 2009).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. Why does a love poem begin with a hymn to Vishnu's ten avatars?

    Think about who Krishna is, the closing prayer of the second hymn, and Jayadeva's signature verses throughout.

  2. How does the spring landscape of the first sarga shape the reader's understanding of Krishna's dalliance?

    Look at the refrain about not living alone and the erotic character of every natural detail.

  3. What is the role of Radha's friend in the poem's structure?

    Count the songs she sings, consider what she tells each lover about the other, and think of the go-between in love poetry.

  4. Compare Krishna's remorse in the third sarga with Radha's despair in the seventh. How does the poem balance the two lovers?

    Consider the language of sin and refuge, and who is the suppliant at different points.

  5. How does the poem use the conventions of Sanskrit love poetry, such as the types of heroine, the abhisara and mana?

    Look at the fifth, seventh, eighth and ninth sargas.

  6. Is the Gita Govinda an allegory? Argue for and against.

    Weigh Arnold's interludes and Jones's reading against Jayadeva's own signature verses and the frankness of the seventh and eighth sargas.

  7. What does the poem suggest about the relationship between God and the human soul in bhakti?

    Think about Krishna's humility before Radha in the tenth sarga and the idea of viraha.

  8. How useful is Arnold's title, The Indian Song of Songs?

    Compare the dialogue, imagery and allegorical history of the biblical Song with Jayadeva's poem, and note what the comparison hides.

  9. What does Arnold's version gain and lose by its free handling of the Sanskrit?

    Look at his trochaic interludes, his treatment of the gopis as 'phantoms', and his omission of the twelfth sarga.

  10. Why might Arnold have ended at the threshold of the bower?

    Consider Victorian decorum, the marriage imagery of his eleventh sarga, and the allegory of the soul reaching heaven.
