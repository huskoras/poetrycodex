The Rape of the Lock

by Alexander Pope

Overview

Written and published
Two-canto version written 1711 and printed in Lintot's Miscellaneous Poems and Translations (May 1712); enlarged five-canto version published by Lintot in March 1714; Clarissa's speech added in the Works of 1717
Genre
Mock-heroic (Pope's subtitle calls it 'An Heroi-Comical Poem'): the forms and machinery of classical epic applied to a trivial social quarrel
Form
Heroic couplets (rhymed iambic pentameter) in five cantos, 794 lines in the standard numbering
Occasion
Robert, Lord Petre, cut a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor; the Catholic gentleman John Caryll asked Pope to write a poem that would laugh the two families back into friendship
Setting
London and the Thames on a single day: Belinda's bedroom at noon, a boat trip upriver, and an afternoon party at Hampton Court; with excursions to the Cave of Spleen and the sphere of the moon
Narrator
A self-conscious epic narrator who invokes the Muse, addresses his characters, comments on the action and finally claims to immortalise Belinda
Protagonist
Belinda, a fashionable young beauty modelled on Arabella Fermor
Epic models
Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Virgil's Aeneid and Milton's Paradise Lost, with Ovid, Catullus, Ariosto and the mock-epics of Boileau (Le Lutrin) and Garth (The Dispensary)
Machinery
Sylphs and gnomes adapted from Rosicrucian lore in the Comte de Gabalis (1670) of the Abbé de Montfaucon de Villars
Key themes
Triviality and greatness; beauty, time and mortality; female honour and the marriage market; commodities and empire; the uses and limits of epic

Context

Alexander Pope (1688–1744) was the son of a Catholic linen merchant in London. Because Catholics were barred from the universities, from public office and from living within ten miles of London, the family moved to Binfield in Windsor Forest, where Pope educated himself largely by reading, above all Homer, Virgil and the English poets from Spenser to Dryden. A tubercular infection of the spine left him stunted and in chronic pain. By 1711 he was already known for his Pastorals (1709) and An Essay on Criticism (1711). The Rape of the Lock made him the most admired poet of his generation, and in 1715 he began publishing the translation of the Iliad that would make him financially independent (Mack 1985) (Rogers 2007).

The poem grew out of a real quarrel within the small, intermarried world of the Catholic gentry. Some time in 1711 Robert, seventh Baron Petre, then about twenty, cut off a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor, a celebrated beauty, and the jest caused a breach between the two families. Pope's friend John Caryll of Ladyholt, a friend of both families, suggested that Pope write a poem that would turn the affair into laughter. The first version, in two cantos and 334 lines, appeared anonymously in Lintot's Miscellaneous Poems and Translations in May 1712. By then Petre had married another heiress; he died of smallpox in 1713, the year before the expanded poem appeared (Tillotson 1962) (Erskine-Hill 1975) (Rumbold 1989).

In 1713 Pope rebuilt the poem in five cantos, against the advice, as he later reported, of Addison, who thought it perfect as it stood. He added what the critics of the day called the 'machinery' of epic, the supernatural agents who intervene in the action, here the sylphs and gnomes; he added Belinda's toilet, the game of ombre and the descent of the gnome Umbriel to the Cave of Spleen; and he prefaced the poem with a letter to Arabella Fermor. The new version was published by Lintot in March 1714. A last major addition came in the Works of 1717: Clarissa's speech in Canto 5 (RL 5.7–34), which Pope said was meant to make the moral of the poem plainer (Tillotson 1962).

Mock-heroic was a well-established form by 1714. Tassoni's La secchia rapita (The Stolen Bucket, 1622), Boileau's Le Lutrin (1674–83), on a quarrel over a church lectern, Dryden's Mac Flecknoe (1682) and Samuel Garth's The Dispensary (1699) had all set the grand style against petty subjects. Pope's originality lay in the delicacy of the treatment and in the density of his allusions. Almost every episode corresponds to an epic convention: the invocation, the arming of the hero, the sacrifice before battle, the speech of the commander to his troops, the epic games, the descent to the underworld, the council, the battle of the gods and the final apotheosis (Broich 1990) (Terry 2005) (Jack 1952).

The text quoted in this guide is the five-canto poem as it stood after 1717, including Clarissa's speech; its line numbers agree with the standard Twickenham Edition, and references are given by canto and line (RL 3.147–54). The prefatory letter to Arabella Fermor is not included in it. In that letter Pope explains the 'machinery' as borrowed from the Rosicrucians, whose doctrine he found in 'a French book call'd Le Comte de Gabalis', and assures Mrs Fermor that the character of Belinda resembles her in nothing but beauty. The letter's tone is gallant and teasing, but also faintly condescending: Pope apologises for using 'hard words before a lady', and the compliment and the joke cannot easily be separated (Tillotson 1962) (Rumbold 1989).

Plot overview

The poet asks the Muse what could make a well-bred lord assault a lady and a lady reject a lord. At noon Belinda is still asleep. Her guardian sylph, Ariel, sends her a dream in which he explains that the spirits of dead coquettes become sylphs, who protect chaste young women from seduction, and warns her that some dreadful event threatens her that day. Woken by her lapdog Shock, she forgets the dream on reading a love letter and sits down at her dressing table, where she and her maid perform the rites of beauty as solemnly as priestesses before an altar.

Belinda sails up the Thames towards Hampton Court, admired by all. Her two curled locks have caught the eye of the Baron, who has already built an altar of French romances and prayed to Love for success. Ariel summons the sylphs, describes their duties, and assigns each a part of Belinda to guard; fifty are set to defend her petticoat. At Hampton Court Belinda defeats the Baron and another player at the card game of ombre, narrated as an epic battle. Over coffee the Baron, armed with a pair of scissors supplied by Clarissa, cuts off one of the locks. Ariel withdraws when he discovers an earthly lover in Belinda's heart, and a sylph who interposes is cut in two.

The melancholy gnome Umbriel descends to the Cave of Spleen and brings back a bag of sighs and passions and a vial of tears. Emptied over Belinda and her friend Thalestris, they turn grief into fury. Thalestris sends her beau Sir Plume to demand the lock, which the Baron refuses to return, and Belinda laments the loss of the curl and her visit to court. In Canto 5 Clarissa urges good humour and good sense, since beauty must fade; nobody listens. Thalestris calls to arms, and the beaux and belles fight a battle of frowns, fans and snuff. Belinda overcomes the Baron with a pinch of snuff and threatens him with a bodkin, demanding the lock.

The lock, however, has vanished. Some think it has gone to the sphere of the moon, where all things lost on earth are kept, but the Muse saw it rise into the heavens as a new star, like the lock of Queen Berenice in ancient poetry. The poet tells Belinda to stop mourning: her own beauty will die, but the lock, and her name, will live in the poem.

Book by book

Book 1: The Dream and the Toilet

Summary

The poem opens with a proposition and invocation in epic form: the poet will sing the dire offence and mighty contests that spring from trivial things, dedicating the verse to Caryll, and he asks the goddess what could have provoked so strange a war between a lord and a belle. The sun peeps through Belinda's curtains, lapdogs shake themselves and lovers wake at noon, but Belinda sleeps on, held there by her guardian sylph.

Ariel sends a dream in the shape of a glittering young courtier, who reveals the secret world of the air. The souls of women keep their vanities after death: fiery termagants become salamanders, soft women water-nymphs, prudes gnomes, and light coquettes sylphs. The sylphs guard the chastity of women who reject mankind, steering them from one beau to the next so that no single man prevails. Ariel, as Belinda's protector, warns her that a dreadful event threatens her before sunset and urges her to beware of man.

Shock licks his mistress awake, and Belinda, reading a billet-doux, forgets the vision. The canto ends with the toilet: the dressing table stands unveiled like an altar, Belinda worships her own image in the mirror, her maid Betty serves as the 'inferior priestess', the treasures of India and Arabia are laid out, and the sylphs invisibly do the real work of arranging her hair and gown.

Analysis

The opening lines imitate the epic proposition and the Virgilian question about divine anger: the Aeneid asks whether such rage can dwell in heavenly minds, Pope asks whether it can dwell in 'soft bosoms'. The whole method of the poem is visible in this inversion. The grand form is kept intact, and the subject is shrunk to fit it, so that the reader measures one against the other at every line. The phrase 'trivial things' is itself double: the poem laughs at the triviality of its occasion while insisting that trivial things can produce real offence, which was precisely the problem Caryll asked Pope to solve (Jack 1952).

Ariel's speech gives the poem its machinery. In epic the gods intervene in human affairs; in Pope's world the supernatural agents are tiny, airy creatures whose concerns are fashion and chastity. The Rosicrucian system comes from the Comte de Gabalis, but the speech also has a Miltonic ring, a guardian spirit warning an innocent woman of danger as Raphael warns Adam and Eve. The doctrine of the sylphs is a sharp piece of social satire. What the world calls a woman's honour is really the unceasing distraction of new vanities, and what looks like inconstancy is the sylphs' strategy: 'Beaux banish beaux, and coaches coaches drive.' Female virtue, on this account, is a function of a crowded social calendar (Tillotson 1962) (Pollak 1985).

The toilet (RL 1.121–48) is a double parody. It is a religious rite, with altar, priestess, offerings and a goddess who is Belinda's own reflection, and it is the arming of the hero, the moment in Homer when Agamemnon or Akhilleus puts on his armour before battle: 'Now awful beauty puts on all its arms.' The famous line 'Puffs, powders, patches, Bibles, billet-doux' lists sacred and profane objects with the same flat equality, and the catalogue of goods from India, Arabia and the tortoise and elephant has become central to readings of the poem as a picture of a society furnished by global trade (Brown 1985) (Brown 1993). The final joke, that Betty is praised for work done by the sylphs, is also a reminder that the poem's elegance rests on labour it renders invisible.

Book 2: The Voyage on the Thames

Summary

Belinda sails up the Thames, rivalling the rising sun. On her breast she wears a sparkling cross, and her smiles fall on everyone equally. Behind her neck hang two curls, the snares in which love catches men. The adventurous Baron has resolved to win them, by force or fraud; before dawn he built an altar to Love out of twelve French romances, burned the trophies of his former loves, and prayed. The gods granted half his prayer.

As the boat glides on in music and sunshine, only Ariel is troubled. He summons the sylphs, a shimmering crowd of insect-winged creatures whose garments change colour in the light, and from the mast he addresses them. He describes the ranks of spirits, from those who guide the planets to those who guard the British throne, and the humbler task of tending the fair: guarding powder, scents, curls and blushes.

Some disaster threatens Belinda, though the Fates have not revealed what: she may stain her honour or her new brocade, lose her heart or her necklace. Ariel assigns the guards: Zephyretta the fan, Brillante the earrings, Momentilla the watch, Crispissa the lock, and Ariel himself the lapdog Shock. Fifty sylphs defend the petticoat. Any spirit who deserts his post will be punished with comic tortures, stopped in vials, pierced with pins or whirled in a chocolate mill. The sylphs take up their places and wait.

Analysis

The portrait of Belinda shows the poem's characteristic doubleness. The cross 'Which Jews might kiss, and infidels adore' is a compliment to her beauty and a sly hint that religion has become ornament, admired for its sparkle rather than its meaning. The lines 'If to her share some female errors fall, / Look on her face, and you'll forget 'em all' praise and accuse in the same breath. Cleanth Brooks took passages like this as evidence that the poem's attitude is neither simple mockery nor simple admiration, but a poised irony that holds both together (Brooks 1947).

The Baron's sacrifice parodies the offerings with which epic heroes seek divine favour, and its half-answered prayer echoes a Homeric and Virgilian formula in which a god grants part of a request and lets the winds scatter the rest. The altar of romances is pointed: the Baron's idea of love has been formed by French fiction, just as Belinda's has been formed by the sylphs. Ariel's speech from the mast imitates the speech of an epic commander to his troops, and more specifically Satan's addresses to the fallen angels in Paradise Lost, with their roll call of 'Thrones, Dominations, Princedoms'. Pope's 'Fays, fairies, genii, elves, and demons' turns Milton's hierarchy into a miniature (Tillotson 1962).

This canto contains the most celebrated example of the poem's chief figure, zeugma or syllepsis, in which one verb governs two objects of very different kinds: 'Or stain her honour, or her new brocade'. The device does more than amuse. By placing chastity and a dress on the same level of grammar, it suggests that Belinda's world places them on the same level of value, and it leaves the reader unsure whether the disaster to come will be moral or merely social. The sylphs' punishments, drawn from the cosmetic and domestic world, end with an allusion to Ixion on his wheel in the underworld: the same scale-shift applied to hell (Jack 1952) (Wasserman 1966).

Book 3: Ombre and the Rape

Summary

The scene moves to Hampton Court, where statesmen plan the fall of tyrants and of nymphs, and Queen Anne sometimes takes counsel and sometimes tea. The courtiers gossip, and at every word a reputation dies. Meanwhile, in the city, judges hang wretches so that jurymen may dine. Belinda challenges two knights, the Baron among them, to the card game of ombre, and the sylphs perch on her important cards.

The game is narrated as an epic battle. The cards are marshalled like armies, kings with forked beards, queens holding flowers, knaves with halberds. Belinda declares spades trumps and leads with her highest cards, the Matadores, but the Baron's queen of spades turns the tide, and his diamonds rout her troops. When the knave of diamonds takes her queen of hearts she faces codille, outright defeat, but on the last trick her king of hearts falls on the Baron's ace, and she exults. The narrator warns that her triumph will soon be snatched away.

Coffee is served, and its vapours inspire the Baron with new stratagems. Clarissa hands him a pair of scissors, as ladies in romance arm their knights. The sylphs thrice blow back the hair and twitch Belinda's earring, but Ariel, searching her thoughts, finds an earthly lover lurking in her heart, loses his power and withdraws. The Baron closes the shears, cutting through a sylph who interposes, and the lock is severed. Belinda screams; the Baron boasts that his honour will live as long as fashionable society; and the narrator asks how hair could resist the steel that destroyed Troy.

Analysis

The opening of the canto widens the poem's satire beyond the dressing table. The zeugma about Queen Anne taking 'counsel' and 'tea' brings politics into the world of the tea table, and the couplet on hanging judges, 'The hungry judges soon the sentence sign, / And wretches hang that jurymen may dine', is a sudden glimpse of the brutal world that the beau monde ignores. The 'charming Indian screen' and the lacquered 'altars of Japan' remind us again that this leisure is furnished by trade (Brown 1985).

The game of ombre is Pope's version of the epic games and the epic battle together. Ombre was a fashionable three-handed Spanish game played with forty cards, nine dealt to each player, hence 'the sacred Nine'. Pope's account is accurate enough for the game to be reconstructed trick by trick, and it is also a miniature Iliad, with catalogues of forces, single combats and the fall of heroes. The simile comparing the routed cards to the troops of Asia and Africa fleeing in confusion gives this battle the scale of empire. The game also anticipates the plot: Belinda's queen of hearts captured by a knave is a warning of what the Baron intends, and her narrow victory makes her 'Too soon dejected, and too soon elate' (Tillotson 1962).

The cutting of the lock is the poem's central event, and its language is deliberately ambiguous. The title word 'rape' meant in the first place 'seizure' or 'carrying off' (Latin rapere), as in the title of Tassoni's poem, but the sexual meaning was always present, and the poem plays on it in the 'two-edged weapon', the joining 'to divide', and Belinda's later wish that he had seized 'any hairs but these'. The sylph cut in two whose 'airy substance soon unites again' parodies Satan wounded by Michael in Paradise Lost 6, whose ethereal substance closed up at once. Ariel's withdrawal on finding an 'earthly lover' in Belinda's heart is the turning point of the machinery: the sylphs can protect only a woman who does not, at some level, want to be won (Brooks 1947) (Wasserman 1966). The canto closes with a parody of Catullus' poem on the lock of Berenice, where hair yields to the iron that levelled mountains; Pope adds Troy and triumphal arches, and the lament for monuments is half serious.

Book 4: The Cave of Spleen

Summary

Belinda is consumed by rage and despair, greater than that of captured kings or lovers robbed of bliss. As Ariel leaves her weeping, the gnome Umbriel descends to the centre of the earth to the cave of Spleen, a goddess who lies on her bed attended by Pain and Megrim, with the handmaids Ill-nature and Affectation beside her. The cave is filled with vapours and phantoms, and with the bodies of those transformed by Spleen: living teapots, a walking pipkin, a talking goose-pie, men who think they are pregnant, maids turned into bottles.

Carrying a sprig of spleenwort for protection, Umbriel asks the goddess to touch Belinda with chagrin, since her ill humour would infect half the world. Spleen grants his prayer and gives him a bag filled with sighs, sobs and the war of tongues, and a vial of fainting fears and tears. Returning, Umbriel empties the bag over Belinda and Thalestris.

Thalestris cries out that Belinda's honour is lost if the Baron displays the lock, and sends her beau Sir Plume to demand it. Sir Plume's speech is a stammer of oaths, and the Baron swears by the lock that he will keep it for ever. Umbriel then breaks the vial, and Belinda, in tears, laments the day she came to Hampton Court, wishes she had stayed at home in some remote place where no one plays ombre or drinks tea, recalls the omens of the morning, and ends by wishing the Baron had taken 'any hairs but these'.

Analysis

Umbriel's journey is the poem's descent to the underworld, modelled on the visits of Odysseus and Aeneas to the dead. The spleenwort he carries corresponds to the golden bough that Aeneas must pluck before entering Hades, and the bag of passions recalls the bag of winds that Aeolus gave to Odysseus, which was opened with disastrous results. Pope's underworld, however, is psychological. Spleen, or 'the vapours', was a fashionable melancholy attributed especially to women of leisure, and the cave is a map of its symptoms: headache, affectation, moralising ill-nature, hallucination. The transformed bodies, teapots and bottles, are both a satire on hysterical fancy and a further instance of the poem's world in which people turn into objects (Gubar 1977) (Fairer 1984).

Thalestris' speech shows what 'honour' means in this society. She does not speak of virtue but of reputation, of what 'the fops' and 'the ladies' will say, and of Belinda as a 'degraded toast'; her line 'Ease, pleasure, virtue, all our sex resign' at the shrine of honour suggests that honour has become the opposite of virtue. Sir Plume's incoherent demand and the Baron's oath, sworn by the lock as Akhilleus swears by the sceptre in Iliad 1, show the men's world equally reduced to gesture (Pollak 1985) (Rumbold 1989).

Belinda's lament is the most emotionally complex passage in the poem. Its wish for solitude in 'some lone isle' parodies the laments of epic and tragic heroines, and its catalogue of omens (the falling patch-box, the silent parrot) is comic. Yet its final couplet, 'Oh hadst thou, cruel! been content to seize / Hairs less in sight, or any hairs but these!', is a famous double entendre. It reveals, as Brooks argued, that Belinda's real concern is appearance: she would mind less a private loss than a public one. Readers continue to disagree about whether the joke is at Belinda's expense, at the expense of a society that values appearances above all, or both (Brooks 1947).

Book 5: The Battle and the Apotheosis

Summary

The Baron is unmoved, as Aeneas was unmoved by Dido. Clarissa waves her fan for silence and makes a speech: why are beauties praised and adored, if not for good sense? Dancing and dressing cannot cure smallpox or old age; since beauty must fade and locks turn grey, women should use their power wisely and keep their good humour, for merit wins the soul when charms only strike the sight.

Nobody applauds. Belinda frowns, Thalestris calls Clarissa a prude and raises the cry 'To arms!', and a battle begins in which the weapons are fans, frowns and glances. Umbriel watches with delight from a candle sconce. Thalestris slays a beau and a witling, one in metaphor and one in song; Sir Plume is killed by a frown from Chloe and revived by her smile. Jove weighs the men's wits against the lady's hair, and the hair prevails.

Belinda flies at the Baron, throws a pinch of snuff in his face, and draws a bodkin with a mock-heroic genealogy, an heirloom remade over generations. The Baron says he fears only leaving her behind. 'Restore the lock!' she cries, but the lock has disappeared. Some say it has gone to the sphere of the moon among all things lost on earth, but the Muse saw it rise as a comet-like star, outshining Berenice's lock. The beau monde will see it from the Mall, lovers will take it for Venus, the astrologer Partridge will make prophecies from it. The poet bids Belinda cease mourning, for when she herself has died the lock will still bear her name among the stars.

Analysis

Clarissa's speech (RL 5.7–34), added in 1717, is a close parody of the speech of Sarpedon to Glaucus in Iliad 12, which Pope had translated as early as 1709 and published in his Iliad in 1717. Sarpedon asks why the two of them are honoured above other men with the best seats and the richest portions, and answers that they must earn that honour by fighting in the front line; since all men must die, it is better to die gloriously. Clarissa asks why beauties are honoured, and answers that since beauty must fade, women should cultivate good humour. The parallel is precise, and its effect is double: it gives Clarissa's moral the dignity of heroic ethics, and it reveals how small the stakes of this society are compared with Sarpedon's. Pope said the speech was added to open the moral more clearly, but its reception complicates it. It is Clarissa who lent the scissors, the speech is rejected at once, and it starts the very battle it seems to deplore (Mack 1967) (Wasserman 1966).

The battle is the poem's version of the battle of the gods in Iliad 20 and 21, and Pope makes the parallel explicit in a simile that elevates the fight to the scale of a war on Olympus. Its casualties, however, are deaths in the metaphorical sense of love poetry: men 'die' of a woman's glance and revive at her smile. The weighing of wits against hair alludes to the golden scales in which Zeus weighs the fates of Akhilleus and Hector in Iliad 22 and in which Jupiter weighs Aeneas and Turnus in Aeneid 12. The bodkin's genealogy parodies the history of a hero's weapon, like Agamemnon's sceptre in Iliad 2, which passed down from Hephaestus through generations of kings (Tillotson 1962).

The apotheosis of the lock combines two classical sources: the lunar sphere of lost things comes from Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, where Astolfo travels to the moon to recover Orlando's lost wits, and the lock turned into a star comes from Callimachus' poem on the lock of Berenice, known to Pope through Catullus 66. The comparison with Romulus, taken up into heaven and seen only by Proculus, makes the poet the sole witness to the miracle. The last lines turn the comedy towards elegy. Belinda will die, her beauty will be laid in dust, and only the poem will preserve her. This claim is both a final mock-heroic joke and a serious statement about the power of poetry, the oldest promise of epic, that song confers lasting fame (Mack 1985) (Brooks 1947).

Characters

The beau monde

Belinda
A young beauty, modelled on Arabella Fermor, the poem's heroine and its epic 'hero'. She is presented as admirable and absurd at once: radiant, self-possessed, triumphant at cards, but also vain, fiercely conscious of her reputation, and capable of a violent rage over a curl. Ariel's discovery of an 'earthly lover' in her heart suggests a hidden desire beneath her coquetry. Critics have read her as the object of Pope's satire, of his admiration, or of a society that makes women into objects.
The Baron
The 'adventurous Baron', modelled on Robert, Lord Petre, who cuts the lock. He is the poem's epic adversary: he prays and sacrifices before his enterprise, fights at ombre, swears by the lock as Akhilleus swears by the sceptre, and in the final battle dies only in the lover's metaphorical sense. His pride in his trophy shows the possessiveness behind gallantry.
Clarissa
The lady who lends the Baron the scissors and who, in the 1717 version, delivers the poem's moral speech on good sense and good humour. Her speech parodies Sarpedon's speech in Iliad 12. Her double role, accomplice and moralist, makes her one of the poem's most debated figures.
Thalestris
Belinda's friend, named after a queen of the Amazons and traditionally identified with Mrs Morley, a member of the circle around the Fermors. She inflames Belinda's anger, speaks for honour as reputation, calls Clarissa a prude and leads the attack in the final battle.
Sir Plume
Thalestris' beau, vain of his amber snuff-box and clouded cane, sent to demand the lock. His speech is a string of oaths and fillers. He is usually identified with Sir George Browne, who is said to have resented the portrait.
Betty
Belinda's maid, the 'inferior priestess' at the toilet. She is praised for work done by the sylphs, a small joke that also points to the unseen labour behind fashionable display.

The sylphs and gnomes

Ariel
Belinda's guardian sylph and chief of the sylphs. His name recalls both the spirit in Shakespeare's The Tempest and a rebel angel in Paradise Lost. He sends the warning dream, marshals the sylphs like a general and assigns their posts, but must withdraw when he finds an earthly lover in Belinda's heart.
The sylphs
Airy spirits, once coquettes, who protect women's chastity by distracting them with new vanities. Among them are Zephyretta, Brillante, Momentilla and Crispissa, who guard the fan, the earrings, the watch and the lock. They are the poem's equivalent of the gods of epic, beautiful, fragile and absorbed in trifles.
Umbriel
A 'dusky, melancholy sprite', a gnome, once a prude. He descends to the Cave of Spleen and returns with the bag of sighs and the vial of tears that turn Belinda's loss into fury and grief. He watches the final battle with delight.
Spleen
The goddess of melancholy and ill humour who lies in her cave attended by Pain, Megrim, Ill-nature and Affectation. She personifies the fashionable malady of 'the vapours'.

Others

Shock
Belinda's lapdog, who wakes her with his tongue, and whom Ariel himself undertakes to guard. Shock stands for the poem's world of pets and ornaments, which Belinda's society values as highly as people.
The narrator
The epic poet, who invokes the Muse, addresses Belinda and the Baron, comments on the folly of mortals, and finally claims to immortalise the lock. His voice moves between mock solemnity, gallant compliment and real moral observation.
John Caryll
Pope's friend, a Catholic gentleman, to whom the poem is dedicated in its third line ('This verse to Caryll, Muse! is due'). He suggested the poem as a means of reconciling the families of Petre and Fermor.

Themes

The trivial and the great

The poem's first couplet announces its subject as 'mighty contests' that 'rise from trivial things'. Throughout, the gap between epic forms and fashionable trifles is the main source of comedy: the arming of a hero becomes the application of cosmetics, a battle becomes a card game, a descent to the underworld becomes a visit to the home of bad temper. The method exposes a society that treats trifles as if they were matters of life and death.

Yet the poem never quite dismisses the trivial. Its world of lacquer, silk and china is rendered with such loving precision that it acquires its own beauty, and the poem itself is a work made out of 'trivial things'. Pope's ability to find poetry in teacups and petticoats is the reason why readers have long debated whether the poem satirises or celebrates the world it describes (Brooks 1947) (Jack 1952).

Honour, reputation and female virtue

The word 'honour' recurs at key moments. Ariel says that what men call honour is really the work of the sylphs; Thalestris speaks of honour as reputation, at whose shrine virtue itself is sacrificed; the Baron's honour lies in his trophy. The poem separates honour as public reputation from virtue as an inner quality, and suggests that the beau monde has lost sight of the second.

Feminist critics have explored how the poem represents a society in which women's value depends on their beauty and their marriageability, and in which their 'honour' is policed by gossip. Whether the poem criticises that system or reinforces it, by mocking Belinda and offering Clarissa's advice to accept it, is a central question of modern criticism (Pollak 1985) (Rumbold 1989).

Beauty, time and mortality

Beneath the comedy runs an awareness of time. The day moves from noon to evening; beauty is 'frail' and 'must decay'; Clarissa reminds her listeners that 'locks will turn to gray'; and the poem ends with Belinda's death. The lock is a part of her that can be preserved only by being cut off and transformed into poetry.

This concern links the poem to the epic tradition, in which the hero's awareness of death drives his pursuit of glory. Clarissa's speech, modelled on Sarpedon's in the Iliad, makes the connection explicit: beauty, like heroism, is honoured because it is mortal (Mack 1967) (Wasserman 1966).

Commodities, trade and empire

Belinda's world is made of things from distant places: gems from India, perfumes from Arabia, combs of tortoiseshell and ivory, coffee, tea, china, lacquered tables from Japan, an Indian screen. The poem registers the flood of goods produced by British commercial expansion, and its rhetoric often makes people and objects interchangeable.

Laura Brown has argued that the poem's representation of Belinda's toilet participates in the ideology of empire, presenting the goods of global trade as natural tributes to English beauty and erasing the labour and violence by which they were acquired. The routed cards compared to 'Asia's troops, and Afric's sable sons' bring the imagery of colonial war into the drawing room (Brown 1985) (Brown 1993).

Appearance and reality

The poem is preoccupied with surfaces: mirrors, cosmetics, painted china, the shimmering, colour-changing sylphs. Belinda is a work of art made at the dressing table, and her concern after the rape is chiefly what will be seen. The sylphs themselves are invisible to the characters, and their power is a kind of social illusion.

Pope's own art is a matter of polished surfaces, and the couplet is an instrument of display. The poem is thus implicated in the culture of appearance it describes, and part of its achievement is to make the reader aware of this complicity (Brooks 1947) (Deutsch 1996).

Epic and its uses

The poem is a sustained conversation with epic, especially with Homer, whom Pope was preparing to translate, and with Milton. It uses epic conventions to measure the beau monde, but it also tests those conventions, showing how much in epic, its quarrels over prizes, its divine interventions, its boasts and its scales of fate, can look absurd when transposed.

The question of whether mock-heroic belittles the epic or honours it is among the poem's richest. Pope reveres Homer, but by setting Sarpedon's speech in Clarissa's mouth or Akhilleus' oath in the Baron's he lets the heroic and the fashionable comment on each other (Jack 1952) (Doody 1985) (Williams 1993) (Terry 2005).

Motifs

Zeugma

The figure in which one word governs two others in different senses, as in 'stain her honour, or her new brocade' or 'sometimes counsel take--and sometimes tea'. Pope uses it to set moral and trivial things on the same level, revealing a society's confused values in the grammar of a single line.

Catalogues

Lists in the epic manner run through the poem: the objects on the dressing table, the duties of the sylphs, the possible disasters, the cards at ombre, the transformed bodies in the Cave of Spleen and the lost things on the moon. Pope's catalogues mix sacred and profane, serious and trivial, and their order is often itself the joke.

Omens and fate

Ariel's dream, the 'black omens' of Canto 2, the narrator's warnings during the card game, the patch-box that falls three times and the silent parrot all parody the portents of epic. The poem plays at a world governed by Fate in which nothing more serious than a curl is at stake.

Religious ritual

Worship is constantly displaced onto secular objects: Belinda adores the 'cosmetic powers' at her altar, the Baron builds an altar to Love, coffee is served at 'shining altars of Japan', and the lock is 'sacred'. The motif shows a society whose devotion is directed at beauty and pleasure.

Breakable china

China vessels appear repeatedly as emblems of fragility, often beside female chastity: 'some frail China jar receive a flaw', the screams at the rape compared to those when 'rich China vessels' fall. The association suggests that a woman's honour, like porcelain, is valuable because it can break.

Symbols

The lock

Belinda's curl is at once a trivial ornament, a trap for men, a symbol of her beauty and chastity, and a synecdoche for her whole person. Its cutting enacts a symbolic violation; its transformation into a star turns it into a symbol of poetic immortality. It is the poem's equivalent of the prizes over which epic heroes quarrel, like Briseis in the Iliad.

The sylphs

The sylphs are the social conventions and vanities that govern a fashionable woman's life, made visible as tiny spirits. They are delicate, beautiful and ultimately powerless before real desire, and they symbolise both the charm and the fragility of Belinda's world.

The dressing table

The toilet stands for the construction of beauty as art and for the global trade that supplies it. It is altar, armoury and shop at once.

The Cave of Spleen

The underworld of the poem symbolises the dark side of the beau monde: melancholy, affectation, malice and hysteria, the passions that lie beneath polished manners and turn grief into rage (Gubar 1977).

The star

The lock's final transformation into a star symbolises the power of poetry to preserve what time destroys. It also completes the mock-heroic pattern by giving the trivial the classical honour of a constellation.

Critical debates

Satire or celebration?

Is the poem a satire on the vanity and moral confusion of the beau monde, or an affectionate celebration of its beauty? Early readers mostly saw a gentle satire with a clear moral. In 'The Case of Miss Arabella Fermor' (1943), reprinted in The Well Wrought Urn (1947), Cleanth Brooks argued that the poem is neither, but a work of complex irony: Pope admires Belinda's beauty and recognises its value even as he exposes its absurdities, and the poem holds these attitudes in balance rather than choosing between them.

Brooks's essay became a model for New Critical reading. Later critics have accepted his sense of the poem's doubleness but questioned whether its balance is as neutral as he suggested, asking whose values it finally endorses (Brooks 1947) (Hunt 1968).

Gender and the poem

Feminist critics have examined the poem's representation of women. Ellen Pollak argued that the poem reproduces the myth of passive womanhood characteristic of its period: Belinda is admired as an object, mocked for her pride, and offered through Clarissa the advice to accept her place and marry. On this reading the poem's charm makes its ideology seem natural.

Others, such as Valerie Rumbold, have set the poem in the context of the actual women Pope knew, including Arabella Fermor herself, and have noted the constraints on the lives of Catholic gentlewomen. Discussion has also focused on the title's 'rape', whose sexual sense the poem keeps in play, and on whether the comedy trivialises sexual violence or exposes a society in which a woman's reputation counts for more than her person (Pollak 1985) (Rumbold 1989) (Gubar 1977).

Commodity culture and empire

Laura Brown's Alexander Pope (1985) read the poem in relation to the commercial expansion of early eighteenth-century Britain. The dressing table, loaded with goods from India and Arabia, presents the products of trade as if they were natural tributes to Belinda's beauty, and the poem's elegance depends on the invisibility of the labour and violence behind those goods.

Brown developed this argument in Ends of Empire (1993), linking the representation of women as consumers and adorned objects to the ideology of imperial expansion. Her readings have made the toilet scene and the card battle central to discussions of the poem and of commodity culture more generally (Brown 1985) (Brown 1993).

Mock-heroic and epic

Does the poem mock the epic, or does it use the epic to mock a society that falls short of it? Ian Jack, in Augustan Satire (1952), described the poem as a distinctive 'heroi-comical' work in which the epic is not the target but the standard. Maynard Mack and others emphasised that Pope was the period's great translator of Homer and that the poem's allusions honour the tradition.

More recent work on the genre, such as that of Ulrich Broich and Richard Terry, has suggested that the relation is less stable. The comic transposition of epic conventions can expose what is excessive or absurd in the epic itself, and mock-heroic may be one sign of a culture's growing distance from heroic values. Carolyn Williams has studied how Pope's Homer and his mock-heroic share an anxiety about manliness (Jack 1952) (Broich 1990) (Terry 2005) (Williams 1993).

Clarissa's moral and its irony

Pope said that Clarissa's speech was added in 1717 'to open more clearly the moral of the poem', and some readers take it as the poem's authoritative statement: good sense and good humour are the remedies for vanity and anger. Others point out its ironies. Clarissa provided the scissors; her speech is ignored; it is answered by a call to arms; and its advice, that women should keep their power by keeping their temper, accepts the system it describes.

The parody of Sarpedon's speech complicates matters further. Earl Wasserman argued that Pope's allusions carry their whole original context into the poem, so that the reader is meant to hear Sarpedon's heroic ethic beneath Clarissa's social prudence, measuring each against the other (Wasserman 1966) (Pollak 1985) (Tillotson 1962).

The machinery added in 1714

The sylphs, the game of ombre and the Cave of Spleen were added when Pope expanded the poem to five cantos. Pope later told Joseph Spence that Addison had advised against the change, and he was proud of having ignored the advice. Critics have generally praised the additions for transforming a slight occasional piece into a fully realised mock-epic, with its own supernatural order and its own underworld.

The machinery raises interpretive questions. The sylphs may be read as allegories of social convention, as the poem's imitation of epic gods, or as a parody of Rosicrucian occultism. Their withdrawal at the moment of the rape, when Ariel finds an earthly lover in Belinda's heart, places the turning point of the plot in her psychology rather than in fate (Tillotson 1962) (Brooks 1947) (Rousseau 1988).

The occasion and the real people

How far should the poem be read in relation to its occasion? Pope's own account, the dedication to Arabella Fermor and his correspondence with Caryll suggest a poem meant to reconcile, and also suggest that it may not have entirely succeeded: Pope later wrote that some of those concerned took offence. Historical criticism has recovered the Catholic world of the Petres, Fermors and Carylls, a minority bound by intermarriage and by legal disabilities.

Knowledge of this context changes the poem's tone. The light comedy becomes a tactful intervention in a close community, and the dedication's mixture of compliment and condescension can be read as a careful negotiation with a real woman who had been made a public figure (Erskine-Hill 1975) (Rumbold 1989) (Sherburn 1956) (Mack 1985).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. What does Pope gain by expanding the two-canto poem of 1712 into the five-canto poem of 1714?

    Consider the sylphs, the toilet, the game of ombre and the Cave of Spleen. How does each correspond to an epic convention, and how do they change the poem's scope and tone?

  2. How does the toilet scene (RL 1.121–48) work as both a religious rite and an arming scene?

    Look at the words 'altar', 'priestess', 'rites' and 'arms'. Compare the arming of Homeric heroes, and think about what is being worshipped and what kind of battle Belinda is preparing for.

  3. Choose three examples of zeugma and explain what each reveals about the values of Belinda's world.

    Begin with 'stain her honour, or her new brocade' and 'counsel take--and sometimes tea'. Ask whether the figure equates the two terms or invites the reader to separate them.

  4. What is the function of the sylphs? Are they a satire on fashionable women, a parody of epic gods, or something else?

    Consider Ariel's speech in Canto 1 on the origin and duties of the sylphs, the commands in Canto 2, and why Ariel abandons Belinda in Canto 3.

  5. How does the game of ombre anticipate the cutting of the lock?

    Follow the fate of Belinda's queen of hearts and her narrow escape from codille. What do the narrator's warnings at RL 3.101–4 suggest?

  6. What is the significance of the Cave of Spleen as the poem's underworld?

    Compare the descents of Odysseus and Aeneas. What does Pope locate in his underworld instead of the souls of the dead? Consider the handmaids and the transformations.

  7. Is Clarissa's speech the moral of the poem?

    Compare it with Sarpedon's speech in Iliad 12. Consider who speaks it, how it is received, and what its advice asks women to accept.

  8. How does the poem play with the double meaning of 'rape' in its title?

    Consider the Latin sense of seizure, the language of the cutting scene, the word 'ravish'd', and Belinda's last couplet in Canto 4.

  9. Does the poem satirise or celebrate the world it describes?

    Consider Brooks's argument for a balanced irony, then consider passages of loving description and passages of sharp satire, such as the hanging judges in Canto 3.

  10. How seriously should we take the final claim that the Muse will make the lock and Belinda immortal?

    Compare the epic promise of fame and the classical sources (Berenice's lock, Romulus). Note the turn to mortality in the last lines.
