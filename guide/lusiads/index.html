The Lusiads

by Luís Vaz de Camões

Overview

Published
Lisbon, 1572, under a royal privilege of 1571 and with the licence of the Inquisition's censor
Genre
Renaissance literary epic on the Virgilian model, celebrating a nation rather than a single hero
Metre
Ottava rima (eight ten-syllable lines rhyming abababcc), the stanza of Ariosto and Tasso
Length
10 cantos, 1,102 stanzas, 8,816 lines
Frame story
Vasco da Gama's first voyage from Lisbon round the Cape of Good Hope to Calicut in India (1497–99)
Setting
The East African coast (Mozambique, Mombasa, Melinde), the open sea, Calicut on the Malabar coast, Olympus and the palace of Neptune; Portuguese history from its legendary founder Lusus to King Manuel
Narrators
The poet, who frames the work and often interrupts it; Vasco da Gama, who tells Portuguese history and the voyage to the king of Melinde (Cantos 3–5); Paulo da Gama, Fernão Veloso, a prophesying nymph and Tethys in later cantos
Protagonist
Collectively 'the Lusiads', the sons of Lusus, that is, the Portuguese; Vasco da Gama is their representative captain
Conflict
Venus, who loves the Portuguese, against Bacchus, who fears that their arrival in the East will eclipse his own fame there
Key themes
Empire and its cost; fame and true honour; the sea and discovery; history as epic; pagan myth in a Christian poem; love; the poet's neglect and disillusion

Context

Luís Vaz de Camões was born around 1524, probably in Lisbon, into a family of the lesser nobility, and seems to have received a good humanist education, perhaps at Coimbra. What we know of his life comes from a handful of documents, from his own poems and from early biographies that mix fact with legend. As a young man he served as a soldier at Ceuta, the Portuguese stronghold in North Africa, and lost his right eye there. In 1552 he wounded a court official in a street brawl in Lisbon, was imprisoned, and was released in return for service in the East. He sailed for India in 1553 and spent some seventeen years in Asia, at Goa, on campaigns, and, according to a strong tradition, in an official post at Macau.

On the voyage back from the Far East he was shipwrecked at the mouth of the Mekong in Cambodia. The story told from early on, and alluded to by the poet himself in Canto 10, is that he swam ashore holding the manuscript of his epic above the water; later legend added that his Chinese beloved drowned. He was found destitute in Mozambique in the late 1560s and returned to Lisbon in 1570. The Lusiads appeared in 1572 with a royal privilege, licensed by the Dominican censor of the Inquisition, who noted that the pagan gods were poetic ornament and did not touch the truth of the faith. King Sebastião granted Camões a small pension. Camões died in 1580, by tradition on 10 June, the date that is now Portugal's national day (Bell 1923) (Hart 1962) (Saraiva 1959).

The poem is dedicated to the young King Sebastião (reigned 1557–78), whom the poet urges in the opening and closing stanzas to carry the crusade into Morocco. In 1578 Sebastião did exactly that and was killed, with much of the Portuguese nobility, at the battle of Alcácer-Quibir. He left no heir, and in 1580 Philip II of Spain took the Portuguese crown. The epic of national glory was thus read, almost from the beginning, in the shadow of national catastrophe; the hope that Sebastião would one day return to save Portugal became a lasting myth.

The Lusiads takes its title from Lusus, legendary founder of Lusitania, and its hero is the whole Portuguese people. Its frame is Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1497–99, which opened the sea route to India; into this frame Camões fits the history of Portugal, told by Gama to the king of Melinde, and a prophecy of the Portuguese empire in the East. The model is Virgil's Aeneid, with its gods, its narrated past and its prophesied future, but the stanza and much of the manner come from Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, and the poet's frequent personal interventions are his own (Bowra 1945) (Hart 1962).

English readers have had the poem since 1655, when the royalist poet Sir Richard Fanshawe published the first English translation during the Interregnum (Camões 1655) (Camões 1940) (Camões 1963). William Julius Mickle's free version of 1776, with additions of his own, made it widely known in the eighteenth century (Camões 1776). Sir Richard Francis Burton, the explorer, published his translation in 1880, keeping the ottava rima and a deliberately archaic diction, and followed it with a two-volume commentary (Burton 1881). All three are on this site; the quotations in this guide come from Burton. Modern translations include those of Leonard Bacon, William C. Atkinson and Landeg White (Camões 1950) (Camões 1952) (Camões 1997).

Plot overview

The poet announces his subject, the Portuguese who sailed beyond Ceylon and founded an empire in the East, and dedicates the poem to King Sebastião. On Olympus Jupiter declares that the Portuguese are destined to reach India. Venus supports them; Bacchus, who once conquered the East and fears losing his fame there, opposes them. Gama's fleet, already in the Indian Ocean, meets treachery at Mozambique and Mombasa engineered by Bacchus, and is saved by Venus and her nymphs. At Melinde the friendly king receives the Portuguese and asks Gama to tell him about his country.

Gama's narrative (Cantos 3–5) describes Europe and recounts Portuguese history: Lusus and Viriathus, the founding of the kingdom by Afonso Henriques and his victory at Ourique, the tragic love of Prince Pedro and Inês de Castro, the victory of João I and Nuno Álvares Pereira over Castile at Aljubarrota (1385), the conquests in Africa, and King Manuel's dream that sends Gama to India. Gama then tells of the departure from Lisbon, where an old man on the beach of Restelo curses the voyage, and of the journey down Africa, culminating in the apparition of the giant Adamastor at the Cape of Good Hope.

Leaving Melinde with a pilot, the fleet sails for India. Bacchus descends to Neptune's palace and persuades the sea gods to raise a storm; the sailor Fernão Veloso entertains the night watch with the tale of the Twelve of England; the tempest nearly sinks the ships until Venus sends her nymphs to calm the winds. The Portuguese reach Calicut (Canto 7), where the Samorim receives Gama. Bacchus, through a dream and through the Muslim merchants, turns the court against them; Gama is detained, then released, and finally sails for home (Cantos 8–9).

On the way home Venus rewards the sailors with an enchanted Isle of Love, where sea nymphs give themselves to them and Tethys takes Gama as her consort (Canto 9). At a banquet a nymph prophesies the deeds of the Portuguese viceroys and captains in the East, and Tethys leads Gama to a summit to show him the 'machine of the world', the whole universe and the lands that Portugal will reach (Canto 10). The sailors return to Lisbon. The poet closes in weariness and bitterness at his country's indifference, with a final appeal to Sebastião.

Book by book

Book 1: Proposition, Council of the Gods, Mozambique

Summary

The poem opens with its proposition: the poet will sing the Portuguese who sailed seas never sailed before, beyond Taprobane (Ceylon), and founded a new kingdom in the East, together with the kings who spread faith and empire. Let the Greek and the Trojan cease to boast of their voyages, he says; a greater valour now arises. He invokes the Tagides, nymphs of the Tagus, and then addresses a long dedication to King Sebastião, 'new terror to the Moorish spear', urging him to read the poem and to take up the war in Africa (1.6–18).

The narrative begins in the middle of things, with Gama's fleet already in the Indian Ocean off East Africa. On Olympus Jupiter summons the gods and announces that Fate has destined the Portuguese to reach India. Bacchus objects, fearing that his own glory as conqueror of the East will be forgotten; Venus, who sees in the Portuguese the virtues of her Romans and hears Latin in their language, takes their side. Mars speaks for Venus, and Jupiter confirms his decree.

The fleet reaches Mozambique, whose Muslim governor at first welcomes the Portuguese. Bacchus, disguised as a respected Muslim, persuades him that they are pirates and conquerors. An ambush at the watering place is beaten off, and the governor supplies a treacherous pilot, who tries to lead the fleet to its ruin. The canto ends with the poet's meditation on the insecurity of human life by sea and land.

Analysis

The opening stanzas announce a programme of rivalry with ancient epic. Camões names Odysseus, Aeneas, Alexander and Trajan only to dismiss them: the Portuguese are real, and their deeds outdo fictions. This claim of historical truth over fable recurs throughout the poem and sits in productive tension with the poem's own fictions, above all its pagan gods. The dedication to Sebastião makes the epic a political act: it offers the king a national past as a model for a future crusade, and so ties the poem's fortunes to his (Quint 1993).

The council of the gods imitates Homer and Virgil, but its conflict is new. Bacchus is not a god of wrath like Virgil's Juno but a jealous rival for fame in the East, and his opposition makes the voyage a contest over who will own the history of Asia. Venus's support links the Portuguese to Rome and to love. The closing lament, a passage of personal reflection unusual in classical epic, shows from the start that the poet's voice will not simply celebrate (Bowra 1945).

Book 2: Mombasa, Venus before Jupiter, Melinde

Summary

The pilot leads the fleet to Mombasa, where Bacchus has prepared another trap: the king invites the Portuguese into the harbour intending to destroy them. Two convicts sent ashore are shown a false Christian altar, before which Bacchus himself, disguised as a priest, pretends to worship. As the ships weigh anchor to enter the port, Venus and the Nereids push against their prows and drive them back; the panic of the Muslim pilots betrays the plot, and they leap overboard.

Venus then rises to Olympus, beautiful and tearful, and pleads with Jupiter for her Portuguese. Jupiter, moved, kisses her and prophesies their future glories in the East, their victories at Diu, Goa, Hormuz and Malacca, and their surpassing of the Greeks and Romans. He sends Mercury to Gama in a dream, telling him to leave Mombasa and seek Melinde, where he will find an honest king.

At Melinde the king and his people welcome the Portuguese with festivities, and the king himself comes out by boat to visit the fleet. Admiring the ships and their artillery, he asks Gama to tell him about his homeland, its history and the voyage that has brought him there.

Analysis

Venus's appeal to Jupiter closely follows her complaint in Aeneid 1, and Jupiter's answer, like his speech in Virgil, unrolls a prophecy of empire. The imitation is deliberate: Camões claims for Portugal the destiny Virgil claimed for Rome. But Camões's Venus also uses her erotic power quite openly to move Jupiter, a touch of Ariosto's sensuous wit that anticipates the Isle of Love.

The canto sets the pattern of the first half of the poem: hosts on the East African coast prove false or true according to whether Bacchus has reached them, and the Christian Portuguese are saved by a pagan goddess. Melinde, the good kingdom, serves the plot as Carthage serves the Aeneid, a place of hospitality where the hero tells his story; unlike Carthage it brings no fatal love (Bowra 1945).

Book 3: Gama's History I: from Lusus to Inês de Castro

Summary

After invoking Calliope, the poet gives the floor to Gama. Gama describes the geography of Europe, ending with Portugal at its western edge, and tells its history: the shepherd-warrior Viriathus who fought Rome, the gift of the county of Portugal to Count Henry of Burgundy, and the career of his son Afonso Henriques, who wins independence and defeats five Moorish kings at Ourique (3.42–54), where Christ appears to him in the sky. The loyal Egas Moniz offers his own life to the king of León to redeem a broken promise.

Gama continues through the kings of the first dynasty: the conquest of Lisbon and the Algarve, the weakness of Sancho II, the good government of Dinis, and the battle of the Salado (1340), where Afonso IV helps Castile defeat the Moors. Then comes the episode of Inês de Castro (3.118–35). Prince Pedro loves the Galician lady Inês, who lives in retreat at Coimbra by the river Mondego. His father Afonso IV, persuaded by his counsellors that the match endangers the kingdom, has her killed despite her plea for her children. The nymphs of the Mondego weep, and their tears become the Fountain of Loves.

Pedro, once king, takes a savage revenge on the murderers and is remembered as a harsh but just ruler. His son Fernando, weak and ruled by his love for Leonor Teles, brings the kingdom close to ruin, and the poet draws the lesson that a feeble king weakens even a strong people.

Analysis

Gama's narrative turns the epic flashback of Aeneid 2–3 into national chronicle. Its heroes are kings and knights spread across four centuries, and the effect is episodic; critics have long debated whether Camões succeeds in giving epic unity to a history (Bowra 1945) (Sena 1970). Ourique, with its vision of Christ, supplies the founding myth of a crusading nation and links the history to the dedication's call for war in Africa.

The Inês de Castro episode is the most famous in Portuguese literature. Its tone is lyrical and tragic rather than heroic, and its villains are the king and his reasons of state; Love itself is denounced as a cruel god demanding human blood. Inês's plea, which appeals to the mercy that even wild beasts have shown to the helpless, makes her an innocent victim of the very political order the epic praises. The episode shows Camões's lyric gift and his readiness to set private feeling against public power (Hart 1962) (Camões 1997).

Book 4: Gama's History II: Aljubarrota to the Departure

Summary

The death of Fernando leaves Portugal threatened by Castile. The people choose João, master of the order of Avis, as king. Nuno Álvares Pereira rouses the hesitant nobles with a fiery speech and leads the outnumbered Portuguese to victory over the Castilians at Aljubarrota (1385), a battle told with Virgilian similes, with Nuno's own brothers fighting on the enemy side. Peace follows, and João carries the war to Africa with the capture of Ceuta (1415).

Gama narrates the reigns of Duarte, of Afonso V with his African and Castilian wars, and of João II, who sends explorers overland to the East. King Manuel then dreams that two old men, the rivers Ganges and Indus, rise before him and prophesy that he will rule the East (4.67–75). He chooses Vasco da Gama to lead the expedition. The sailors take leave of their families at Belém, near Lisbon, amid lamentations.

As the ships are about to sail, an old man of venerable appearance among the crowd on the beach of Restelo raises his voice against the voyage (4.94–104). He curses the lust for fame and command, which brings ruin on the kingdom, asks why Portugal seeks distant enemies when the Moors are close at hand in Africa, and damns the first man who put a sail on a tree. The fleet sails nonetheless.

Analysis

Aljubarrota is the poem's great land battle and its defining national victory: independence won against a larger neighbour by a new dynasty and a hero both pious and fierce. Placed just before the voyage, it makes the expansion overseas a continuation of the struggle for national existence, and Manuel's dream gives that expansion the sanction of prophecy.

The Old Man of Restelo is the poem's most remarkable voice, because he speaks against the enterprise the epic celebrates, at the very moment of departure, and nobody answers him. Readers have taken him as the voice of prudent conservatism, of a landed nobility hostile to the spice trade, of Christian moralism against vainglory, or of Camões himself. Quint sees in such figures the voices of critics and losers that epic includes even as it overrides them (Quint 1993). That the speech ends in a lament for the human condition gives it the force of tragedy rather than mere argument (Macedo 1980).

Book 5: The Voyage to the Cape: Adamastor

Summary

Gama tells of the voyage itself: past Madeira, the Canaries and Cape Verde, along the African coast, until the Pole Star disappears and the Southern Cross rises. The sailors see marvels that the ancients never knew, among them St Elmo's fire and a waterspout that drinks up the sea. In a bay on the African coast Fernão Veloso goes inland with the local people and has to run back to the boats pursued, to the crew's amusement.

Five days later a dark cloud rises over the ships, and from it a monstrous giant appears: Adamastor, spirit of the Cape of Storms (5.37–60). He rebukes the Portuguese for violating his seas and prophesies shipwrecks and disasters for the fleets that will follow, among them the deaths of Bartolomeu Dias and Francisco de Almeida and the wreck of Manuel de Sousa Sepúlveda and his wife. Asked by Gama who he is, he tells his story: one of the Titans, he loved the sea nymph Thetis, was tricked into embracing a rock, and was turned into the rocky headland itself, forever encircled by the sea in which she lives. Weeping, he vanishes.

The fleet rounds the Cape and continues up the East African coast, through calms and the scurvy that kills many sailors, until it reaches Mozambique, where Canto 1 began. Gama ends his story. The poet then complains that the Portuguese care nothing for poetry, and that Gama's own family scarcely deserves the honour the Muses do them.

Analysis

Adamastor is Camões's most original creation, a classical giant invented for a modern geography. He embodies the terror of the unknown sea and the price of discovery: his prophecies give the epic a dark future and a list of real disasters suffered by the Portuguese. Yet his own story is a love elegy, and the monster ends as a pitiable lover turned to stone by the gods. The episode moves from epic terror to lyric pathos, as the Inês episode had moved from chronicle to lament (Bowra 1945) (Hart 1962).

Modern criticism has read Adamastor as a figure of Africa itself, giving voice to the land the Portuguese pass and will violate, and as a projection of European fears about the continent (Blackmore 2009). His prophecies of shipwreck also connect the epic to the Portuguese shipwreck narratives of the following decades, which tell the darker side of empire (Blackmore 2002). He has had a long afterlife in South African writing, from Roy Campbell's collection Adamastor (1930) onwards (Campbell 1930) (Van Wyk Smith 1988) (Monteiro 1996).

Book 6: Bacchus and Neptune, the Twelve of England, the Storm

Summary

After festivities at Melinde the fleet sails for India with a pilot. Bacchus, desperate, descends to the underwater palace of Neptune and persuades the assembled sea gods that the Portuguese threaten their rule over the ocean. Neptune agrees to send the winds against them.

Unaware, the sailors of the night watch pass the time with stories. Fernão Veloso tells the tale of the Twelve of England (6.42–69): twelve English ladies, insulted by English knights, can find no champion at home, and the Duke of Lancaster sends to Portugal for twelve knights to defend their honour. Eleven sail together; the twelfth, Magriço, travels overland and arrives just in time for the combat, which the Portuguese win. The story is cut short by a terrible storm.

The storm nearly destroys the fleet. Gama prays to the God who led Israel through the Red Sea and saved Paul from shipwreck (6.80–84). Venus, seeing her Portuguese in danger, sends her nymphs to calm the winds by seduction, and the storm subsides. At dawn the pilot sights Calicut. The poet closes with a reflection on true honour: it is won not by inherited rank or ease but by hardship, danger and experience (6.95–99).

Analysis

The undersea council of Neptune is a mythological set piece in the manner of Ovid and Ariosto, and the Twelve of England a chivalric romance in miniature, with a joke at the expense of English knights. These interludes give the voyage variety, and the tale also links the sailors to the old chivalric ideal at the moment they are about to found a new kind of commercial empire (Bowra 1945).

The storm combines pagan and Christian frames: Gama prays to the Christian God, but it is Venus and her nymphs who answer. Camões does not resolve the double causation here, though in Canto 10 Tethys will explain the gods as figures for the agents of providence. The closing meditation on honour, one of the poet's moral excursions, defines the poem's ethics: fame is earned by suffering, not inherited.

Book 7: Calicut, the Samorim and the Poet's Complaint

Summary

The canto opens with an exhortation to the other Christian nations of Europe, Germans, English, French and Italians, who fight one another or fall into heresy instead of uniting against the Turk, while the few Portuguese carry the faith to the ends of the earth. The poet then describes India and the Malabar coast. At Calicut the Portuguese meet Monsaydé, a Muslim from North Africa who speaks Castilian and becomes their interpreter and friend.

Gama goes ashore and is received by the Catual, the Samorim's minister, and then by the Samorim himself, to whom he offers the friendship and trade of King Manuel. The Samorim promises to consider it. The Catual visits the flagship, where Paulo da Gama, Vasco's brother, begins to explain the painted banners that show the heroes of Portuguese history.

Before the explanation proceeds, the poet breaks off to address the nymphs of the Tagus and Mondego (7.78–87). He recalls his own sufferings: exile, poverty, war, shipwreck, writing with a pen in one hand and a sword in the other. He complains that those for whom he sings have repaid him with new troubles, and vows that he will praise none who serve their own interest, flatter the powerful or oppress the poor, but only those who risked their lives for God and King.

Analysis

The exhortation to Europe sets the Portuguese enterprise within the crusade against the Ottoman Turks, a live political issue in the years of the poem's completion (the battle of Lepanto was fought in 1571). Monsaydé, the friendly Muslim who later becomes a Christian, plays the part of the helpful native go-between that colonial narratives often need, and his position between cultures has drawn the attention of modern critics.

The poet's complaint at the end of the canto is one of the most personal passages in Renaissance epic. It makes Camões himself a hero of hardship like his sailors, but one unrewarded by the nation he celebrates, and his promise to praise only the deserving turns the epic into a judgment on its readers. The image of pen and sword together sums up the ideal of the soldier-poet that later readers made central to his legend (Monteiro 1996) (Saraiva 1959).

Book 8: The Banners, Bacchus's Dream, the Detention of Gama

Summary

Paulo da Gama explains the figures on the banners to the Catual: Lusus, Ulysses as legendary founder of Lisbon, Viriathus, Count Henry, Afonso Henriques, Egas Moniz, Nuno Álvares Pereira and many others. The gallery recapitulates the history of Cantos 3–4 in pictures, rather as the shield of Aeneas shows Roman history in the Aeneid.

Bacchus appears in a dream to a Muslim priest in the form of the Prophet and warns him that the Portuguese have come to destroy his faith. The Muslim merchants of Calicut, who fear for their trade, and the Samorim's soothsayers turn the king against the strangers. The Samorim questions Gama, who answers that he comes in peace for trade and asks to return to his ships.

The Catual, bribed by the Muslims, detains Gama on shore and demands that the fleet be brought into the harbour, where it could be destroyed. Gama refuses, and is released only when he agrees to land goods for trade. He returns to his ships. The poet reflects on the power of gold to corrupt kings, friends, maidens and judges alike (8.96–99).

Analysis

The banners extend the poem's habit of retelling Portuguese history through different narrators and media, and show the Portuguese presenting themselves to Asia as a nation with a heroic past. The episode is a scene of cultural translation, mediated by Monsaydé as interpreter, in which images must stand in for a history the hosts cannot know.

Bacchus's appearance as Muhammad turns the opposition between the pagan god and the Christian heroes into religious polemic, with the Muslim merchants as his instruments. The commercial rivalry behind the episode is plain, and the poet's tirade against gold, though aimed at the Catual, also reflects back on a voyage undertaken for spices (Quint 1993).

Book 9: The Departure from Calicut and the Isle of Love

Summary

Two Portuguese agents remain on shore with the goods. Monsaydé warns Gama that the Muslims are delaying him until ships arrive from Mecca to destroy him. Gama seizes some Malabar merchants who have come aboard and exchanges them for his men, then sails for home with samples of spices and with Monsaydé, who will become a Christian.

Venus decides to reward the sailors for their labours. With the help of Cupid, who is waging war on the world's base and misdirected loves, she prepares an enchanted island in the ocean and fills it with sea nymphs wounded by love. The sailors land to hunt and find the nymphs; a playful chase ends in love. The soldier Leonardo, always unlucky in love, pleads with the fleeing Ephyre and wins her. Tethys herself takes Gama by the hand and leads him to her palace.

The poet then explains the allegory (9.88–95): the nymphs, Tethys and the island are nothing other than the honours that make human life glorious, the rewards of virtue; the ancient gods were men raised to divinity by fame. He urges his readers to wake from idleness and seek true glory through just and brave deeds.

Analysis

The Isle of Love is the most debated episode in the poem. Its sensuous descriptions owe much to Ariosto and to the enchanted gardens of Renaissance romance, and its frank eroticism has embarrassed and delighted readers in equal measure. The poet's own gloss makes the island a figure of the reward of fame, the marriage of the heroes with the sea they have mastered, and a kind of earthly apotheosis (Bowra 1945).

Other readers insist that the allegory does not exhaust the episode. Helder Macedo reads the voyage as a journey of initiation whose goal is the union of love and knowledge on the island (Macedo 1980); others see a fantasy of possession in which the conquered sea becomes a female body, with uncomfortable implications for the colonial project. The episode also shows Camões turning from history to myth for his ending, as if the historical return to Lisbon could not supply a fitting reward.

Book 10: The Prophecy, the Machine of the World, the Epilogue

Summary

At a banquet in Tethys's palace a nymph sings, in prophecy, the deeds of the Portuguese in the East after Gama: Duarte Pacheco Pereira's defence of Cochin, the viceroy Francisco de Almeida and his son Lourenço, Afonso de Albuquerque's conquests of Goa, Hormuz and Malacca (with a rebuke for his harsh execution of a soldier, 10.45–49), and the later governors down to João de Castro.

Tethys then leads Gama to a summit and shows him the 'machine of the world' (10.75–141), a globe of transparent spheres representing the Ptolemaic universe, with the Empyrean beyond and the earth at the centre. She admits that she and the other pagan gods are fables, useful to poets (10.82). She surveys the earth: Africa, Arabia, Persia, India with the martyrdom of the apostle Thomas, Ceylon, Malacca, Siam and the Mekong, where she foretells the shipwreck of the poet and the rescue of his song (10.127–28), China, Japan and the spice islands, and in the West Brazil and the voyage of Magellan.

The sailors leave the island with their nymphs and return to Lisbon. The poet ends: 'No more, my Muse' (10.145). His lyre is out of tune, not because he is tired of singing but because he sings to a people deaf to poetry and sunk in greed and 'vile melancholy'. He urges Sebastião to honour and reward good men, offers his own service with arm and pen, and imagines the king's victory in Morocco, which his Muse would make famous throughout the world.

Analysis

The machine of the world is the climax of the poem's movement from history to vision: Gama, like Aeneas in the underworld or Scipio in Cicero's Dream, sees the future and the whole cosmos. But Camões's universe is Christian and Ptolemaic, and the vision brings the admission that the gods who have driven the plot are fictions. This concession, which may answer the censor's concerns, invites us to reread the whole machinery as an allegory of providence (Hart 1962) (Sena 1970).

The epilogue reverses the confidence of the proposition. The poet who began by silencing Homer and Virgil ends with an untuned lyre and a nation that does not listen. His appeal to Sebastião to win glory in Africa became, after Alcácer-Quibir, one of the most painful ironies in European literature, and it is here that the poem's triumphalism most clearly meets its limits (Quint 1993).

Characters

Portuguese

Vasco da Gama
Commander of the fleet (c. 1469–1524) and the poem's representative hero. He is less a personality than a model of Portuguese virtue: prudent, pious, eloquent and steadfast. He narrates Cantos 3–5 to the king of Melinde, prays in the storm, endures detention at Calicut, and is rewarded with the love of Tethys and the vision of the universe. The poet also uses him to reproach his descendants, who did not honour poetry.
Paulo da Gama
Vasco's brother and captain of one of the ships. In Cantos 7–8 he explains to the Catual the banners showing Portuguese heroes, so becoming another narrator of national history.
Fernão Veloso
A sailor (Burton's 'Velloso') whose misadventure inland in Canto 5 provides comedy, and who tells the tale of the Twelve of England to the night watch in Canto 6. He represents the boastful good humour of the ordinary sailor.
The Old Man of Restelo
An unnamed old man on the beach at Belém who, as the fleet sails, condemns the lust for fame and empire that drives the voyage (4.94–104). He is the epic's internal critic, and his speech remains unanswered.
Leonardo
A soldier always unlucky in love who, on the Isle of Love, pursues the nymph Ephyre and wins her with his plea; a small comic romance within the episode.
The Poet
Camões's own voice, which interrupts the narrative at the end of several cantos to lament human frailty, attack greed and the neglect of poetry, define true honour, and recall his own exile and shipwreck. His growing disillusion shapes the poem as much as Gama's voyage.
King Sebastião
The young king (1554–78) to whom the poem is dedicated and whom it urges to war in Africa. He is addressed at the beginning and the end; his death at Alcácer-Quibir six years after publication changed how the poem was read.

Gods

Venus
Goddess of love and protector of the Portuguese, in whom she sees the virtues of her Romans. She saves them at Mombasa, pleads for them with Jupiter, calms the storm, and rewards them with the Isle of Love.
Bacchus
The god who once conquered India and fears losing his fame there. He opposes the voyage through disguises, dreams and intrigue, and stirs up the sea gods. His role is modelled on Juno's in the Aeneid, but his motive is rivalry for glory.
Jupiter
Ruler of the gods, who declares the Portuguese destiny in the council of Canto 1 and prophesies their triumphs to Venus in Canto 2.
Mars
God of war, who speaks for Venus and the Portuguese in the council of the gods.
Neptune
God of the sea, persuaded by Bacchus in Canto 6 to unleash the winds against the fleet.
Tethys
Sea goddess who on the Isle of Love becomes Gama's consort, and who in Canto 10 shows him the machine of the world and confesses that the pagan gods are fables.
Adamastor
The Titan who became the Cape of Good Hope, punished for his love of the sea nymph Thetis. He appears to the fleet in Canto 5, prophesies disasters, and tells his own sad story.

Peoples of the voyage

The King of Melinde
The friendly Muslim ruler of Malindi on the East African coast, who welcomes the Portuguese and asks Gama to tell the history of his nation. He is the honourable host, in contrast to the rulers of Mozambique and Mombasa.
The rulers of Mozambique and Mombasa
Muslim lords of the East African coast who, incited by Bacchus, try to destroy the fleet by treachery.
The Samorim
The Zamorin, Hindu ruler of Calicut, who receives Gama courteously but is turned against him by the Muslim merchants and his soothsayers.
The Catual
The Samorim's minister, who escorts Gama, hears Paulo da Gama explain the banners, and then, bribed by Gama's enemies, detains him.
Monsaydé
A Muslim from North Africa living at Calicut who speaks Castilian, serves as interpreter, warns Gama of the plot, and sails with the Portuguese to become a Christian.

Figures of the history

Afonso Henriques
First king of Portugal (d. 1185), who wins independence and defeats the Moors at Ourique after a vision of Christ; the founding hero of Gama's history.
Inês de Castro
Galician lady loved by Prince Pedro and killed in 1355 on the orders of his father Afonso IV (3.118–35; Burton's 'Ignèz'). She is the poem's great tragic victim, whose death the Mondego nymphs mourn in the Fountain of Loves.
Nuno Álvares Pereira
Constable of Portugal and hero of Aljubarrota (1385), who rouses the nobles to support João I against Castile; a model of pious, selfless valour.
João I
Master of Avis, chosen king in the crisis of 1383–85, victor at Aljubarrota and conqueror of Ceuta; founder of the dynasty under which the discoveries began.
King Manuel
King (1495–1521) who, after a prophetic dream of the Ganges and the Indus, sends Gama to India.

Themes

Empire and its cost

The poem celebrates the founding of a Portuguese empire in the East as the fulfilment of a national destiny and a Christian mission. Its prophecies list victories and conquests, and Jupiter promises that the Portuguese will add 'new worlds' to the old. In this the Lusiads takes up the Aeneid's union of epic and empire.

Yet the poem keeps counting the cost. The Old Man of Restelo condemns the voyage before it begins; Adamastor prophesies shipwrecks and deaths; the poet laments the dangers of life at sea and attacks the greed that the spice trade feeds. The rebuke to Albuquerque in Canto 10 shows that even the heroes of empire can be judged. The epic of conquest carries its own counter-voice (Quint 1993).

Fame and true honour

The poem is about fame: the poet's task is to make the deeds of the Portuguese famous, and Bacchus fights to keep his own. But the poem also asks what fame is worth. The Old Man calls it vanity and a word to deceive the crowd; the poet distinguishes true honour, earned by hardship and virtue (6.95–99), from rank, wealth and court favour.

The Isle of Love allegorises fame as the reward of virtue, while the epilogue shows the poet denied the reward his song deserves. Fame thus becomes both the goal of the heroes and the measure of a nation that fails to honour the deserving.

The sea and discovery

The Lusiads is the epic of the sea. It lists marvels the ancients never saw, the Southern Cross, St Elmo's fire, the waterspout, and boasts that experience surpasses ancient learning. The voyage replaces the war of classical epic as the great heroic test.

The sea is also a realm of gods and monsters: Neptune's palace, Adamastor, the nymphs. Its dangers are real and recurrent, from the storm of Canto 6 to the shipwrecks of later fleets and of the poet himself. At the end the sea becomes the bride of the heroes, as Tethys takes Gama as her consort.

History and prophecy

The poem fits some four centuries of Portuguese history into a single voyage through narration, painted banners and prophecy. Past (Cantos 3–4 and 8) and future (Cantos 2 and 10) frame the present of the voyage and make Gama's journey the hinge of national history.

This structure presents history as destiny, guaranteed by Jupiter and by the Christian God. But the poet's laments and Adamastor's prophecies show that history can also be a story of loss, and readers of 1572, still more those after 1578, knew how the empire's fortunes were already turning.

Love

Love runs through the poem in many forms: Venus's protective love for the Portuguese, the tragic love of Pedro and Inês, the hopeless love of Adamastor for Thetis, the ruinous passion of Fernando, and the reward of love on the island. Love can destroy, as in Inês's death, or ennoble.

The prominence of love links Camões's epic to his lyric poetry and to the romance tradition. On the Isle of Love, love becomes the reward of heroism, while Cupid's campaign against the world's base loves suggests that the poem also wants to reform desire.

The poet and his nation

Camões repeatedly interrupts his narrative to speak in his own voice. He presents himself as soldier and poet, an exile who has suffered as much as any sailor, and he bitterly reproaches his countrymen for despising poetry and rewarding flatterers.

The poem thus records not only the nation's greatness but its poet's estrangement from it. The famous ending, with its untuned lyre and deaf audience, gives the Lusiads its distinctive note of disenchantment within celebration.

Motifs

The poet's interventions

At the ends of Cantos 1, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 10, and elsewhere, the poet steps forward to moralise or lament. These passages on human frailty, the neglect of poetry, true honour, greed and his own misfortunes form a second, personal poem alongside the narrative of the voyage.

Rivalry with the ancients

The poem constantly compares the Portuguese with Odysseus, Aeneas, Alexander and the Romans and claims that they surpass them, because their deeds are true and their sea is greater. The claim is made in the proposition, repeated by Jupiter, and echoed by Adamastor's boast that Ptolemy, Strabo and Pliny never knew his Cape.

Prophecy

Jupiter, Manuel's dream, Adamastor, the nymph's song and Tethys's vision all foretell the future. Prophecy lets the poem include events after Gama and balance glory against disaster.

Treachery and hospitality

The voyage is a sequence of welcomes, true and false: Mozambique and Mombasa offer friendship to conceal ambush, Melinde gives honest hospitality, Calicut moves from courtesy to detention. The pattern dramatises the uncertainty of contact between peoples.

Narrated history

The poem tells history through several voices and media: Gama's speech, the painted banners explained by Paulo, Veloso's tale, the nymph's song. These framed narratives make history something performed for an audience, often a foreign one.

Symbols

The fleet

The ships stand for the nation itself: few, fragile and daring, carrying Portugal across the world. The poet's own 'fragile barque' of song (7.78) mirrors them.

Adamastor

The giant of the Cape symbolises the dangers of the unknown sea, the boundary the Portuguese break, and the suffering of those who pay for discovery; for modern readers he can also stand for Africa.

The Fountain of Loves

The fountain the Mondego nymphs name after Inês's death symbolises love's sorrow and its survival in memory and landscape.

The Isle of Love

The island stands, by the poet's own allegory, for the honours that reward heroic virtue; it is also an image of paradise regained and of the sea possessed.

The machine of the world

The globe Tethys shows Gama symbolises the totality of knowledge the voyage opens, and God's order behind the visible universe, which no human mind can fully grasp.

Pen and sword

The poet's image of himself writing with one hand and fighting with the other (7.79) symbolises the Renaissance ideal of arms and letters united, and his claim to be both hero and singer.

Critical debates

Epic of empire and its critics

The Lusiads has been read as the epic of Portuguese imperialism, a poem that glorifies conquest and the crusade against Islam, and as such it was enlisted by later nationalist regimes. David Quint places it in a tradition of epics of the victors, descended from the Aeneid, which tell history as a purposeful triumph (Quint 1993).

Other readers stress the poem's internal critics: the Old Man of Restelo, Adamastor, the laments against greed, the rebuke to Albuquerque. Quint himself shows how such figures give voice to the losers and to the voyagers' fears, and how the poem's romance elements pull against its imperial plot. Whether these voices undermine the celebration or are contained by it remains a central question (Quint 1993) (Blackmore 2009).

Pagan machinery in a Christian epic

The divine machinery of Venus, Bacchus, Jupiter and Neptune drives the plot of a poem about Christian heroes on a crusading mission. The censor who licensed the book already felt the need to say that the pagan gods were mere poetic ornament. Tethys's confession in Canto 10 that the gods are fables, and figures for the spirits that serve providence, may have been designed to meet such objections.

Critics differ on whether the machinery is decorative, allegorical (Venus as love or providence, Bacchus as the forces opposing Christianity), or a deliberate source of tension. Bowra found it essential to the poem's beauty and meaning while recognising its awkwardness (Bowra 1945) (Hart 1962) (Sena 1970).

Adamastor and colonial violence

Adamastor has become a key figure in postcolonial readings. As the spirit of the Cape whose seas the Portuguese 'violate', he can be read as Africa resisting European intrusion, or as Europe's projection of its own fears onto the continent. Josiah Blackmore studies how Portuguese writing of the period imagines Africa through such figures, and how shipwreck narratives record the disasters that Adamastor foretells (Blackmore 2009) (Blackmore 2002).

In South Africa Adamastor became a literary myth. Roy Campbell named a collection after him, and later poets and novelists used him to reflect on the history of colonisation. The anthology Shades of Adamastor collects poems in this tradition, and George Monteiro traces Camões's presence in Southern African literature (Campbell 1930) (Van Wyk Smith 1988) (Gray 1979) (Monteiro 1996).

The Isle of Love: allegory or erotic fantasy?

The poet himself explains the Isle of Love as an allegory of the honours that reward heroism (9.89–92), and many critics accept the reading, seeing the episode as an earthly apotheosis modelled on the classical deification of heroes (Bowra 1945).

Others find the allegory a thin cover for an erotic fantasy that the poem plainly relishes. Helder Macedo reads the island as the goal of an initiatory journey in which love and knowledge unite (Macedo 1980); feminist and postcolonial critics see in it a fantasy of male and imperial possession of the sea and its women. The coexistence of these readings reflects the episode's own mixture of romance, myth and moral.

Camões, Virgil and Ariosto

Camões openly rivals Virgil: the council of the gods, Venus's plea, the narrated history and the prophetic vision all have models in the Aeneid. Bowra presents the Lusiads as one of the great Renaissance attempts to write a modern epic on Virgil's pattern, and as the most successful in joining history to heroic poetry (Bowra 1945).

But the ottava rima, the romance episodes and the ironic and erotic touches come from Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, which Camões also claims to surpass because his heroes are real. Critics debate how far the poem's mixture of Virgilian seriousness and Ariostan play forms a coherent design; Jorge de Sena argued for a carefully calculated structure beneath the apparent variety (Sena 1970) (Quint 1993).

Nationalism and reception

After 1580, under Spanish rule, the Lusiads became a symbol of Portuguese identity, and in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries poem and poet were central to nationalist movements. Under the Estado Novo the anniversary of Camões's death, 10 June, was celebrated as the 'Day of the Race'; since the revolution of 1974 it has been the Day of Portugal, of Camões and of the Portuguese Communities.

English reception has its own history, from Fanshawe and Mickle to Burton, who identified with the wandering soldier-poet. Richard Helgerson compares the national project of the Lusiads with Elizabethan writing about English voyages, and Monteiro traces Camões's influence in England, America and Southern Africa (Helgerson 1992) (Monteiro 1996) (Camões 1655) (Camões 1776) (Burton 1881).

The poet's voice and his disillusion

The personal interventions have been read as signs of the poet's disenchantment with a Portugal sunk in greed and indifference, and as the bitterness of a soldier-poet denied reward. The ending, with its 'No more, my Muse', can even be read as a farewell to epic itself.

Biographical critics have used these passages to reconstruct Camões's life, often too literally; others see them as a deliberate part of the poem's structure, a counter-epic of the individual set against the nation (Saraiva 1959) (Macedo 1980) (Hart 1962) (Bell 1923).

Questions for study

  1. Who is the hero of the Lusiads: Vasco da Gama, the Portuguese people, or the poet?

    Consider the title, the proposition, Gama's role as narrator, and the weight of the poet's interventions, especially at the ends of Cantos 7 and 10.

  2. How does Camões rival Homer and Virgil? Where does he imitate them and where does he claim to surpass them?

    Look at the proposition, the council of the gods, Venus's plea to Jupiter and the vision of Canto 10. Think about the claim that his story is true.

  3. Why does the poem give the Old Man of Restelo the last word before the fleet sails?

    Weigh his arguments against the poem's celebration of the voyage, and note that no character answers him.

  4. What does Adamastor represent?

    Consider his prophecies of disaster, his love story, his identity with the Cape, and modern readings of him as a figure for Africa.

  5. How does the Inês de Castro episode fit into a national epic?

    Think about its lyric tone, its criticism of reason of state, and its place in Gama's history between battles.

  6. How do the pagan gods function in a Christian poem?

    Compare Gama's prayer in the storm with Venus's intervention, and read Tethys's confession in 10.82.

  7. Is the Isle of Love an allegory, a fantasy, or both?

    Read the poet's explanation in 9.89–92 against the descriptions of the nymphs and the chase. What does the island reward, and how?

  8. How does the poem combine history, narrated history and prophecy?

    List the different narrators (Gama, Paulo, Veloso, the nymph, Tethys) and the periods each covers.

  9. What is the poet's view of fame and honour?

    Compare the Old Man's speech, the reflection on honour at the end of Canto 6, and the allegory of the island.

  10. How does knowledge of Sebastião's defeat at Alcácer-Quibir change the way we read the poem?

    Reread the dedication and the epilogue, and think about the poem's later reception in Portugal.
