The Lady of the Lake

by Sir Walter Scott

Overview

Author
Sir Walter Scott (1771–1832), Edinburgh lawyer, antiquary and editor of the Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, already famous for The Lay of the Last Minstrel (1805) and Marmion (1808)
Published
Edinburgh, May 1810, by John Ballantyne and Co., first as a costly quarto, then reprinted again and again in cheaper formats
Sales
Among the fastest-selling poems of the age: some 25,000 copies within about eight months, by the usual estimate, an unheard-of figure for a long poem
Form
A verse romance in six cantos, mostly in rhyming octosyllabic couplets; each canto opens with Spenserian stanzas, and the narrative is broken by songs in other metres
Setting
Loch Katrine, the Trossachs and Stirling, in the reign of James V of Scotland (the 1530s); the action takes six days
Main characters
James Fitz-James, the Knight of Snowdoun (James V in disguise); Ellen Douglas; her banished father James of Douglas; Roderick Dhu of Clan Alpine; Malcolm Graeme; the minstrel Allan-bane
Famous songs
'Soldier, rest! thy warfare o'er', the boat song 'Hail to the Chief', the 'Coronach', and Ellen's hymn 'Ave Maria', set by Schubert in 1825 as Ellens dritter Gesang
Afterlives
Rossini's opera La donna del lago (Naples, 1819); 'Hail to the Chief' became the musical salute of the President of the United States; a tourist rush to Loch Katrine that never stopped
Central conflict
Highland clan against Lowland crown, worked out through a love triangle, a feud and a single combat that ends in royal mercy

Context

Walter Scott was the most successful poet in Britain when he published The Lady of the Lake in May 1810. Trained as an advocate and serving as a clerk of the Court of Session in Edinburgh, he had spent his youth collecting ballads in the Borders, published them as the Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03), and turned that learning into the verse romances The Lay of the Last Minstrel (1805) and Marmion (1808). The new poem was issued by John Ballantyne, whose firm Scott himself secretly backed. It sold faster than any poem before it: contemporaries spoke of 20,000 to 25,000 copies within the first year, and modern book historians confirm that no long poem of the period reached so many buyers so quickly (Lockhart 1837) (St Clair 2004) (Sutherland 1995).

Scott set the story in country he had known since boyhood: Loch Katrine and the wooded pass of the Trossachs, on the edge between the Lowland plain of Stirling and the Highlands of Perthshire. He revisited the district in 1809 to check his topography, and is said to have tested whether a rider could cover the distance from Loch Vennachar to Stirling in the time the plot allows. The accuracy and beauty of the descriptions turned the region into a destination. Visitors arrived with the poem in hand, boatmen showed them 'Ellen's Isle', and the Trossachs became one of the first great sites of literary tourism in Britain (Durie 2003) (Gold 1995) (Rigney 2012).

The historical frame is the reign of James V (1513–1542), who in 1528 escaped from the control of his stepfather Archibald Douglas, Earl of Angus, and banished the house of Douglas. Scott invents a member of that family, James of Douglas, once lord of Bothwell, who lives in hiding with his daughter Ellen under the protection of the outlawed Highland chief Roderick Dhu. Scott also drew on the popular tradition that James V liked to wander his kingdom in disguise, the 'Gudeman of Ballengeich' of folk tales. In his notes he explains the Highland customs used in the plot: the fiery cross that summoned a clan to arms, the divination called the Taghairm, and the coronach or funeral lament (Johnson 1970) (Robertson 2012).

The poem belongs to the long Romantic reimagining of the Scottish Highlands that began with James Macpherson's Ossian poems in the 1760s. After the defeat of the Jacobite rising in 1746 the clans had been disarmed and their society broken up; by 1810 Highland regiments were fighting for Britain against Napoleon, and the Highlands could be presented as a land of heroic, picturesque and safely past violence. Scott offers a Highland chief of real grandeur, but his story ends with the chief dead and the king's peace restored. Readers have argued ever since about whether this is an elegy for a lost culture or a story that makes its loss look natural (Womack 1989) (Trumpener 1997) (Davis 1998).

The Lady of the Lake marked the height of Scott's fame as a poet. Rokeby (1813) and The Lord of the Isles (1815) sold well but less spectacularly, and from 1812 Byron's Childe Harold's Pilgrimage drew away the public's excitement. Scott turned to prose fiction with Waverley (1814), where many of the poem's concerns, especially Highland and Lowland, loyalty and rebellion, return on a larger scale. The poem itself lived on in music, opera, painting and school anthologies throughout the nineteenth century, and its songs travelled further than its story (Hayden 1970) (Rigney 2012) (Pittock 2007).

Plot overview

A stag hunted from Glenartney leads a lone huntsman deep into the Trossachs, where his horse dies under him. Lost on the shore of Loch Katrine, he blows his horn and is answered by a girl in a skiff, Ellen, who takes him to a hidden lodge on an island. He calls himself James Fitz-James, Knight of Snowdoun. Next day he leaves, and we learn that Ellen's father is the banished James of Douglas, sheltering with his kinsman Roderick Dhu, chief of Clan Alpine. Roderick wants to marry Ellen and to bind Douglas to a Highland rising against the king; Douglas refuses, and Ellen loves the young Malcolm Graeme. Roderick and Malcolm quarrel, and Malcolm leaves the island as an enemy of the clan.

Roderick sends the fiery cross through his country to summon his men. Brian, a half-mad hermit, consecrates it and later performs a divination which declares that the side which spills the first enemy blood will win. Douglas leaves for Stirling to give himself up and so save his friends, while Ellen and the minstrel Allan-bane hide in a cave. Fitz-James returns to ask Ellen to fly with him; she tells him that she loves another, and he gives her a ring that will win any favour from the king. On his way back he is betrayed by his guide, sees the mad Lowland captive Blanche of Devan killed by an arrow meant for him, and spends the night by the fire of a Highlander who promises to lead him safely out of the clan's land.

At dawn the Highlander leads him towards the edge of Clan Alpine's territory, revealing on the way that he is Roderick Dhu and that his warriors are hidden in every bush. At Coilantogle ford the two fight. Fitz-James wins and has the wounded chief carried to Stirling. Douglas, meanwhile, wins the prizes at the Stirling games and then gives himself up to the guards. A royal army marches against Clan Alpine in the Trossachs.

Ellen comes to Stirling Castle with the ring. In prison Roderick dies listening to Allan-bane's account of the battle of Beal' an Duine. Ellen is led into the royal presence by Fitz-James, and finds that he alone keeps his cap on: the Knight of Snowdoun is the King of Scotland. James pardons Douglas, restores him to favour, frees Malcolm and gives Ellen to him. The poem closes with a farewell to the minstrel's harp.

Part by part

Part 1: Canto I: The Chase

Introduction and stanzas I–XXXV

Summary

An introduction in Spenserian stanzas calls on the 'Harp of the North', hanging silent on a tree by Saint Fillan's spring, to wake again. The story opens with a stag that has spent the night in Glenartney and is roused at dawn by a hunting party. The chase sweeps across Menteith; one by one the hounds and riders fall back, until a single huntsman follows the stag into the Trossachs, where his gallant grey horse collapses and dies. The stag escapes. Alone, the hunter admires the wild beauty of Loch Katrine, imagines it softened by castles and abbeys, and winds his horn to call his companions.

The horn is answered by a girl rowing a skiff, who calls out for her father and then for Malcolm before she sees a stranger. This is Ellen. Bound by Highland hospitality, she takes him to a lodge on an island, hung with weapons and trophies, among them a sword so heavy that only one man, her father, can wield it. The household asks no names, but he gives his: James Fitz-James, Knight of Snowdoun. The matron of the house, Lady Margaret, and Ellen say little of themselves. Ellen sings him to sleep with 'Soldier, rest!' and a second verse for a huntsman; his sleep is troubled by dreams in which the girl's face changes into the features of the Douglas family, and he rises in the night, uneasy.

Analysis

The opening chase is one of the most admired set pieces in Scott's verse: brisk couplets, place names chosen for their sound and their accuracy, and a narrative speed that carries the reader into unknown country along with the hunter. The death of the horse turns the king's sport into the crossing of a boundary. The hunter's daydream of turrets and cloisters on the lake shows him seeing the Highlands with Lowland eyes, as land to be improved, and the poem quietly sets that view beside the wilderness he actually meets (Womack 1989).

Ellen enters as an image from romance, the 'Lady of the Lake' of Arthurian legend, compared to a Greek statue and a Naiad. Scott keeps the reader almost as ignorant as the guest: the sword, the withheld names and the uneasy dream all point to the Douglas secret without revealing it. The songs, which interrupt the narrative in a different metre, establish a pattern that runs through the poem, in which music carries feelings and dangers that the characters do not state openly (Robertson 2012).

Part 2: Canto II: The Island

Stanzas I–XXXVII

Summary

In the morning Fitz-James takes his leave, and the old minstrel Allan-bane plays as he goes. Ellen teases Allan about her suitors, but he answers gravely: her father is a banished Douglas, disowned by the court, and their only refuge is the protection of Roderick Dhu, a feared outlaw who once killed a knight at Holyrood and who now hopes to marry her. Ellen confesses that she prefers Malcolm Graeme and will not be bought by a refuge. Then pipes sound on the water, and Roderick's galleys come up the lake, the oarsmen singing 'Hail to the Chief' in praise of the pine tree that is his badge.

Douglas arrives from hunting with Malcolm. That evening Roderick tells them that the king is coming to the district, under pretext of hunting, after executing Border chiefs, and that Douglas has been seen. He proposes a league of the western clans and asks for Ellen's hand to seal it. Douglas refuses both: he will not raise arms against his king, nor give his daughter where her heart is not. Roderick's jealousy turns on Malcolm; the two grapple and are parted by Douglas. Malcolm refuses the chief's safe-conduct, plunges into the lake and swims to the shore, now Roderick's open enemy.

Analysis

The canto lays out the triangle and its politics. Ellen's three suitors, the unknown knight, the Highland chief and the young Lowland noble, represent three possible futures for Douglas and for Scotland. Allan's speeches give the background as legend and memory. This is the role of the bard that Scott gives his minstrel throughout: a figure of national tradition who remembers and laments rather than acts (Trumpener 1997).

Roderick is drawn with real complexity. The boat song shows him as the glory of a clan that lives by its chief, yet Allan's report of the Holyrood killing and of his raids on the Lowlands makes him a danger to everyone he shelters. Douglas's refusal states the poem's central principle of loyalty: a wronged subject may suffer exile, but he does not make war on his king. That principle, voiced by the noblest of the exiles, is set against the Highland code of kinship and revenge (Lincoln 2007).

Part 3: Canto III: The Gathering

Stanzas I–XXXI

Summary

The canto opens with a reflection on time, which sweeps away the old people who told the tales of the past. Roderick prepares for war. The hermit Brian, said to be the son of a woman and a spirit and driven half-mad by solitude, makes the fiery cross from yew, scorches it, quenches it in the blood of a goat and curses any clansman who ignores it. Roderick's henchman Malise carries it across the lochs and glens. At Duncraggan the cross reaches a house in mourning for its chief, Duncan; the women sing the 'Coronach', and Duncan's young son Angus must leave the bier to carry the symbol on.

At the chapel of Saint Bride the cross interrupts the wedding of Norman of Armandave and Mary, and the bridegroom leaves his bride at the church door to answer the summons. Roderick goes in search of the Douglas party, which has left the island: Douglas has gone towards Stirling, and Ellen and Allan have taken shelter in the goblin cave on the slopes of Ben Venue. Passing nearby in the evening, Roderick hears Ellen singing her hymn to the Virgin, 'Ave Maria', and turns away to join his gathering army without seeing her.

Analysis

The fiery cross is Scott's most memorable use of Highland custom, and his notes record that it was still used in the rising of 1745. In the poem it becomes a device of narrative as well as of war: following the cross from house to house, the reader sees a whole society drop its private life at the chief's command, funeral, wedding and all. The effect is double. It displays the loyalty and energy of the clan, and it shows the cost of a way of life in which every bond is subordinate to the feud (Womack 1989) (Davis 1998).

The songs here are among Scott's finest. The coronach for Duncan uses natural images of transience in short, falling lines that imitate a Gaelic lament. Ellen's 'Ave Maria', the prayer of a girl outcast and hiding among rocks, gives the Catholic world of sixteenth-century Scotland a tenderness that Scott's Protestant readers could enjoy as romance; in Schubert's setting it became one of the most widely known songs in the world (Pittock 2007).

Part 4: Canto IV: The Prophecy

Stanzas I–XXX

Summary

Clan Alpine gathers. Norman and Malise exchange news while Roderick waits: Brian has performed the Taghairm, wrapping himself in the hide of a slaughtered bull beside a waterfall to receive a vision, and his answer is that the side that spills the first foeman's life will conquer. Roderick takes this as good news, since a Lowland spy is in the hills and will not leave them alive. In the cave, Ellen and Allan wait in fear; Allan sings the ballad of 'Alice Brand', a tale of a fairy curse and a brother's blood.

Fitz-James appears, guided by Roderick's man Murdoch, and asks Ellen to come with him to Stirling under his protection. She tells him plainly who her father is and that she loves Malcolm; he accepts her refusal and gives her a ring, a pledge from the king that will grant one request. On his way back Murdoch tries to lead him into an ambush. A mad Lowland woman, Blanche of Devan, seized by Roderick's men on her wedding day, sings wild songs and warns him; Murdoch's arrow, meant for the knight, kills her, and Fitz-James kills Murdoch. Lost and alone, he comes to a Highlander's campfire. The stranger shares his plaid and promises on his honour to guide him as far as Coilantogle ford.

Analysis

The prophecy is a classic oracle, true but misleading. Roderick reads it as a promise of victory, but the first death in the war is that of Murdoch, killed by Fitz-James; the riddle works against the side that trusts it. Scott enjoys both the Gothic atmosphere of the Taghairm and the irony of a people led by a superstition they do not understand (Robertson 2012).

Blanche of Devan is the poem's darkest figure. She is the Lowland victim of Highland raiding, reduced to madness, and her songs, like Ophelia's, speak truth in fragments. Her death draws Fitz-James into a personal vow of revenge on Roderick, so that the political conflict becomes a private one. Yet the canto ends with the two enemies sharing a fire and a plaid under the law of hospitality, a scene that shows Highland honour at its best just before the reader learns who the host is (Lincoln 2007).

Part 5: Canto V: The Combat

Stanzas I–XXXIII

Summary

At dawn the Highlander leads Fitz-James through the hills. They speak of the coming war; the stranger defends Clan Alpine's raids on the Lowlands as the reclaiming of land taken from the Gael, while the knight swears enmity to Roderick. The guide whistles, and the hillside fills with armed men rising from the heather: he is Roderick Dhu. Fitz-James sets his back to a rock and defies them all. Roderick waves his men out of sight again and keeps his word, leading his guest to Coilantogle ford, past the last of his guards. There he throws down his plaid and challenges the knight to single combat.

Fitz-James, the more skilful swordsman, offers to spare him, but Roderick will not yield; at last the knight overcomes him, and his huntsmen arrive to carry the wounded chief to Stirling. In Stirling, Douglas, resolved to surrender, enters the town during the festival games and wins the prizes for archery, wrestling and throwing the stone. The king, presiding over the games, rewards him coldly, without acknowledging him. When Douglas's hound Lufra pulls down the stag kept for the royal hounds, a quarrel breaks out, the crowd turns against him, and Douglas gives himself up to the guards to prevent a riot. News comes that the Earl of Mar is marching against Clan Alpine.

Analysis

The revelation on the hillside is the most famous coup de théâtre in Scott's verse. It depends on the honour code that the two men share: Roderick has the stranger in his power and lets him go because he gave his word. Roderick's speech about the 'fertile plains' gives the Highland case its strongest statement in the poem, the claim of a dispossessed people. Scott lets the claim be heard, and then answers it not with argument but with the outcome of a fight (Trumpener 1997) (Davis 1998).

The combat sets two kinds of strength against each other, the Highland chief's fierce courage and the trained swordplay of the court. The scene at the Stirling games then turns from Highland to Lowland and shows another kind of disorder: a fickle crowd and a court that does not honour the noblest man among them. Douglas's surrender is the counterpart of Roderick's defiance, and the poem clearly prefers it (Lincoln 2007).

Part 6: Canto VI: The Guard-Room

Stanzas I–XXIX and Conclusion

Summary

In the guard-room of Stirling Castle, mercenary soldiers drink and sing after the night's fighting. Ellen arrives with Allan-bane, and the soldiers, seeing the ring, treat her with respect. Allan is taken to Roderick, who lies dying in a cell, and the chief asks him to sing of the battle. Allan describes the fight at the pass of Beal' an Duine, where the royal troops were driven back by Clan Alpine until a herald stopped the battle with news that Douglas had surrendered and Roderick was a prisoner. As the song ends, Roderick dies; Allan sings a lament over him.

Ellen, waiting in a chamber, hears a captive singing 'My hawk is tired of perch and hood' from a nearby tower. Fitz-James comes to lead her to the king. In the crowded hall she looks for the monarch and sees that all stand bareheaded except her guide: the Knight of Snowdoun is the king. James raises her, pardons Douglas and restores him to favour, and invites her to ask a further boon. She thinks first of Roderick, who is beyond help, and then cannot bring herself to name Malcolm; the king calls him forward, throws a golden chain over his neck and lays the clasp in Ellen's hand. The poem ends with a farewell to the Harp of the North.

Analysis

The final canto brings the threads together in the royal castle. Roderick's death is placed, characteristically, inside a song: the old bard's account of the battle is so vivid that the chief seems to fight it again on his bed, and he dies as the song turns to defeat. The Highland world is given its elegy at the very moment of its subjection. Many readers have found this the emotional climax of the poem, more moving than the happy ending that follows (Rigney 2012).

The recognition scene answers the whole plot. The king who wandered disguised in the wilds now reveals himself among the furs and silks of the court, still in simple Lincoln green, and his power is shown chiefly as mercy: forgiveness for Douglas, freedom and a bride for Malcolm. The ending enacts the idea of a benevolent monarch who knows his subjects personally and reconciles a divided kingdom, an idea with a clear political appeal in the Britain of 1810 (Duncan 2007) (Lincoln 2007).

Characters

The Douglases and their friends

Ellen Douglas
The 'Lady of the Lake', daughter of the banished James of Douglas, living in hiding on an island in Loch Katrine. She is graceful, loyal and quick, gentle in her songs but firm in her choices: she refuses Roderick, tells Fitz-James honestly that she loves Malcolm, and goes to Stirling to plead for her father. Her journey from the island to the court links the Highland and Lowland halves of the poem.
James of Douglas
Ellen's father, once lord of Bothwell, exiled with the rest of the house of Douglas by James V. Of great strength and dignity, he shelters with Roderick but will not join his rebellion or force his daughter to marry him. He gives himself up at Stirling to protect his friends, and is pardoned and restored at the end. He embodies the poem's ideal of loyal suffering.
Malcolm Graeme
A young nobleman of the Graemes, Ellen's beloved and a protégé of Douglas. His quarrel with Roderick in Canto II makes him an enemy of Clan Alpine; he swims from the island rather than accept the chief's safe-conduct. Held prisoner at Stirling, he is freed by the king and given Ellen's hand. He is the least developed of the three suitors.
Allan-bane
The old minstrel of the Douglas family, white-haired and devoted. He tells the family's history, warns Ellen about Roderick, sings ballads and lays, and witnesses Roderick's death, for which he sings the lament. He is Scott's figure of the bard, the voice of tradition and memory within the poem.

Clan Alpine

Roderick Dhu
Black Roderick, chief of Clan Alpine, outlawed for killing a knight at the royal court and famous for raids on the Lowlands. Proud, passionate and brave, he loves Ellen jealously and hopes to unite the clans against the king. He keeps his word as a host even to an enemy, fights Fitz-James at Coilantogle ford and dies a prisoner at Stirling. Many readers find him the most vivid character in the poem.
Lady Margaret
Roderick's mother and the matron of the island lodge. She receives the guest in Canto I and favours her son's suit for Ellen.
Brian the Hermit
A wild, half-mad monk said to be the child of a mortal woman and a spirit, living in the wilderness. He makes the fiery cross and pronounces the curse on those who ignore it, and performs the Taghairm, whose prophecy misleads Roderick. He mingles Christian rite with pagan superstition.
Malise
Roderick's henchman, who carries the fiery cross on the first stage of its journey and later exchanges news with Norman in the gathered army.
Angus, Norman and Mary
Clansfolk who receive the fiery cross. Angus leaves his father Duncan's funeral to carry it; Norman of Armandave leaves his bride Mary at the church door. Their sacrifices show what the summons demands of ordinary lives.
Red Murdoch
Roderick's man, who serves as Fitz-James's guide and tries to lead him to his death. His arrow kills Blanche of Devan; he is killed by Fitz-James, and so becomes the 'foremost foeman' of the prophecy.

The King and the Lowlands

James Fitz-James, Knight of Snowdoun
The lost huntsman of Canto I, really James V of Scotland travelling in disguise; Snowdoun is an old poetic name for Stirling. Courteous, brave, impulsive and drawn to Ellen, he accepts her refusal generously, defeats Roderick in single combat, and at the end reveals himself and uses his power to forgive and reconcile. He is Scott's portrait of the ideal king.
Blanche of Devan
A Lowland girl carried off in Roderick's raid on her wedding day, when her bridegroom was killed. Driven mad, she wanders the Highlands singing. She warns Fitz-James of Murdoch's treachery and is killed by the arrow meant for him; the knight vows to avenge her.
John of Brent
A rough soldier in the guard-room at Stirling who sings a drinking song and, moved by Ellen's distress, treats her with kindness. He represents the mercenary world of the royal army.

Themes

Highland and Lowland

The poem is built on the contrast between two Scotlands, divided by the line of hills at the edge of the Trossachs. The Highlands are wild, beautiful, poor and governed by clan loyalty; the Lowlands are fertile, ordered and governed by the crown. Scott gives each side a voice. Roderick's speech in Canto V presents the Highland raids as the recovery of land seized long ago, while Blanche of Devan stands for the Lowlanders who suffer them.

The plot moves steadily from Highland to Lowland, from the island to Stirling Castle, and resolves the conflict on Lowland terms. The Highland chief dies; the Douglas family returns to court; the Highland world survives only in song and memory. Critics read this structure as Scott's characteristic way of honouring a culture while presenting its absorption into a larger state as inevitable (Womack 1989) (Davis 1998).

Loyalty, honour and the law of hospitality

Almost every important choice in the poem is a test of loyalty. Douglas is loyal to a king who has wronged him; Roderick to his clan and his feud; the clansmen to the summons of the cross; Ellen to her father and her love. The Highland law of hospitality, which protects any guest under one's roof or at one's fire, binds even enemies, so that Roderick guides and protects the man he means to fight.

Scott admires these codes but shows their conflicts. Roderick's honour as a host and his honour as a chief pull in opposite directions, and his refusal to yield to a better swordsman costs him his life. The poem's resolution makes loyalty to the crown the supreme value, but it is a crown that recognises and rewards the honour of its subjects (Lincoln 2007).

Disguise and recognition

The hero spends the poem in disguise, and his true identity is the secret on which the story turns. Fitz-James's courtly manners, the royal ring and his promises of influence at Stirling are clues an attentive reader can follow. The disguise allows the king to see his kingdom from below, to be judged as a man rather than as a monarch, and to win Ellen's trust honestly.

The motif belongs to a long tradition of tales about kings who wander among their people, and Scott connects it to the Scottish legends of James V. The final recognition turns all that has happened into a demonstration of royal benevolence, in which the king's private experience becomes the basis for public mercy (Johnson 1970).

Song, minstrelsy and memory

The poem is framed by an address to the Harp of the North and filled with songs: lullabies, a boat song, laments, a hymn, ballads and a battle piece. Scott had built his career on collecting and imitating ballads, and here he uses song to vary the rhythm of the poem and to voice feelings that the narrative keeps in reserve. Allan-bane, the bard, is both a character and an image of the poet himself.

Song is also the poem's way of remembering a vanished world. The coronach, the boat song and the lament for Roderick preserve Highland life in the very act of mourning it. Katie Trumpener's account of 'bardic nationalism' describes how Romantic writers made the bard a symbol of national memory, and The Lady of the Lake is one of the most successful examples (Trumpener 1997) (Robertson 2012).

Landscape and the picturesque

Scott's descriptions of Loch Katrine, Ben Venue and the Trossachs are exact enough to be followed on the ground, and the landscape is treated almost as a character. The poem teaches its reader to see the scenery in the language of the picturesque and the sublime: crags, torrents, mist and the sudden view of the lake.

That way of seeing had consequences. The poem made the region famous and helped create the Romantic image of the Highlands as a place to be visited and admired, an image on which the Scottish tourist industry was later built (Durie 2003) (Gold 1995).

Motifs

The hunt

The poem begins with a stag hunt and keeps returning to it. The king is a huntsman, Douglas is compared to a stricken deer, his hound Lufra pulls down the stag at the Stirling games, and the captive in the tower sings of his longing to hunt in the green forest. Hunting links royal sport to pursuit and capture, and suggests that the political conflict is itself a kind of chase.

Hospitality

The guest who is asked no name, the meal shared on the island, the plaid shared at the campfire: the code of hospitality recurs at key moments and protects strangers on both sides. It is the Highland virtue the poem praises most consistently, even in Roderick.

Interpolated songs

Nearly every canto contains songs in metres different from the narrative couplets. They slow the action, vary the music of the verse and often foreshadow events, as Ellen's lullaby for the soldier anticipates the rest that Roderick finds only in death.

Horns and calls

The hunter's horn on the shore of Loch Katrine, the pipes of Roderick's galleys, the whistle that raises the clan from the heather, and the bugle-horn whose silence decides the battle: sounds summon, reveal and decide the action throughout the poem.

Symbols

The Harp of the North

The harp hanging on the witch-elm in the introduction stands for Scottish minstrelsy and the poetry of the past, silent until the poet wakes it. The poem's final stanzas send it back to its tree, framing the whole story as a song from a vanished world.

The fiery cross

A cross of yew, scorched and dipped in blood, carried from hand to hand to summon the clan. It stands for the absolute claim of the chief on his people and the fusion of religion, kinship and war in Highland society.

The ring

Fitz-James's gift to Ellen, a pledge from the king that grants one request. It is a token of the giver's hidden identity and the means by which the Lowland court and the Highland refugee are finally brought together.

The pine of Clan Alpine

The evergreen pine on Roderick's banner, praised in 'Hail to the Chief', stands for the endurance and pride of the clan; the song's hope that it will flourish is cut short by the outcome of the poem.

Critical debates

Romanticising the Highlands

Scott's poem is a central text in the making of the Romantic Highlands, a process that began with Macpherson's Ossian and continued through the tartan pageantry of George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822, which Scott himself stage-managed. Hugh Trevor-Roper's essay in The Invention of Tradition, and his posthumous book The Invention of Scotland, argued that much of what passes for ancient Highland culture, from the kilt to the clan tartans, was invented or embellished in this period (Hobsbawm 1983) (Trevor-Roper 2008) (Stafford 1988).

Peter Womack showed how eighteenth-century ideas of 'improvement' and 'romance' combined to make the Highlands appear at once backward and picturesque, a land whose violence could be enjoyed once it was safely past. Critics of 'tartanry' blame Scott for turning a real society into scenery; others reply that he took Highland grievances seriously and gave them memorable expression (Womack 1989) (McCracken-Flesher 2005).

Union, loyalty and Jacobite memory

The poem is set in the sixteenth century, but it was written a century after the Union of 1707 and two generations after the last Jacobite rising. A plot in which a rebellious Highland chief is defeated, a wronged noble refuses to rebel, and a king forgives his enemies can be read as a parable of reconciliation within Britain. Leith Davis reads Scott's work as one of many literary 'negotiations' of the Union, in which Scottish difference is preserved within a British identity (Davis 1998).

Written during the Napoleonic wars, the poem also belongs to a moment when Highland soldiers were celebrated as defenders of Britain. Simon Bainbridge places Scott's martial romances in the context of the war, as poems that made historical combat thrilling and patriotic for readers at home (Bainbridge 2003).

The songs and the ballad revival

Scott came to verse romance through ballad collecting, and the interpolated songs are a large part of the poem's appeal. Some critics have seen them as the best poetry in it, free of the occasional slackness of the narrative couplets. They also travelled on their own: set by Schubert, sung in drawing rooms and adapted for the stage, they made the poem known to people who never read it through (Pittock 2007).

Katie Trumpener argues that the Romantic bard, the remembering singer of a defeated nation, became a key figure in British and Irish writing of the period. Allan-bane's role, and the many laments in the poem, fit this pattern: the poem presents itself as the last song of a dying tradition, sung for a modern audience (Trumpener 1997).

The disguised king and benevolent monarchy

The ending makes the king the poem's real hero. Some readers have found this politically comfortable to the point of complacency: a Tory poet's fantasy of a monarch who personally repairs every injustice. Others point out that Scott shows James as impulsive and fallible, and that the court and the Stirling crowd are criticised as sharply as the Highlanders (Sutherland 1995) (Lincoln 2007).

Ian Duncan's study of Scott and Romantic Edinburgh shows how his fiction imagines the modern state as a framework within which older loyalties can be preserved as sentiment. The Lady of the Lake already displays this pattern, in which history's losers receive pardon and a place in the story rather than power (Duncan 2007).

Popularity, reputation and Byron

The poem's success was a phenomenon of the book trade. William St Clair's study of reading in the Romantic period shows how exceptional Scott's sales were and how they shaped the market for long narrative poems. Reviewers such as Francis Jeffrey in the Edinburgh Review praised its vividness and energy while finding fault with its looseness of style; the range of contemporary responses is gathered in John O. Hayden's Critical Heritage volume (St Clair 2004) (Hayden 1970).

Scott's fame as a poet was eclipsed by Byron after 1812, and later critics have tended to treat his verse as a prelude to the novels. Edgar Johnson and John Sutherland describe this turn in his career, and more recent criticism has begun to reconsider the poems on their own terms, as experiments in popular historical narrative (Johnson 1970) (Sutherland 1995) (Robertson 2012).

Ellen as heroine

Ellen can be read as a conventional romance heroine, beautiful, musical and passive, whose fate is decided by the men who desire her. But she also makes some of the most consequential choices in the plot: she refuses Roderick and Fitz-James, keeps faith with Malcolm, and makes the journey to Stirling that leads to the resolution.

Ina Ferris has shown how Scott's literary authority was shaped by the gendered terms of contemporary reviewing, which associated romance with women readers and history with masculine seriousness. Ellen's place at the centre of a poem about war and politics, and the poem's large female readership, make it a useful test case for that argument (Ferris 1991).

Afterlives: tourism, music and memory

Ann Rigney's study of Scott's afterlives traces how his works lived on in places, objects, performances and commemorations. The Lady of the Lake is a striking case: the Trossachs became a tourist route, Loch Katrine acquired its 'Ellen's Isle', and a steamer named after the author still sails the lake. Historians of Scottish tourism treat the poem as a turning point (Rigney 2012) (Durie 2003) (Gold 1995).

Its music travelled even further. Rossini's La donna del lago (1819) was among the first operas based on Scott; Schubert's songs of 1825 made the 'Ave Maria' world-famous; and 'Hail to the Chief', set by James Sanderson for a London stage version, crossed to the United States and became the official salute to the President (Pittock 2007) (Kirk 1986).

Questions for study

  1. How does the opening stag hunt prepare for the rest of the poem?

    Consider the crossing from Lowland to Highland, the comparison of Douglas to a stricken deer, and the hunting images in Cantos V and VI.

  2. Compare Ellen's three suitors. What does each represent for Scotland?

    Think of Roderick's clan, Malcolm's Lowland nobility and Fitz-James's crown, and why the poem gives Ellen to Malcolm with the king's blessing.

  3. Is Roderick Dhu a villain, a tragic hero or both?

    Weigh the Holyrood killing and the raid on Devan against his hospitality, his speech about the 'fertile plains' and his death in Canto VI.

  4. What part do the songs play in the poem?

    Look at what each song expresses that the narrative does not, and at how the songs vary the metre and pace.

  5. How does the fiery cross sequence in Canto III show both the strength and the cost of the clan system?

    Follow the cross from Brian's curse through Duncan's funeral and Norman's wedding.

  6. Why does Scott keep the king's identity secret until Canto VI, and what clues does he give?

    Look at the ring, Fitz-James's promises about Stirling, and the way the final recognition is staged.

  7. How does the prophecy of the Taghairm work in the plot?

    Ask who is the 'foremost foeman' to die, and what the misunderstanding says about Roderick and his clan.

  8. What is the function of Blanche of Devan?

    Consider her as a Lowland victim of Highland raiding, as a figure of madness and truth, and as the motive for Fitz-James's vow.

  9. Does the poem mourn the Highland world or justify its end?

    Think about the laments, the fate of Roderick, the king's mercy, and the critical debate about 'tartanry' and the Union.

  10. Why do you think the poem drew so many tourists to the Trossachs?

    Consider the precision of the descriptions, the use of real place names, and the Romantic taste for picturesque scenery.
