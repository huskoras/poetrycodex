Overview

Composed
Over several centuries: the central narrative probably took shape around the middle of the first millennium BC, and the poem reached roughly its present extent in the early centuries AD
Attributed to
Vālmīki, the sage-poet whom Indian tradition calls the ādikavi, 'the first poet'
Language and metre
Sanskrit, almost entirely in the śloka (anuṣṭubh), a stanza of four eight-syllable quarters; the tradition counts about 24,000 ślokas
Divisions
Seven kāṇḍas ('books'): Bāla, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundara, Yuddha and Uttara, each divided into sargas ('cantos')
Standard text
The critical edition prepared at the Oriental Institute, Baroda (1960–75), under G. H. Bhatt and U. P. Shah; references here follow it as 'Rām. kāṇḍa.sarga'
Genre
Epic; Indian tradition calls it the first kāvya (artful poem), as distinct from the Mahābhārata, which it calls itihāsa ('history')
Setting
The kingdom of Kosala and its capital Ayodhyā, the forests of central and southern India, the monkey kingdom of Kiṣkindhā, and the island fortress of Laṅkā
Quoted translation
Romesh Chunder Dutt's condensed version in long rhymed couplets (1899), arranged in twelve books; Dutt spells many names in their modern Indian forms (Ravan, Bali, Bibhishan, Bharat, Lakshman)
Also on this site
Ralph T. H. Griffith's complete verse translation, The Rámáyan of Válmíki (1870–74), of which Books I–VI are given canto by canto
Main themes
Dharma and the keeping of one's word; kingship and the ideal kingdom (Rāmarājya); exile and loss; love, chastity and suspicion; the divine in human form

Context

The Rāmāyaṇa, 'the journey of Rāma', tells how Rama, eldest son of King Dasharatha of Ayodhya, is exiled to the forest on the eve of his consecration, loses his wife Sita to the demon king Ravana, and wins her back with an army of monkeys and bears. With the Mahābhārata it is one of the two great Sanskrit epics, and it has been for more than two thousand years one of the most widely told stories in Asia. Indian tradition treats it as the first poem of all, and its author Vālmīki as the ādikavi, the first poet.

The poem tells the story of its own birth. In the opening sargas the sage Nārada summarises the life of Rama for Vālmīki (Rām. 1.1). Walking by a river, Vālmīki then sees a hunter shoot one of a pair of mating krauñca birds; the female's cry moves him to a curse, and he finds that his grief (śoka) has come out in a new, measured form, the śloka (Rām. 1.2). The god Brahmā tells him to use this metre to tell Rama's story, and Vālmīki teaches the finished poem to Rama's twin sons, Lava and Kuśa, who sing it before their father. The legend makes compassion the source of poetry, and it frames the epic as a song heard by its own hero.

The poem is divided into seven kāṇḍas. Most scholars since Hermann Jacobi have held that the core of the work is Books 2 to 6, from the exile to the war, and that Book 1 (Bāla, Rama's childhood and marriage) and Book 7 (Uttara, 'the later book', which includes the banishment of Sita) were added later; the first and last books make Rama an avatar of the god Viṣṇu much more insistently than the rest (Jacobi 1893) (Brockington 1998). The text survives in two main recensions, northern and southern, which differ considerably. The critical edition published by the Oriental Institute of Baroda between 1960 and 1975, under the general editorship of G. H. Bhatt and U. P. Shah, reconstructs the oldest recoverable text from the manuscripts of both (Bhatt 1975), and it is the basis of the complete Princeton translation by Robert Goldman and his colleagues (Goldman 2017).

Dates are uncertain. The central books reflect a world of small kingdoms in the Gangetic plain and probably took shape around the fifth or fourth century BC; John Brockington traces a gradual growth in stages that continued into the early centuries AD (Brockington 1984). The epic was certainly transmitted by reciters before it was fixed in writing, and the poem itself presents Lava and Kuśa as wandering singers.

The Rāmāyaṇa quoted in this guide is the condensed English verse version by Romesh Chunder Dutt (1848–1909), a Bengali civil servant, historian and novelist, published in 1899 (Dutt 1899). Dutt chose the most celebrated episodes, rendered them in long rhymed couplets that imitate the rhythm of the śloka, and joined them with short prose summaries, which this site omits; his twelve books do not match Vālmīki's seven. For the full text of the first six kāṇḍas, readers can turn to Ralph T. H. Griffith's verse translation, also on this site (Griffith 1874).

Plot overview

King Dasharatha of Ayodhya has four sons by three queens: Rama by Kausalya, Bharata by Kaikeyi, and the twins Lakshmana and Shatrughna by Sumitra. Rama wins Sita, daughter of King Janaka of Videha, by bending and breaking the great bow of Śiva. When the aged king decides to make Rama his heir, Kaikeyi, urged by her maid Manthara, claims two boons he once promised her: that Bharata be consecrated and Rama exiled to the forest for fourteen years. Rama obeys at once; Sita and Lakshmana insist on going with him, and Dasharatha dies of grief. Bharata refuses the throne, seeks Rama in the forest, and returns with his brother's sandals to rule as regent in his name.

In the forest, Rama protects the hermits from the rākṣasas, demons who disrupt their sacrifices. When the demoness Shurpanakha tries to seduce Rama and then attacks Sita, Lakshmana mutilates her. Her brother Ravana, ten-headed king of Lanka, sends the demon Mārīca in the form of a golden deer to lure Rama away; Lakshmana is driven off by Sita's reproaches, and Ravana, disguised as a mendicant, carries Sita off through the air.

Searching for her, Rama allies himself with Sugriva, an exiled monkey king, and kills Sugriva's brother Vali from ambush so that Sugriva can regain his throne. The monkey Hanuman leaps across the ocean to Lanka, finds Sita in Ravana's aśoka grove, gives her Rama's ring and sets the city on fire. Ravana's brother Vibhishana, who has urged him to return Sita, defects to Rama. The monkeys build a causeway to Lanka, and in a long war Rama's army kills Ravana's brother Kumbhakarna, his son Indrajit, and finally Ravana himself.

Rama publicly refuses to take back Sita, who has lived in another man's house; she enters a fire, and the fire god Agni returns her unharmed. Rama is consecrated king in Ayodhya and rules over a golden age. In the Uttarakāṇḍa, rumours among his subjects lead him to banish the pregnant Sita, who gives birth to Lava and Kuśa in Vālmīki's hermitage. Years later the boys sing the Rāmāyaṇa at Rama's horse sacrifice; Rama recognises them and asks Sita to prove her purity again. Instead she calls on the Earth, her mother, who opens and takes her back.

Book by book

Book 1: The Bridal of Sita

Summary

Dutt opens with an idealised portrait of Ayodhya under King Dasharatha: a city without envy, poverty or deceit, ruled by a king compared to Manu, the first lawgiver. He then moves directly to the court of Janaka, king of Videha in Mithila, who has promised his daughter Sita to whoever can bend the bow of Rudra (Śiva). Suitors have come and failed. The sage Viśvāmitra brings the young Rama and Lakshmana; the bow is wheeled out on an iron cart, and Rama bends it until it snaps with a sound like thunder.

Envoys carry the news to Ayodhya, and Dasharatha travels to Mithila with his priests and army. Bharata's uncle Yudhajit arrives from the kingdom of Kaikeya. The four sons of Dasharatha are married at a single ceremony, Rama to Sita, and the wedding party returns home amid festivity.

Analysis

Dutt condenses Vālmīki's Bālakāṇḍa drastically. He leaves out the frame story of the poem's own origin (Rām. 1.1–4), the sacrifice by which Dasharatha obtains his sons, and Rama's early adventures with Viśvāmitra, including the killing of the demoness Tāṭakā and the release of Ahalyā from her curse. Readers should be aware that the scholarly view of this book as a later addition rests partly on such material, which presents Rama as Viṣṇu incarnate from the start (Goldman 2017).

What remains is the portrait of the ideal city and the bow contest. The opening description of Ayodhya establishes the standard of righteous kingship that the rest of the poem tests; the contest presents Rama's strength as almost superhuman, a feat that kings have tried in vain. Sita's own origin, which she later recounts, is just as significant: Janaka found her in a furrow while ploughing, so that she is the daughter of the Earth, to which she will return at the end.

Book 2: The Banishment

Summary

While Bharata and Shatrughna are away visiting their grandfather in Kaikeya, Dasharatha, feeling his age, calls an assembly of princes and citizens and proposes to consecrate Rama as heir. The people approve with joy. Rama and Sita keep a night of fasting and prayer, and the city is decorated for the ceremony.

Kaikeyi's hunchbacked maid Manthara poisons her mistress's mind: Rama's elevation will make Kausalya chief queen and Bharata his rival's servant. Kaikeyi reminds Dasharatha of two boons he once granted her and claims them: Bharata's consecration and Rama's exile for fourteen years. The king, bound by his promise, faints, rages and pleads, but cannot refuse. In the morning Rama finds his father broken with grief and, learning the cause from Kaikeyi, accepts the sentence without complaint.

Analysis

This book shows the central conflict of the Ayodhyākāṇḍa: dharma requires that a promise be kept, even when keeping it destroys the person who made it. Dasharatha is trapped between his word to Kaikeyi and his love for Rama, and the poem pictures him as a deer before a tigress. Kaikeyi is no simple villain; she is the fond stepmother of the scene's opening, turned by Manthara's argument about rank and dependency in a polygamous court.

Rama's response defines his character for the rest of the poem. He does not argue the justice of the decree; for him, obedience to a father's word is itself the highest duty. Readers have long debated whether this obedience is admirable or troubling. The Ayodhyākāṇḍa as a whole explores the fragility of royal succession and the dangers of desire within the royal household.

Book 3: The Death of the King

Summary

Rama tells Sita to stay behind and serve Bharata; she refuses, insisting that a wife's place is with her husband, and wins her point. Lakshmana too demands to come. Kausalya blesses Sita, and the three leave Ayodhya in Sumantra's chariot while the citizens follow, weeping. They cross the Tamasā, the Ganges (where the tribal chief Guha helps them and the princes put on bark garments and matted hair), and the Yamunā, and settle at Citrakūṭa.

Back in Ayodhya, Sumantra delivers Rama's farewell. On the sixth night Dasharatha remembers a crime of his youth: hunting by night, he shot a hermit's son who was filling a water pitcher, and the boy's blind parents cursed him to die grieving for a son. Confessing to Kausalya, the old king dies.

Analysis

Sita's speech is the first time she acts on her own will, and she does so with spirit, mocking Rama's counsel as unworthy of a warrior. Her argument rests on ideals of wifely devotion, yet it is also a choice, made against her husband's wish; Sutherland notes that Sita's submissiveness in the epic coexists with considerable self-assertion (Sutherland 1989). Lakshmana's devotion to his brother is equally absolute and will be tested throughout.

The tale of the hermit's son, told in Vālmīki at the end of the Ayodhyākāṇḍa, explains Dasharatha's suffering as the fruit of his own past act (karma), and it echoes the krauñca episode of the poem's opening: a hunter's arrow, a death beside water, a curse born of grief. Dutt's Dasharatha draws the moral explicitly. The death of a father through grief for a son mirrors the parents' grief that cursed him.

Book 4: The Meeting of the Princes

Summary

Bharata returns to Ayodhya to find his father dead and his brother exiled. He rejects a kingdom won by crime and leads the queens, the priest Vasiṣṭha and the army to Citrakūṭa to bring Rama home. The brothers meet in tears. Rama questions Bharata about the state of the kingdom, then refuses to return: their father's word must stand. Kausalya's grief and the arguments of the Brahman Jābāli, who calls filial duty an idle fancy and urges Rama to enjoy his inheritance, do not move him.

Bharata finally asks for Rama's sandals. He will place them on the throne and rule in his brother's name, living as an ascetic himself until Rama returns. Rama then moves deeper into the forest, and at the hermitage of the sage Atri, Atri's wife Anasūyā welcomes Sita, who tells her the story of her birth from the furrow and her marriage.

Analysis

The meeting at Citrakūṭa is a contest of virtue rather than of arms: each brother tries to give the kingdom to the other. Bharata's refusal of power vindicates him from any share in his mother's plot and makes him a model of selfless loyalty. The sandals on the empty throne are one of the epic's great images, the sign of a legitimate king ruling in absence.

Jābāli's speech is unusual in Sanskrit epic: a materialist argument that kinship is a passing encounter, like a night at an inn, and that the afterlife is a fiction. Rama's indignant reply affirms truth (satya) as the foundation of the world and of kingship. The scene shows that the poem knows the arguments against its own values and chooses to answer them.

Book 5: On the Banks of the Godavari

Summary

Rama, Sita and Lakshmana enter the vast Daṇḍaka forest, dotted with hermitages. The sage Agastya receives them warmly, praises Sita's devotion and advises them to settle at Pañcavaṭī by the river Godāvarī. Lakshmana builds a cottage there, and the exiles live peacefully through the autumn.

One winter morning, as they go down to the river to bathe, Lakshmana describes the season: frost on the fields, villagers by the fire, harvest rites for gods and ancestors. His thoughts turn to Bharata, who is enduring the same cold in his ascetic life in Ayodhya.

Analysis

The Araṇyakāṇḍa places Rama in a different world from the court: the forest of hermits and demons. In Vālmīki, the sages ask Rama to protect them from the rākṣasas who disrupt their rites, and Rama's promise to do so commits him to the conflict that leads to Sita's abduction. Dutt's version keeps the idyll and the praise of Sita, and largely passes over the hermits' appeal.

Agastya's praise of Sita contrasts her with fickle women who desert their husbands in misfortune; the comparison with the star Arundhatī, wife of the sage Vasiṣṭha and emblem of fidelity, prepares for the trials to come. The winter description is an early example of a feature that Sanskrit poets would develop into the formal set piece of seasonal description, and it shows the epic's attention to landscape as a mirror of feeling.

Book 6: Sita Lost

Summary

The rākṣasī Shurpanakha, sister of Ravana, sees Rama and falls in love with him. Rama mockingly sends her to Lakshmana, who sends her back; when she lunges at Sita, Lakshmana cuts off her nose and ears. Her brothers Khara and Dūṣaṇa attack with an army and are destroyed, and she flies to Ravana in Lanka. Ravana enlists the demon Mārīca, who takes the form of a golden deer. Sita begs Rama to catch it; he pursues it, and when he shoots it the dying demon cries out in Rama's voice.

Sita, terrified, orders Lakshmana to go to Rama's help; when he hesitates, she accuses him of wanting her for himself, and he leaves. Ravana arrives disguised as a mendicant. Sita receives him as a guest and tells him her story; he reveals himself, offers to make her his queen, and when she scorns him, seizes her by the hair and carries her off in his flying chariot while she cries Rama's name.

Analysis

The Shurpanakha episode is one of the most uncomfortable in the epic. The demoness is a figure of unrestrained female desire, and the brothers' joking cruelty followed by her mutilation has drawn much modern criticism. Within the story, the injury to his sister gives Ravana a motive and shows how one act of violence draws others after it.

The golden deer is the poem's emblem of illusion (māyā), beautiful and deadly. Sita's desire for it, and her harsh words to Lakshmana, are the only faults the poem attributes to her, and later versions often soften them; some even create a 'shadow Sita' so that the real Sita is never touched by Ravana. In Vālmīki and in Dutt, nature itself falls silent at the abduction, which marks it as a cosmic offence. Ravana's disguise as a holy man makes it also a violation of the sacred duty of hospitality.

Book 7: In the Nilgiri Mountains

Summary

Rama searches the forests, grieving. On a mountain to the south he meets Sugriva, a monkey king driven from Kiṣkindhā by his elder brother Vali, and Sugriva's counsellor Hanuman. Rama and Sugriva make a pact: Rama will kill Vali, and Sugriva will help find Sita. Sugriva challenges his brother; Vali's wife Tārā begs him not to fight, but Vali refuses to ignore the insult.

The brothers fight with fists, trees and rocks. As Sugriva weakens, Rama, hidden, shoots Vali with an arrow. Tārā mourns her husband, Sugriva is consecrated king, and Vali's son Aṅgada is made heir. The monsoon delays the search; when it ends, Sugriva sends troops of monkeys to the four directions to search for Sita.

Analysis

The killing of Vali is the most debated act of Rama's career. He shoots a warrior engaged with another, from concealment, in a quarrel that was not his own. In Vālmīki the dying Vali reproaches Rama at length, and Rama answers that Vali had taken his brother's wife, that as a king Rama punishes wrongdoers, and that as a mere animal Vali may be hunted from cover (Rām. 4.17–18) (Lefeber 1994). Dutt passes straight from the fall of Bali to the mourning and the consecration, leaving out the debate.

The Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa also mirrors the story of Ayodhya: another pair of royal brothers, another disputed throne, another exile, and a wife taken from her husband. But where Rama and Bharata compete in renunciation, Vali and Sugriva compete in violence. Rama's description of the rains is one of the poem's celebrated set pieces, in which the season expresses the lover's longing.

Book 8: Sita Discovered

Summary

Hanuman leaps across the ocean to the island of Lanka and searches the city at night. In the aśoka garden he finds Sita, pale and guarded by demonesses, refusing Ravana's advances. Hidden in a tree, he sings the story of Rama's ancestry and virtues to reassure her, then shows himself and gives her Rama's ring, engraved with his name.

Sita receives the token with joy. She gives Hanuman a jewel from her hair to take back as proof, and he returns across the sea. Rama weeps over the jewel, recognising it as the gift Janaka gave Sita at her wedding.

Analysis

Dutt's eighth book corresponds to the Sundarakāṇḍa ('the beautiful book'), long among the most loved and recited parts of the epic. Its hero is Hanuman, whose leap over the ocean, search of Lanka and burning of the city (reported in Dutt only by Ravana in the next book) made him a figure of devotion in his own right; Lutgendorf traces his growth from Vālmīki's clever minister to a major god of popular Hinduism (Lutgendorf 2007).

The exchange of tokens, a ring for a jewel, joins the separated lovers through objects. Sita's recovery of hope is described through the image of the moon freed from eclipse by Rāhu, the demon who swallows the moon; the poem regularly casts Sita as the moon and Ravana as darkness.

Book 9: The Council of War

Summary

After Hanuman has burned his city, Ravana summons his council and asks for advice. His generals Prahasta, Durmukha, Vajradaṃṣṭra, Nikumbha and Vajrahanu boast that they will destroy the 'homeless hermits' and their monkeys. Only his younger brother Vibhishana warns that Rama is righteous and that Sita must be returned. Kumbhakarna, roused from his long sleep, agrees that Ravana acted foolishly, but declares that he will fight for him; Indrajit, Ravana's son, scorns Vibhishana as a coward.

Ravana turns furiously on Vibhishana as a faithless kinsman. Vibhishana replies that Ravana is surrounded by flatterers and is rushing to his doom, and leaves Lanka to join Rama.

Analysis

This book is a sequence of speeches, a political debate about war. The poem gives Ravana's court real grandeur: Ravana opens by setting out a theory of good counsel, and the rākṣasa warriors speak with the courage of epic heroes. Ravana is a learned and powerful king, a devotee of Śiva, undone by desire and pride rather than mere wickedness.

Vibhishana and Kumbhakarna represent two forms of loyalty. Vibhishana places righteousness above kinship and defects; Kumbhakarna admits his brother's fault yet fights for his family. Later traditions have judged them differently: Vibhishana is a model devotee for some, a traitor to his own people for others, and in some southern Indian readings Ravana himself becomes a sympathetic figure (Richman 1991).

Book 10: The War in Ceylon

Summary

Rama's army besieges Lanka. Indrajit, fighting invisibly, binds Rama and Lakshmana with serpent-arrows, and Ravana has Sita shown her husband apparently dead. The brothers recover, and one by one the rākṣasa champions fall. Kumbhakarna, the giant, devours monkeys by the hundred before Rama kills him. Indrajit creates an illusory Sita and kills her before the monkeys; at last Lakshmana, with Vibhishana's help, kills him. Ravana mourns his son and goes out to battle himself, striking Lakshmana down with a javelin; Rama laments his brother, who is saved by Hanuman's healing herbs.

Indra sends Rama his own chariot, driven by Mātali. In the final duel the two heroes fight until Rama kills Ravana with a celestial weapon. Ravana's chief queen Mandodarī laments over him, and Vibhishana performs his brother's funeral rites.

Analysis

The Yuddhakāṇḍa is the epic's battle book, full of magical weapons, illusions and single combats. Dutt selects its most famous moments, including Rama's lament for Lakshmana, which shows that his deepest attachment is to his brother. Indrajit's illusions continue the theme of deceptive appearance that began with the golden deer.

Mandodarī's lament is significant for the question of Rama's divinity. She realises that no mere man could have killed her husband, and identifies Rama with Viṣṇu. Pollock argues that the epic's logic requires such a figure: Ravana had obtained a boon making him invulnerable to gods and demons, but he had disdained to ask for protection from men, so only a god in human form could kill him (Pollock 1984) (Pollock 1991).

Book 11: Rama's Return and Consecration

Summary

When Sita is brought to Rama, he receives her coldly: she has lived in Ravana's house, and rumour stains a woman's name. Sita protests her innocence, asks why he troubled to rescue her if he doubted her, and asks Lakshmana to build a pyre. She calls the fire to witness her purity and enters it. The fire god Agni rises with Sita unharmed and declares her faithful, and Rama accepts her.

Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and their allies return to Ayodhya in the flying chariot Puṣpaka, and Rama points out to Sita the places of their adventures. Bharata welcomes them with festivity, and Rama is consecrated king with water from every sacred river.

Analysis

The fire ordeal (agniparīkṣā) is one of the most discussed scenes in Indian literature. Rama's rejection of Sita can be read as a king's concern for public reputation, as a test he never doubted she would pass, or as a cruel betrayal; Sita's reply, full of indignation, is one of the strongest speeches given to a woman in the epic. Hess surveys the long history of Indian discomfort with this scene and with Sita's later banishment (Hess 1999).

In Vālmīki the gods appear after the ordeal and reveal to Rama that he is Viṣṇu (Rām. 6.105), one of the passages scholars have suspected of being added. The consecration that follows inaugurates Rāmarājya, the reign of Rama, a byword in India for perfect government; Mahatma Gandhi used it as an image of a just and peaceful self-rule.

Book 12: Sacrifice of the Horse

Summary

Years later, Rama reigns alone in a joyless palace; Sita has been banished. He holds a horse sacrifice (aśvamedha) in the Naimiṣa forest. Vālmīki attends with two young pupils, Lava and Kuśa, whom he tells to sing the Rāmāyaṇa everywhere, and at last before Rama himself. The court listens in wonder, and Rama realises that the singers are his own sons, born to Sita in the forest.

Rama sends for Sita, asking her to prove her purity before the assembly. Vālmīki vouches for her, and Rama admits that he banished her to satisfy his subjects. Sita appears, sees her husband and sons, and calls on the Earth to receive her if she has been faithful. The ground opens; a golden throne rises, borne by serpents, and the Earth takes her daughter back.

Analysis

Dutt's last book draws on the Uttarakāṇḍa. He does not narrate the banishment itself, which in Vālmīki follows the report of gossip among the citizens about Sita's stay with Ravana; Rama's own confession here supplies it. Other parts of the Uttarakāṇḍa, such as the origin of the rākṣasas and Rama's killing of the śūdra ascetic Śambūka, are left out (Goldman 2017).

The ending closes the circle opened in the first book. Vālmīki, the poet, now appears as a character in his own poem, and the poem is sung before its hero, who learns his own story from his unrecognised sons. Sita, born from the furrow, returns to the Earth. Her final act is a refusal of a second public trial, and many readers find in it an indictment of Rama's choice of public reputation over private love. The epic ends not in triumph but in loss.

Characters

Ayodhya

Rama
Eldest son of Dasharatha and Kausalya, the hero of the epic. He is the model of the dutiful son, loyal brother, devoted husband and just king, who accepts exile rather than let his father break his word. Yet his killing of Vali from ambush and his treatment of Sita after the war have troubled readers for centuries. In the central books he is mainly a human hero; in the first and last books, and in later devotional tradition, he is an avatar of Viṣṇu.
Sita
Daughter of Janaka, found in a furrow and so born of the Earth; wife of Rama. She insists on sharing his exile, rejects Ravana throughout her captivity, passes the ordeal by fire, and finally, banished and asked for proof again, returns to the Earth. She became the classic Indian model of the faithful wife, and is also read as a figure of quiet resistance and moral authority.
Lakshmana
Son of Sumitra and Rama's devoted younger brother, who follows him into exile and serves him constantly. Quick to anger, he mutilates Shurpanakha and kills Indrajit. His leaving Sita alone, under pressure from her reproaches, makes the abduction possible.
Bharata
Son of Kaikeyi. Absent when his mother secures him the throne, he rejects it, seeks Rama in the forest, and rules for fourteen years as regent in Rama's name, with Rama's sandals on the throne. He is the epic's model of loyalty without ambition.
Dasharatha
King of Kosala, ruling from Ayodhya. Bound by the boons he once promised Kaikeyi, he must exile his favourite son, and he dies of grief, remembering his own youthful killing of a hermit's son.
Kaikeyi
Dasharatha's youngest queen and Bharata's mother. Persuaded by her maid Manthara, she claims two boons to have Rama exiled and Bharata crowned. Her son rejects her, and she became a byword for the scheming stepmother, though some later versions try to excuse her.
Kausalya
Dasharatha's senior queen and Rama's mother, who grieves bitterly at his exile and blesses Sita as she leaves.
Manthara
Kaikeyi's hunchbacked old maid, who persuades her mistress that Rama's elevation will ruin her and her son.
Janaka
King of Videha in Mithila and Sita's father, who found her in a furrow while ploughing and promised her to whoever could bend Śiva's bow; a royal sage famous for wisdom.

Monkeys and bears

Hanuman
Son of the wind god and counsellor of Sugriva. He leaps over the ocean, finds Sita, burns Lanka and brings healing herbs from the Himalaya. Clever, eloquent and devoted, he became one of the most popular gods of Hinduism, worshipped as the ideal servant of Rama.
Sugriva
Monkey king exiled by his brother Vali, who also took his wife. Rama kills Vali for him, and in return Sugriva commands the monkey army in the search for Sita and the war.
Vali
(Dutt: Bali) Sugriva's elder brother and king of Kiṣkindhā, a mighty warrior. Rama shoots him from hiding while he fights Sugriva. His dying reproach in Vālmīki raises the epic's most famous ethical question about Rama.
Tara
Vali's wise wife, who warns him not to answer Sugriva's challenge, laments him, and later becomes Sugriva's queen.
Angada
Vali's son, made heir of Kiṣkindhā; a leader of the search for Sita and a hero in the war.
Jambavan
The aged king of the bears, an adviser in Rama's army, who in Vālmīki encourages Hanuman to make his leap.

Lanka

Ravana
(Dutt: Ravan) The ten-headed king of the rākṣasas, ruling from Lanka. Learned, powerful and protected by a boon against gods and demons, he abducts Sita in revenge for his sister's mutilation and out of desire. His grandeur makes him a tragic antagonist, and in some later traditions a sympathetic hero.
Vibhishana
(Dutt: Bibhishan) Ravana's righteous younger brother, who urges him to return Sita, is insulted, and goes over to Rama. After Ravana's death he becomes king of Lanka.
Shurpanakha
(Dutt: Surpa-nakha) Ravana's sister, whose advances to Rama and Lakshmana end with Lakshmana cutting off her nose and ears. Her complaint to Ravana sets the abduction in motion.
Kumbhakarna
Ravana's gigantic brother, who sleeps for months at a time. He blames Ravana's folly, but fights for him out of loyalty and is killed by Rama.
Indrajit
Ravana's son, a master of magic and illusion who once defeated Indra (hence his name). He nearly kills Rama and Lakshmana and is finally killed by Lakshmana.
Mandodari
Ravana's chief queen, whose lament over his body recognises Rama as divine.

Sages and gods

Valmiki
The sage credited with the poem, the 'first poet'. He discovers the śloka in his grief at the killing of a krauñca bird, gives shelter to the banished Sita, and teaches the epic to Lava and Kuśa.
Vishvamitra
(Dutt: Viswa-mitra) A royal sage who takes the young Rama and Lakshmana to fight demons and brings them to Mithila for the bow contest.
Agastya
A great sage of the southern forests, who welcomes the exiles, praises Sita and directs them to Pañcavaṭī.
Jabali
A Brahman who urges Rama, with sceptical arguments, to give up his vow and return to Ayodhya.
Agni
The god of fire, 'witness of our sins and virtues', who returns Sita unharmed from the pyre and declares her pure.

Themes

Dharma and the kept word

Dharma, a word meaning law, duty, righteousness and the order of things, is the poem's central value. Its demands are often in conflict: Dasharatha must choose between his promise and his son, Rama between obedience and the claims of his mother and brothers, Vibhishana between righteousness and kinship. Rama consistently gives priority to truth (satya), the keeping of one's word, even at great cost.

The poem does not present dharma as simple. Jābāli's sceptical argument, Vali's reproach and Sita's protest at the ordeal are all given eloquent voice. The epic's continuing power partly comes from the unresolved tension between Rama's commitment to dharma and the suffering it causes.

Kingship and Rāmarājya

The Rāmāyaṇa is a political poem about legitimate rule. It opens with an ideal Ayodhya, shows a succession crisis caused by desire within the royal household, and ends with Rama's reign as a golden age. Bharata ruling in the name of the sandals models a power exercised in trust. Rama's banishment of Sita to satisfy his subjects shows the cost of a king who places public duty above private happiness.

Pollock argues that the epic constructs the ideal of a divine king who protects order against demonic forces, and that this image has been used repeatedly in Indian political history (Pollock 1984) (Pollock 1993).

Exile and the forest

Most of the poem takes place in the forest, a world of hermits, demons and animals, outside the order of the city. The exile purifies and tests the heroes: Rama and Lakshmana take on the dress of ascetics, and Sita shares their hardships. The forest is also where boundaries break down, as when demons take on human or animal forms and a king's wife can be carried off.

Love, chastity and suspicion

The love of Rama and Sita is the emotional centre of the poem, expressed in Rama's laments at her loss and her steadfastness in captivity. But it is overshadowed by the question of Sita's chastity, which she must prove twice. The poem's treatment of Sita as the model wife has been both celebrated and criticised; it also shows her as articulate, resolute and, finally, defiant (Sutherland 1989) (Hess 1999).

The divine in human form

Rama acts mainly as a human hero, grieving and making mistakes, but he is also presented as an incarnation of Viṣṇu who has come to destroy Ravana. The relationship between these two aspects is one of the great questions of the poem and of its later devotional tradition, in which the story became a form of worship.

Motifs

Tokens and recognitions

The poem advances through objects that stand for absent people: Rama's sandals on the throne, his ring given to Sita, her jewel sent to Rama. At the end, recognition comes through a song, when Rama knows his sons as they sing his story.

Boons and curses

Kaikeyi's boons start the exile, the curse of the blind hermit causes Dasharatha's death, and Ravana's boon of invulnerability to gods and demons explains why he must be killed by a man. Words once spoken have binding power in the epic.

Illusion (māyā)

Demons constantly change their form: Mārīca as a golden deer, Ravana as a holy man, Indrajit fighting invisibly and creating a false Sita. Appearances in the poem are dangerous, and much depends on seeing through them.

Pairs of brothers

The epic is full of pairs of brothers: Rama and Bharata compete in renunciation, Vali and Sugriva fight over a throne and a wife, Ravana and Vibhishana divide over righteousness. Each pair illuminates a different face of kinship and loyalty.

Seasons and landscape

Descriptions of the seasons, such as the winter at Pañcavaṭī and the monsoon at Kiṣkindhā, reflect the characters' moods and became models for later Sanskrit court poetry.

Symbols

The bow

Rama's breaking of Śiva's bow wins Sita and marks him as a hero beyond ordinary men; the bow remains his emblem, and his arrows kill Vali and Ravana.

The golden deer

Beautiful, illusory and fatal, the deer stands for desire for what is false, and has become a proverbial image in Indian languages.

Fire

Agni witnesses Rama and Sita's wedding and the fire ordeal, which proves her purity. Fire both tests and protects.

The Earth

Sita is born from a furrow and returns into the ground. The Earth is her mother and final refuge, and links her to fertility, patience and endurance.

The sandals

Placed on the throne of Ayodhya, Rama's sandals represent legitimate kingship exercised in absence and loyalty without ambition.

Critical debates

The growth of the epic

Since the nineteenth century, scholars have argued that the Rāmāyaṇa grew over a long period. Hermann Jacobi's Das Rāmāyaṇa (1893) set out the view that Books 2–6 form the original poem, with Books 1 and 7 added later, and that the Viṣṇu passages are additions; his arguments rested on differences of style, the two tables of contents in Book 1, and the way Rama's divinity is treated (Jacobi 1893).

John Brockington refined the analysis into several stages of growth, using linguistic and stylistic criteria, from an oral core to the later expansions and the regional recensions (Brockington 1984) (Brockington 1998). Goldman accepts the priority of Books 2–6 but stresses the poem's artistic unity as it has come down to us (Goldman 2017).

Rama's divinity

Is Rama a man or a god in Vālmīki? The view that he becomes divine only in the later books has been challenged. Sheldon Pollock argues that the epic's whole plot requires a divine king: Ravana's boon makes him invulnerable to every being except man, so that only a god in human form can destroy him. The figure of Rama thus expresses an ancient Indian ideology of kingship as divine (Pollock 1984) (Pollock 1991).

Others still read the core books as presenting a human hero whose divinity was progressively emphasised, especially in devotional retellings such as Tulsidas's Rāmcaritmānas (Lutgendorf 1991).

Sita and gender

Sita has long been held up as the model of the devoted wife, and this has made her a central figure in debates about gender in India. Sally Sutherland (later Sutherland Goldman) contrasts her with Draupadī of the Mahābhārata and argues that Sita turns her anger inward rather than against her husband, a pattern that the culture held up as ideal (Sutherland 1989).

Linda Hess traces the long history of Indian discomfort with Rama's treatment of Sita, from poets who soften or omit the banishment to modern audiences who openly criticise it (Hess 1999). Feminist retellings in Indian languages have made Sita a figure of resistance (Richman 2001).

The killing of Vali

Rama shoots Vali from hiding while he fights his brother, an act that seems to violate the warrior's code. In Vālmīki the dying Vali accuses Rama of unrighteous conduct, and Rama answers that Vali had taken his brother's wife, that a king may punish crimes in his territory, and that animals may lawfully be hunted from concealment (Rām. 4.17–18) (Lefeber 1994).

Commentators and later poets have found these answers inadequate or have tried to supplement them; some versions explain that Vali had a boon that let him absorb the strength of anyone who faced him. The episode remains a test case for whether the epic's ideal hero can do wrong.

The many Ramayanas

The Rāmāyaṇa exists in hundreds of tellings in Indian and Southeast Asian languages, in literature, theatre, painting and ritual. Among the best known are Kampaṉ's Tamil Irāmāvatāram (perhaps twelfth century), Tulsidas's Hindi Rāmcaritmānas (sixteenth century), and versions in Thailand (Ramakien), Cambodia, Laos, Java and Bali (Hart 1988) (Lutgendorf 1991) (Iyengar 1983).

A. K. Ramanujan's essay 'Three Hundred Rāmāyaṇas: Five Examples and Three Thoughts on Translation' argued that these are not simply versions of Vālmīki's 'original' but distinct tellings, each shaped by its own culture, drawing on a common pool of story (Ramanujan 1991) (Richman 1991). In 2011 the University of Delhi removed the essay from a history reading list after protests by some who objected to its account of variant tellings, which provoked a public debate about scholarship and religious sentiment. The essay is reprinted in Ramanujan's collected essays (Ramanujan 1999).

The Ramayana and political imagination

In 1993, after the destruction of the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya, Sheldon Pollock argued that the Rāmāyaṇa had served for centuries as a political resource: from the twelfth century, kings in India used the image of Rama and the 'othering' of the demonic enemy to represent their wars and their legitimacy (Pollock 1993).

In the late 1980s the hugely successful television serial Ramayan, directed by Ramanand Sagar, gave the story a new national audience; Lutgendorf studied its reception (Lutgendorf 1990). The epic's role in modern politics, including the movement for a temple at Rama's supposed birthplace, remains contested.

The Uttarakāṇḍa

The seventh book is widely regarded as a later addition, but it contains the episodes that most trouble modern readers: the banishment of Sita on the basis of gossip and the killing of the śūdra ascetic Śambūka for practising austerities forbidden to his class. Goldman and Sutherland Goldman, in their translation, discuss both its late position in the textual history and its place in the poem's reception (Goldman 2017).

Some traditions omit or alter the book; Tulsidas, for example, ends with Rama's happy reign. Others find in it the poem's deepest moral seriousness, since it shows the costs of the ideal kingship that the earlier books celebrate (Richman 2001).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. Why does the Rāmāyaṇa begin with the story of how it was composed, and what does the legend of the krauñca birds suggest about the nature of poetry?

    Consider the link between śoka (grief) and śloka (verse), and the fact that the poem is later sung by Rama's sons before Rama himself.

  2. Is Rama right to obey his father's command to go into exile? What does his decision reveal about the epic's idea of dharma?

    Consider Dasharatha's promise, Kausalya's grief, Lakshmana's anger and Jābāli's argument; think about the value of truth (satya).

  3. How does the poem portray Kaikeyi? Is she simply a villain?

    Look at her first reaction to the news of Rama's consecration and at the role of Manthara.

  4. Compare the brothers Rama and Bharata with the brothers Vali and Sugriva and Ravana and Vibhishana. What does each pair show about kinship and loyalty?

    Consider competition in renunciation versus violence, and the question of where loyalty is owed.

  5. Can Rama's killing of Vali be justified?

    Consider Vali's offence against Sugriva, Rama's promise, the warrior's code and Rama's arguments in Vālmīki (Rām. 4.17–18), which Dutt omits.

  6. How does the epic present Ravana? Is he a tragic figure?

    Think about his learning and power, his council of war, his grief for Indrajit and Mandodarī's lament.

  7. What is the role of Hanuman in the poem, and why might he have become an object of devotion?

    Consider his leap, his eloquence with Sita and his total devotion to Rama.

  8. How should we read Sita's fire ordeal? Is Rama's behaviour cruel, prudent or something else?

    Consider the role of rumour and public opinion, Sita's own words, and the comparison with her banishment and final return to the Earth.

  9. In what sense is Rāmarājya an ideal, and what does the Uttarakāṇḍa suggest about its costs?

    Compare the description of Ayodhya in Book I with Rama's joyless palace and his confession in Book XII.

  10. What does Dutt's condensed version leave out, and how do his choices shape our understanding of the epic?

    Compare with the summary of the seven kāṇḍas; consider the frame story, Vali's reproach, the burning of Lanka and the banishment of Sita, and look at Griffith's fuller translation on this site.
