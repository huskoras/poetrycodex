The Ring and the Book

by Robert Browning

Overview

Author
Robert Browning (1812–1889)
Published
Four volumes, November 1868 to February 1869, by Smith, Elder and Co., London
Form
Twelve books of blank verse: ten dramatic monologues framed by two books in the poet's own voice
Length
Some 21,000 lines, Browning's longest poem
Source
The 'Old Yellow Book', a bound collection of printed legal pleadings and manuscript letters from a Roman murder trial, bought by Browning on a market stall in Florence in June 1860
Historical events
The murder of Pompilia Comparini and her parents by her husband, Count Guido Franceschini, in Rome on 2 January 1698, and his execution on 22 February 1698
Setting
Rome and Arezzo in the 1690s; the frame is Florence and London in the 1860s
Speakers
Half-Rome, the Other Half-Rome, Tertium Quid, Guido (twice), Caponsacchi, Pompilia, the lawyers Arcangeli and Bottini, Pope Innocent XII, and the poet
Governing image
The ring: gold mixed with alloy so that it can be worked, then purified with acid, as the poet's imagination is mixed with the facts and then withdrawn
Text quoted
The Cambridge Edition of Browning's Complete Poetic and Dramatic Works (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1895); references give book and line

Context

In June 1860, crossing the Piazza San Lorenzo in Florence, Robert Browning picked up from a second-hand stall a vellum-bound quarto for one lira. It was a collection, assembled by a Florentine lawyer, of printed pleadings, legal summaries and a few manuscript letters from a Roman criminal trial of 1698. Browning read it, he later claimed, as he walked home to Casa Guidi, and it became the 'Old Yellow Book' of his poem. A year later Elizabeth Barrett Browning died in Florence, and Browning returned to London with their son. He set the story aside for some years and only in the mid-1860s began the poem in earnest. It appeared in four volumes between November 1868 and February 1869, when Browning was in his late fifties (Browning 1971) (DeVane 1955).

The case itself was sordid and sensational. Guido Franceschini, an impoverished middle-aged nobleman of Arezzo in minor religious orders, married Pompilia, the thirteen-year-old daughter (as everyone believed) of the elderly Roman couple Pietro and Violante Comparini, in 1693, each side hoping to gain by the match. The marriage turned to misery. The Comparini returned to Rome, and Violante then declared that Pompilia was not her child at all but the baby of a prostitute, bought and passed off as her own to secure an inheritance. In April 1697 Pompilia fled from Arezzo with a young priest, Giuseppe Caponsacchi; Guido overtook them at Castelnuovo, near Rome, and a court sent Caponsacchi into a mild exile and Pompilia to a convent and then to her parents' house, where she gave birth to a son. On the night of 2 January 1698 Guido and four hired men from his estate gained entrance to the house, killed Pietro and Violante, and stabbed Pompilia, who lived four days and testified against him. Guido pleaded the honour of a wronged husband and, as a cleric, appealed to the Pope; Innocent XII refused clemency, and Guido was beheaded in the Piazza del Popolo on 22 February 1698 (Hodell 1908) (Gest 1925) (Altick 1968).

Browning's great formal decision was to tell this story not once but ten times. After an opening book in which the poet introduces his source, his method and his speakers, the case is narrated in turn by three representative Roman gossips, by the murderer, by the priest, by the dying wife, by the two lawyers, by the Pope, and by the murderer again on the night before his execution; a closing book gathers up the aftermath. Each book is a dramatic monologue, the form Browning had perfected in Men and Women (1855) and Dramatis Personae (1864), but here the monologues interlock, contradict one another and gradually build a picture which no single speaker possesses. The result has often been called a novel in verse, and Henry James later imagined the novel he would have made of it (James 1914) (Langbaum 1957).

The poem is also a meditation on how the past can be known at all. In the 1860s historical criticism was unsettling confidence in the written record, including the record of the Gospels, and Browning's poem keeps asking what a 'fact' is, how testimony distorts, and whether imagination can bring dead truth back to life. The opening image of the ring, gold mixed with alloy to be worked and then purified, is Browning's answer: the facts of the book are the gold, the poet's fancy is the alloy, and the finished poem should leave the truth standing on its own (Armstrong 1993) (Buckler 1985).

The Ring and the Book made Browning's reputation. After decades in which his poetry had been thought wilfully obscure, reviewers greeted it with something like awe; Robert Buchanan in the Athenaeum ranked it among the greatest achievements of English literature since Shakespeare. Browning became a public sage, the Browning Society was founded in 1881, and the poem was read throughout the late Victorian period as a work of moral and religious wisdom. Twentieth-century criticism, more interested in irony and point of view, turned it into a test case for the dramatic monologue and for questions of truth and interpretation (Litzinger 1970) (Langbaum 1957) (Ryals 1993).

Plot overview

Book I, 'The Ring and the Book', presents the poet holding up a gold ring of Etruscan design and the Old Yellow Book. He explains the goldsmith's method that gives the poem its title, tells how he found the book in Florence, summarises the murder case, and introduces in turn each of the voices to follow. The book ends with an invocation to his dead wife, the 'lyric Love' whose blessing he asks.

Books II to IV give the opinion of Rome. Half-Rome, a married man with suspicions of his own, sides with Guido as a husband wronged by his wife and a priest. The Other Half-Rome, a bachelor, takes Pompilia's part with sentimental warmth. Tertium Quid, 'the third something', a man of fashion addressing a Prince and an Excellency at an evening party, weighs both sides with cool cynicism and refuses to decide. Each tells the whole story from courtship to murder, and each reveals himself as much as the case.

Books V to VII bring the three principals before us. Guido, just released from torture, addresses the court with suave self-pity, presenting himself as a poor nobleman cheated by greedy in-laws and dishonoured by his wife. Caponsacchi, recalled from exile, tells the judges passionately how he came to rescue Pompilia and accuses them of failing her. Pompilia, dying of her wounds, tells her own life in simple, luminous terms: her childhood, the marriage she did not understand, her terror of Guido, her rescue by the priest she calls a soldier of God, and the birth of her son, Gaetano.

Books VIII to XII move to judgement. The defence lawyer Arcangeli drafts his Latin plea for Guido while thinking about his son's birthday dinner; the prosecutor Bottini rehearses an ornate speech that defends Pompilia while half assuming her guilt. The aged Pope Innocent XII, alone on a winter evening, reviews the case and the moral world it reveals, and resolves that Guido shall die. In Book XI Guido, condemned, talks through the night to two churchmen, drops his earlier pose, rages against the world and its faith, and breaks down as the executioners arrive. Book XII relates the execution and its aftermath through letters and a sermon, and the poet closes by drawing the lesson of the ring.

Book by book

Book 1: The Ring and the Book

Summary

The poet holds up a ring made in Rome by the goldsmith Castellani in imitation of Etruscan work and explains the craft behind it. Pure gold is too soft to shape, so the craftsman mixes it with alloy, works the ring, and then washes it with acid, which carries off the alloy and leaves the gold alone in its new form. He then shows the Old Yellow Book, tells how he found it on a stall in the Piazza San Lorenzo in Florence and read it through on his way home, describes its contents, recounts the case, and tells how, on a night at Casa Guidi, the story came alive for him.

The rest of the book previews the poem. Browning outlines how the case was argued and settled, and introduces each of the speakers to come with a short characterising portrait, from Half-Rome to Guido in his cell. He addresses the 'British Public', who, he admits, do not like him, and promises a truth that will stand by itself once his part in shaping it is removed. The book ends with a famous invocation to his dead wife, Elizabeth Barrett Browning, as 'lyric Love', asking her blessing on the work.

Analysis

Book I is both preface and argument. The ring metaphor makes a claim about poetry and history: raw facts cannot be shaped into a living form without the poet's imagination, yet the aim is a truth from which that imagination can finally be withdrawn. The metaphor is less stable than it looks. The poet admits that his 'fancy' has passed into the facts, and that 'fancy with fact is just one fact the more', which suggests that the alloy can never be wholly removed (Buckler 1985) (Armstrong 1993).

The book also sets up the poem's peculiar contract with its readers. By telling the whole story and its outcome at once, Browning removes suspense about what happened and directs attention to how it is told and judged. The speaker presents himself with a showman's energy, tossing the book in the air, and with a claim to an almost necromantic power over the dead. The closing invocation to Elizabeth Barrett Browning, written years after her death, frames the whole enterprise as an act of love and mourning as well as of art.

Book 2: Half-Rome

Summary

The day after the murder, a Roman citizen catches an acquaintance in the crowd at the church of San Lorenzo in Lucina, where the bodies of Pietro and Violante are displayed, and offers to tell him the true story. His version is Guido's: an honourable but poor nobleman, tricked into marriage by the scheming Comparini, who then revealed that Pompilia was not even their child; a wife who wrote love letters to a dashing priest and ran away with him; courts that gave the husband no redress. In this telling the murder is a grievous but understandable act of a man pushed past endurance.

The speaker dwells on what he sees as the scandal: the elopement, the letters found at the inn at Castelnuovo, the light sentences passed on the pair, and the timing of Pompilia's pregnancy. At the end it emerges that he has a personal interest. His listener is the cousin of a man who has been paying too much attention to the speaker's own wife, and he wants a warning passed on: the husband's rod hangs ready behind the door.

Analysis

Half-Rome demonstrates one of the poem's central insights, that opinion is shaped by interest. His sympathy for Guido is the sympathy of a jealous husband, and the final twist reveals the whole monologue as an indirect threat. His portrait of Pompilia as a calculating adulteress, and of Caponsacchi as a frivolous gallant, will be overturned by later books, but the reader first meets the facts through his distortion (Sullivan 1969).

The style establishes the social range of the monologues: colloquial, digressive, knowing, full of proverbs and asides. Browning lets the speaker be shrewd and lively as well as prejudiced. The book therefore asks readers to judge the judges, and to notice that accurate details (the letters, the flight) can be arranged into a false story.

Book 3: The Other Half-Rome

Summary

A second speaker, a bachelor, tells the story on the following day, while Pompilia still lies dying at the hospital of Saint Anna and Rome crowds the gate for news. His sympathy is entirely with her. He describes the Comparini as foolish but kindly old people, Violante's deception as a sin committed partly out of love, and the marriage as a cold transaction in which a child was sold to a needy, ageing aristocrat. Guido's household at Arezzo appears as a place of meanness and cruelty.

He presents Pompilia's flight with Caponsacchi as a rescue rather than a seduction, doubts the love letters, since Pompilia could neither read nor write, and ends with the murder, the flight of the killers and their capture near Baccano. His sympathy is generous but sentimental, and he too has his reasons for telling the story as he does.

Analysis

The Other Half-Rome answers Half-Rome, but Browning does not make him a reliable narrator. His pity is attractive, yet it rests as much on Pompilia's pathos (her youth, her patient smile, her wounds) as on evidence, and he too reshapes facts to fit a story. Setting two opposed versions side by side shows how much of 'fact' is selection and emphasis (Sullivan 1969) (Langbaum 1957).

Several points that later books will confirm first appear here, among them Pompilia's illiteracy and therefore the forgery of the letters. The book also introduces the question of the flower and the weed, of what Pompilia really is, which recurs through the poem until the Pope calls her his rose.

Book 4: Tertium Quid

Summary

The third Roman speaker, a man of the world, addresses a Prince and an Excellency at an evening reception, hoping to impress them. He offers a cool, educated account of the case, above the passions of the street, and sets out each party's interests in turn: the Comparini's vanity and greed, Guido's poverty and pride, Pompilia's ambiguous conduct, Caponsacchi's doubtful motives.

He weighs every argument and reaches no conclusion, inviting his noble audience to decide and implying that the truth is probably a balance of faults. As his listeners drift away to the card tables, he is left muttering to himself that his performance has won him nothing.

Analysis

Tertium Quid represents a kind of sophistication that Browning distrusts: a balanced, worldly neutrality that sees everyone's interest and no one's goodness. His refusal to judge is not wisdom but social calculation, and his monologue shows that detachment can distort as much as partisanship. He is the cleverest of the three Roman voices and the least capable of sympathy (Sullivan 1969).

The book completes the panorama of public opinion and leads into the principals. Its ending, in which the aristocrats lose interest and he is left complaining, is one of the poem's sharpest comic moments, and a reminder that for fashionable Rome the case is chiefly entertainment.

Book 5: Count Guido Franceschini

Summary

Guido speaks before the judges after being tortured with the cord, and begins with ironic courtesy, thanking them for the wine and remarking that bodily pain is nothing beside the humiliation he has endured. He tells his story with great skill: the decline of his ancient family, his long and fruitless service in Rome, his marriage as a fair bargain, the Comparini's fraud, his wife's disobedience and her flight with the priest, and the courts' failure to give him justice.

He presents the murder as the act of a wronged husband who took the law into his own hands only when the law failed him, and appeals to the code of honour, to the Church, and to the judges' fellow-feeling as husbands. He ends by imagining himself acquitted, telling his son how he dared to trust God and the law, and passing off the torture as a small accident in the search for truth.

Analysis

Guido's first monologue is a brilliant piece of persuasion. He uses irony, humility, wit and an air of candour, admitting small faults to gain credit for honesty. Readers who have heard the two halves of Rome may find him more plausible than they expected, and that is Browning's point: the murderer's eloquence is a test of the reader's moral judgement (Sullivan 1969) (Karlin 1993).

The mask slips repeatedly. His contempt for Pompilia shows through his phrasing, his account of the letters is evasive, and his jaunty closing reference to the torture irons hints at the cruelty beneath his civility. The book prepares for Book XI, where the same man, with nothing left to gain, will speak without disguise.

Book 6: Giuseppe Caponsacchi

Summary

Caponsacchi, recalled from his relegation at Civita Vecchia to testify after the murder, addresses the same judges who earlier treated his flight with Pompilia as a minor scandal. He describes his former life as a worldly young priest of a good Arezzo family, writing verses and paying compliments to ladies, and the night at the theatre when he first saw Pompilia. He insists that he never wrote or received love letters and explains how a forged correspondence was used to trap them both.

He then tells how Pompilia appealed to him from her window to save her, how he resolved to take her to Rome, and the long night journey that ended at the inn at Castelnuovo, where Guido overtook them. He rebukes the judges bitterly for having failed her, and ends with a cry of anguish at her coming death.

Analysis

Caponsacchi's monologue is the most passionate in the poem. His rescue of Pompilia is told as a spiritual conversion: the courtly priest discovers in her a call to real service, and the flight takes on the colours of chivalric romance and the legend of Saint George. Browning makes him the figure the Pope will call a 'warrior-priest', who breaks the rules for a higher good (Sullivan 1969).

His love for Pompilia is evident, and readers have long debated how far it is pure devotion and how far suppressed desire. Browning leaves the tension visible, above all in the last line, where praise of God turns into personal grief. Caponsacchi's anger at the court's caution is also a criticism of institutions that weigh appearances above goodness.

Book 7: Pompilia

Summary

Pompilia, dying of her many wounds, speaks to the nuns, the friar and the people around her bed. She begins with her exact age and her baptismal record, rejoices that her baby son Gaetano was born, baptised and hidden in time, and tells her life simply: her happy childhood with the Comparini, her marriage at thirteen without understanding what it meant, the shock of learning that her parents were not her parents, and the cruelty of Guido and his household at Arezzo.

She describes how she turned to Caponsacchi, how he came to rescue her, and the night of the flight; she forgives Guido and asks that no one misjudge the priest. Her monologue ends in an act of faith, as she imagines Caponsacchi as a soldier of God and herself rising towards God's light.

Analysis

Pompilia's monologue is the emotional centre of the poem. Her voice is unlearned and innocent, yet she understands more than the clever speakers do: that the sale of a child into marriage was a wrong, that obedience has limits, and that her coming motherhood gave her the strength to resist. She sees goodness clearly where others see only scandal, and her forgiveness of Guido goes beyond the code of honour that governs everyone else (Brady 1988).

Critics have argued about whether Browning makes her too saintly. Her innocence can look like a male idealisation of woman as pure victim, and she is unusual among Browning's speakers in having almost no ironic distance from herself. Others point out that her monologue is full of practical intelligence and of anger, and that she alone refuses the language of property and contract in which the rest of the poem speaks (Brady 1988) (Armstrong 1993).

Book 8: Dominus Hyacinthus de Archangelis, Pauperum Procurator

Summary

Arcangeli, the Procurator of the Poor assigned to defend Guido, sits at home composing his Latin plea on the day of his little son Giacinto's eighth birthday. His thoughts move constantly between the case and the family feast that evening, and his pride in the boy's progress in Latin.

His plea argues that Guido acted in defence of his honour, which in law could excuse the killing of an adulterous wife; that the hiring of accomplices and the savagery of the deed do not alter this; and that the court should show mercy. He polishes his phrases with relish, more interested in the display of learning than in the people involved.

Analysis

The two lawyer books are the poem's comedy, and many readers have found them tedious. Their function is satirical. Arcangeli, an affectionate father and a gourmand, is no villain, but he turns murder into an occasion for professional cleverness and dinner. The juxtaposition of his tenderness for his own son with his defence of a man who killed the mother of his son is quietly ironic (Altick 1968).

Browning's Latin-studded legal rhetoric draws on the real pleadings in the Old Yellow Book. The book suggests how far the law's procedures are from moral truth, yet Arcangeli's case is not absurd: the 'honour' defence was a serious legal position, and the book shows how human law could legitimise violence against women.

Book 9: Juris Doctor Johannes-Baptista Bottinius, Fisci et Rev. Cam. Apostol. Advocatus

Summary

Bottini, advocate of the Fisc and the Apostolic Chamber and so Guido's prosecutor, rehearses alone the oration he wishes he could deliver rather than merely print. He is a vain stylist who loves elaborate comparisons and classical ornament, and he imagines his performance as a work of art.

Paradoxically, though he prosecutes Guido, his speech assumes the worst of Pompilia. He argues that even if she had been unchaste Guido would not have been entitled to kill her, and he explains away the evidence of her intimacy with the priest by a string of ingenious excuses, defending her in terms that blacken her.

Analysis

Bottini is the more unpleasant of the two lawyers. Where Arcangeli is genial, Bottini is cold, fastidious and self-admiring, and he shows no real interest in whether Pompilia was innocent. His defence of her, which concedes so much to win the case, illustrates the law's treatment of truth as a matter of rhetorical advantage (Sullivan 1969).

Placed between Pompilia's monologue and the Pope's, the two lawyer books also slow the poem after its emotional climax and prepare, by contrast, for the Pope's moral seriousness. In Book XII Bottini will reappear, ready to argue the opposite case when it suits him.

Book 10: The Pope

Summary

Pope Innocent XII, an old man in his eighties, sits alone on a winter evening, having read all the documents of the case, and prepares to rule on Guido's appeal. He begins with a chronicle of Pope Stephen's trial of the corpse of his predecessor Formosus, a reminder that popes have erred and contradicted one another. He then reviews each person in the case: Guido as wholly corrupt, his brothers and accomplices, the Comparini, Caponsacchi, whom he praises, and Pompilia, whom he calls the perfect flower of the story.

He goes on to ask whether his judgement is sound and his faith true. He imagines the reproach of the Greek dramatist Euripides, who without Christianity saw virtue clearly, and the arguments of those who would counsel mercy for reasons of state. He fears that the coming age will be one of doubt, but resolves to act on the light he has, and orders that Guido die.

Analysis

The Pope's monologue is the moral summit of the poem. He sees the case as a test that revealed each person's true nature, and his verdicts agree with what most readers conclude. Many critics take him as the nearest thing in the poem to Browning's own view, while others stress that his judgement is presented as fallible and provisional, a human act of faith rather than a final truth (Langbaum 1957) (Altick 1968).

The book is also a meditation on Victorian religious doubt projected back into 1698. The Pope's sense that the Church's institutions have failed, his admiration for the goodness of a pagan, and his fear of a coming age of scepticism echo debates of the 1860s. His defence of temptation as the occasion of moral growth expresses a characteristically Browningesque ethic of struggle.

Book 11: Guido

Summary

In the condemned cell at night, waiting to be led to execution, Guido talks to the two churchmen sent to prepare him for death, Cardinal Acciaiuoli and Abate Panciatichi. He begins with flattery and argument, trying to win a last-minute reprieve, and then, as hope fades, abandons his former pose. He mocks the hypocrisy of the Church, the Pope's judgement and Christianity itself, and declares his hatred of Pompilia, whose goodness he could never forgive.

He likens himself to a wolf, insists that he has merely been honest about what others conceal, and praises an older, pagan ethic of strength and self-assertion. When the brotherhood who escort the condemned are heard on the stair, he breaks down entirely, calling in terror on everyone he can name, and ending with a cry to Pompilia herself.

Analysis

Guido's second monologue is perhaps the most powerful in the poem. Freed from the need to persuade a court, he reveals a nihilism that is energetic, intelligent and appalling. His hatred of Pompilia's goodness makes him a study of evil as resentment of the good, and his rhetoric of wolf-nature and honest appetite has struck readers as anticipating later philosophies of the will (Karlin 1993).

The final line has been read in very different ways. Some critics take Guido's cry to Pompilia as the first stirring of recognition, the moment the Pope had hoped for when he trusted that the shock of death might save the soul. Others hear only the last reflex of a coward seizing any protection he can find. Browning leaves the moment open (Langbaum 1957) (Buckler 1985).

Book 12: The Book and the Ring

Summary

The poet returns to describe the aftermath through a series of documents. A Venetian visitor writes a gossipy letter about the execution in the Piazza del Popolo and the crowd that watched it. Arcangeli writes to a colleague, regretting the lost case. Bottini writes too, quoting with scorn the sermon in which the Augustinian friar who attended Pompilia took as his text 'Let God be true, and every man a liar', and announcing that he will now argue that Pompilia was a sinner, so that her estate passes to the convent of the Convertites.

A final legal document records that the court rejected that claim and restored Pompilia's good name. The poet then draws his moral: human testimony is false and human fame mere words and wind, and only art, speaking indirectly, can tell the truth, as the ring, purified of its alloy, stands for the truth of the poem.

Analysis

Book XII brings the poem back to its frame and tests its claims. The documents show how quickly public interest moves on and how institutions recover their own interests: Bottini's change of sides is the final satire on law. The friar's warning against trusting human judgement reinforces the poem's doubts about testimony, while the court's vindication of Pompilia shows that the truth can, belatedly, be recognised (Altick 1968).

The poet's closing lesson is at once modest and grand. He insists that human speech is unreliable, yet claims that art, by speaking indirectly, can convey truth. Critics have debated whether this ending resolves the poem's questions about perspective or simply restates them; the ring is handed back to the reader as a figure for a truth the poem has both shaped and tried to withdraw from (Armstrong 1993) (Buckler 1985).

Characters

The Comparini and Pompilia

Pompilia Comparini
Guido's wife, married to him at thirteen and murdered at seventeen. The child of a Roman prostitute, she was bought as a baby by Violante Comparini and brought up as her daughter. Unable to read or write, she is accused of adultery through forged letters. She flees from Arezzo with Caponsacchi, gives birth to a son, Gaetano, and dies four days after the attack, forgiving her husband. Her monologue in Book VII is the moral and emotional centre of the poem.
Pietro Comparini
An elderly Roman of modest means, Pompilia's supposed father. Easy-going and fond of comfort, he agrees to the marriage in the hope of a noble connection and is quickly disillusioned. After Violante's revelation he sues to recover Pompilia's dowry. He is killed in the attack on his house.
Violante Comparini
Pietro's wife, who bought the newborn Pompilia and passed her off as her own child, partly to secure an inheritance for the family. She arranges the marriage to Guido behind her husband's back, and later reveals the deception to break it. Her mixture of love, vanity and calculation makes her one of the poem's most morally ambiguous figures. She is murdered with her husband.
Gaetano
Pompilia's son, born in December 1697 at her parents' house and hidden away after his baptism. Named after a newly canonised saint, he represents for Pompilia a new beginning; for Guido, an heir whose birth may have helped trigger the murder.

The Franceschini

Count Guido Franceschini
An impoverished nobleman of Arezzo, about fifty, in minor religious orders, who has spent years seeking advancement in Rome. He marries Pompilia for her dowry, treats her cruelly, and, after her flight and the birth of her son, murders her and her parents with four hired men. He speaks twice: in Book V as a wronged husband appealing to the court, and in Book XI as a condemned man who drops all pretence.
Paolo Franceschini
Guido's brother, an abbot in Rome, who negotiates the marriage with the Comparini and later manages the family's interests and lawsuits. He leaves Rome when the scandal grows.
Girolamo Franceschini
Guido's younger brother, a canon, who lives in the family house at Arezzo and, according to Pompilia, makes advances to her.
Beatrice Franceschini
Guido's mother, who presides over the cold and miserly household at Arezzo where Pompilia lives after her marriage.
The four accomplices
Young farmhands from Guido's estate at Vittiano, hired to help him kill the Comparini. They quarrel with Guido over payment as they flee, are caught with him at Baccano, and are hanged on the day of his execution.

Law and Church

Giuseppe Caponsacchi
A young priest of a good Arezzo family, worldly and gallant until his encounter with Pompilia changes his life. He helps her flee to Rome, is caught with her at Castelnuovo, and is sentenced to three years' relegation at Civita Vecchia. Recalled after the murder, he gives a passionate account of the flight in Book VI. The Pope calls him a 'warrior-priest'.
Pope Innocent XII
Antonio Pignatelli, Pope from 1691 to 1700, an old man in his eighties who hears Guido's appeal. His long monologue in Book X reviews the case, judges each person in it, and asks whether his own faith and judgement can be trusted. He refuses clemency and orders Guido's execution.
Dominus Hyacinthus de Archangelis
Giacinto Arcangeli, the Procurator of the Poor who defends Guido. A cheerful family man, he writes his Latin plea in Book VIII while planning his son's birthday dinner.
Johannes-Baptista Bottinius
Giovanni Battista Bottini, Advocate of the Fisc and the Apostolic Chamber, the prosecutor of Guido. In Book IX he rehearses an ornate speech that defends Pompilia while assuming she was guilty; in Book XII he prepares to claim her estate as a sinner's.
Cardinal Acciaiuoli and Abate Panciatichi
The two Tuscan churchmen who sit with Guido in his cell on the night before his execution, and to whom his second monologue in Book XI is addressed.
Fra Celestino
The Augustinian friar who hears Pompilia's last confession and preaches a sermon after the execution, quoted in Book XII, on the falsity of human judgement.

Rome: the city and its voices

Half-Rome
The speaker of Book II, a married man who takes Guido's side, partly because he suspects his own wife of encouraging an admirer.
The Other Half-Rome
The speaker of Book III, a bachelor whose sympathy for Pompilia is warm, generous and sentimental.
Tertium Quid
The 'third something' of Book IV, a man of fashion who presents the case to aristocratic listeners with worldly detachment and refuses to decide between the parties.
The poet
The speaker of Books I and XII, Browning himself as showman, historian and goldsmith, who presents his source and his method, introduces the voices, and draws the final lesson about art and truth.

Themes

Truth and perspective

The poem's structure is its first statement about truth. The same events are told ten times by speakers whose interests, temperaments and positions shape what they see; no one has the whole truth, and even the facts that all accept are arranged into incompatible stories. Readers must assemble a judgement from partial testimony, as a court does, and the poem makes them conscious of how they do it (Langbaum 1957).

Yet the poem is not simply relativist. Some speakers see more than others, and the movement from the gossip of Rome to Pompilia and the Pope is a movement towards moral insight. Browning seems to believe that truth exists but is reached by sympathy and moral perception rather than by evidence alone (Buckler 1985) (Altick 1968).

Fact and imagination

The ring metaphor states the poem's theory of historical art. Facts by themselves are inert; the poet's imagination must enter them to make them live; and yet the aim is to let the truth stand without the poet. Book I and Book XII return to this question, and the poem's use of its source, sometimes faithful and sometimes free, puts the theory into practice (Hodell 1908) (Armstrong 1993).

This makes the poem a reflection on Victorian historiography. Browning wrote in the age of Carlyle, Macaulay and the historical criticism of scripture, when the reliability of records and the role of the historian's imagination were urgent questions. The Ring and the Book argues that history needs imagination, and that imagination needs the discipline of fact.

Law, institutions and justice

The case passes through the courts of Arezzo, Florence and Rome, and the law is satirised in the lawyers' books as a game of rhetoric and precedent, indifferent to truth. The 'honour' defence, which almost saves Guido, shows how human law can endorse violence against women (Altick 1968).

The Church is judged as severely. Guido is a cleric, the clergy of Arezzo fail Pompilia, the archbishop and governor she appeals to refuse to help, and the Pope himself acknowledges that the institutions he heads are corrupt. Justice comes, when it does, from individuals who act beyond their roles: Caponsacchi breaking clerical decorum, the Pope acting on conscience.

Good and evil

The poem is unusual among Browning's works for its stark moral opposition between Pompilia's goodness and Guido's evil. The Pope sees the case as a revelation of souls, in which ordinary people were tested and showed what they were (Langbaum 1957).

Evil, in Guido, is not simple wickedness but resentment of goodness, and his second monologue makes it eloquent. Goodness, in Pompilia, is not mere passivity but the capacity to resist, forgive and love. The poem's ethics of struggle, expressed in the Pope's praise of temptation, values action and moral risk over safety and conformity.

Marriage, property and the position of women

The marriage at the centre of the poem is a commercial transaction in which a child is exchanged for a title and a title for a dowry. Almost every speaker talks of Pompilia as property, and the law treats her body and reputation as her husband's possessions. Her monologue protests against this, in its quiet way, by insisting on her own experience (Brady 1988).

Browning, writing after the Divorce Act of 1857 and amid debates about married women's property, gives the case contemporary force. The poem's sympathy with Pompilia has been read as a critique of patriarchal marriage, though some critics see its idealisation of her as part of the same system (Brady 1988) (Armstrong 1993).

Love and art

The invocation to Elizabeth Barrett Browning at the end of Book I and the return to it at the end of the poem frame the whole work as an offering to the dead. Browning's grief and his love for the poet he married are woven into the story of a rescue and of a love that is chaste yet profound.

Caponsacchi's devotion to Pompilia has often been read as a reflection of Browning's own rescue of Elizabeth Barrett from her father's house in 1846, and Pompilia's spiritual intuition as a tribute to his wife's (Irvine 1974) (Ryals 1993).

Motifs

Monologue and audience

Every monologue has a specific listener and setting: a cousin in the street, aristocrats at a reception, the judges, the nuns around a deathbed, the churchmen in a cell. Browning uses these audiences to show how speech is shaped by its occasion, and readers must read each speaker against the situation that conditions what he or she says.

Legal language and Latin

Phrases of Latin law, citations of authorities and the formulas of pleading run through the poem, especially in the lawyer books. They express the law's distance from the people it judges and the way legal language can make a murder into a technical problem.

Saint George and the dragon

Caponsacchi's rescue of Pompilia is imagined, from Book I onwards, as Saint George's rescue of the princess from the dragon; Caponsacchi himself takes up the comparison in Book VI, and the Pope's 'warrior-priest' extends it. The imagery dignifies the flight as heroic and spiritual, while the speakers who doubt it mock it as romance.

Flowers and weeds

Pompilia is described as flower or weed, rose or poisonous growth, according to the speaker. The Pope's image of her as the rose he gathers for God resolves the question in his own terms.

Symbols

The ring

The ring of Book I, made of gold mixed with alloy and purified by acid, symbolises the poem itself: the true story (gold) worked into a shape by the poet's imagination (alloy), then cleansed so that the truth remains. At the end of the poem it also suggests the wedding ring and the poet's love for his wife.

The Old Yellow Book

The book of records is the source and symbol of 'pure crude fact', the material of history. Its yellowed covers and mix of print and manuscript embody the past as a collection of documents that need interpretation.

The wolf

Guido calls himself a wolf in Book XI and the image runs through his monologue: the predator among sheep, honest about the appetite others conceal. It symbolises his self-justifying egoism and his revolt against Christian meekness.

Light

Light and its source in God recur in the speeches of Pompilia, Caponsacchi and the Pope. Pompilia's last line sees God's light descending; the Pope's monologue is set at evening in a darkening age; and Caponsacchi's act lets 'light into the world'.

Critical debates

Truth and relativism

Robert Langbaum's The Poetry of Experience (1957) made The Ring and the Book central to the theory of the dramatic monologue. He read it as a 'relativist poem', in which truth is not a fixed object but emerges from the play of perspectives and from the reader's sympathy and judgement (Langbaum 1957).

Others have argued that the poem endorses a hierarchy of insight that culminates in Pompilia and the Pope, and that Browning's own moral convictions are clear. Altick and Loucks emphasised the poem's moral and religious design, while Buckler read it as a poem about how truth is apprehended through art (Altick 1968) (Buckler 1985). The question is whether the Pope is the poem's spokesman or one more voice.

The poem and its sources

Because the Old Yellow Book survives (it is now at Balliol College, Oxford), Browning's use of his source can be checked. Charles Hodell's edition of 1908 reproduced and translated it, and John Marshall Gest, a Philadelphia judge, published a new translation in 1925 with chapters arguing that Browning had distorted the case (Hodell 1908) (Gest 1925).

Gest, reading the documents as a lawyer, was inclined to think the historical Pompilia less innocent and Guido less monstrous than Browning made them. Later scholars, including Beatrice Corrigan, who published further documents, and Altick and Loucks, have shown how Browning selected and shaped the evidence while keeping close to its facts, and have defended his interpretation as plausible (Corrigan 1956) (Altick 1968).

Pompilia: saint or idealisation?

Victorian readers found Pompilia one of the great creations of English poetry, a figure of innocence and spiritual grace. Twentieth-century critics often found her too good to be true, a male idealisation of the woman as pure victim, and set her beside the Victorian 'angel in the house'.

Ann Brady's feminist reading (1988) argued that Pompilia is a figure of resistance as well as suffering, who refuses the roles of chattel wife and objectified saint, and whose monologue speaks of experience the patriarchal institutions around her deny (Brady 1988). Isobel Armstrong reads the poem's representation of gender within a wider analysis of Victorian poetry's politics (Armstrong 1993).

The lawyers' comedy

Books VIII and IX have often been called the dullest in the poem, and early reviewers and later readers alike have wished them shorter. Their pedantic Latin and digressions can try a reader's patience.

Defenders see them as essential satire, a grotesque comedy that shows the law's distance from truth and sets off the seriousness of Pompilia and the Pope. Mary Rose Sullivan's study of the poem's voices treats them as skilful character studies, in which Browning's mimicry of legal rhetoric is itself a critique (Sullivan 1969).

A Victorian epic?

The Ring and the Book is the length of an epic, in twelve books like the Aeneid and Paradise Lost, and it begins with an invocation. Yet it has no hero in the epic sense and its subject is a sordid domestic crime. Critics have asked whether it is an epic, a novel in verse, or a new form.

Herbert Tucker's history of the British epic places it as a crucial work of the Victorian epic tradition, which renewed the form by turning it towards modern questions of evidence and interpretation (Tucker 2008). Henry James's essay 'The Novel in The Ring and the Book' (1912) read it instead as material for a great novel (James 1914).

The poem and Victorian historiography

The poem's treatment of evidence has been linked to Victorian debates about historical knowledge and especially to the historical criticism of the Bible associated with D. F. Strauss, whose Life of Jesus questioned the Gospels' reliability. The sermon in Book XII, on the text 'Let God be true, and every man a liar', and the Pope's fears of a coming age of doubt, suggest these concerns (Armstrong 1993) (Ryals 1993).

The poem can thus be read as a defence of imaginative truth against both naive fact and sceptical criticism, though how far it succeeds is debated.

Reception and reputation

The poem transformed Browning's reputation. Reviewers who had found his earlier work obscure praised it extravagantly, and it made him a public figure and, for many Victorians, a religious teacher (Litzinger 1970). G. K. Chesterton's study of 1903 still read the poem as a great epic of free speech, showing the many sides of a single truth (Chesterton 1903).

Twentieth-century critics, influenced by modernism, often preferred the shorter monologues and found the length and moralising of The Ring and the Book hard to take. More recent criticism has returned to it as one of the most ambitious experiments in Victorian poetry (Karlin 1993) (Woolford 1996).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. What does the metaphor of the ring and its alloy suggest about the relation between historical fact and poetic imagination?

    Consider the goldsmith's process in Book I and the line 'Fancy with fact is just one fact the more'. Can the alloy really be removed? Return to the ring in Book XII.

  2. Why does Browning reveal the whole story and its outcome in Book I?

    Think about what replaces suspense as the poem's interest: how the story is told, by whom, and with what motives.

  3. Compare Half-Rome, the Other Half-Rome and Tertium Quid. How does each speaker's situation shape his version of the case?

    Look at the endings of Books II and IV and at the audience each speaker addresses.

  4. How do Guido's two monologues differ, and what does the difference reveal about him?

    Compare the audience, the purpose and the tone of Books V and XI. Is the second Guido the 'real' one?

  5. Is Pompilia a convincing character or an idealisation?

    Consider her voice, her account of her marriage and motherhood, and the arguments of feminist critics such as Brady.

  6. What is the function of the two lawyers' books in the structure of the poem?

    Think about their placement between Pompilia and the Pope, their comedy and their satire on law.

  7. Does the Pope speak for Browning?

    Weigh the authority of his verdicts against his own admissions of uncertainty and his fear of the coming age. Consider Langbaum's view of the poem as relativist.

  8. How does Caponsacchi understand his rescue of Pompilia, and how do others see it?

    Compare the imagery of Saint George with the gossip of Half-Rome and the Pope's praise of the 'warrior-priest'.

  9. What does Book XII add to the poem?

    Look at the letters, Bottini's change of position, the sermon and the final lesson to the British Public.

  10. In what sense, if any, is The Ring and the Book an epic?

    Consider its length, its twelve books and its invocation, and compare it with the novel. See Tucker's account of the Victorian epic.
