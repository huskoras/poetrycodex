Overview

Full title
Konrad Wallenrod. Powieść historyczna z dziejów litewskich i pruskich (Konrad Wallenrod: A Historical Tale from Lithuanian and Prussian History)
Author
Adam Mickiewicz (1798–1855), born near Nowogródek in the former Grand Duchy of Lithuania; sent into the interior of Russia after the Philomath trial of 1823–24
Written and published
Written in Russia, chiefly in Moscow, in about 1825–27; published in St Petersburg in 1828, after passing the Russian censorship
Form
An introduction and six cantos of narrative verse, with a hymn, songs, a ballad and a framed tale set into them; Mickiewicz added a preface and historical notes
Setting
Marienburg (Malbork), capital of the Teutonic Order in Prussia, and, in the inset tale, Kowno and the Lithuanian lands along the Niemen
Time of action
The late fourteenth century, compressing events from the battle of Rudau (1370) to the brief rule of Grand Master Konrad von Wallenrode (1391–93)
Epigraph
Machiavelli, The Prince, chapter 18: there are two ways of fighting, by law and by force, and a ruler must know how to be both fox and lion
Genre
Romantic verse tale on the Byronic model, presented as a historical poem and read from the first as a political parable
Translation quoted
Maude Ashurst Biggs, blank verse with rhymed songs (London, 1882); the edition held here omits the prefaces, the epigraph and the notes
Famous for
The Song of the Wajdelote, the ballad of Alpujara, the hero who betrays from within, and the political idea of 'wallenrodism'

Context

Mickiewicz wrote Konrad Wallenrod as a political exile. In 1823 the Russian authorities in Wilno uncovered the student societies of the Philomaths and Philarets, to which he belonged, and after a long investigation directed by Senator Nikolai Novosiltsev he and several friends were sentenced to be sent into the interior of Russia. He spent 1824–29 in St Petersburg, Odessa, the Crimea and Moscow, where he was welcomed in literary society, came to know Pushkin, and had met Kondraty Ryleev and Aleksandr Bestuzhev, soon to be leaders of the Decembrist revolt of December 1825. The poem was written chiefly in Moscow, in the years when the Decembrists were tried and punished, and it is shaped by the experience of a subject who must speak and act under watchful eyes (Koropeckyj 2008) (Weintraub 1954).

The subject comes from the long war between the Teutonic Order and pagan Lithuania. The Order, founded in the Holy Land, had by the late thirteenth century conquered the Baltic Prussians and built a state ruled from Marienburg; for more than a century its campaigns into Lithuania drew knights from all over Christian Europe. Lithuania under princes such as Kiejstut (Kęstutis) and his son Witold (Vytautas) resisted, raided in return and played the Order against its rivals, until the union with Poland and the official conversion of 1386–87 changed the balance (Christiansen 1997) (Urban 2003) (Rowell 1994). The real Konrad von Wallenrode ruled as Grand Master from 1391 to 1393 and was remembered by some chroniclers as harsh and unlucky; Mickiewicz turned him into a Lithuanian in disguise.

The poem was printed in St Petersburg in 1828. Mickiewicz framed it as history, with a prose preface and learned notes on Lithuanian antiquities, and set at its head an epigraph from chapter 18 of Machiavelli's The Prince, on the two ways of fighting, one by law and proper to men, the other by force and proper to beasts, and on the ruler's need to be both fox and lion (Machiavelli 1998). The historical framing helped the poem through the censorship. Readers in Poland and Lithuania nevertheless took it at once as a parable of their own situation under Russia, and there are stories that Novosiltsev saw its meaning only after it had been passed; such anecdotes belong to the poem's legend and should be treated with caution (Koropeckyj 2008) (Welsh 1966).

Formally the poem is a Romantic verse tale in the manner made famous by Byron's The Giaour, The Corsair and Lara: a mysterious, melancholy hero, a hidden past revealed in fragments, abrupt transitions, and lyric songs set into the narrative. Mickiewicz joins this to historical colour in the manner of Scott and to the figure of the bard who preserves a nation's memory, so that the central disclosure of the plot is made in a song within the poem (Weintraub 1954) (Krzyżanowski 1978) (Lednicki 1956). Biggs's translation, quoted here, renders the narrative in English blank verse and the songs in rhyme (Mickiewicz 1882); a modern version is that of Irene Suboczewski (Mickiewicz 1989).

The poem's afterlife was political as much as literary. The young officers and cadets who began the November Uprising in Warsaw in 1830 belonged to a generation that knew it well, and the word 'wallenrodism' (wallenrodyzm) came to name a strategy of feigned loyalty and betrayal from within, admired by some as patriotic heroism and condemned by others as a corruption of honour. The story also passed into music, notably in Amilcare Ponchielli's opera I Lituani (1874), and Joseph Conrad's Polish name Konrad is usually connected with Mickiewicz's heroes (Davies 2005) (Janion 1978) (Najder 2007).

Plot overview

In the late fourteenth century the Niemen divides pagan Lithuania from the lands of the Teutonic Order. At Marienburg the knights elect a new Grand Master. Their favourite is Konrad Wallenrod, a stranger who has won fame fighting the Moors in Spain and the Turks at sea, and who is admired for his austerity, though he is gloomy, sometimes drinks alone and sings in a foreign tongue. His only friend is an old monk, Halban. During the election the knights overhear a walled-up recluse in a tower by the lake cry out that Konrad will be Master in order to destroy them; Halban persuades them to treat this as a prophecy in Konrad's favour, and he is chosen.

As Master, Konrad disappoints the Order's hopes of war. He imposes penances and discipline, delays the campaign against Lithuania, and spends his nights at the recluse's tower, where it emerges that she is the woman he loves and that he is pursuing some terrible design. At the Patron's feast, with the Lithuanian prince Witold present as the Order's ally, an old Lithuanian bard (the Wajdelote) sings of Lithuania's sufferings and tells the story of Walter Alf: a Lithuanian boy captured by the Germans and raised by the Grand Master Winrych, who escaped to Lithuania, married Aldona, daughter of Prince Kiejstut, and then, convinced that Lithuania could not win in open war, left her and went back to the Germans with the old bard to destroy the Order from within. Aldona entered a convent.

Konrad, drunk and shaken, answers with the ballad of Alpujara, in which a defeated Moor feigns surrender and infects his Spanish victors with the plague by his embrace. War follows: Witold deserts the Order, the Pope proclaims a crusade, and Konrad leads a great army into Lithuania, where by delay and neglect he allows it to be destroyed by hunger, winter and Witold's attacks. He returns to Marienburg with a remnant.

A secret tribunal of the Order, told that the Master is not the real Wallenrod and has spoken Lithuanian at the tower, condemns him. Konrad goes to the tower and begs Aldona to come away with him to Lithuania; she refuses, fearing that he would no longer recognise her, and asks only that he visit her more often. Warned by the tribunal's cry of 'Woe', Konrad arranges a signal with her, a lamp in his window, and takes poison as the judges break in. He boasts of the ruin he has caused, compares himself with Samson, and dies, throwing down the lamp. A cry from the tower tells that Aldona has died too. Halban survives to carry the story to Lithuania in song.

Canto by canto

Canto 0: The Niemen

Introduction (Polish: Wstęp)

Summary

A century after the Order first waged its crusade against the heathen north, the Prussians have been crushed or driven out, and the Teutonic Knights press on towards Lithuania. The Niemen now marks the frontier. On one bank stand the Lithuanian sanctuaries and sacred woods and the young warriors in lynx-skin caps; on the other the German knight sits armoured on his horse, loading his gun and telling his rosary, beneath a great cross that seems to stretch its arms over Lithuania.

The river that once joined brother peoples has become a threshold that no one crosses except at the cost of freedom or life. Only nature still ignores the border: the hop vine climbs across to the Prussian poplar, and the nightingales on both banks still converse in Lithuanian. The narrator foresees armies and the axe on the river's shores, and promises that two lovers, divided by the hatred of nations, will be united again in the bard's song.

Analysis

The introduction sets the poem's governing opposition in a single landscape. The cross on the hill is drawn as an image of conquest rather than faith, and the knight who counts his beads while loading his weapon gives in one line the poem's charge of religious hypocrisy against the Order. The vine and the nightingales, which cross the frontier freely, suggest that the division of peoples is unnatural, and they prepare the love story, which is also a story of a border crossed (Weintraub 1954).

The last lines announce the double subject, a national war and a private love, and a song that will bring them together. The Wajdelote, a Lithuanian bard, is named before he appears, and the promise that the lovers will be united in his song points to the poem's frame, in which history survives as poetry (Koropeckyj 2008). The geography also served the allegory. After the partitions the Niemen had marked the frontier between Prussian and Russian rule, and Napoleon's army crossed it at Kowno in 1812, so that the prophecy of hosts rushing over its fords would have stirred recent memories in the first readers (Welsh 1966) (Davies 2005).

Canto 1: The Election

Canto I (Polish: Obiór)

Summary

The bells of Marienburg ring as the commanders (Komturs) of the Order gather in chapter to elect a Grand Master. Opinion favours Konrad Wallenrod, a knight unknown in Prussia but famous abroad for his deeds against the Moors in Castile and the Turks at sea, and admired for poverty, humility and contempt for the world. He has no courtly graces and seems indifferent to praise and to women; though young, he is already grey and worn.

His character has strange depths. Certain words, 'Fatherland', 'Duty', 'Beloved', the crusades and Lithuania, cloud his rare moments of gaiety. Alone, he drinks, and then he sings melancholy songs in a foreign language, songs that touch every feeling except mirth and hope. When he breaks into wild threats, his friend and confessor Halban, an old monk, calms him with a look, as a keeper masters a lion with his eye.

Analysis

The canto presents Konrad from outside, through what the knights say of him, and so begins the poem's method of concealment and gradual disclosure. The reader is given clues the knights cannot interpret: the foreign songs, the words that wound him, the night drinking. Each points to a hidden identity, and the knights' praise of his austerity becomes heavily ironic once the plot is known (Weintraub 1954).

The simile of the lion and its keeper fixes the relation between Konrad and Halban. Konrad is the force, Halban the controlling will, and the image already suggests that the hero is not wholly free in his own design. The emphasis on song matters too: the hero's true self survives only in music, in a language his hearers cannot understand, a figure for national identity under foreign rule (Koropeckyj 2008) (Walicki 1982).

Canto 2: The Recluse's Prophecy

Canto II

Summary

The chapter sings a hymn to the Holy Spirit asking for a sign of the worthiest candidate. In the pause before the next session the Archkomtur takes Halban and the leading knights for a walk at dawn, and they come to a tower by the lake where, ten years before, an unknown woman asked to be walled up as a recluse. No one sees her; only her hymns are heard at night, and a glimpse of her white hand is sometimes noticed at the grate.

The knights hear her voice cry out that Konrad will be Master so that he may destroy them, and that the Crusaders would know him even if he came back from the grave. A gleam like a helmet vanishes in the distance. Halban at once declares the words a prophecy in Konrad's favour, and the knights agree to elect him. Left alone, Halban sings of the Wilija, the river that runs into the Niemen and is lost in it, as a Lithuanian girl is lost to the foreign youth she loves.

Analysis

The canto turns on misreading. The recluse's words reveal the plot exactly, but Halban persuades the knights to hear them as a blessing, and their pious expectation of a sign makes them easy to deceive. The irony works against the Order's own religious language, set out in the solemn hymn that opens the canto (Weintraub 1954).

Halban's song of the Wilija is among the most admired lyrics in the poem. The river that abandons her valley for the Niemen and perishes with him in the sea is an image of Aldona, who has left her homeland for love; the final line, with the girl weeping in a prison tower, applies the image to the recluse and so tells the attentive reader who she is. Like the Wajdelote's later song, it hides the truth in a form that only Lithuanians, or readers, can decipher (Welsh 1966) (Miłosz 1983).

Canto 3: Konrad and the Recluse

Canto III

Summary

Konrad receives the sword and cross of the Grand Master with a fierce, ambiguous look. The knights expect war, but a whole year passes in peace. Konrad declares that the Order has broken its rule, enforces penance and harsh discipline, and does nothing while Lithuanian raiders burn villages near Marienburg. The moment seems ideal for war, since Lithuania is torn by internal conflict and Witold, displaced by Jagiełło, has come to seek the Order's help; but Konrad delays.

At night he goes to the recluse's tower. She sings of the happy life she once had as a prince's daughter in Lithuania and of the youth who taught her of the Christian God and a greater world; Konrad answers bitterly. In the dialogue that follows she tells how she followed him to Marienburg and walled herself up only to be near him, and he speaks of the vengeance for which he renounced happiness. He confesses that he delays the war to spend more evenings with her, and leaves at dawn like an infernal spirit fleeing the matin bell.

Analysis

The canto shows the cost of the plan before the plan is fully revealed. Konrad's policy of reform, penance and peace looks like piety and is in fact a means of weakening the Order; but the dialogue at the tower shows that his delays also come from love. The tension between the nation's cause and private feeling, which the plot resolves only in death, is here at its sharpest (Weintraub 1954) (Janion 1978).

The recluse's song is a compressed autobiography. The line about three daughters, which Konrad repeats in anguish, and the image of the cross from which thunder flies, sum up how Christianity entered her life as both promise and destruction. Konrad's language of greatness, and his picture of himself as a man who weeps only in order to kill, belong to the Byronic hero; but the poem lets Aldona answer him and question what such greatness is worth (Koropeckyj 2008). The final simile, in which the hero vanishes like a spirit of hell at the morning bell, shows the narrator's refusal to idealise him.

Canto 4: The Festival

Canto IV (Polish: Uczta)

Summary

On the Patron's day Konrad holds a feast for the knights, with Witold and his Lithuanian captains as guests. Bored by an Italian singer's flattery and a troubadour's love songs, he calls for a harsh song fit for monks who kill. An old man in Prussian or Lithuanian dress, his face veiled, offers to sing. He shames the Lithuanians who serve the Germans, which moves Witold to tears, and sings the Song of the Wajdelote: a vision of the plague-maiden and of the Order as a worse plague, and a hymn to native song as an ark that preserves a people's memory.

He then tells the Wajdelote's Tale. A Lithuanian boy, captured in a German raid and raised by the Grand Master Winrych under the name Walter Alf, kept his Lithuanian soul with the help of a captive bard, who taught him that a slave's only weapon is treachery. Walter fled to Lithuania, served Prince Kiejstut and married his daughter Aldona. When defeat showed that Lithuania could not win in the field, he left her, with the bard, to wage war on the Order by a 'dreadful way'; Aldona took refuge in a convent tower. Konrad, wildly drunk, seizes the lute and sings the ballad of Alpujara, in which the Moor Almanzor feigns surrender and kills his victors with the plague by his embrace; then he falls asleep amid general bewilderment.

Analysis

The longest canto is the centre of the poem, and it is built as a series of frames: the narrator tells of a feast at which a bard tells a story that is the hero's own, and the hero replies with a ballad that is also his own story in disguise. The plot is revealed openly, but in a form the Germans cannot understand, while Witold and the reader can. Song is both the keeper of national memory and a coded political weapon (Weintraub 1954) (Koropeckyj 2008).

The Song of the Wajdelote, with its address to native song as the ark of the covenant between generations, became a classic statement of the Romantic idea that a nation without a state survives in its poetry (Walicki 1982) (Miłosz 1983). The tale gives the poem's moral argument in the old bard's words: free knights may choose their weapons, slaves have only treachery. The ballad of Alpujara is the most compressed image of the strategy, in which the embrace of surrender carries death. Konrad's outburst against the bard's 'traitorous song', which poisons the infant with love of glory and country, shows that the hero himself experiences national poetry as a fate laid upon him (Janion 1978).

Canto 5: War

Canto V (Polish: Wojna)

Summary

Konrad can no longer restrain the demand for war. Witold, abandoning his alliance after the banquet, leads his men away, enters the Order's castles with forged orders from the Master and destroys their garrisons. The Pope proclaims a crusade, and knights flood in from all Europe. The army marches into Lithuania, its progress marked by burning on the horizon; then the news stops. In winter the people of Marienburg see Konrad and a few commanders struggle back through the snow, followed by a broken, starving rabble, with frozen corpses standing by the road.

The narrator explains the disaster: Konrad, once famous for prudence, besieged Wilno sluggishly, ignored Witold's snares, let hunger waste the army, held secret parleys and at last was the first to flee the field. Yet he returns with a strange, almost satanic joy in his eyes. Meanwhile, in a dungeon beneath Malbork, the Order's secret tribunal of twelve masked judges hears an accuser declare that the Master is not the real Wallenrod but the count's squire, suspected of his death, who has spoken Lithuanian at the tower. The judges silently consult the law and pronounce the single word 'Woe!'

Analysis

The canto shows the plan succeeding, in a deliberately rapid and distanced narration. The great crusade is reported from the walls of Marienburg, first by the absence of news and then by the image of the returning army, so that the catastrophe is seen through the eyes of the Order's own people. The scene of the frozen dead would have recalled to many first readers the retreat of Napoleon's army from Moscow in 1812 (Weintraub 1954) (Davies 2005).

The tribunal scene, with its masks, its law-book turned with dagger points and its silent sentence, owes more to Gothic fiction and to the legend of secret courts than to anything known of the Order's institutions. It also mirrors Konrad's own method: the judges act in secret and by stealth, as he has done. The poem thus suggests that the Order and the strategy used against it share the same moral darkness, a point later critics of 'wallenrodism' took up (Janion 1978) (Miłosz 1983).

Canto 6: The Parting

Canto VI (Polish: Pożegnanie)

Summary

At a stormy winter dawn Konrad comes to the tower and calls to Aldona that his vows are fulfilled: the Order has been struck to the heart, and he wants no more vengeance. He describes his visit to their old valley in Lithuania, where the dry willow twigs he once planted have grown into trees, and begs her to come away with him. She refuses. She swore never to leave the tower except for the grave, and she fears that he would not know in her the Aldona he remembers; she asks only that he come more often, and that Lithuanian children be brought to sing near the tower.

Wandering by the lake, Konrad hears the tribunal's 'Woe' and understands. He arranges a signal with Aldona: a black cloth on his balcony by day and a lamp at his window by night. That evening the judges break into his turret. He takes poison, and Halban, offered the rest of the cup, chooses to live and spread the story in song. Konrad tramples his Master's badge, claims the ruin of the Order as his work, compares himself to Samson, and dies, throwing down the lamp. A cry from the tower marks Aldona's death. The narrator closes by leaving the end of her song to heaven and to the reader.

Analysis

The final canto turns from political triumph to private loss. Konrad's declaration that Germans too are men, and his wish to forget nations in Aldona's arms, suggest that the avenger has outlived his vengeance; Aldona's refusal shows that the sacrifice cannot be undone. Her image of the moth in amber, keeping its form for ever, makes her a figure of memory: she would rather keep the past whole than meet him changed (Welsh 1966) (Janion 1978).

The death scene is staged as a public act. Konrad's last speech is a boast, and the comparison with Samson, who pulled down the temple on himself and his enemies, presents his suicide as heroic self-sacrifice. Halban's decision to survive, so that the deed may be sung and an avenger may arise, gives the poem a political future beyond the hero's death (Koropeckyj 2008). The closing lines, which break off like a snapped string and hand the song over to heaven and the reader, return to poetry as the medium in which the story lives on (Weintraub 1954).

Characters

Lithuanian in disguise

Konrad Wallenrod (Walter Alf)
The hero, a Lithuanian carried off as a child in a German raid, raised by the Grand Master Winrych as Walter Alf, and later known by the name of a dead German count. Brave, austere, melancholy and given to solitary drinking, he rises to Grand Master in order to lead the Order to ruin. He is a Byronic figure whose inner life appears only in fragments, songs and outbursts; his sacrifice of love and honour to the nation's cause makes him the original of 'wallenrodism'.

Lithuanians

Aldona
Daughter of Prince Kiejstut, who fell in love with Walter when he taught her about the Christian world, and married him. When he left to carry out his plan, she followed him and walled herself up as a recluse in a tower near Marienburg, to live near him. She is the poem's voice of private love and memory, and her refusal to come out at the end shows that some sacrifices cannot be reversed.
Halban (the Wajdelote)
An old Lithuanian bard (wajdelota), long a captive of the Germans, who nursed Walter's patriotism and taught him that a slave's weapon is treachery. He follows him into the Order as a monk and his confessor, and keeps his resolve alive with a look or a word; at the feast he appears, it is implied, as the veiled old singer. At the end he chooses to live so that the deed will be sung, an embodiment of the poet as the nation's memory.
Kiejstut (Kęstutis)
The historical Lithuanian prince (d. 1382), here a warrior who has always fought the Germans and does not think about the future. He welcomes Walter, makes him his son-in-law, and is shaken by Walter's warning that Lithuania cannot win in open war.
Witold (Vytautas)
Kiejstut's son, historically the future Grand Duke of Lithuania. In the poem he has sought the Order's help against Jagiełło and sits at the feast as its ally; the bard's words about traitors move him to tears, and he then turns against the Order and, in secret contact with Konrad, destroys its army.

Teutonic Order

Winrych (Winrich von Kniprode)
The Grand Master (historically 1351–82) who raised Walter as his own son. He appears only in the Wajdelote's tale, where the boy's secret damage to his palace is an early sign of hidden hatred.
The Archkomtur and the chapter
The leaders of the Order who elect Konrad. Pious, warlike and easily deceived, they read the recluse's words as a prophecy and later grumble at the Master's delays.
The secret tribunal
Twelve masked judges who meet under Malbork to punish the crimes of the powerful. They condemn Konrad with the single word 'Woe' and come to execute the sentence.

Inset ballad

Almanzor
The Moorish leader in Konrad's ballad of Alpujara, who feigns surrender to the Spaniards and infects them with the plague by his embrace, a mirror of Konrad's own strategy.

Themes

Patriotism and treason

The poem's central question is whether deceit and betrayal can be justified in the cause of a conquered nation. Konrad betrays the Order he has sworn to serve, and the poem gives the reason in the old bard's words: the free may fight in the open, but a slave's only weapon is treachery. The Machiavelli epigraph on the fox and the lion frames the story as a study of political means.

Yet the poem does not simply praise the strategy. Konrad pays with his love, his happiness and his moral integrity; he calls his life 'foul hypocrisy', and the narrator likens him to a spirit of hell. Readers have argued ever since about whether the poem endorses or warns against what came to be called 'wallenrodism' (Janion 1978) (Miłosz 1983).

Love and the nation

The love of Walter and Aldona is sacrificed to Lithuania, and the poem makes the sacrifice the heart of its tragedy. In the Wajdelote's tale even the sweetest words, family and love, yield to the word 'country'. Aldona's choice to wall herself up near him makes her a partner in the sacrifice, but her voice also questions the greatness for which they suffer.

The poem ends with her death rather than with the fall of the Order, and its last lines are about her fate. Private loss is not a sideline to the political plot but its measure (Welsh 1966) (Weintraub 1954).

Song and national memory

Poetry is the medium through which the nation survives. The Wajdelote's apostrophe to native song as an ark that no stroke can break makes the bard the guardian of a people's identity when its political existence is threatened.

Song is also an instrument of action. The bard's songs keep Konrad's vengeance alive, the plot is revealed in a song the Germans cannot understand, and Halban survives to turn the deed into a song that will raise future avengers. Konrad himself feels this as a poison that has destroyed the possibility of a private life (Walicki 1982) (Koropeckyj 2008).

Mask and identity

Konrad lives under several names: a German name given by his captors, the borrowed name of a dead count, and a Lithuanian self beneath both. The poem dwells on visors, cloaks, veils and masks, and on the gap between appearance and the self beneath it. The capture of the child, renamed and raised by his enemies, makes identity something that has to be remembered and defended against assimilation.

The theme had obvious resonance for subjects of the Russian Empire, but it also raises a general question: how long can a man live behind a mask before it becomes his face (Koropeckyj 2008) (Janion 1978)?

Holy war and its hypocrisy

The Teutonic Order presents its wars as crusades, and the poem gives its hymns and prayers. Konrad's own phrase for the knights, men who sanctify and murder, and the knight who loads his gun while saying his prayers, point to the hypocrisy of violence in the name of religion.

The poem's view of the Order is partisan, and it compresses and simplifies the history of the Baltic crusades, which modern historians describe in more complex terms (Christiansen 1997) (Urban 2003).

Motifs

Songs within the poem

The hymn, the song from the tower, Halban's song of the Wilija, the Song of the Wajdelote and the ballad of Alpujara: the narrative is constantly interrupted by songs, which carry its secrets and its emotional climaxes.

Night and dawn

The lovers meet only at night and part at dawn, when Konrad lowers his visor. Daylight brings back the public mask; darkness is the time of truth.

Wine

Konrad drinks alone and at the feast, and only in drink does he show his feelings. His last cup is poison.

Fire and burning

Burning villages, the fires of war on the horizon, the hidden fire that hollows the oak and the final lamp link vengeance with destruction.

'Woe'

The tribunal's single word, echoed three times by the walls and later by the shadow on the snow, works as a refrain of doom in the last cantos.

Symbols

The tower

Aldona's tower is both a living grave and the place of the lovers' only meetings. It stands for fidelity, imprisonment and the private life that the nation's cause has walled up.

The white mantle and black cross

The dress of the Teutonic Knights. In the Song of the Wajdelote the plumed helmet and the mantle with the black cross become a plague worse than the pestilence.

The Niemen and the Wilija

The frontier river and its tributary. The Niemen divides peoples once joined; the Wilija lost in it is an image of Aldona lost in her love.

The sand and the meadow

The old bard shows Walter the sea-sand burying a coastal meadow: the drifting sand is the Order, the buried herbs the conquered peoples.

The falcon

The caged falcon who knows his homeland as soon as he rises into the sky is Walter returning to Lithuania.

The oak burned from within

The hunters' fire that hollows an oak of Białowieża is the image of vengeance consuming Walter's capacity for love.

The moth in amber

Aldona's image for memory: the beloved kept unchanged in the heart, at the price of never being seen again.

The lamp

The agreed signal between the lovers, which Konrad throws down as he dies. Its last ring of light is the sign of death to Aldona.

Critical debates

Hero or traitor: the problem of 'wallenrodism'

For many nineteenth-century readers Konrad was a model of patriotic self-sacrifice, the man who gives up even his honour for his people. For others his method of deception and betrayal was a moral poison that could corrupt political life. The word 'wallenrodism' has kept both meanings in Polish political language (Janion 1978) (Davies 2005).

Critics such as Weintraub and Miłosz stress that the poem itself is ambivalent: it presents Konrad's deed as necessary and terrible, and shows its cost in drink, despair and the loss of love. The question is whether the poem endorses the strategy, warns against it, or deliberately leaves the matter open (Weintraub 1954) (Miłosz 1983).

Historical poem or political allegory?

Mickiewicz presented the poem as a historical tale drawn from the chronicles of the Order, with a learned preface and notes. Readers in Poland and Lithuania read it at once as an allegory of their own position under Russia, and the poet's situation, an exile writing in Moscow after the Decembrist revolt, made that reading natural (Koropeckyj 2008) (Kleiner 1948).

Some scholars warn against reducing the poem to a coded political message: it is also a Romantic tragedy of love and identity, and its medieval world has its own coherence. The question is how far the historical surface is a disguise, a device for the censor, or a genuine interest in the Lithuanian past (Welsh 1966) (Weintraub 1954).

The Byronic model

The poem's hero, structure and tone owe much to Byron's oriental tales, which were very popular in Poland and Russia in the 1820s. Critics have debated how far Mickiewicz imitates Byron and how far he transforms him. The Byronic hero is usually a rebel against society; Konrad rebels on behalf of a community, and his isolation is a means rather than an end (Weintraub 1954) (Lednicki 1956) (Kridl 1951).

This national turn given to the Byronic tale has been seen as one of Mickiewicz's main contributions to the genre, and as a link between Konrad Wallenrod and the Konrad of Forefathers' Eve, Part III (Krzyżanowski 1978) (Koropeckyj 2008).

The bard and the hero

Halban, the Wajdelote, is the force behind Konrad's plan, and some readers see him as the poem's real protagonist: the poet who shapes the hero's will, masters him like a lion, and survives to make his story a song. On this reading the poem is about the power of poetry to create political action (Koropeckyj 2008) (Walicki 1982).

Others stress the darker side of the relation. Konrad accuses the bard's song of poisoning the infant's soul with the desire for praise and love of country, and the poem can be read as a critique of a national poetry that sends young men to self-destruction (Janion 1978) (Miłosz 1983).

The poem and the November Uprising

The insurrection of November 1830 began with a conspiracy of cadets and young officers in Warsaw, a generation steeped in Romantic poetry. It became a commonplace that Konrad Wallenrod had helped to inspire them, and the poem's reputation as a revolutionary text dates from this time (Davies 2005) (Koropeckyj 2008).

Historians are more cautious about direct influence, since the uprising had many political causes. Mickiewicz himself took no part in the fighting, which weighed on him for years. The connection is better described as part of the poem's legend and reception than as a proven cause (Welsh 1966) (Koropeckyj 2008).

Aldona: voice or emblem?

Aldona can be read as an emblem of the sacrifice the nation demands, a figure whose love and suffering exist to measure the hero's greatness. But she also speaks in her own right, questions Konrad's greatness, refuses his plea to leave the tower, and has the poem's last moment (Welsh 1966).

Recent readers have asked how the poem stages a woman's choice within a male plot of war and vengeance, and whether her walled-up existence criticises or idealises feminine sacrifice (Janion 1978) (Koropeckyj 2008).

Lithuanian or Polish?

The poem is set in Lithuania and its hero is Lithuanian, but it was written in Polish for Polish readers, and the Lithuania it celebrates is in part the Lithuania of the old Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Lithuanian readers have also claimed the poet, as Adomas Mickevičius, and the work has a place in both national traditions (Snyder 2003) (Davies 2005).

Historians of medieval Lithuania describe a powerful pagan state that fought and bargained with its Christian neighbours; Mickiewicz's version, drawn from chronicles and antiquarian lore, is a Romantic reconstruction of that past (Rowell 1994) (Koropeckyj 2008).

Questions for study

  1. How does the introduction's picture of the Niemen prepare the themes of the poem?

    Think about the frontier, the vine and the nightingales, and the promise of the bard's song.

  2. Why does Mickiewicz reveal the hero's past through the Wajdelote's tale rather than by direct narration?

    Consider who understands the song at the feast, and the role of song in preserving memory.

  3. What does the Machiavelli epigraph on the fox and the lion add to a reading of the poem?

    Think of the two ways of fighting, by law and by force, and of which methods Konrad chooses.

  4. Is Konrad a hero, a traitor, or both? Support your answer from the text.

    Look at the bard's argument about slaves and treachery, and at Konrad's judgement of himself in Canto VI.

  5. What is the function of the ballad of Alpujara within Canto IV?

    Compare Almanzor's strategy with Konrad's, and ask why Konrad sings it at that moment.

  6. Does Aldona's role challenge or confirm the poem's celebration of sacrifice for the nation?

    Look at her dialogue with Konrad in Canto III and her refusal in Canto VI.

  7. What is the relationship between Halban and Konrad? Who is in control?

    Recall the lion-keeper simile, the songs, and Halban's choice at the end.

  8. How does the poem portray the Teutonic Order, and how fair is that portrayal?

    Consider the hymns, the feast, the secret tribunal, and what historians say about the Baltic crusades.

  9. Why might the poem have passed the Russian censorship, and why was it nevertheless read as a political text?

    Think of the historical framing, the notes, and the situation of readers in 1828.

  10. What does the poem suggest about the power and the danger of national poetry?

    Compare the Song of the Wajdelote with Konrad's outburst against the bard's 'traitorous song'.
