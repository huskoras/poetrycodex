Overview

Composed
Around 1200, probably in the Danube region between Passau and Vienna; the poem was known by about 1205, when Wolfram von Eschenbach alludes to it in Parzival
Author
Anonymous. A cleric or educated court poet, perhaps in the circle of Wolfger von Erla, Bishop of Passau (1191–1204); the poet never names himself
Language
Middle High German, the literary language of the courts of the Hohenstaufen age
Length
39 'adventures' (aventiuren) and 2,379 strophes in the standard Bartsch–de Boor numbering, which follows manuscript B
Metre
The Nibelungenstrophe: four rhyming long lines (aabb), each divided by a caesura; the last half-line of the strophe has an extra stress, which gives every strophe a weighty close
Manuscripts
More than thirty manuscripts and fragments; the three chief ones are A (Munich), B (St Gall) and C (Karlsruhe, formerly Donaueschingen). A and B end with the 'Not' of the Nibelungs, C with their 'Lied'
Setting
Worms on the Rhine, Xanten in the Netherlands, Brünhild's Iceland, and Etzel's Hungary, reached by the Danube through Passau, Pöchlarn and Vienna
Historical core
The destruction of the Burgundian kingdom on the Rhine by Hunnish troops in 436–437; Attila (Etzel), who died in 453; and Theoderic the Great (Dietrich of Bern), who died in 526
Companion poem
The Klage ('Lament'), a sequel in rhymed couplets describing the mourning and burial of the dead, copied after the Lied in nearly all the complete manuscripts
Key themes
Loyalty (triuwe) and honour (êre); revenge; the clash of courtly manners with heroic violence; deceit and its discovery; treasure and power; the fate of women in a world of men; joy turning to sorrow

Context

The Nibelungenlied was written down around the year 1200 by a poet who did not give his name. Several details point to the Danube country of Austria and Bavaria: the poet knows the route from Passau to Vienna in detail, and he introduces into the story a tenth-century Bishop Pilgrim of Passau as Kriemhild's uncle, a compliment that makes most sense if the work was composed at the court of a later bishop of Passau, Wolfger von Erla, who was a known patron of poets. Wolfram von Eschenbach refers to an episode of the poem in Parzival, so it was in circulation within a few years of 1200 (Heinzle 1987) (McConnell 1998).

The poem is written in strophes of four long lines, each divided by a caesura and rhymed in couplets; the last half-line of each strophe is heavier than the others, and the poet often uses it for a grim forecast of what is to come. This Nibelungenstrophe resembles the lyric strophe of the early Danube love poets, especially the Kürenberger, and it shows that the poet worked within a native German tradition rather than in the French-derived rhyming couplets of the courtly romances. George Henry Needler's English version of 1904, used for all quotations here, keeps the strophic form and marks the caesura with a stroke (Needler 1904). Poetry Codex also holds William Nanson Lettsom's verse translation of 1850, The Fall of the Nibelungers, and Daniel Bussier Shumway's prose translation of 1909 (Lettsom 1850) (Shumway 1909). Among modern versions, A. T. Hatto's Penguin prose translation and Cyril Edwards's Oxford translation are the most widely read in English, and Siegfried Grosse's bilingual edition is a standard way into the Middle High German text (Hatto 1965) (Edwards 2010) (Gentry 1995) (Grosse 1997).

Behind the poem lies history that had been reshaped by centuries of oral tradition. In 436–437 the kingdom of the Burgundians on the middle Rhine, under King Gundaharius, was destroyed by Hunnish troops in Roman service; a Burgundian law code of about 500 names the royal family as Gibica, Gundomaris, Gislaharius and Gundaharius, who correspond to the poem's Gibeche, Gernot, Giselher and Gunther. Attila, who had nothing to do with that defeat, died in 453 on the night of his marriage to a Germanic bride, which gave rise to tales that a woman had killed him in revenge. Theoderic the Great, the Ostrogothic king of Italy who died in 526, entered the story as Dietrich of Bern (Verona), although he was born after Attila died. The figures of Siegfried and Brünhild belong to another strand of legend whose oldest surviving forms are Norse: the heroic poems of the Poetic Edda and the thirteenth-century Völsunga saga, where Sigurd and Brynhild meet a different fate (Haymes 1996) (Andersson 1980) (Byock 1990).

The work survives in more than thirty manuscripts and fragments, which shows how widely it was read in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. The three most important are A (Munich), B (St Gall) and C (formerly at Donaueschingen, now in Karlsruhe). A and B end with the words 'daz ist der Nibelunge nôt', 'this is the Nibelungs' doom', and are called the Not version; C ends 'daz ist der Nibelunge liet' and is a careful revision that tends to blame Hagen and excuse Kriemhild. Nearly all complete manuscripts also contain the Klage, a sequel in rhyming couplets in which the survivors bury the dead and argue about who was to blame (Brackert 1963) (Bumke 1996) (Heinzle 2013).

After the late Middle Ages the poem was forgotten until manuscript C was found at Hohenems Castle in 1755. Once rediscovered, it was quickly hailed as a German Iliad, and in the age of Napoleon and the wars of liberation it became a national epic. Richard Wagner drew mainly on the Norse sources for Der Ring des Nibelungen (first performed complete at Bayreuth in 1876), but the poem's name was attached to his work. In 1909 the German chancellor Bernhard von Bülow spoke in the Reichstag of German 'Nibelungentreue' towards Austria-Hungary, and in January 1943 Hermann Göring compared the army at Stalingrad to the Burgundians fighting to the end in Etzel's hall. This history of use and abuse is now itself a subject of research (Heinzle 1991) (Münkler 2009) (Gentry 2002).

Plot overview

At Worms on the Rhine the beautiful Kriemhild lives under the protection of her brothers, the Burgundian kings Gunther, Gernot and Giselher, and their mighty vassal Hagen of Tronje. She dreams of a falcon killed by two eagles, and her mother tells her it means a noble husband she will lose. Siegfried, prince of Xanten, who has won the treasure of the Nibelungs, a cloak of invisibility and a skin made horn-hard by dragon's blood, comes to Worms to win her. He fights for the Burgundians against the Saxons and Danes and at last meets Kriemhild. In return for her hand he helps Gunther win Brünhild, the warrior queen of Iceland, by defeating her in athletic contests while invisible and pretending to be Gunther's vassal. When Brünhild refuses Gunther on their wedding night and hangs him from a nail, Siegfried, again invisible, overpowers her for him, and takes away her ring and girdle, which he gives to Kriemhild.

Years later, at a feast in Worms, the two queens quarrel over whose husband is greater. Brünhild calls Siegfried a vassal; Kriemhild calls Brünhild Siegfried's concubine and shows the ring and girdle as proof. Hagen, seeing his queen in tears, vows Siegfried's death. He tricks Kriemhild into marking the one vulnerable spot on her husband's back with a cross, and during a hunt he spears Siegfried as he drinks from a spring. Kriemhild suspects Hagen from the first, and the wounds bleed afresh when he approaches the bier. She remains at Worms in mourning; when the Nibelung treasure is brought to her and she begins to win followers with it, Hagen seizes it and sinks it in the Rhine.

Thirteen years after Siegfried's death, the widowed Etzel, king of the Huns, sends the margrave Rüdiger to woo Kriemhild. She accepts only after Rüdiger has secretly sworn to avenge any wrong done to her. After many more years in Hungary she persuades Etzel to invite her brothers to a feast. Hagen foresees what she intends but will not be called a coward. On the journey water-sprites prophesy that only the chaplain will return; Hagen tries to drown him, sees him saved, and destroys the boat. The Burgundians are entertained by Rüdiger at Pöchlarn, where Giselher is betrothed to his daughter, and are warned by Dietrich of Bern on their arrival.

At Etzel's court Hagen openly admits that he killed Siegfried and sits with Siegfried's sword across his knees. Fighting begins when Kriemhild's ally Blödel attacks the Burgundian squires; Hagen strikes off the head of Kriemhild's son Ortlieb, and the Burgundians hold the great hall against the Huns. Kriemhild has the hall set on fire; the survivors drink blood to quench their thirst. Rüdiger, torn between his oath to Kriemhild and his friendship with the guests, fights them and dies with Gernot. Dietrich's men join the fighting and are all killed except old Hildebrand. Dietrich himself overpowers the exhausted Gunther and Hagen and hands them to Kriemhild. When Hagen refuses to reveal the treasure, she has Gunther beheaded and then kills Hagen with Siegfried's sword, and Hildebrand, enraged that a woman has slain such a warrior, cuts her down. Etzel and Dietrich are left weeping among the dead.

Part by part

Part 1: Kriemhild's Dream and Siegfried's Youth

Adventures 1–2

Summary

The poem opens with a single strophe announcing old tales of heroes, feasting, weeping and battle. It then introduces Kriemhild, the most beautiful maiden in Burgundy, who lives at Worms on the Rhine under the care of her mother Ute and her three royal brothers, Gunther, Gernot and the young Giselher. The poet lists the great vassals of the court: Hagen of Tronje and his brother Dankwart, Ortwin of Metz, the margraves Gere and Eckewart, Volker of Alzey, and the court officials Rumold, Sindold and Hunold.

Kriemhild dreams that she has reared a falcon which two eagles tear to pieces. Ute explains that the falcon is a noble husband whom she will lose unless God protects him. Kriemhild replies that she will never marry, since love always ends in sorrow, but the narrator at once tells us that she will marry the falcon of her dream, and that her vengeance for him will cost many lives.

The second adventure moves to Xanten on the lower Rhine, where Siegmund and Sieglind bring up their son Siegfried. He grows into a handsome and much admired young man, and is knighted at a splendid midsummer festival, where gifts are given freely to the guests. He refuses to take the crown while his parents live, preferring to protect their lands.

Analysis

The opening adventure establishes the poem's method. Within a few strophes the narrator tells us the end of the story: the Burgundian kings will die 'beneath two noble women's hate'. Suspense in the Nibelungenlied lies not in what will happen but in how it will happen, and the poet's frequent forecasts give even the brightest scenes a shadow of doom.

Kriemhild's resolve never to marry, and her mother's answer that only love can make a woman happy, set out the poem's central contrast between joy and sorrow (liebe and leit). The poem will end with the same thought: joy always turns to sorrow at the last. The dream itself belongs to a long tradition of prophetic dreams in Germanic legend; its two eagles are usually read as Gunther and Hagen (Mowatt 1967).

Siegfried is introduced not as the dragon-slayer of legend but as a model courtly prince, well educated, generous and admired by ladies. The fantastic deeds of his youth will be told later, at second hand, by Hagen. From the beginning the poet sets the world of the court beside the older world of heroic legend.

Part 2: Siegfried at Worms

Adventures 3–5

Summary

Siegfried hears of Kriemhild's beauty and resolves to win her. Against his parents' advice he rides to Worms with only twelve companions. No one at court knows him except Hagen, who has heard of his deeds: he won the treasure of the Nibelung princes, killed them when they quarrelled with him, took the sword Balmung, overcame the dwarf Alberich and won from him the cloak of invisibility, and bathed in the blood of a dragon so that his skin cannot be wounded. Hagen advises that he be received with courtesy.

Instead of a courteous greeting, Siegfried challenges Gunther to fight for his lands and crown. The Burgundians are angered, but Gernot calms the quarrel, and Siegfried stays at Worms for a year. He excels in the court's games, and Kriemhild watches him secretly from her window, though he does not see her.

When the Saxon king Liudeger and the Danish king Liudegast declare war on the Burgundians, Siegfried offers to lead the army. He takes both kings prisoner, and a messenger brings the news to Kriemhild, who rewards him richly. At the victory feast at Whitsun Gunther allows Siegfried to meet his sister. She appears like the morning sun breaking through clouds, greets him, and for twelve days he walks at her side.

Analysis

Siegfried's challenge is one of the most puzzling moments in the poem. He arrives as a wooer and behaves as a conqueror, claiming the right to seize the lands of the strongest. The scene shows the older heroic world of strength and conquest breaking into a court governed by ceremony, and it leaves behind a distrust that Hagen will remember. Some critics see in it the poet's reworking of an older tale in which Siegfried really did come as a hostile invader (Andersson 1987).

Hagen's account of Siegfried's youth is the only place where the fairy-tale material of dragons, dwarfs and the magic cloak appears in the poem, and it is told as report rather than action. The poet keeps the marvellous at a distance, and its most important legacy is practical: the cloak, the treasure and the single vulnerable spot will all become instruments of the plot.

The first meeting of Siegfried and Kriemhild is courtly love in its classic form: a year of longing without sight, service in war, and a meeting ruled by ceremony. The similes of sun and moon, among the few in the poem, mark the scene as a high point of joy.

Part 3: The Wooing of Brünhild

Adventures 6–8

Summary

Gunther hears of Brünhild, queen of Iceland, who is beautiful and immensely strong: any suitor must defeat her in throwing the spear, hurling a stone and leaping after it, and if he fails in any one contest he loses his head. Siegfried warns him against the venture but agrees to help in return for Kriemhild's hand. Siegfried, Gunther, Hagen and Dankwart sail to Isenstein, and Siegfried instructs his companions to say that he is Gunther's vassal.

Brünhild greets Siegfried first, as if she knew him; he tells her that Gunther is his lord. In the contests Siegfried, made invisible and given the strength of twelve men by his magic cloak, does all the throwing and leaping while Gunther makes the gestures. Brünhild, defeated, tells her people that they are now Gunther's subjects. When she gathers her warriors, the Burgundians fear treachery, and Siegfried sails secretly to his own land of the Nibelungs.

There he tests the loyalty of his men by fighting, in disguise, the giant who guards the gate and the dwarf Alberich, before revealing himself. He returns with a thousand Nibelung warriors, and the company sets out for Worms.

Analysis

The wooing of Brünhild depends on two deceptions: the false claim that Siegfried is Gunther's vassal, and the invisible help in the games. Both are presented as clever expedients, but both will return to destroy their authors. The pretence of vassalage, in particular, is the seed of the queens' quarrel, since Brünhild believes what she is told (Haymes 1986).

Brünhild is the most archaic figure in the poem. Her island realm, her contests and her greeting of Siegfried as someone already known all suggest the older legend, preserved in Norse sources, in which Sigurd had known or been betrothed to Brynhild before he won her for Gunnar. The German poet suppresses that earlier bond but leaves traces of it, which have long fascinated readers (Andersson 1980).

Siegfried's journey to Nibelungenland shows him once more in the role of fairy-tale hero, fighting a giant and a dwarf. Yet the episode also makes clear the scale of his power: he is lord of a kingdom and a treasure far greater than Gunther's, which makes his pretended service all the stranger.

Part 4: The Double Wedding

Adventures 9–11

Summary

Siegfried is sent ahead to Worms to announce the king's return, and Kriemhild receives him gladly. Brünhild is welcomed with great ceremony, and Gunther gives Kriemhild to Siegfried as he had promised. At the wedding feast Brünhild weeps when she sees Kriemhild seated beside a man she believes to be a vassal; Gunther's evasive answers do not satisfy her.

On the wedding night Brünhild refuses Gunther until she learns the truth, ties him hand and foot with her girdle and hangs him on a nail until morning. Gunther confides in Siegfried, who returns the next night in his cloak of invisibility and wrestles Brünhild into submission in the dark. Before leaving he takes her ring and her girdle, and later gives them to Kriemhild. Gunther then lies with Brünhild, and with her virginity she loses her extraordinary strength.

Siegfried and Kriemhild go to Xanten, where Siegmund gives his crown to his son. Kriemhild bears a son named Gunther, and Brünhild a son named Siegfried. For about ten years they live in great prosperity.

Analysis

The bedroom scenes mix comedy and violence. The image of a king hanging helpless on the wall is grotesque, but the second night, when Siegfried overpowers a struggling woman so that her husband may have her, is disturbing, and modern critics have read it as a scene of sexual violence that the poem passes over too lightly. The narrator insists that Siegfried did not take Brünhild's virginity himself, yet the episode creates exactly the suspicion on which the later accusation depends (McConnell 1998).

Siegfried's taking of the ring and girdle is unexplained; the narrator says he does not know whether Siegfried did it out of pride. These tokens, like the cloak and the treasure, are objects that carry the story's guilt from one part to the next. Their transfer to Kriemhild turns a private secret into a public weapon.

Brünhild's loss of strength marks her transformation from the warrior queen of legend into an ordinary queen of the court. From now on she has influence only through words and through men, above all through Hagen.

Part 5: The Quarrel of the Queens

Adventures 12–14

Summary

Brünhild broods on the fact that Siegfried, supposedly Gunther's vassal, never comes to court or pays service. She persuades Gunther to invite Siegfried and Kriemhild to a festival at Worms, and they come with Siegmund and a great retinue.

While watching a tournament, Kriemhild declares that her husband is fit to rule all the kingdoms around. Brünhild answers that Gunther is greater, and that Siegfried himself said he was Gunther's man. The quarrel grows until the two queens meet before the cathedral and Brünhild orders Kriemhild to stand back, since a vassal's wife may not go before a queen. Kriemhild replies that Brünhild is Siegfried's concubine, since he, not Gunther, first possessed her, and she enters the church first. Afterwards she shows the ring and the girdle as proof.

Brünhild weeps and complains to Gunther. Siegfried offers to swear that he never boasted of such a thing, and Gunther accepts his oath without letting him complete it. The two men agree that their wives must be disciplined. But Hagen, finding his queen in tears, vows that Siegfried shall pay for it, and Ortwin and others join him; only Giselher protests. Gunther hesitates, but Hagen keeps whispering that Siegfried's death would bring him many lands.

Analysis

The quarrel is the turning point of the first half. It begins as a rivalry of praise and becomes a dispute about rank, the most serious matter in a feudal society. Both queens have been deceived, and both speak a version of the truth: Siegfried did pose as a vassal, and Siegfried did overpower Brünhild. The scene at the cathedral door turns a private wrong into a public humiliation, and public humiliation demands revenge (Haymes 1986).

The men's response is revealing. Siegfried and Gunther treat the matter as a women's squabble to be settled by beating their wives, but Hagen treats it as a stain on his lord's honour. His question about rearing 'a race of bastards' shows that what is at stake for him is the legitimacy of the royal line. The motives he gives are mixed, however: the narrator also lets him dwell on the lands Gunther would gain.

Critics have asked whether Siegfried's oath is sincere and whether he boasted to Kriemhild of the night with Brünhild. The poem leaves this open, as it leaves open much of what happened in the dark of the bedroom (Mowatt 1967).

Part 6: The Murder of Siegfried

Adventures 15–17

Summary

Hagen arranges for false messengers to announce a new war with the Saxons. Siegfried at once offers to fight, and Kriemhild, fearing for him, asks Hagen as her kinsman to protect him. She tells him that when Siegfried bathed in the dragon's blood a linden leaf fell between his shoulders, leaving one vulnerable place, and at Hagen's suggestion she sews a small silk cross on his tunic to mark it. The war is then called off and a hunt is arranged instead.

Kriemhild dreams of disaster and begs Siegfried not to go. At the hunt in the forest across the Rhine he outdoes everyone and captures a bear alive for sport. No wine has been brought, and Hagen proposes a race to a spring. Siegfried wins, but courteously waits for the king to drink first; as he bends to drink, Hagen spears him through the cross. Siegfried strikes Hagen down with his shield, then falls among the flowers. Dying, he reproaches the Burgundians for their treachery and commends Kriemhild to Gunther's loyalty.

Hagen has the body laid at Kriemhild's door, where she finds it before morning mass and knows at once who is responsible. When the Burgundians deny their guilt, she asks that they approach the bier, and the wounds bleed again as Hagen comes near. Siegfried is buried with great mourning; Siegmund returns home, but Kriemhild stays at Worms.

Analysis

The murder is constructed from acts of trust turned against those who offer them. Kriemhild betrays her husband's secret in order to protect him; Siegfried dies because of his courtesy in letting the king drink first. The narrator underlines both ironies, and he condemns the deed in his own voice: no knight will ever do such evil again (Andersson 1987).

Siegfried's last speech moves from fury to forgiveness of a kind. He accuses his killers of shaming their whole family, then asks Gunther to keep faith with his wife. This appeal to triuwe, loyalty, is bitterly ironic, since loyalty to Gunther and Brünhild is exactly what Hagen claims as his motive.

The bleeding of the corpse in the presence of the murderer, a test known from medieval law and belief, makes Hagen's guilt public. From this point Kriemhild knows, the court knows, and the reader knows; what remains open is only when and how the debt will be paid.

Part 7: The Treasure Sunk in the Rhine

Adventures 18–19

Summary

Siegmund urges Kriemhild to return with him to Xanten and her son, but her brothers persuade her to stay at Worms near Siegfried's grave. Siegmund and the Nibelung warriors ride home in grief.

For more than three years Kriemhild refuses to speak to Gunther. At length, at Hagen's urging, Gunther makes peace with her, so that the Nibelung treasure, her morning-gift from Siegfried, may be brought to Worms. Giselher and Gernot fetch it from the mountain where Alberich guards it; it fills many wagons. Kriemhild gives it away so generously that she begins to win followers, and Hagen warns that she is gathering men against them. When the kings are away he seizes the treasure and sinks it in the Rhine near Loche, and the kings, though angry, forgive him. They swear never to reveal where it lies while any of them lives.

Analysis

The reconciliation of Kriemhild and Gunther is political, not personal: she never forgives Hagen. The treasure makes her dangerous because in this society wealth is power, given away to bind men in loyalty. Hagen understands this perfectly, and his theft of the hoard is a second injury, almost as great as the first (Haymes 1986).

The sinking of the treasure is the origin of the modern legend of the 'Rhinegold'. In the poem it matters chiefly as a secret shared by the Burgundians and as a grievance that will reappear at the end, when Kriemhild demands its return and Hagen answers that only God and he know where it is.

Kriemhild's choice to stay with her kin rather than return to her son is often noted. It leaves the boy Gunther out of the rest of the story and shows that her bond with Siegfried, and with his memory, outweighs every other tie.

Part 8: Etzel's Wooing

Adventures 20–22

Summary

Etzel, king of the Huns, has lost his queen Helche, and his counsellors advise him to marry Siegfried's widow. He hesitates because he is a heathen and she a Christian, but sends the margrave Rüdiger of Bechelaren to woo her. Hagen warns the kings not to agree, since Kriemhild will use Etzel's power against them; Giselher accuses him of malice. Kriemhild at first refuses to marry again. She yields only when Rüdiger privately swears that he and his men will avenge any wrong done to her.

Kriemhild travels down the Danube, staying with her uncle Pilgrim, Bishop of Passau, and with Rüdiger and his wife Gotelind at Bechelaren. Etzel meets her at Tulln, and the wedding is celebrated at Vienna for seventeen days, with lavish gifts. She is received in Etzel's land as a great queen.

Analysis

Rüdiger's oath is one of the most important moments in the poem. He understands it as a general promise of protection; Kriemhild understands it as a promise of vengeance, and she thinks immediately of Siegfried's death. The ambiguity will trap him in the climax of the second half (McConnell 1998).

The poet presents Etzel's court with surprising sympathy. Etzel is mighty, generous and tolerant, and his household includes Christians and heathens, Germans, Russians, Greeks and many other peoples. There is nothing of the savage 'scourge of God' of Latin tradition.

The detailed route of Kriemhild's journey, through Passau, Pöchlarn, Melk, Mautern, Traismauer, Tulln and Vienna, is a main reason scholars place the poet in the Danube region. The welcome given by Bishop Pilgrim in particular looks like a tribute to a patron at Passau (Heinzle 1987).

Part 9: The Invitation and the Journey

Adventures 23–27

Summary

After thirteen years in Hungary, during which she has borne Etzel a son, Ortlieb, Kriemhild still grieves for Siegfried and the treasure. She persuades Etzel, who suspects nothing, to invite her brothers to a midsummer festival, and sends the minstrels Werbel and Swemmel to Worms, telling them to make sure Hagen comes.

Hagen warns that Kriemhild has never forgiven them, but when Gernot and Giselher hint that he is afraid, he insists on going, and on going armed with a large force. Ute dreams that all the birds of the land lie dead. The Burgundians, now called Nibelungs because they possess the Nibelung treasure, set out with thousands of men.

At the flooded Danube Hagen meets water-women who first promise him honour in Hunland and then tell him the truth: none of them will return except the king's chaplain. He kills the ferryman, takes his boat, and ferries the army across himself. To test the prophecy he throws the chaplain overboard and pushes him under; the priest reaches the far bank alive, and Hagen breaks up the boat, knowing that there will be no return. In Bavaria the ferryman's lord Gelfrat attacks them by night and is killed by Dankwart.

At Bechelaren Rüdiger, his wife Gotelind and their daughter entertain the guests with every courtesy. Giselher is betrothed to Rüdiger's daughter, and Rüdiger gives gifts: a sword to Gernot, and to Hagen, at his own request, the shield of Gotelind's dead son Nuodung.

Analysis

The second half of the poem turns on a reversal of roles. Hagen, the murderer of the first half, becomes the far-sighted counsellor who knows that the journey means death and goes anyway, because he will not be thought a coward and will not leave his lords. His destruction of the boat is the moment the Burgundians pass beyond the possibility of return, and it is one of the most memorable gestures in medieval literature (Andersson 1987).

The water-women's prophecy is one of the few supernatural moments in the second half. Hagen's response is characteristic: he does not trust it, tests it with ruthless violence, and accepts what the test reveals. His treatment of the chaplain also shows the limits of the poem's Christianity: the priest survives through God's help, but the heroic world goes on to destruction without him (Heusler 1921).

The stay at Bechelaren is an idyll of courtly hospitality placed just before the catastrophe. Every gift given there will reappear in the fighting: Gernot will kill Rüdiger with the sword Rüdiger gave him, and the betrothal of Giselher binds Rüdiger to the guests by kinship as well as friendship.

Part 10: At Etzel's Court

Adventures 28–33

Summary

Dietrich of Bern rides to meet the Burgundians and warns them that Kriemhild still mourns Siegfried. At court Kriemhild kisses only Giselher, and asks Hagen at once whether he has brought the Nibelung treasure; he answers mockingly that he had enough to carry with his shield and armour. Hagen and the minstrel-knight Volker sit on a bench before the queen's hall and do not rise when she approaches. Hagen lays across his knees Siegfried's sword Balmung, which she recognises, and openly admits that he killed Siegfried. The Huns who were to attack him dare not do so.

That night Volker plays his fiddle to lull the weary guests to sleep, then keeps watch with Hagen at the door, and the armed Huns sent by Kriemhild withdraw. In the morning the Burgundians go to mass in armour. At a tournament Volker kills a Hun, but Etzel keeps the peace.

Kriemhild persuades Etzel's brother Blödel to attack the Burgundian squires, who are lodged apart under Dankwart. Dankwart kills Blödel, but all the squires are slaughtered, and Dankwart, covered in blood, fights his way to the hall where the kings are feasting. Hagen at once strikes off the head of the child Ortlieb, so that it falls into his mother's lap. Fighting breaks out in the hall; Dietrich, given safe-conduct, leads Etzel and Kriemhild out, and Rüdiger also leaves with his men. The Burgundians kill every Hun left inside.

Analysis

Hagen's refusal to rise before Kriemhild is a deliberate breach of court etiquette that announces a state of feud. By displaying Siegfried's sword, the trophy of his crime, he provokes her grief, and by confessing openly he takes the whole guilt upon himself. The scene shows him at his most heroic and at his most brutal, and readers have never agreed whether to admire or condemn him (Mowatt 1967).

Volker, a poet-warrior who wields his fiddle-bow like a sword, is the poem's most attractive invention in its second half. His friendship with Hagen gives the defiant pair a warmth that their cause hardly deserves.

The killing of Ortlieb is shocking, and it has puzzled critics because in the B version Kriemhild seems to have brought the child to the table precisely to provoke violence. The C version, more sympathetic to her, smooths over this point. Either way, the deed makes reconciliation impossible: Etzel, who has so far been neutral, now has his own son to avenge (Heinzle 2013).

Part 11: The Burning Hall and Rüdiger's Dilemma

Adventures 34–37

Summary

The Burgundians throw the bodies of seven thousand dead down the steps of the hall, and Hagen taunts Etzel for his cowardice. Kriemhild offers Etzel's shield full of gold to anyone who will kill Hagen. Iring of Denmark wounds him, but is killed, and his lords Irnfried and Hawart die with their men.

The Burgundians ask for peace or at least for a fight in the open. Kriemhild will grant it only if they hand over Hagen, which Giselher refuses on behalf of all. She has the hall set on fire. Tormented by heat and thirst, the warriors drink the blood of the dead at Hagen's suggestion, and they shelter under their shields from the falling embers. In the morning six hundred are still alive.

Kriemhild and Etzel kneel before Rüdiger and demand that he fulfil his oath. He offers to give back everything he has received from them, and laments that whatever he does he will break faith. At last he arms his men and goes to the hall. Hagen asks him for a shield to replace the one Gotelind gave him, which the Huns have hacked to pieces, and Rüdiger gives him his own. Hagen and Volker refuse to fight him. Rüdiger kills many Burgundians and is then killed by Gernot with the sword he had given him; they die together.

Analysis

The burning of the hall, with its warriors drinking blood, takes the poem beyond the courtly world entirely. Yet the poet does not simply celebrate the warriors' endurance: he lets the reader see what they have become. Kriemhild, who orders the fire, is from this point described as a 'devil' by her enemies, while the Burgundians appear as heroic sufferers. The poem's sympathies have shifted with its plot (Andersson 1987).

Rüdiger's dilemma is the moral climax of the work. His lament states plainly that he cannot satisfy both his oath to his lord and lady and his bonds of friendship and kinship with the guests, and that either choice will bring him shame before God and the world. He chooses service, but dies as the poem's most honourable man. The exchange of the shield, which moves even Hagen to tears, shows courtly generosity surviving at the very heart of the slaughter (Gentry 1975).

The theme of gifts, so prominent at Bechelaren, reaches its conclusion here. The sword given to Gernot kills its giver, and Rüdiger's last gift is the shield he hands to the man he has come to kill.

Part 12: The End of the Nibelungs

Adventures 38–39

Summary

The death of Rüdiger is mourned throughout Etzel's court. Dietrich sends old Hildebrand to ask the Burgundians for Rüdiger's body, but his hot-tempered nephew Wolfhart and the Amelung warriors quarrel with Volker and Hagen, and against Dietrich's orders a battle begins. Hildebrand kills Volker; Wolfhart and Giselher kill each other; Dankwart falls. Every Amelung dies except Hildebrand, who is wounded by Hagen and flees to tell Dietrich. Of the Burgundians only Gunther and Hagen remain.

Dietrich, who has lost all his men, arms himself and goes to the hall. He offers Gunther and Hagen safe-conduct home if they surrender to him; Hagen refuses. Dietrich overcomes first Hagen and then Gunther, binds them and gives them to Kriemhild, asking her to spare them. She has them kept apart and demands of Hagen the return of what he took from her. He replies that he has sworn not to reveal the treasure while any of his lords lives. She has Gunther beheaded and carries his head to Hagen, who answers that now no one knows the treasure but God and himself.

Kriemhild draws Siegfried's sword and beheads Hagen with it. Etzel laments that the best warrior who ever bore a shield should die at a woman's hand, and Hildebrand, enraged, cuts Kriemhild down. Etzel and Dietrich weep for their dead. The poet ends by saying that he can tell no more: joy has turned to sorrow, and this is the Nibelungs' doom.

Analysis

Dietrich is the poem's figure of moderation, who stays out of the fighting as long as he can and offers the defeated an honourable surrender. His failure shows that by now no one can stop the cycle of revenge. His lament that God has forsaken him gives the end of the poem something of a religious lament, though no consolation follows (Haymes 1996).

Hagen's last words turn the treasure into his final victory. Having seen his lord dead, he is free of his oath, yet he still withholds the secret, and he calls Kriemhild a devil. She kills him with the sword of the husband she has avenged; but her revenge, completed, destroys her too. Etzel's and Hildebrand's response shows that even her allies see a woman killing a bound warrior as a breach of the order of the world.

The final strophe sums up the poem's view of history in a proverb: joy always ends in sorrow. Its last words, 'der Nibelunge nôt' in A and B, 'der Nibelunge liet' in C, gave the versions their names. The poem leaves judgment open; the Klage, which follows in the manuscripts, takes up the question of guilt that the Lied refuses to settle (Bumke 1996).

Characters

Burgundians

Kriemhild
Sister of the Burgundian kings, wife of Siegfried and later of Etzel. In the first half she is the ideal courtly lady, beautiful, loving and proud; her boast in the queens' quarrel and her trust in Hagen contribute to Siegfried's death. In the second half she devotes her life to avenging him and recovering his treasure, and is called a devil by her enemies. Whether she is a loving widow destroyed by grief, a monster of revenge, or both, is the poem's most debated question.
Gunther
King of the Burgundians at Worms, eldest son of Dancrat and Ute. He is courteous and generous but weak: he wins Brünhild only through Siegfried, is humiliated on his wedding night, and consents to Siegfried's murder. In the second half he dies as a king among his men, beheaded at his sister's command.
Hagen of Tronje
Gunther's chief vassal and kinsman, the most complex character in the poem. He knows the old stories of Siegfried, plans and carries out his murder, and sinks the treasure in the Rhine. In the second half he foresees the catastrophe, goes to it with open eyes, and becomes the defiant hero of the last stand. Loyal to his lords to the end, he dies by Kriemhild's hand with Siegfried's sword.
Gernot
The second of the royal brothers, brave and moderate. He calms Siegfried's challenge at Worms, but joins the plot against him. At Bechelaren he receives a sword from Rüdiger, with which he later kills Rüdiger as they slay each other.
Giselher
The youngest brother, innocent of Siegfried's murder and loyal to his sister, the only one she kisses on arrival. He is betrothed to Rüdiger's daughter, refuses to give up Hagen, and dies in combat with Wolfhart.
Ute
Mother of Kriemhild and the kings. She interprets Kriemhild's falcon dream and, before the journey to Hunland, dreams that all the birds of the land are dead.
Dankwart
Hagen's younger brother and the Burgundian marshal. He kills Gelfrat on the journey and Blödel at Etzel's court, survives the massacre of the squires and brings the news to the hall.
Volker of Alzey
A noble warrior and minstrel, Hagen's companion and friend in the second half. He plays the guests to sleep, keeps watch at night, wields his bow like a sword, and is killed by Hildebrand.
Ortwin of Metz
A Burgundian vassal, nephew of Hagen, who offers to take vengeance on Siegfried after the queens' quarrel.

Siegfried and Xanten

Siegfried
Son of Siegmund and Sieglind of Xanten, lord of the Nibelung land and its treasure, owner of the sword Balmung and the cloak of invisibility, invulnerable except for one spot between his shoulders. A courtly and generous hero, he wins Kriemhild by serving Gunther, and deceives Brünhild for him twice. He is murdered by Hagen at a spring during a hunt.
Siegmund
King of the Netherlands at Xanten, Siegfried's father. He gives his crown to his son, comes to Worms for the fatal festival, and returns home after Siegfried's death without Kriemhild.
Alberich
The dwarf who guards the Nibelung treasure for Siegfried after being defeated by him and losing his cloak of invisibility. He hands the treasure over when it is brought to Worms.

Iceland

Brünhild
Queen of Iceland (Isenstein), of superhuman strength, who will marry only the man who defeats her in contests. Deceived by Siegfried and Gunther, she loses her strength when the marriage is consummated. Her complaint after the queens' quarrel sets Hagen on Siegfried; after the first half she disappears from the poem.

Huns and their court

Etzel
King of the Huns, the poem's version of Attila. A powerful, generous and tolerant heathen ruler, he marries Kriemhild after the death of his queen Helche and invites her brothers without suspecting her purpose. He is drawn into the fighting only when his son is killed.
Rüdiger of Bechelaren
A margrave in Etzel's service, exiled from his own land, who woos Kriemhild for Etzel and secretly swears to avenge her wrongs. He entertains the Burgundians and betroths his daughter to Giselher. Forced to choose between his oath and his friendship, he fights the guests and dies at Gernot's hand. He is the poem's model of courtly virtue.
Gotelind
Rüdiger's wife, who welcomes Kriemhild and later the Burgundians, and gives Hagen the shield of her dead son.
Ortlieb
The young son of Etzel and Kriemhild, whose head Hagen strikes off at the feast.
Blödel
Etzel's brother, who attacks the Burgundian squires at Kriemhild's bidding and is killed by Dankwart.
Iring
A Danish warrior at Etzel's court who wounds Hagen in single combat and is killed on his second attack.
Werbel and Swemmel
Etzel's minstrels, who carry the invitation to Worms. Hagen strikes off Werbel's hand in the hall.

Others

Dietrich of Bern
An exiled king living at Etzel's court, the poem's version of Theoderic the Great. He warns the Burgundians, stays out of the fighting until all his men are killed, and then captures Gunther and Hagen, whom he hands to Kriemhild in the hope of saving their lives.
Hildebrand
Dietrich's old armourer and mentor, the only survivor of his warriors. He kills Volker, is wounded by Hagen, and at the end kills Kriemhild.
Wolfhart
Hildebrand's fiery nephew, whose insults start the battle between Dietrich's men and the Burgundians. He and Giselher kill each other.
Pilgrim
Bishop of Passau and Kriemhild's uncle, who receives her on her journey to Hunland. The historical Pilgrim was bishop from 971 to 991; his presence is usually taken as a sign that the poem was written at Passau.

Themes

Loyalty (triuwe)

Triuwe, loyalty or good faith, is the virtue on which every relationship in the poem depends: between lord and vassal, husband and wife, brother and sister, host and guest. The tragedy arises because these bonds conflict. Hagen kills Siegfried out of loyalty to his queen and his king; Kriemhild destroys her brothers out of loyalty to her murdered husband; Rüdiger must break one bond to keep another (Gentry 1975).

The poem does not present loyalty as simply good. Hagen's loyalty is unswerving and deadly, and Kriemhild's is so absolute that it consumes everyone she has. The phrase 'Nibelungentreue', later used for patriotic devotion, turns a tragic theme into a slogan the poem itself would hardly support.

Honour and shame (êre)

Êre means both reputation and the material signs of rank. Characters act constantly to preserve it: the queens quarrel over precedence at the church door, Hagen refuses to rise because rising would look like fear, and the Burgundians go to Hunland because refusing would seem cowardly. Public shame is unendurable, and it must be paid for in blood (Haymes 1986).

Rüdiger's lament shows the deepest form of the problem: his honour before the world and his honour before God point in different directions, and he cannot keep both.

Revenge

Revenge structures the whole poem. The first half ends with a murder, the second with the vengeance for it, and each act of violence creates new obligations of revenge: for Siegfried, for the stolen treasure, for Blödel, for Ortlieb, for Rüdiger, for Hagen. By the end, the duty of revenge has destroyed both the Burgundian and the Hunnish courts.

The poem's treatment of Kriemhild's revenge is notably ambivalent. It is presented as justified grief for a real crime, and yet as a demonic force that sacrifices kin, son and guests alike. The poet leaves the reader to weigh the two views.

Court and heroic world

The poem is written for a courtly audience of around 1200 and is full of the courtly world: tournaments, clothing, festivals, gifts, greetings and kisses. But it tells an old heroic story whose values are older and harsher. Again and again the polished surface breaks: Siegfried challenges his hosts, a wedding night becomes a wrestling match, a hunt becomes a murder, a feast becomes a massacre (McConnell 1998).

Some critics see the poem as a critique of courtly culture, which cannot contain the violence it covers; others see it as an attempt to honour the heroic past by dressing it in contemporary forms. Both readings recognise the tension.

Deceit and its discovery

The poem's crimes depend on secrets: the invisible help in the games, the false vassalage, the substitution in the bedroom, the false war, the cross on the tunic, the hiding-place of the treasure. Each secret is eventually exposed, in the queens' quarrel, at the bier, or in the last words of Hagen. The poem shows a world in which deceit is effective in the short term and fatal in the long.

Women and power

The two queens are the most powerful figures of the poem, and both are deprived of power by men: Brünhild by Siegfried's strength and Gunther's deception, Kriemhild by the loss of her husband and her treasure. Each then acts through men, Brünhild through Hagen and Kriemhild through Etzel's warriors. The poem's final judgment, spoken by Etzel and carried out by Hildebrand, is that a woman who kills a hero has broken the order of the world, but modern readers have often found Kriemhild's case more sympathetic than her killers do (McConnell 1998).

Joy turning to sorrow

The poem begins and ends with the idea that joy is followed by sorrow (liebe and leit). Kriemhild says it in the first adventure, and the narrator repeats it in the last. Between these points each festival, from the knighting at Xanten to the wedding at Vienna and the feast in Etzel's hall, becomes the scene or the cause of later grief. This view of the world is closer to medieval reflections on the transience of fortune than to Christian hope.

Motifs

Forecasts of doom

The narrator constantly tells the audience what will happen, often in the last half-line of a strophe: a gift will later cause grief, a man will soon lose his life. These forecasts remove suspense about outcome and replace it with a sense of fate and of the irony of the characters' ignorance.

Prophetic dreams

Kriemhild's falcon dream, her dream before the hunt, and Ute's dream of dead birds before the journey all foretell disaster. They are always understood, and never heeded.

Festivals and feasts

Almost every major event happens at a festival: the knighting at Xanten, the victory feast at Worms, the double wedding, the feast that leads to the quarrel, the wedding at Vienna, and the midsummer festival at Etzel's court. The repeated pattern turns celebration into the setting of catastrophe.

Gifts and their return

Gifts bind people together in the poem and also carry fate. Kriemhild's distribution of treasure makes her dangerous; Rüdiger's gifts at Bechelaren return as the sword that kills him and the shield he gives away; even the ring and girdle are, in a sense, gifts that destroy their givers.

Messengers and reports

Much of the story is driven by messengers, from the youth who tells Kriemhild of the Saxon war to the false messengers of Hagen's plot and the minstrels who carry Kriemhild's invitation. News, true or false, is one of the poem's main instruments of action.

Symbols

The Nibelung treasure

Siegfried's hoard stands for power, since wealth in this society buys followers. It becomes Kriemhild's morning-gift, is stolen by Hagen and sunk in the Rhine, and is finally the secret Hagen dies to keep. It passes its name to whoever holds it: the Burgundians become 'Nibelungs' once they possess it.

The ring and girdle

Brünhild's ring and girdle, taken by Siegfried and given to Kriemhild, represent Brünhild's virginity and her strength, and in the quarrel they become evidence of her shame.

The cross on Siegfried's tunic

The little silk cross sewn by Kriemhild to protect her husband marks the place where he is killed. A sign of faith and loving care becomes the target of treachery.

Balmung

Siegfried's sword passes to Hagen after the murder and is displayed before Kriemhild at Etzel's court. She finally uses it to kill Hagen, so that the sword itself completes the circle of revenge.

Critical debates

The two halves and Kriemhild's character

The poem falls into two halves, the story of Siegfried and the destruction of the Burgundians, which derive from separate legends. Critics have long asked whether the poet made them into a unity, and the question centres on Kriemhild, who seems to change from a loving courtly lady to a ruthless avenger. Some read her as a single consistent character whose love becomes the motive for her revenge; others see in her the seam where two traditions, each with its own Kriemhild, were joined (Andersson 1987) (Mowatt 1967).

In the older tradition preserved in the Norse sources, Gudrun avenges her brothers on Atli; in the German poem Kriemhild avenges her husband on her brothers. This reversal, made somewhere in the German tradition, is what binds the two halves, and much criticism has focused on how plausibly the poet makes it (Haymes 1996).

Hagen: villain or hero?

Hagen is the murderer of the first half and the heroic defender of the second. Nineteenth-century readers often admired him as the embodiment of loyalty; others have seen him as a cold political schemer whose motives include envy and greed for Siegfried's power. The C version blames him more sharply than B. Recent criticism tends to see the poet's portrait as deliberately divided, so that the reader's judgment shifts with the action (McConnell 1998) (Heinzle 1987).

Oral tradition and the literate poet

Andreas Heusler reconstructed the poem's prehistory as a series of earlier lays: a Brünhild lay and a lay of the Burgundians' fall, expanded in stages until the Austrian poet of about 1200 united them in a written epic. His model shaped scholarship for decades, while Theodore Andersson, working against the lay theory, stressed instead the shaping hand of the single poet who adapted inherited stories (Heusler 1921) (Andersson 1987).

Under the influence of oral-formulaic theory, Franz Bäuml and others asked whether the poem itself bears the marks of oral composition, such as its formulas and repetitions, an approach Haymes applied to the poem in 1986. Bäuml argued instead that the text is a written work that imitates the style of oral poetry for an audience that knew such poetry only by hearing. Most scholars now see the Nibelungenlied as a literate composition drawing on a living oral tradition (Bäuml 1980) (Haymes 1986).

The manuscript question

Since the nineteenth century scholars have argued which manuscript best represents the original. Karl Lachmann favoured A; later scholars defended C; and Wilhelm Braune's study of 1900 made B the basis of the standard text edited by Karl Bartsch and Helmut de Boor. In 1963 Helmut Brackert challenged the very idea of recovering a single original, arguing that the versions should be seen as parallel forms of a work that was always being reshaped (Brackert 1963).

Joachim Bumke's work on the Klage developed this into a general theory of medieval 'versions' (Fassungen) of equal standing. Modern editions often present B or C in their own right rather than as witnesses to a lost original, and the differences between them, especially in the judgment of Kriemhild and Hagen, are now studied as interpretations in their own right (Bumke 1996) (Heinzle 2013).

The poem and the Norse tradition

The story of Siegfried and Brünhild survives in fuller form in Old Norse: in the heroic poems of the Poetic Edda and in the Völsunga saga, where Sigurd was first betrothed to Brynhild and his death follows from her jealousy. Scholars have used these sources to reconstruct a common Germanic legend and to explain puzzling features of the German poem, such as Brünhild's prior knowledge of Siegfried. The relation is complex, because the Norse texts survive in manuscripts later than the Nibelungenlied and in places were probably influenced by German tradition (Andersson 1980) (Byock 1990) (Haymes 1996).

Christianity and the heroic past

The poem's characters go to mass, Siegfried's cross becomes a target, and Etzel worries about marrying a Christian as a heathen. Yet the action is governed by honour, revenge and fate, and the only figure saved by God, the chaplain, is excluded from the story's end. Critics disagree whether the poet offers an implicit Christian criticism of heroic values, as the Klage seems to do, or simply presents the old world on its own terms (Mowatt 1967) (Hoffmann 1961).

Nationalism and reception

After its rediscovery the Nibelungenlied was turned into a national epic, issued in field editions for soldiers in the First World War and taught in schools as the German Iliad. Bülow's 'Nibelungentreue' of 1909, Göring's speech on Stalingrad in 1943, and the use of the legend in Nazi propaganda gave the poem a long political afterlife. Since the 1980s scholars have studied this reception critically, distinguishing the medieval poem from the myths built on it (Heinzle 1991) (Münkler 2009) (Gentry 2002).

Questions for study

  1. Why does the narrator reveal the ending of the story in the first adventure?

    Consider the forecasts of doom throughout the poem, the audience's knowledge of old tales, and the effect of irony on scenes of joy.

  2. Is Kriemhild the same character in the two halves of the poem?

    Compare her words to Ute in Adventure 1, her trust in Hagen in Adventure 15, and her actions in Adventures 23 and 39.

  3. What does the poem gain by making Siegfried pretend to be Gunther's vassal?

    Think about Brünhild's tears at the wedding, her complaint in Adventure 12, and the dispute at the church door.

  4. Who is responsible for Siegfried's death?

    Weigh the parts of Brünhild, Kriemhild, Hagen, Gunther and Siegfried himself, including his taking of the ring and girdle.

  5. How does the poet make Hagen sympathetic in the second half without excusing the murder?

    Look at his warnings in Adventure 24, the boat and the chaplain, the scene on the bench, and his response to Rüdiger's shield.

  6. What role do gifts play in the poem?

    Consider Kriemhild's distribution of the treasure, the gifts at Bechelaren, and how they return in Adventures 37 and 39.

  7. Why is Rüdiger's dilemma insoluble?

    Read his lament in Adventure 37 and ask what he owes to Etzel, to Kriemhild, to the Burgundians, and to God.

  8. How does the courtly world of the poem relate to its heroic violence?

    Compare the festivals, tournaments and greetings with the hunt, the burning hall and the drinking of blood.

  9. What does Dietrich of Bern represent?

    Consider his warning on the Burgundians' arrival, his neutrality, his offer to Gunther and Hagen, and his lament for his men.

  10. Why might later readers have turned this tragic poem into a national symbol, and what does that use leave out?

    Think about 'Nibelungentreue' and the last stand in the hall, and compare them with what the poem actually says about loyalty and its costs.
