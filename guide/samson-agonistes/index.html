Overview

Full title
Samson Agonistes: A Dramatic Poem. 'Agonistes' is Greek for a contender or champion in the games, and so also a performer before spectators
Published
1671, in one octavo volume with Paradise Regain'd, printed by J. M. for John Starkey; the title page reads 'Paradise Regain'd. A Poem. In IV Books. To which is added Samson Agonistes'
Composed
Disputed. The traditional view places it after Paradise Lost, in the later 1660s; W. R. Parker argued for a start in the late 1640s; many scholars now settle on the years after the Restoration of 1660
Length
1,758 lines in the standard numbering, including the nine lines (1527–1535) printed in 1671 only in a list of omissions (Omissa) at the end of the volume
Source
Judges 13–16, with the hero's place among the heroes of faith in Hebrews 11:32
Models
Greek tragedy, especially Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus and Ajax and Aeschylus' Prometheus Bound; a prose preface defends tragedy on Aristotelian and Christian grounds
Setting
Before the prison in Gaza, on the festival day of the Philistine god Dagon; the action keeps the unities of place and time ('within the space of 24 hours')
Form
Blank verse for the dialogue, with irregular lyric choruses of varying line length and occasional rhyme; not divided into acts and scenes, and, the preface says, 'never was intended' for the stage
Persons
Samson; Manoa his father; Dalila his wife; Harapha of Gath; a Public Officer; a Messenger; a Chorus of Danites
Shape
A lament, a series of visits, a decision and a catastrophe reported by a messenger, ending in the Chorus's 'calm of mind all passion spent'

Context

Samson Agonistes was published in 1671, in the same small volume as Paradise Regain'd. Milton was sixty-two, blind for nearly twenty years, and living under the restored monarchy whose return in 1660 had ended the cause he served as Latin Secretary to the Commonwealth. The pairing is deliberate. The brief epic shows the Son of God resisting temptation by patient refusal; the tragedy shows a fallen Hebrew champion who, after a day of trials, destroys his enemies and himself. Readers have long argued about what the two works mean side by side (Lewalski 2000) (Knoppers 2008).

When the play was written is uncertain. Milton's early biographers placed it last among his major poems, after Paradise Lost, and this remained the accepted view until 1949, when William Riley Parker argued that it was begun in the late 1640s and largely finished by the early 1650s, in the years when Milton went blind. Parker's case rested on the play's metrical experiments, its closeness to his early plans for a biblical tragedy, and the absence of any evidence that ties it to the 1660s. Most later scholars, studying Milton's metrical habits and the play's apparent engagement with defeat, have returned to a date after the Restoration, though no external evidence settles the matter (Parker 1949) (Parker 1968) (Low 1974) (Lewalski 2000).

The story comes from Judges 13–16: the angel's announcement of Samson's birth to Manoa and his wife, the Nazarite vow that forbade wine and the razor, the marriage at Timnath, the slaughter with the jawbone of an ass, the gates of Gaza, Delilah's betrayal, the blinding, and the death in the temple of Dagon. Milton begins near the end, with Samson already blind and grinding at the mill, and adds much that the Bible lacks. Dalila becomes Samson's wife; Manoa's ransom attempt and the giant Harapha are his inventions; and Samson's biblical prayer to be 'avenged of the Philistines for my two eyes' (Judges 16:28) disappears into the Messenger's uncertain report. Christian tradition had treated Samson as a type of Christ and as a hero of faith, named in Hebrews 11:32 (Krouse 1949).

The form is Greek. Milton's preface, 'Of that sort of Dramatic Poem which is call'd Tragedy', invokes Aristotle's doctrine that tragedy raises pity and fear in order to purge such passions, defends tragedy by the examples of Gregory Nazianzen and of the theologian David Pareus, who read the Book of Revelation as a tragedy in acts, and condemns the English stage for mixing comic matter with tragic. The play keeps the unities, uses a chorus, reserves its violence for a messenger's speech, and recalls in its situation Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus (a blind old hero near death), his Ajax (a shamed warrior who dies by his own act) and Aeschylus' Prometheus Bound (a bound sufferer visited by a series of callers) (Parker 1937) (Lewalski 1970).

The poem's reception has been divided from the start. Samuel Johnson complained in The Rambler that it had a beginning and an end but no middle, since nothing in the visits hastens or delays the catastrophe. Twentieth-century critics answered that the middle is Samson's inner regeneration, worked out through each encounter. More recently the ending has itself become the problem: whether Samson's last act is inspired by God or is a desperate man's revenge, and whether a modern reader should admire it, especially after the attacks of 11 September 2001 (Johnson 1751) (Radzinowicz 1978) (Carey 2002).

Plot overview

On a feast day of Dagon, when the Philistines rest from labour, the blind Samson is led out of the prison in Gaza to sit in the open air. Alone, he laments his blindness and shame and asks why his birth was foretold by an angel if he was to end 'Eyeless in Gaza at the Mill with slaves'. A Chorus of his fellow Danites comes to comfort him; they recall his former feats, and he blames himself for revealing the secret of his strength to a woman, while also blaming Israel's leaders for refusing to follow him.

His old father Manoa arrives, grieving and baffled by God's treatment of his son, and announces that he is negotiating with the Philistine lords for Samson's ransom. He also reports that the feast is a thanksgiving to Dagon for delivering Samson into Philistine hands, and Samson feels most keenly that he has brought dishonour on God. He refuses to be ransomed, wishes only for death, and sinks into despair after Manoa leaves.

Next comes Dalila, richly dressed, who asks forgiveness, offers excuses (weakness, love, and the duty she owed her country and religion), and proposes to take him home and nurse him. Samson rejects her, refusing even to let her touch his hand, and she leaves declaring that her own people will honour her. The giant Harapha of Gath then comes to mock him; Samson challenges him to single combat and drives him off with threats. A Public Officer summons Samson to perform feats of strength at the feast. Samson refuses, then, feeling 'rouzing motions' within, agrees to go, promising to do nothing unworthy of his God or his vow.

Manoa returns, full of hope that the ransom can be arranged. They hear a shout, then a terrible noise. A Hebrew Messenger arrives and relates the catastrophe: Samson performed his feats in the theatre, asked to rest between the two main pillars, stood as one who prayed, then pulled the building down on the Philistine lords and on himself. The Chorus sings of his fulfilled vocation, Manoa resolves to bring the body home and build a monument, and the Chorus ends by declaring that God's servants are dismissed with 'calm of mind all passion spent'.

Part by part

Part 1: Samson's Lament

1–114

Summary

Before the play begins, Milton prints his preface on tragedy, a prose Argument summarising the action, and the list of persons; the scene is 'before the Prison in Gaza'. Samson enters led by an unseen guide, asks to be left on a bank where there is 'choice of Sun or shade', and explains that the Philistines are holding a festival to Dagon, so he is spared his labour for the day. Rest from work only gives his thoughts room to attack him.

He asks why his birth was foretold twice by an angel, and why he was raised as a Nazarite to deliver Israel, if he was to end blind and enslaved. Checking himself, he admits that the prophecy may have failed through his own fault, since he could not keep the secret of his strength. Then he turns to his chief grief, the loss of sight, in the most famous passage of the play: he is 'dark in light', 'a moving Grave', living half dead. Hearing footsteps, he fears enemies come to stare at him.

Analysis

The opening is a soliloquy of self-examination, and its movement from complaint to self-accusation sets the pattern for the play. Samson's first questions accuse providence ('O wherefore was my birth from Heaven foretold'); within a few lines he stops himself and accepts the blame. The tension between these two attitudes, that God has abandoned him and that he has abandoned God, is what the following visits work on.

The lament on blindness draws on Genesis ('Let there be light'), on the speculation that the soul is 'all in every part' of the body, and on the inescapable fact of Milton's own blindness, although the poem never names its author and critics differ on how far to read it as autobiography. The phrase 'Eyeless in Gaza at the Mill with slaves' (41) compresses Samson's fall into a single line of places and conditions; the cry 'O dark, dark, dark, amid the blaze of noon' (80) gave Anthony Low the title of his study of the play's inner drama (Low 1974).

The setting matters too. Samson has been brought from the prison into fresh air, and the play's action never leaves this threshold between confinement and the open world. The holiday that frees his hands also prepares the catastrophe, since it is the same feast of Dagon that will call him to the temple.

Part 2: The Chorus of Danites

115–325

Summary

The Chorus, friends and equals from Samson's tribe of Dan, from Eshtaol and Zora, approach softly and are shocked by the change in him. They recall his feats: the lion torn apart, armies routed by an unarmed man, a thousand slain with the jawbone of an ass at Ramath-lechi, the gates of Gaza carried to the hill by Hebron. Now he is a prisoner twice over, in jail and in his own blind body.

Samson welcomes their visit and condemns himself as a 'foolish Pilot' who wrecked the vessel God gave him by revealing his secret 'for a word, a tear'. The Chorus asks why he married Philistine women. He answers that his first marriage, at Timna, was prompted by God through an 'intimate impulse', as a means to begin Israel's deliverance, and that he thought his marriage to Dalila lawful for the same reason. He also blames Israel's governors, who failed to support his victories and handed him over to the enemy. The Chorus compares the ingratitude shown to Gideon and Jephthah, and ends with an ode justifying God's ways, including his freedom to exempt a chosen servant from his own law.

Analysis

The Chorus speaks in irregular verse, lines that lengthen and shorten without regular stanza, which Milton's preface calls Apolelymenon, 'freed' verse. The effect suits a group that is moved, uncertain and sometimes wrong. They are sympathetic but limited: they think in terms of examples, maxims and consolations, and they often reach for an explanation before the action has earned it.

Samson's account of his marriages raises the question on which the whole play turns: how can anyone know that an inner prompting comes from God? He trusts an 'intimate impulse' for the marriage at Timna, and the Chorus accepts it, arguing that God 'with his own Laws he can best dispence' (314). The same problem returns, much more dangerously, with the 'rouzing motions' of 1382 (Fish 2001).

His complaint against Israel's leaders, who would 'love Bondage more then Liberty' (270), has a political edge that many readers connect with Milton's prose of 1660, The Readie and Easie Way, written as the English people were choosing to restore the king. The parallel is suggestive, not proof of date or intention (Hill 1977).

Part 3: Manoa

326–724

Summary

Manoa, Samson's aged father, arrives and laments the change in his son, asking why God grants prayers only to turn them into curses. Samson answers that his sufferings are just: he alone is to blame for revealing God's secret to Dalila, as he had earlier been betrayed by his wife at Timna. Manoa tells him the worst news: the Philistines are celebrating Dagon as the god who delivered Samson to them. Samson feels this as his chief affliction, but finds hope in the thought that the contest is now between God and Dagon, and that God will not let it rest.

Manoa says he is already negotiating with the Philistine lords for a ransom. Samson refuses, preferring to pay his punishment; Manoa warns him against being 'selfrigorous' and urges him to accept the means God may offer. Samson describes his former pride and fall, his temperance in drink that did not protect him from desire, and his wish only to die. After Manoa leaves, Samson's despair deepens in a lyric outburst on the torments of the mind and the 'sense of Heav'ns desertion'. The Chorus responds with 'God of our Fathers, what is man!', asking why God so often casts down those he has chosen, and then sees a richly dressed woman approaching.

Analysis

Manoa represents a loving, practical and worldly piety. He wants his son home, alive and comfortable, and his hopes, first for ransom and later for a miracle of restored sight, are always a little behind the real action. The irony grows as the play goes on, because the ransom he labours for is overtaken by an act he never imagines.

This episode contains the play's lowest point. Samson's speech at 606–651 on torment of mind is a precise description of what the seventeenth century called despair, and the Chorus's complaint that God treats his elect 'with hand so various' (668) comes close to accusing providence. Critics who read the play as a drama of regeneration see Samson's hope that 'all the contest is now / 'Twixt God and Dagon' (461–462) as the first sign of recovery, since it turns him from his own shame to God's honour (Krouse 1949) (Radzinowicz 1978).

The scene also introduces the language of medicine, of wounds that 'fester' and of 'humours black' (600), that links the drama to the preface's account of tragedy as a homeopathic purging of the passions.

Part 4: Dalila

725–1060

Summary

Dalila approaches like 'a stately Ship / Of Tarsus' with all sails filled. Samson cries 'My Wife, my Traytress, let her not come near me', but she weeps and speaks. She asks forgiveness and offers several defences in turn: weakness, a fault common to her sex and one Samson shared in revealing his secret; love, which made her want to keep him safe at home as her own prisoner; and finally duty, since the magistrates and priests of her country pressed on her the maxim that private feelings must yield to the public good. She offers to obtain his release and nurse him in her house.

Samson answers each plea in turn: all wickedness is weakness; love does not betray; a wife's duty passes to her husband, and a country that asks for treachery has no claim on her. He refuses her offer, calling his prison a house of liberty compared with hers, and will not let her touch his hand. Dalila gives up and declares that, though infamous in Israel, she will be honoured among the Philistines like Jael among the Hebrews. The Chorus sings a long ode on the mystery of woman's love and the man's 'despotic power' over his wife, and then sees a storm approaching.

Analysis

Dalila is the most dramatically alive of Samson's visitors, and her case is argued well. Her final appeal to 'the public good' (867) uses exactly the kind of reasoning Milton himself used in political argument, and her comparison with Jael, who killed Sisera by guile and was praised for it in Judges 5, is a real difficulty for an Israelite audience. Samson's reply depends on the claim that the Philistine cause is unjust and its gods false; within the play there is no neutral ground from which to judge.

Whether Dalila is sincere is left open. The Chorus and Samson judge her a 'manifest Serpent' once she reveals her pride in her future fame, but some critics read her as a genuinely divided woman whose offer of domestic care is real, and who represents the private life that Samson's public vocation sacrifices (Wittreich 1986).

The Chorus's ode on women (1010–1060) is among the most disputed passages in Milton. It ends by claiming that God's law gave the husband 'despotic power' over his wife. Some readers take it as Milton's view; others note that the Chorus has misjudged before and will misjudge again, and that the play may be dramatising, not endorsing, a bitter and partial response.

Part 5: Harapha

1061–1267

Summary

The giant Harapha of Gath arrives, not to condole but to look at the hero whose fame he has heard of, regretting that he never fought him and now cannot win honour by fighting a blind man. Samson challenges him to single combat, offering to meet Harapha in full armour with only an oaken staff. Harapha claims that Samson's strength came from magic; Samson replies that his trust is in the living God and challenges Dagon to support his champion.

Harapha says God has abandoned Samson and calls him a murderer, rebel and robber, citing his killing of thirty men at Askalon. Samson answers that he was not a private person but one raised with command from Heaven to free his country, and that he acted against conquerors. When Harapha refuses to fight a condemned slave, Samson threatens to throw him in the air and dash him down. Harapha leaves, threatening revenge, and the Chorus fears he will stir the lords against Samson.

Analysis

Harapha is Milton's invention. His name comes from the Hebrew of 2 Samuel 21, where the giants of Gath are called sons of 'the giant' (ha-rapha), and Samson later calls him father of five sons, Goliath chief. He is a braggart, a figure close to comedy, and the encounter shows Samson recovering physical courage and confidence in God without any act yet being possible.

The crucial moment comes at 1171–1172, when Samson, for the first time, says that he will 'despair not' of God's 'final pardon'. In the drama of regeneration this is often read as the decisive turn: Samson is ready to act as God's champion again, though he does not know how (Low 1974) (Radzinowicz 1978).

Samson's defence against the charge of rebellion, that he was 'no private but a person rais'd' by God to free his country (1211–1213), uses the language of seventeenth-century debates on resistance to tyranny. Readers who see the play as a work of the defeated republic hear in it Milton's own defences of the regicide (Hill 1977) (Loewenstein 2001).

Part 6: The Officer and the 'Rousing Motions'

1268–1440

Summary

The Chorus sings of God's deliverers, who quell oppressors with heroic might, but adds that patience is more often the exercise of saints; Samson may be either. A Public Officer arrives to command Samson to appear at the feast and show his strength before the lords. Samson refuses: Hebrew law forbids his presence at idolatrous rites, and he will not be made a fool or jester for them. The Officer leaves, warning that the lords will be offended.

The Chorus points out that he already serves the Philistines with his strength; Samson distinguishes honest labour from performance in honour of Dagon. Then, suddenly, he tells the Chorus to take courage: he feels 'Some rouzing motions' that dispose his thoughts to something extraordinary, and will go with the Officer, doing nothing to dishonour his law or his vow. The Officer returns with a harsher summons, Samson agrees, bids his friends farewell, and leaves. The Chorus prays that the angel of his birth may go with him.

Analysis

This is the hinge of the play. Samson's refusal is principled and reasoned; his acceptance follows a change he cannot explain except as an inner prompting. Earlier, he reasons that God may 'dispense' with him for 'some important cause' (1377–1379), so that the decision to go to the temple is not a simple breach of law, though he cannot know the cause in advance.

The 'rouzing motions' (1382) are the central crux of interpretation. For readers in the tradition of Krouse, Low and Radzinowicz they are the Spirit of God returning to a regenerate man, as in Judges 13:25, when 'the Spirit of the Lord began to move him'. For Stanley Fish the point is that neither Samson nor the reader can verify them: the play forces us to act, as Samson does, without proof. For Joseph Wittreich they are dangerously ambiguous, and the play invites us to question Samson's certainty (Krouse 1949) (Fish 2001) (Wittreich 1986).

The double movement, from 'I will not come' to 'I am content to go', also has political overtones. Samson's apparent submission to 'Masters commands' (1404) masks an independent purpose, and readers have compared it with the situation of Nonconformists after 1660, who had to decide when outward conformity was lawful (Achinstein 2003).

Part 7: The Messenger and the Close

1441–1758

Summary

Manoa returns, delighted, and reports his progress with the lords: some refused a ransom, others wanted bribes, some were willing. He resolves to spend his whole inheritance and to tend his son at home, and hopes God will restore Samson's sight. A great shout interrupts him, and then a terrible noise. The Chorus imagines Samson, his sight restored by miracle, dealing slaughter among his foes; Manoa calls this joy presumptuous.

A Hebrew Messenger arrives, distraught, and in brief exchanges reveals that Gaza stands but its nobility has fallen, killed by Samson, who is dead by his own hand. He then gives the full account: the theatre with two main pillars, Samson's feats, his request to rest between the pillars, his stillness 'as one who pray'd', his speech to the lords, and the collapse of the roof on all beneath him. The Chorus and semichoruses sing of his 'dearly-bought revenge' and compare his virtue to the phoenix reborn from its ashes. Manoa declares that Samson has 'quit himself / Like Samson', that there is nothing here for tears, and resolves to build him a monument. The Chorus closes the play in a rhymed sonnet-like stanza on God's unsearchable wisdom.

Analysis

As in Greek tragedy, the violence happens offstage and is narrated. The Messenger reports only what he saw, and what he heard from those 'nearer'. He does not know whether Samson prayed or only 'some great matter in his mind revolv'd' (1638), and Samson's words to the lords are ironic: the 'other tryal' of his strength 'of my own accord' (1643–1644) is announced as his own choice. Milton has removed the biblical prayer for vengeance, so the reader must judge Samson's motives from the outside, as the Chorus and Manoa do.

The responses to the catastrophe are themselves part of the drama. The Chorus first calls the event a 'revenge'; the semichoruses turn to images of the dragon, the eagle and the phoenix; Manoa thinks of honour, freedom and fame; and the final chorus speaks of God's providence. Each interpretation is partial, and critics differ on whether the play endorses or questions them (Wittreich 1986) (Shawcross 2001).

The last line, 'And calm of mind all passion spent', answers the preface's account of tragedy as tempering and reducing the passions 'to just measure with a kind of delight'. The servants of God, characters and audience alike, are dismissed 'With peace and consolation', having passed through pity and fear (Radzinowicz 1978).

Characters

Samson and his people

Samson
The Nazarite champion of Israel, of the tribe of Dan, chosen by God from birth to begin Israel's deliverance from the Philistines. Blind, enslaved and in despair at the opening, he passes through a series of visits that test his shame, his anger and his faith, until he goes to the temple of Dagon and pulls it down on the Philistine lords and himself. Whether he is regenerated, deluded or both is the central question of the play.
Manoa
Samson's aged father, to whom the angel announced Samson's birth. Loving, practical and hopeful, he seeks to ransom his son and later to bring him home to an honoured old age. He grieves over God's apparent cruelty, and at the end declares that Samson has 'quit himself / Like Samson' and resolves to build him a monument.

Chorus and messengers

The Chorus
Men of Dan, Samson's 'friends and equals' from Eshtaol and Zora, who stay on stage throughout as in Greek tragedy. They comfort, question, misjudge and interpret Samson's experience; their odes on God's justice, on women and on patience are among the most discussed passages in the play. At the end they split into semichoruses.
The Messenger
A Hebrew, an eyewitness of the catastrophe, who reports the destruction of the theatre in the long speech that ends the action. Like messengers in Greek tragedy, he narrates what cannot be shown.

Philistines

Dalila
Samson's Philistine wife (in Judges she is not called his wife), who betrayed the secret of his strength to the lords. She comes to ask forgiveness and offer him a home, and defends herself with arguments from weakness, love and patriotic duty. Rejected, she leaves confident of fame among her own people. Her sincerity is one of the play's open questions.
Harapha
A giant of Gath, invented by Milton, of the race of Og and Anak and said to be father of Goliath. He comes to look at Samson and mock him, refuses his challenge to combat, and leaves threatening revenge.
Public Officer
A messenger of the Philistine lords, who twice summons Samson to perform at the feast. His second, threatening message meets Samson's consent after the 'rouzing motions'.
Dagon
The 'Sea-Idol' of the Philistines, whose festival gives the play its occasion. He never appears, but Samson and Manoa see the day as a contest between Dagon and the God of Israel.

Themes

Regeneration and inner change

The play's action takes place largely inside Samson. He begins in self-pity and despair, accepts responsibility, resists temptations to ease (Manoa's ransom, Dalila's home), recovers courage and hope (with Harapha), and finally receives or believes he receives a new impulse from God. Many critics read this as a Protestant drama of repentance and regeneration, in which each visit is a trial that prepares him for the final act (Krouse 1949) (Low 1974) (Radzinowicz 1978).

The structure that Johnson thought empty is, on this view, the substance of the play. Yet the change is never stated, only inferred, and the evidence can be read differently. The play leaves room for doubt about how far Samson has truly changed.

Providence and justice

The Chorus says 'Just are the ways of God' and then complains that God treats his champions 'with hand so various'. Manoa asks why God's gifts turn into curses. The question of Paradise Lost, how to justify God's ways, here becomes a question asked by people who cannot see the end. Only the final chorus claims that all is best 'in the close', and even it admits that God 'seems to hide his face'.

The play shows providence working through human choices and misunderstandings: the feast, the summons and Samson's own decisions all lead to the catastrophe without any visible divine intervention.

Blindness and inner light

Blindness is Samson's worst affliction and the play's richest image. The Philistines are blinded by idolatry and drink; Samson, 'though blind of sight', is 'With inward eyes illuminated'. The contrast between outer darkness and inner light draws on a Christian tradition that Milton had used of himself in Paradise Lost and the sonnets, though the play never makes the connection explicit.

Liberty and servitude

Samson moves between kinds of bondage: the prison and the mill, his former 'servitude' to Dalila, the slavery of nations that 'love Bondage more then Liberty'. He calls the jail a 'house of Liberty' compared with Dalila's home, and finds freedom in obeying God rather than men. The theme links the play to Milton's political writing and to the experience of those defeated in 1660 (Hill 1977).

Marriage and the sexes

Samson's two Philistine marriages, his betrayal and Dalila's visit make marriage a central subject. Samson's bitterness, the Chorus's ode on women, and Manoa's final regret at his son's 'nuptial choice' present a harsh view; Dalila's speeches give the other side some force. The play's treatment of gender is now one of its most debated aspects.

Heroism and fame

Samson is a hero in the Old Testament mode, a man of strength who wins by violence. Harapha represents a degraded version of heroic honour; Dalila expects to be famous in Philistia; Manoa wants a monument and 'sweet Lyric Song'. The play asks what kind of heroism is left to a blind slave, and whether patience or action is the true fortitude.

Motifs

Visits and trials

Samson receives a sequence of visitors, as Prometheus does in Aeschylus' play. Each tempts or tests him in a different way, and their order, from friends and father to wife, enemy and master, has been read as a sequence of temptations to despair, sensuality, pride and conformity (Parker 1937).

Riddles and secrets

Samson's riddle at his wedding, the secret of his strength and the 'Secret refreshings' the Chorus hopes for connect knowledge, trust and betrayal. 'my riddling days are past', he says, but the play ends with an act whose meaning is still a riddle.

Noise and report

Much of the play happens offstage and is known through sounds and stories: footsteps, shouts, the 'hideous noise', the Messenger's account. Fame and report, 'double-mouth'd', shape how Samson and Dalila will be remembered.

Fire and the phoenix

From the angel who ascended in flames at Samson's birth to the 'fierie vertue rouz'd / From under ashes', fire marks divine presence and renewal. The phoenix, the 'self-begott'n bird', is the semichorus's emblem of virtue reborn.

Symbols

Samson's hair

The pledge of his Nazarite vow and the seat of his strength, shorn by Dalila and now grown again. Its regrowth gives Manoa hope and suggests to readers that God's favour has returned, though the hair is a sign, not the source, of strength.

The mill

Grinding at the mill, the work of slaves and animals, is the emblem of Samson's degradation. The mill's circular labour contrasts with the decisive act at the end.

The theatre and its pillars

The building where Samson dies is described as 'a spacious Theatre'. Samson brings down the place of spectacle where he was to be shown, a detail that has been read as a comment on idolatrous display and on the theatre that Milton's preface criticises (Knoppers 1994).

Light and noon

Samson's 'blaze of noon' becomes the high noon of the feast at which he acts. Light, lost outwardly, returns as inward illumination.

Critical debates

The date of composition

The early biographers, and especially Jonathan Richardson in 1734, placed Samson Agonistes after Paradise Lost, and this order was long assumed. In 1949 W. R. Parker argued that the play was begun in 1646–47 and largely finished by 1653, pointing to Milton's early plans for biblical tragedies in the Trinity manuscript, to the play's metrical irregularity, and to the lack of positive evidence for a late date. He restated the case in his biography and in an essay of 1971 (Parker 1949) (Parker 1968) (Wittreich 1971).

Others answered that the metrical evidence points the other way and that the play's concern with defeat, captivity and the uncertain return of God's favour fits the years after 1660. Most recent biographers and editors favour a Restoration date, while acknowledging that it cannot be proved (Low 1974) (Lewalski 2000) (Knoppers 2008).

The poem and the Restoration

Christopher Hill read the play as Milton's response to the defeat of the English Revolution: Samson, captive and blind among the Philistines, figures the defeated godly cause, and the destruction of the Philistine lords expresses the hope that God might again act through his servants. Later historicist critics refined this reading. David Loewenstein emphasised the play's radical religious politics and its apocalyptic violence; Laura Lunger Knoppers connected the Philistine feast with the spectacles of the restored monarchy, including the public punishment of the regicides; Sharon Achinstein placed it among the writings of the Nonconformists (Hill 1977) (Loewenstein 2001) (Knoppers 1994) (Achinstein 2003).

Such readings are persuasive but indirect, since the play never refers to contemporary events. They depend partly on the late date, and they leave open whether the play urges action, patience or a mixture of both.

Samson as terrorist?

In September 2002, a year after the attacks of 11 September, John Carey asked in the Times Literary Supplement whether Samson Agonistes was 'a work in praise of terrorism'. Carey, whose edition of the shorter poems is a standard text (Carey 1997), took issue with Stanley Fish's argument in How Milton Works that the play's value lies in Samson's willingness to act on an inner conviction that cannot be verified. Carey argued that this makes Samson's act indistinguishable from that of the hijackers, and that the play itself is far more uncertain, allowing the reader to doubt that the impulse comes from God (Carey 2002) (Fish 2001).

The debate drew on earlier work. Joseph Wittreich had already argued that Milton's play questions the tradition of Samson as a hero of faith; Feisal Mohamed argued that critics should confront, not explain away, the play's sympathy with divinely sanctioned violence; Janel Mueller related Samson to the experience of London Nonconformity in the 1660s; Michael Lieb studied the culture of violence in Milton more broadly (Wittreich 1986) (Wittreich 2002) (Mohamed 2005) (Mueller 2002) (Lieb 1994).

Regeneration and providence

For much of the twentieth century the standard view was that the play depicts Samson's regeneration. F. Michael Krouse showed how the Christian tradition treated Samson as a saint and type of Christ; Anthony Low read the play as a tragedy of the soul's recovery; Mary Ann Radzinowicz traced the play as a summation of Milton's thought, in which Samson learns through each encounter (Krouse 1949) (Low 1974) (Radzinowicz 1978).

Wittreich and others challenged this view, arguing that Milton's Samson is a flawed figure whose violence the play exposes, and that the Chorus and Manoa misread the ending. John Shawcross argued that the play deliberately leaves the reader in uncertainty. Fish sits between the camps: he denies that the play gives evidence, but treats Samson's acting without evidence as faith (Wittreich 1986) (Shawcross 2001) (Fish 1989).

Dalila and gender

Dalila's speeches give the play's most eloquent counterargument, and readers have increasingly asked whether she is a hypocrite, a patriot, or a woman whose love is real but misdirected. The Chorus's ode on women and Manoa's final regret present a harsh view of women, and it is unclear whether these represent Milton's own opinion or the limited perspective of the characters. Feminist and historicist critics have read the scene in terms of the division between public and private life (Wittreich 1986) (Achinstein 2003).

Tragedy, structure and catharsis

Johnson's objection that the play has no middle was answered by critics who saw the visits as a necessary inner progress. The preface's theory of catharsis, which purges passions 'by raising pity and fear', and the final line 'calm of mind all passion spent' have encouraged readings of the whole play as a demonstration of tragic purgation, for the characters as well as the reader (Johnson 1751) (Radzinowicz 1978).

Barbara Lewalski connected the preface's reference to Pareus with a tradition that read Revelation as a tragedy, suggesting that Milton's tragedy has an apocalyptic dimension. The relation between classical form and biblical content, between Greek tragic fate and Christian providence, remains central to the play's criticism (Lewalski 1970) (Parker 1937).

Questions for study

  1. Johnson said the play has a beginning and an end but no middle. Is he right?

    Consider what changes in Samson across the visits of Manoa, Dalila and Harapha, and whether any of them makes the catastrophe possible.

  2. How does Milton use the conventions of Greek tragedy, and where does he depart from them?

    Look at the preface, the Chorus, the unities, the Messenger's speech, and the role of providence instead of fate.

  3. What is the significance of Samson's blindness in the play?

    Compare the opening lament with the semichorus's 'inward eyes illuminated', and think about the Philistines' blindness.

  4. Are the 'rouzing motions' from God? How does the play let us decide?

    Compare the earlier 'intimate impulse' for the marriages, the Messenger's 'as one who pray'd', and the responses of Manoa and the Chorus.

  5. How convincing is Dalila's defence of herself?

    Weigh her three arguments (weakness, love, public duty), Samson's replies, and her comparison with Jael.

  6. What is the function of Harapha?

    Think about heroic honour, Samson's recovery of courage, and his claim to have been raised by God to free his country.

  7. In what ways might Samson Agonistes reflect the political situation after 1660?

    Consider Samson's criticism of Israel's leaders, the theme of captivity, the feast of Dagon, and whether the date affects the reading.

  8. Is Samson's final act heroic, tragic, or terrible? Can it be all three?

    Look at the Chorus's 'dearly-bought revenge', Manoa's verdict, and the debate begun by Carey in 2002.

  9. How does the Chorus shape our understanding of the action?

    Consider its odes on God's justice, on women and on patience, and whether its judgments are always reliable.

  10. What does 'calm of mind all passion spent' mean in relation to the preface on tragedy?

    Compare the preface's account of catharsis with the final chorus, and ask whose passions are spent.
