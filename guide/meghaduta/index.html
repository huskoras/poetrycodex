Study guide

The Cloud Messenger (Meghaduta)

by Kalidasa

Open the interactive study guide →

Overview · Part by part · Characters · Themes · Critical debates · Key passages · Questions · Further reading

Overview

Author
Kalidasa, the most admired poet and dramatist of classical Sanskrit; probably active in the later fourth or the fifth century CE, though his dates are uncertain
Language and form
Classical Sanskrit kavya (court poetry); a single poem of a little over a hundred stanzas, the exact number varying between about 110 and 120 in the different recensions
Metre
Mandakranta ('slowly advancing'), a stanza of four lines of seventeen syllables each, with a fixed pattern of long and short syllables, used throughout
Parts
Two: the Former Cloud (Purvamegha), describing the cloud's route north, and the Latter Cloud (Uttaramegha), describing Alaka, the wife and the message itself
Speaker
An unnamed yaksha, a semi-divine servant of Kubera, god of wealth, exiled for a year for neglecting his duty; apart from the opening stanzas and a closing line in some versions, the whole poem is his speech
Setting
Ramagiri, a hill in central India where the yaksha lives in exile, and Alaka, Kubera's city in the Himalayas near Mount Kailasa, where his wife waits
Genre
The model for the duta-kavya or sandesha-kavya, the 'messenger poem', imitated in Sanskrit and in many Indian languages for more than a thousand years
Key themes
Love in separation (viraha); memory and imagination; the monsoon as the season of longing; landscape seen as a lover; friendship and the duty to help; time and the ending of a curse
Translation quoted
Arthur W. Ryder (1912), in rhymed five-line stanzas; Ryder sometimes condenses or expands the Sanskrit, and two stanzas of the Former Cloud survive only in part in the text used here

Context

Almost nothing certain is known about Kalidasa's life. Later Indian legend made him a court poet of a king Vikramaditya of Ujjain, and many scholars connect him with the Gupta dynasty, often with Chandragupta II (reigned c. 375–415 CE), whose title was Vikramaditya. The firm evidence is slighter: an inscription at Aihole dated 634 CE already names Kalidasa as a famous poet, and the polish of his Sanskrit, the political world of his plays and his evident knowledge of central and northern India fit the Gupta age well. Most scholars therefore place him somewhere in the fourth or fifth century CE (Keith 1928) (Warder 1977) (Mirashi 1969).

Six works are generally accepted as his: three plays, the Malavikagnimitra, the Vikramorvashiya and the famous Abhijnanashakuntala (the recognition of Shakuntala); two long court epics (mahakavya), the Raghuvamsha on the dynasty of Rama and the Kumarasambhava on the birth of Shiva's son; and the Meghaduta. A poem on the six seasons, the Ritusamhara, is attributed to him but its authorship is doubted. Across this body of work Kalidasa became, for later Sanskrit critics, the model of a poet whose ornaments never obscured feeling, and his plays were among the first Sanskrit works to reach Europe (Miller 1984) (Lienhard 1984).

The Meghaduta belongs to kavya, the learned poetry of the courts, which valued compressed expression, elaborate figures of speech (alamkara) and the evocation of a dominant aesthetic mood (rasa). Its mood is the erotic (shringara) in its form of separation (vipralambha or viraha), which Sanskrit theorists regarded as the most moving variety of love poetry. The whole poem is composed in one metre, the mandakranta ('slowly advancing'): each line has seventeen syllables in a fixed pattern, four long syllables, then five short, then the group long-long-short twice, and two final longs, with pauses after the fourth and tenth syllables. Its heavy opening and slow pace suit a lover's lament, and later critics singled out Kalidasa's handling of it. The poem is short, but it carries a great deal of learning: myth, geography, the customs of cities and villages, and the conventions by which Sanskrit poets read nature as a set of signs of love (Ingalls 1965) (Gerow 1971).

The text has come down in many manuscripts and with numerous commentaries. The earliest surviving commentary is that of Vallabhadeva of Kashmir (probably tenth century), whose text is somewhat shorter than the version read by Mallinatha, the fourteenth- or fifteenth-century South Indian commentator whose text became the standard one in modern printed editions. Scholars agree that some stanzas were added by later hands, but they disagree over which; S. K. De's critical edition of 1957 attempted to sort the evidence (Hultzsch 1911) (De 1957).

The first English translation, by Horace Hayman Wilson, appeared in Calcutta in 1813, and Goethe, who read it, praised the poem warmly. Arthur W. Ryder, who taught Sanskrit at the University of California, Berkeley, published the version quoted here in 1912 in a volume of Kalidasa translations. Ryder renders the four-line Sanskrit stanzas as rhymed English stanzas of five lines, the last an alexandrine, and keeps the poem's stanza-by-stanza structure, though he sometimes simplifies its proper names and mythological allusions (Wilson 1813) (Ryder 1912).

Plot overview

A yaksha, one of the semi-divine attendants of Kubera, the god of wealth, has neglected his duties and has been cursed by his master to spend a year in exile, far from his young wife. He lives among the hermitages on Ramagiri, a hill in central India hallowed by memories of Rama and Sita. After some months of grief, at the beginning of the rainy season, he sees a great cloud resting on the peak. Distracted by longing, he decides to send it as his messenger to his wife in Alaka, Kubera's city in the Himalayas.

In the first part, the Former Cloud, the yaksha greets the cloud with flowers and flattery and then describes the route it must take northwards: across the Vindhya hills and the Narmada river, through the country around Vidisha, by a deliberate detour to the city of Ujjain and its temple of Shiva, across a series of rivers each imagined as a woman awaiting her lover, over the old battlefield of Kurukshetra, to the place where the Ganges comes down from the mountains, and finally through the Himalayan pass to Mount Kailasa and the city of Alaka.

In the second part, the Latter Cloud, he describes the splendours of Alaka, a city of endless pleasure, and then his own house and garden, by which the cloud will recognise it. He imagines his wife wasted with grief, counting the days, trying to sing or to paint his portrait, and lying awake at night. He then gives the cloud the message itself: he lives, he suffers as she does, he sees her likeness everywhere in nature, the curse will end in four months, and their love has only grown in absence. He closes by asking the cloud to grant his request and wishing that it may never be parted from its own beloved, the lightning.

Part by part

Part 1: The Former Cloud: The Journey North

stanzas 1–63

Summary

The poem opens in the third person. An 'erring' yaksha, exiled for a year by his master, lives on Rama's peak; months of separation have made him so thin that his bracelet slips from his wrist. When the first cloud of the rains appears on the mountain, playfully butting the slope like an elephant, he is overcome with longing and resolves to send it to his wife with news that he lives. The narrator notes the absurdity, a messenger made of 'fire and water, smoke and wind', and excuses it: lovers cannot tell living things from lifeless ones (stanzas 1–5).

From stanza 6 the yaksha speaks. He praises the cloud's noble family and its power to take any form, explains his plight, and names the destination: Alaka, city of the yakshas, lit by the moon on Shiva's head. He promises good omens, the company of cranes and of swans flying north to the Himalayan lake, and asks the cloud first to take leave of the mountain on which it rests (6–13).

He then lays out the route. The cloud will be welcomed by farmers' wives on the plateau of Mala, rest on the Mango Peak, see the Reva (Narmada) spreading across the Vindhya rocks, and pass the hills and villages of Dasharna and its royal city. He urges it to turn aside to Ujjain, to watch the women on its balconies and to serve as Shiva's drum at the evening worship in the great temple there. Further north it will pass the Deep River (Gambhira), the hill of Skanda, the river that recalls the sacrifices of King Rantideva, the land of the Ten Cities, the sacred country of Kurukshetra where Arjuna fought, the Ganges in its descent from the mountains, the snowy Himalaya, the Swan-gate in the range, and Mount Kailasa. Beyond lies Alaka, seated on the mountain's lap like a woman with clouds for hair (14–63).

Analysis

The opening stanzas establish the poem's central fiction and also apologise for it. A cloud cannot carry a message, and the poet says so plainly before defending the yaksha's folly as the natural condition of a lover. The device frees the poem to do what it most wants to do: to look at the whole of the subcontinent through the eyes of a man in love. The references in the first stanza to Rama and Sita also matter. Rama, separated from Sita, sent the monkey Hanuman to her with a token in the Ramayana, and the yaksha's situation quietly repeats the most famous story of separated lovers in Indian tradition (Nathan 1976) (Mallinson 2006).

The route is not a list of places but a sequence of encounters, almost all of them erotic or devotional. Mountains are friends who offer hospitality, the earth becomes a woman whose breast the cloud crowns, and the rivers are women who receive the cloud as their returning lover: the dwindling stream 'that brides deserted wear in single braid' (Former Cloud, st. 29) is a picture of the abandoned wife whom the reader will meet in the second part. In this way the journey constantly anticipates its goal, and the cloud's pleasures along the road are a rehearsal of the reunion the yaksha cannot have (Ingalls 1965).

The detour to Ujjain is the most discussed passage of the Former Cloud. The yaksha admits that the city lies off the direct route, yet insists that the cloud must not miss it, and he lingers over its women, its temple and its night life longer than over any other place on the way. Many readers have seen here the poet's own affection for a city with which tradition associates him. The shrine of Shiva and the later passages on Kailasa also show how the poem's eroticism coexists with piety: the cloud is invited to serve the god, to dance with him at twilight and to honour his footprint, so that the journey is also a pilgrimage (Keith 1928) (Mirashi 1969).

Part 2: The Latter Cloud: Alaka and the Message

stanzas 1–52

Summary

The second part begins with a description of Alaka in a long series of stanzas each opening with 'where'. The palaces rival the cloud itself, with lightning-bright women, drums like thunder and towers that touch the sky; flowers of all the seasons bloom there at once, the wishing-tree supplies wine, clothes and ornaments, and lovers enjoy pleasures that leave their traces at dawn in fallen flowers and broken necklaces (stanzas 1–11).

The yaksha then guides the cloud to his own house north of Kubera's palace, with its rainbow gateway, the little coral-tree his wife has raised like a child, the pool with its emerald steps, the pet peacock that dances when she claps her hands, and the conch and lotus painted by the door. He asks the cloud to shrink to the size of a young elephant and look in. He describes his wife as she must now be: pale and thin, her hair in the single braid of a woman separated from her husband, singing a song about him and forgetting the words, asking her caged bird whether it remembers its master, counting the months with flowers laid on the floor, and lying awake through the night (12–35).

Finally he gives the cloud the words it is to speak. The cloud must first tell her that she is 'no widow' and that her husband's friend has come. The message proper says that he lives and suffers as she does; that he sees her limbs, glances and hair in vines, deer and peacock feathers but never her whole likeness; that he paints her on a rock and cannot see the picture for tears; that he embraces the north wind because it may have touched her. He reminds her that fortune turns like a wheel, that the curse will end when Vishnu wakes from his sleep in four months' time, and he recalls a private memory, a jealous dream she once had, as a token that the message is genuine. The poem ends with his plea to the cloud and his blessing on it and its lightning (36–52).

Analysis

Alaka is a paradise of pleasure, and its description is deliberately excessive. Yet the abundance of the city sharpens the contrast with the one house in it where nothing is enjoyed. The descriptions of the garden and the house move from public splendour to private memory: the plants the wife tends and the bird she feeds are named with the affection of a man who knows every detail of a home he cannot enter. The painted conch and lotus, auspicious signs of Kubera's wealth, lose their charm, he says, when he is away, as the lotus closes at sunset (Nathan 1976).

The portrait of the waiting wife is one of the classic images of viraha in Sanskrit literature, and it follows the conventions by which the theorists described the separated heroine: thinness, pallor, sleeplessness, neglect of ornament, the single braid, attempts at distraction that only renew grief. Kalidasa's achievement is to make these conventions intimate. The details are imagined by the yaksha, not observed, so that every sign of her suffering is also a projection of his own. The reader sees two separated lovers through one mind (Ingalls 1965) (Lienhard 1984).

The message itself is surprisingly restrained. After the elaborate journey and the description of the city, it is simple in content: I am alive, I think of you, the separation will end. Its central figure, the lover who finds his beloved scattered in parts of the natural world but never whole, turns the whole poem's method into a confession, since the Former Cloud too has seen woman everywhere in the landscape. The private memory of her dream, used as a proof of identity, ends the message on a note of shared intimacy rather than grief. The poem never says whether the cloud delivers it (Mallinson 2006).

Characters

Speaker

The yaksha
An unnamed attendant of Kubera, banished for a year for neglecting his duty. The poem does not say exactly what his fault was; later commentators supplied stories, for example that he left his post to be with his wife. He is passionate, learned, courteous to the cloud and wholly absorbed in his longing, and almost the entire poem is his speech. His knowledge of the route, the gods and the city makes him a kind of poet within the poem.

Messenger

The cloud
A great monsoon cloud, addressed as a friend, a brother and a noble person of high birth able to take any form it likes. It never speaks. The yaksha imagines it as a traveller, a lover of the rivers, a worshipper of Shiva and, in the end, the husband of the lightning. It is the poem's means of travel and also its silent audience.

Alaka

The yaksha's wife
Also unnamed, she appears only in the yaksha's imagination: young, beautiful, faithful and wasted with grief. He describes her trying to pass the time, painting his image, singing about him, counting the months, and sleepless at night. She is both the goal of the journey and a model of the separated heroine of Sanskrit love poetry.
Kubera
God of wealth and lord of the yakshas, whose capital is Alaka. He is the yaksha's master and the source of the curse, but he never appears. The poem mentions him as a distant authority whose 'royal wrath' (Former Cloud, st. 7) has caused the separation, and whose gardens and palace dominate Alaka.

The gods

Shiva
The great god whose presence runs through the poem: his crescent moon lights Alaka, his temple at Ujjain receives the cloud's thunder as a drum, his footprint and his mountain Kailasa lie on the route. He and his consort Parvati (Gauri) are the divine model of wedded love that the poem's human lovers lack.

Myth

Rama and Sita
The hero and heroine of the Ramayana, who lived in exile and were later separated when Sita was abducted. They do not appear in the action, but Ramagiri, the yaksha's place of exile, is named after Rama, and their story of separation and messages stands behind the poem.

Themes

Love in separation (viraha)

Sanskrit theory divided erotic poetry into love in union and love in separation, and held the second to be the richer source of feeling. The Meghaduta is its classic expression. Nothing happens in the poem except longing: the lovers are apart at the beginning and still apart at the end, and the whole work consists of what the separated lover imagines, remembers and hopes.

Separation in the poem is creative. It drives the yaksha to invent the cloud's journey, to picture his wife, to paint her portrait and to compose his message, so that the poem itself is presented as a product of absence. His final claim, that love grows rather than dies in separation, turns suffering into a kind of proof of love (Ingalls 1965).

Nature as lover

The landscape of the poem is full of desire. Mountains, rivers, trees and birds respond to the cloud as lovers or as friends, and the language of the body is applied to the earth throughout. This is not a private eccentricity but a convention of kavya, in which seasons and natural phenomena have fixed emotional associations, and the rainy season is above all the time when separated lovers suffer and travellers hurry home.

Kalidasa uses the convention with unusual consistency. Because every feature of the landscape is seen through the yaksha's longing, the poem's descriptions of nature are also descriptions of his mind, and the parallel between the rivers waiting for the cloud and the wife waiting for her husband binds the two halves of the poem together (Nathan 1976).

Imagination and memory

Almost everything in the poem is imagined. The yaksha has not seen the route from the cloud's height, cannot see his wife, and does not know how the cloud will be received. The poem repeatedly reflects on this: the wife draws his image and talks to a bird, he paints her on a rock and sees fragments of her in the world. Images are what separated lovers have, and they are never enough.

Memory gives the imagination its material. The account of the house and garden, and the closing recollection of the wife's jealous dream, rest on remembered detail, and the poem suggests that such intimate memories are the only reliable token of identity and of love.

Friendship and service

The yaksha's appeal to the cloud is framed in the language of friendship and of the duty of the great to help those in distress. He flatters it, promises it pleasures, and reminds it that 'kindness is its own reward' (Latter Cloud, st. 38). The poem thus links the private world of love to a public ethic of generosity, which it also illustrates in the hospitality offered to the cloud by the mountains it serves with rain.

Motifs

Women and rivers

The rivers of the Former Cloud are imagined as women, flirtatious, shy, deserted or eager, who receive the cloud as a lover. The motif anticipates the wife of the Latter Cloud and turns the geography of India into a sequence of love scenes.

Birds

Cranes and swans accompany the cloud, peacocks dance to its thunder, the chataka bird is said to live on raindrops, and the wife keeps a pet starling and a peacock. Birds act as companions and as images of mated or separated pairs.

Shiva and his worship

Shiva's temples, moon, mountain and footprint recur along the route and in Alaka. The motif gives the journey a devotional dimension and offers, in Shiva and Parvati, a picture of united love.

Signs and tokens

The house is to be recognised by signs, the conch and lotus by the door and the coral-tree; the message is to be believed because of a private memory. The motif reflects the old use of tokens in messenger stories, such as the ring Hanuman carries to Sita in the Ramayana.

Symbols

The cloud

The monsoon cloud brings rain to a parched land and is the traditional sign that travellers should return home. It stands for fertility, renewal and hope, and also for the poem itself, which travels from the lover to the beloved.

The lightning

The cloud's 'bride', always with it. The yaksha's closing wish that the cloud may never be parted from its lightning measures his own loss against a union that nature makes permanent.

The single braid

A wife whose husband was away traditionally wore her hair in one plain braid, to be loosened only on his return. It marks the rivers of the Former Cloud and the wife of the Latter Cloud as women in separation.

The lotus

The lotus opens by day and closes at night. In the poem it marks beauty, the auspicious wealth of Kubera's city, and in the yaksha's comparison of his wife to a lotus withered by frost, the effects of grief.

Critical debates

Kalidasa's date

Estimates of Kalidasa's date once ranged from the first century BCE to the sixth century CE. The older tradition linked him with a King Vikramaditya and a legendary era of 57 BCE; most modern scholars favour the Gupta period of the fourth and fifth centuries CE, because of his language, the cultural world of his works and the inscriptional evidence that he was famous by the early seventh century (Keith 1928) (Mirashi 1969).

No contemporary source names him, so the question cannot be settled with certainty. The Meghaduta has been used as evidence: its affectionate treatment of Ujjain has suggested to some readers that the poet lived there, and its geography of central India has been compared with the extent of Gupta rule. These arguments are suggestive rather than conclusive (Warder 1977).

The text and its interpolations

The poem's popularity encouraged imitation within the text itself. The versions known to the commentators differ in length: the Kashmiri commentator Vallabhadeva knew a shorter text than Mallinatha, whose version underlies most printed editions. Several stanzas are absent from the older witnesses and are widely regarded as additions (Hultzsch 1911) (De 1957).

Deciding which stanzas are genuine is not merely a technical matter, since the added stanzas often elaborate the erotic or descriptive passages and so affect the proportions of the poem. Translators have had to choose a text, and their versions differ in length: Ryder's has 115 stanzas, 63 in the first part and 52 in the second. Editions with the Sanskrit printed beside the English, such as the Edgertons', allow students to see the problem for themselves (Edgerton 1964) (Mallinson 2006).

The real geography of the route

The cloud's route can largely be traced on a map: the Narmada, Vidisha, Ujjain, the Chambal, Kurukshetra, the Ganges at Kanakhala and the Himalayan passes are real places, and many of the poem's names have been identified with confidence. The starting point, Ramagiri, is disputed; it is most often identified with Ramtek near Nagpur, though other sites associated with Rama have been proposed (Mirashi 1969).

Critics also debate how far the route should be read literally. The detour to Ujjain shows that the order of places is shaped by the poem's interests as well as by geography, and the journey has been read as a celebration of the cultural map of northern India, joining court, temple, village and sacred battlefield into a single world. This fits a wider argument that Sanskrit court poetry imagined a shared cultural space reaching far beyond any one kingdom (Pollock 2006).

Eroticism and devotion

The poem's sensuality, in its descriptions of women, rivers and the pleasures of Alaka, was fully in keeping with Sanskrit court taste, but it has been read in different ways. Some commentators and modern readers stress its devotional passages on Shiva and treat the journey partly as a pilgrimage; others see the religious references as part of the decorative and cultural richness of court poetry rather than as a spiritual allegory (Lienhard 1984).

European readers of the nineteenth century often found the poem's frankness surprising, and early translators sometimes softened it. Modern translators have generally tried to restore its physical immediacy while keeping its tenderness (Nathan 1976) (Schwab 1984).

Does the message arrive?

The poem ends with the yaksha's request and blessing; it does not say whether the cloud agrees or delivers the message. Some versions add a final stanza stating that the curse was lifted and the lovers reunited, but such endings are generally regarded as later additions (De 1957).

The open ending can be read as part of the poem's meaning. The work exists entirely in the space of hope and imagination between separation and reunion, and its value for the speaker lies in the act of speaking. The silence of the cloud leaves the reader, like the yaksha, waiting (Nathan 1976).

Goethe and European reception

H. H. Wilson's English verse translation of 1813 made the Meghaduta one of the first Sanskrit poems known in Europe after the Shakuntala. Goethe read it with enthusiasm; in the notes to his West-östlicher Divan he named it among the Indian works he most admired, and the stanza of his later poem in honour of the meteorologist Luke Howard that invokes 'Camarupa', the Indian power of taking any shape at will, is usually traced to Wilson's notes on the cloud's 'changing forms' (Wilson 1813) (Goethe 1819) (Willson 1964).

For German Romantic writers the poem confirmed an image of India as a land of natural poetry and tender feeling, an image that told as much about European longings as about Sanskrit literature. Later scholars have stressed instead the poem's learning and artifice as court poetry, and in India its reception never depended on Europe: it was imitated, commented on and retold continuously, and modern writers such as Rabindranath Tagore returned to it as a classic of their own tradition (Schwab 1984) (Willson 1964).

Origins of the messenger poem

Kalidasa did not invent the idea of a messenger between lovers. The Ramayana tells of Hanuman carrying Rama's message and ring to Sita, and the Mahabharata contains the story of a swan carrying messages between Nala and Damayanti. The Meghaduta's first stanza alludes to Rama and Sita, and the parallel with Hanuman was noted by the commentators (Mallinson 2006).

What the Meghaduta seems to have created is a genre: a self-contained poem in which a lover describes a messenger's route and then the message. Later poets copied its structure closely, often in the same metre, replacing the cloud with the wind, a swan, a cuckoo, a bee or a parrot, and some poets in Jain tradition even wove the lines of the Meghaduta into new poems on other subjects (Lienhard 1984) (Warder 1977).

Key passages

Questions for study

  1. Why does the poet admit, in the fifth stanza, that a cloud is an unsuitable messenger?

    Consider how the admission prepares for the way the rest of the poem treats nature, and what it says about the lover's state of mind.

  2. How does the Former Cloud's description of rivers as women prepare for the portrait of the wife in the Latter Cloud?

    Look at the river with its 'single braid' (Former Cloud, st. 29) and compare the wife's braid in Latter Cloud, st. 29.

  3. Why does the yaksha insist on the detour to Ujjain?

    Think about what the city offers the cloud, about devotion as well as pleasure, and about what readers have inferred about the poet.

  4. What is the function of the long description of Alaka before the yaksha reaches his own house?

    Consider contrast: public pleasure and private grief, a city of lovers and one separated household.

  5. The wife is described entirely through her husband's imagination. How does this affect our view of her?

    Ask whose feelings the portrait expresses, and whether the conventions of the separated heroine leave room for an individual.

  6. What is the role of the gods, especially Shiva, in the poem?

    Look at Ujjain, Kailasa and Alaka, and at Shiva and Parvati as a model of married love.

  7. Why might the yaksha end his message with the memory of his wife's jealous dream?

    Think about tokens of identity in messenger stories and the difference between grief and intimacy.

  8. Is the poem's ending, with no word on whether the message is delivered, a weakness or a strength?

    Consider the later stanzas that supply a happy ending and what the poem gains by remaining in the time of waiting.

  9. How does Ryder's rhymed five-line stanza compare with what you can learn about the mandakranta metre of the original?

    Think about pace, length and the effect of rhyme, which Sanskrit verse does not use.

  10. Why do you think the Meghaduta became the model for a whole genre of messenger poems?

    Consider the structure of journey and message, the scope it gives for description, and the adaptability of the messenger figure.
