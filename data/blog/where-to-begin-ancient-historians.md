*In brief: begin with Herodotus, then read Thucydides and Xenophon's Anabasis. Move to Rome with Livy's first book and Caesar's Gallic War, and then read Tacitus. Keep Plutarch and Suetonius beside you for the people, and finish with the late historians, Josephus, Ammianus and Procopius.*

## Why read them in order

The ancient historians read each other. Thucydides wrote partly against Herodotus. Xenophon began his *Hellenica* at the point where Thucydides breaks off. Polybius wrote for Greeks who wanted to understand Rome. Tacitus and Suetonius cover the same emperors in very different ways, and Ammianus chose to start his history where Tacitus had ended. If you read them roughly in the order they wrote, each one makes the next easier to follow. All of the translations below are complete for what survives, and all are in the public domain.

## 1. The Greeks

**Herodotus** (fifth century BC), from Halicarnassus in Asia Minor, is often called the father of history. His *Histories* tell how the Persian Empire grew and how the Greeks fought off its invasions in 490 and 480–479 BC. Read **[G. C. Macaulay's translation (1890)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/herodotus-histories-macaulay/)**. It opens with Herodotus stating his purpose, "to the end that neither the deeds of men may be forgotten by lapse of time". Start with Book I, which has the story of Croesus and Solon, and then read Books VII to IX, on Xerxes' invasion, Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea. The middle books, including Book II on Egypt, are full of travellers' tales and can be read later. ([Study guide](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/herodotus-histories/))

**Thucydides** (fifth century BC) was an Athenian general who was exiled after he failed to save the city of Amphipolis in 424 BC. He used his long exile to write the history of the war between Athens and Sparta. The history is unfinished and breaks off in 411 BC. Thucydides is harder than Herodotus but more gripping. He wrote, in Richard Crawley's words, "a possession for all time". The site has two translations:

- **[Richard Crawley (1874)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/thucydides-peloponnesian-war-crawley/)** is clear Victorian prose and the best version to start with.
- **[Thomas Hobbes (1629)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/thucydides-history-hobbes/)** was the philosopher's first published book. It is dense and old-fashioned, but strong. Hobbes calls the work "an everlasting possession".

Begin with Book II, which contains Pericles' Funeral Oration and the plague at Athens. Then read the Melian Dialogue in Book V, where the Athenian envoys say that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must", and the Sicilian expedition in Books VI and VII. ([Study guide](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/thucydides-peloponnesian-war/))

**Xenophon** (c. 430–c. 354 BC) was an Athenian who joined a Greek mercenary army in Persian service. His **Anabasis** is the best adventure story in ancient prose. Ten thousand Greek soldiers hired by the Persian prince Cyrus are stranded deep in Asia after Cyrus is killed at the battle of Cunaxa in 401 BC, and they fight their way north to the Black Sea. When they finally see it, in Book IV, the cry goes down the column, in Dakyns's version: "The sea! the sea!" Read **[Carleton L. Brownson's Loeb translation (1921–22)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/xenophon-anabasis-brownson/)**, or **[H. G. Dakyns's (1890)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/xenophon-anabasis-dakyns/)**, which has a little more colour. Afterwards, try the **[Hellenica in Brownson's translation](https://poetrycodex.com/work/xenophon-hellenica-brownson/)**, which carries the story of Greece on from 411 to 362 BC, and the **[Memorabilia](https://poetrycodex.com/work/xenophon-memorabilia-dakyns/)**, Xenophon's memoir of Socrates.

**Polybius** (c. 200–c. 118 BC) was a Greek statesman who was taken to Rome as a hostage in 167 BC and became a friend of the Roman general Scipio Aemilianus. His *Histories* explain how Rome came to rule almost the whole known world in "not quite fifty-three years". Of his forty books only the first five survive complete. Read **[Evelyn S. Shuckburgh's translation (1889)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/polybius-histories-shuckburgh-1/)**, starting with Book I and then Book III, on Hannibal's war, including his crossing of the Alps. Book VI, on the Roman constitution, was much read by later political thinkers. The later books, which survive in fragments, are in **[the second volume](https://poetrycodex.com/work/polybius-histories-shuckburgh-2/)**.

## 2. The Romans

**Livy** (c. 59 BC–AD 17) wrote the history of Rome from its founding in 142 books. Thirty-five survive: Books I to X and XXI to XLV. Read **[Canon W. M. Roberts's Everyman translation (1912–24)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/livy-history-of-rome-roberts-1/)**. Begin with the Preface and Book I, the age of the kings, with Romulus and Remus, the Sabine women and Lucretia. In the Preface Livy says that history shows "examples of every possible type", to imitate or to avoid. Then turn to the war with Hannibal in Books XXI to XXX, which begin in **[Roberts's second volume](https://poetrycodex.com/work/livy-history-of-rome-roberts-2/)**. ([Study guide to Books I–V](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/livy-history-of-rome/))

**Julius Caesar** (100–44 BC) wrote his own campaigns. **[The Gallic War, translated by W. A. McDevitte and W. S. Bohn (1869)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/julius-caesar-gallic-war-mcdevitte/)**, covers his conquest of Gaul from 58 to 52 BC. It speaks of Caesar in the third person, in plain, fast prose, and it begins with the famous sentence "All Gaul is divided into three parts". Read Book I and then Book VII, the great revolt of Vercingetorix and the siege of Alesia. Book VIII was added after Caesar's death by his officer Aulus Hirtius. ([Study guide](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/julius-caesar-gallic-war/)) **[The Civil War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/julius-caesar-civil-war-mcdevitte/)**, in the same translation, tells his side of the war with Pompey.

**Sallust** (86–c. 35 BC) wrote two short monographs, which make him the quickest Roman historian to finish. **[The Conspiracy of Catiline](https://poetrycodex.com/work/sallust-catiline-watson/)** is the story of the plot of 63 BC that Cicero exposed. **[The Jugurthine War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/sallust-jugurthine-war-watson/)** is about Rome's war with a North African king who bribed his way through the Senate, and who calls Rome, as he leaves it, "a venal city". Both are in John Selby Watson's translation (1852). Sallust is a moralist, and his theme is Rome's decline through greed and ambition.

**Tacitus** (c. AD 56–c. 120) is the greatest Roman historian and the most difficult. Begin with the short **[Agricola](https://poetrycodex.com/work/tacitus-agricola-church-brodribb/)**, the life of his father-in-law, who governed Britain. It contains the speech of the British leader Calgacus, who says of the Romans, "they make a solitude and call it peace". Then read **[the Annals](https://poetrycodex.com/work/tacitus-annals-church-brodribb/)**, which run from the death of Augustus in AD 14 to the reign of Nero, with large gaps. Tacitus promises to write "without either bitterness or partiality", and the reader must decide whether he kept his word. Read Books I to IV on Tiberius, and Books XIII to XV on Nero, which contain the fire of Rome in AD 64. Both are in the translation by A. J. Church and W. J. Brodribb (1876). ([Study guide](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/tacitus-annals/)) For **the Histories**, on the civil wars of AD 69, the "year of the four emperors", read **[W. Hamilton Fyfe's translation (1912)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/tacitus-histories-fyfe/)**.

**Suetonius** (c. AD 70–after 122) was an imperial secretary under Hadrian. **[The Lives of the Twelve Caesars](https://poetrycodex.com/work/suetonius-twelve-caesars-thomson/)**, in Alexander Thomson's translation as revised by T. Forester (1855), runs from Julius Caesar to Domitian. Suetonius is a biographer, not a historian. He arranges each life by topic (family, career, habits, appearance, death) and he loves gossip. Read the Julius Caesar, with the crossing of the Rubicon, and the Augustus first, then Nero. The same file holds the surviving lives of grammarians, rhetoricians and poets, including short lives of Terence and Horace.

## 3. Plutarch, for the people

**Plutarch** (c. AD 46–after 119), a Greek from Chaeronea, wrote the *Parallel Lives*, which set a famous Greek beside a famous Roman and then compare the two. He was a moralist more than a historian. He says, in the opening of his *Alexander*, that he writes not histories but lives, and that a small thing, a phrase or a jest, can show a man's character better than a battle. Shakespeare built *Julius Caesar*, *Antony and Cleopatra* and *Coriolanus* from Plutarch, in Thomas North's English.

The site has two complete versions:

- **Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb translation (1914–26)**, accurate and readable, which is on the site pair by pair. Start with **[Alexander and Caesar](https://poetrycodex.com/work/plutarch-lives-alexander-caesar-perrin/)**, then read **[Demetrius and Antony](https://poetrycodex.com/work/plutarch-demetrius-antony-perrin/)** and **[Dion and Brutus](https://poetrycodex.com/work/plutarch-lives-dion-brutus-perrin/)**, the lives behind Shakespeare's Roman plays.
- **The "Dryden" translation**, made by many hands under Dryden's name in 1683–86 and revised by Arthur Hugh Clough, in four parts beginning with **[Part I](https://poetrycodex.com/work/plutarch-lives-dryden-clough-1/)**. It is older and more stately, and it is good for reading straight through.

Plutarch can be read at any point in this path. He is best used as a companion: after Thucydides, read his *Pericles*; after Caesar, his *Caesar*.

## 4. The late historians

**Josephus** (AD 37–c. 100) was a Jewish commander in the revolt against Rome who surrendered in AD 67 and lived on under the patronage of the emperors Vespasian and Titus. His **[Jewish War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/josephus-jewish-war-whiston/)** is an eyewitness history of the revolt. Read Books V and VI, on the siege and fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, and Book VII, which ends with the fall of Masada. The translation is William Whiston's (1737), the standard English Josephus for two centuries. His **[Antiquities of the Jews](https://poetrycodex.com/work/josephus-antiquities-whiston/)** retells Jewish history from the Creation, and his short **[Life](https://poetrycodex.com/work/josephus-life-whiston/)** is a defence of his own career.

**Ammianus Marcellinus** (c. 330–c. 395) was a Greek from the East who served as an officer in the Roman army and wrote his history in Latin. It began, like a sequel to Tacitus, with the emperor Nerva, but the first thirteen books are lost, and what survives covers AD 353 to 378. Read **[C. D. Yonge's translation (1862)](https://poetrycodex.com/work/ammianus-marcellinus-roman-history-yonge/)**. Books XXI to XXV tell the reign of the emperor Julian and his fatal Persian campaign of 363, and Book XXXI ends with the Gothic victory at Adrianople in 378. In his last chapter Ammianus describes himself as "a Greek by birth, and formerly a soldier".

**Procopius** (sixth century AD) was the secretary of Justinian's general Belisarius, and he saw much of what he describes. The site holds his *History of the Wars* in **H. B. Dewing's Loeb translation (1914–19)**: the **[Persian War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/procopius-wars-persian-dewing/)**, the **[Vandalic War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/procopius-wars-vandalic-dewing/)** in Africa, and the first two books of the **[Gothic War](https://poetrycodex.com/work/procopius-wars-gothic-dewing/)** in Italy. Start with Book I of the Persian War, which includes the Nika riot of 532 in Constantinople. With the city in revolt, the empress Theodora refuses to flee and quotes an old saying, "royalty is a good burial-shroud".

## A suggested order

1. Herodotus, Books I and VII–IX
2. Thucydides, Books II, V and VI–VII
3. Xenophon, *Anabasis*
4. Livy, Preface and Book I
5. Caesar, *Gallic War*
6. Sallust, *Catiline*
7. Tacitus, *Agricola*, then the *Annals*
8. Suetonius, the Julius Caesar and Augustus, with Plutarch's *Caesar* beside them
9. Josephus, *Jewish War*, Books V–VII
10. Ammianus, Books XXI–XXV and XXXI
11. Procopius, *Persian War*, Book I

Polybius belongs after Livy for readers who want to know why Rome won, and the rest of Plutarch can be read whenever you meet one of his subjects. Tacitus and Suetonius show the emperors from the outside. For one emperor seen from the inside, read the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius in **[George Long's translation](https://poetrycodex.com/work/marcus-aurelius-meditations-long/)**. ([Study guide](https://poetrycodex.com/guide/marcus-aurelius-meditations/))

## A note on the translations

These translations were made between 1629 and 1926, and the older ones use the forms of names that were usual in their day. Several of them use the Latin or anglicised spellings, and Whiston's English is that of the eighteenth century. The Loeb translations (Brownson, Perrin and Dewing) were made to be printed opposite the Greek, and they are the closest to the original. Where a book survives only in fragments, the work page says so.
