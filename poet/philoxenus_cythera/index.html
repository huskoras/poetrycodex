Philoxenus of Cythera

c. 435 – 380 BC

Philoxenus of Cythera was a Greek poet of the dithyramb, the choral song of the festivals of Dionysus. Born on the island of Cythera, he was enslaved as a child when the Athenians took the island, was brought up by one master and educated by another, the lyric poet Melanippides, and made his name at Athens as one of the leading figures of the so-called New Music, which loosened the old forms with elaborate melody and daring compound words. He is credited with some twenty-four dithyrambs and a genealogy of the Aeacids in lyric verse.

His best-known work was the Cyclops, or Galatea, in which the one-eyed Polyphemus sings and plays his lyre to win the sea-nymph Galatea, a comic and tender fancy that Aristophanes and later writers loved to quote and parody. Ancient anecdote connects it with the poet's stay at Syracuse at the court of the tyrant Dionysius, whom he is said to have offended and who is said to have imprisoned him in the stone-quarries; there, the story runs, he turned the quarrel into the Cyclops, with the tyrant as the monster. He was equally famous for his love of good food, and his Banquet, a long description of a dinner party, survives in generous fragments quoted by Athenaeus.

He died at Ephesus, about 380 BC. This archive carries the fragments of his poems as printed in volume III of J. M. Edmonds's Lyra Graeca in the Loeb Classical Library (1927), each with the ancient writer's framing remarks that preserved it.
