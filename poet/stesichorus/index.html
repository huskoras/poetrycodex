Stesichorus

c. 630 – c. 555 BC

Stesichorus of Himera, in Sicily, was one of the great choral poets of archaic Greece, active in the late seventh and early sixth centuries BC. Ancient tradition gave his name a meaning, 'he who sets up the chorus', and said that he was the first to fit a whole narrative to the strophe, antistrophe and epode. His lyrics were composed in a Doric dialect for performance by a trained chorus, and were collected by Alexandrian scholars in twenty-six books.

Where earlier lyric had been short and personal, Stesichorus sang long mythological narratives in a manner close to epic: the Tale of Geryon and the cattle of Heracles, the Funeral Games of Pelias, the Sack of Troy, the Returns from Troy, the Tale of Orestes, and shorter pieces such as the Boar-Hunters and the Eriphyle. He is also known for the tale of Daphnis the herdsman and the love-story of Rhadine, in which later writers saw the beginnings of pastoral and romance.

His most famous story is the Palinode on Helen. He was said to have been struck blind for slandering her in his first Helen, and to have recovered his sight by singing a recantation: that she never went to Troy, and that only a phantom of her was fought over there. Only fragments of his work survive, most of them quoted by later authors; this archive carries them as translated by J. M. Edmonds in volume II of Lyra Graeca (Loeb Classical Library, 1924), where each fragment is printed with the passage of the citing author, and the bracketed restorations are Edmonds's own.
