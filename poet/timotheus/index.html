Timotheus of Miletus

c. 446 – c. 357 BC

Timotheus of Miletus, the son of Thersander according to the Suda, was a lyric poet of the late fifth and early fourth centuries BC, born in the Ionian city of Miletus. He was a celebrated and controversial performer at Athens and at the court of Macedon, and ancient writers connect him with Euripides and with King Archelaus. He is said to have died at an advanced age, in the middle of the fourth century.

He was the leading figure of the so-called New Music, which loosened the older strict forms of Greek song. He wrote nomes and dithyrambs and sang them to the kithara, the large concert lyre, to which tradition says he added strings, making a total of eleven. Conservatives attacked his innovations, and his rivalry with the older composer Phrynis is often recalled.

Most of his work is lost, and for centuries only scattered quotations survived. In 1902 a papyrus found at Abusir in Egypt, now in Berlin, gave the world a long stretch of his nome the Persians, which describes the sea fight of Salamis; it is the oldest Greek book of which an original survives. This archive carries the Persians in J. M. Edmonds's English version, with the other fragments given in his Lyra Graeca, vol. III (1927).
