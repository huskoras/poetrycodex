French

Jean-Baptiste Rousseau

1671–1741

Jean-Baptiste Rousseau (1671–1741), born in Paris, was admired through much of the eighteenth century as the finest French lyric poet since Malherbe. He began with comedies for the stage and then turned to the ode and the cantata. He is remembered above all for his sacred odes, free paraphrases of the Psalms in long, regular stanzas, and for his mythological cantatas, which gave that form its settled shape in French. He is not to be confused with Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who was born forty years later.

In 1712 he was convicted in Paris of circulating defamatory couplets against fellow writers and banished from the kingdom, a sentence he always protested as unjust. He spent the rest of his life abroad, in Switzerland, in Vienna, where Prince Eugene of Savoy was among his patrons, and finally in the Austrian Netherlands, and died at Brussels on 17 March 1741. The pieces here follow the Wikisource transcription of the Paris edition of 1820 and a Paris edition of 1799, in the old spelling of those books (avoit, connoître); the numbering of the odes is that of the 1820 edition. The Philomèle ode and the psalm ode beginning 'Qu'aux accents de ma voix' also stand in The Oxford Book of French Verse (1907).
