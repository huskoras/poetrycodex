Po Chü-i

772–846

Po Chü-i (Bai Juyi, 772–846; also called Lo-t’ien) was one of the great poets of the T’ang dynasty. Born in Shansi and raised in Honan, he passed the examinations in 800, served at court as a scholar of the Han-lin Academy and a censor, and was banished in 815 to Chiang-chou (Hsün-yang) on the Yangtze. Later he governed Hangchow and Soochow and ended his life in retirement at Lo-yang.

He wrote in a deliberately plain style, said to have been tested on an old peasant woman, and he used it for satires on injustice and for quiet poems on illness, idleness, friendship, wine, old age and the death of a small daughter. His close friend was the poet Yüan Chēn. His poetry was admired in Japan during his lifetime. Arthur Waley’s versions, from A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (1918) and More Translations from the Chinese (1919), are presented here in Waley’s spelling of the poet’s name.
