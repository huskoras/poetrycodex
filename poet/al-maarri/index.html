Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri

973–1057

Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri (973–1057) was a blind poet, philosopher and sceptic born in Ma'arrat al-Nu'man in northern Syria. Blinded by smallpox in childhood, he studied in Aleppo and Antioch, spent a short period in Baghdad, and returned to his home town, where he lived for decades as a celebrated ascetic and teacher, a vegetarian who called himself a prisoner of three things: blindness, solitude and the body. His verse questions revelation, priestly authority and the claims of every rival creed, and he has often been called the Voltaire or Lucretius of the Arabs.

His two great collections are Saqt al-zand (Sparks of the Tinder) and the Luzum ma la yalzam (The Necessity of What Is Not Necessary), the latter known as the Luzumiyat. The poems here are from Ameen Rihani's 1918 English version, a free rendering in quatrains selected from those books; they follow the sense and spirit of the Arabic rather than its form, which is monorhymed and in a different metre.

  1. The sable wings of Night pursuing day
  2. O my Companion, Night is passing fair
  3. Hark, in the minarets muazzens call
  4. And hear the others who with cymbals try
  5. Mohammed or Messiah! Hear thou me
  6. Come, let us with the naked Night now rest
  7. The Pleiads follow; and our Ethiop Queen
  8. The secret of the day and night is in
  9. But whether of dust or fire or foam, the glaive
  10. The Grave and Cradle, the untiring twain
  11. Ay, like the circles which the sun doth spin
  12. And what avails it then that Man be born
  13. And he the Messenger, who takes away
  14. But now the funeral is passing by
  15. With tombs and ruined temples groans the land
  16. Of Fate. Alas! and we shall follow soon
  17. And from the last days of Thamud and 'Ad
  18. Tread lightly, for the mighty that have been
  19. Many a grave embraces friend and foe
  20. The silken fibre of the fell Zakkum
  21. And still we weave, and still we are content
  22. Nor king nor slave the hungry Days will spare
  23. Then Jannat or Juhannam? From the height
  24. Or sleep--and shall it be eternal sleep
  25. Upon the threshing-floor of life I burn
  26. And like a spider's house or sparrow's nest
  27. So, too, the creeds of Man: the one prevails
  28. Seek not the Tavern of Belief, my friend
  29. By fearing whom I trust I find my way
  30. Or wilt thou commerce have with those who make
  31. Religion is a maiden veiled in prayer
  32. Why linger here, why turn another page
  33. Some day the weeping daughters of Hadil
  34. Of Ababil! I would the tale were true
  35. Methinks Allah divides me to complete
  36. And round the Well how oft my Soul doth grope
  37. Ah, many have I seen of those who fell
  38. The I in me standing upon the brink
  39. And she: "How long must I the burden bear
  40. But once when we were quarreling, the door
  41. One draught, more bitter than the Zakkum tree
  42. We reach a door and there the legend find
  43. How like a door the knowledge we attain
  44. Hither we come unknowing, hence we go
  45. Apply thy wit, O Brother, here and there
  46. And why so much ado, and wherefore lay
  47. I heard it whispered in the cryptic streets
  48. Another said: "The creeping worm hath shown
  49. And still another: "We are coins which fade
  50. And in the pottery the potter cried
  51. The Earth then spake: "My children silent be
  52. Now, I believe the Potter will essay
  53. With better skill he even will remould
  54. Sooth I have told the masters of the mart
  55. I muse, but in my musings I recall
  56. I laugh, but in my laughter-cup I pour
  57. I wheedle, too, even like my slave Zeidun
  58. But in the fatal coils, without intent
  59. And even the repentant sinner's tear
  60. Between the white and purple Words of Time
  61. How oft, when young, my brothers I would shun
  62. Now, mosques and churches--even a Kaaba Stone
  63. To humankind, O Brother, consecrate
  64. The tavern and the temple also shun
  65. "The wine's forbidden," say these honest folk
  66. Or in the house of lust. The priestly name
  67. For after she declaims upon the vows
  68. Carouse, ye sovereign lords! The wheel will roll
  69. And silent drink. The tumult of our mirth
  70. The Prophets, too, among us come to teach
  71. And though around the temple they should run
  72. Oh! let them in the marshes grope, or ride
  73. "What is thy faith and creed," they ask of me
  74. Swathe thee in wool, my Sufi friend, and go
  75. Ay! suddenly the mystic Hand will seal
  76. Among the crumbling ruins of the creeds
  77. Among us falsehood is proclaimed aloud
  78. And why in this dark Kingdom tribute pay
  79. Thy way unto the Sun the spaces through
  80. Hear ye who in the dust of ages creep
  81. Arise! for lo! the Laughter and the Weeping
  82. Evil and Virtue? Shadows on the street
  83. And thus the Sun will write and will efface
  84. For all my learning's but a veil, I guess
  85. And all my years, as vapid as my lay
  86. Allah, my sleep is woven through, it seems
  87. The subtle ways of Destiny I know
  88. I give and go, grim Destiny,--I play
  89. If my house-swallow, laboring with zest
  90. Thy life with guiltless life-blood do not stain
  91. Oh! cast my dust away from thee, and doff
  92. Behold! the Veil that hid thy soul is torn
  93. A prince of souls, 'tis sung in ancient lay
  94. But I was not. Oh! that the Fates decree
  95. "Thou shalt not kill!"--Thy words, O God, we heed
  96. Oh! that some one would but return to tell
  97. Once I have tried to string a few Pearl-seeds
  98. And in the palaces of wealth I found
  99. Thy wealth can shed no tears around thy bier
  100. For me thy silks and feathers have no charm
  101. The guiding hand of Allah I can see
  102. My life's the road on which I blindly speed
  103. O Rabbi, curse us not if we have been
  104. And like the dead of Ind I do not fear
  105. Now, at this end of Adam's line I stand
  106. Ay, thus thy children, though they sovereigns be
  107. And in the labyrinth both son and sire
  108. If miracles were wrought in ancient years
  109. Fan thou a lisping fire and it will leap
  110. The way of vice is open as the sky
  111. Whence come, O firmament, thy myriad lights
  112. Whence does the nettle get its bitter sting
  113. How many like us in the ages past
  114. Why try to con the book of earth and sky
  115. The Sultan, too, relinquishing his throne
  116. To clay the mighty Sultan must return
  117. Turned to a cup, who once the sword of state
  118. For this I say, Be watchful of the Cage
  119. Yea, kiss the rosy cheeks of new-born Day
  120. But I, the thrice-imprisoned, try to troll
  121. Howbeit, my inner vision heir shall be
