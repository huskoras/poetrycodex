Traditional English Carols

traditional; printed 1833

The English Christmas carol is older than the printed book, and its oldest surviving texts are the fifteenth-century pieces that clerks and minstrels copied into manuscripts: refrain songs in which a macaronic Latin tag, a burden or a "Nowell" turns each stanza back on itself. William Sandys, an antiquary and fellow of the Society of Antiquaries, made the first large printed collection of them in Christmas Carols, Ancient and Modern (London: Richard Beckley, 1833). Part I of his book gathers ancient carols and Christmas songs from the early fifteenth to the end of the seventeenth century, many from manuscript, Wynkyn de Worde's print and early broadsides; Part II prints a selection of the carols still sung in the West of England, which Sandys took down from parish singers and from the cheap carol sheets sold at Christmas.

This selection takes the anonymous English carols of both parts, with Sandys's spelling kept as printed and his introduction, notes, music and French provincial carols left out. Carols whose authors are known, such as the pieces by Robert Herrick, George Wither and Ben Jonson in Sandys's Part I, and Wesley's "Hark! the herald angels sing" and Tate's "While shepherds watched" in Part II, belong with those poets, and carols the archive already holds elsewhere (the Coventry Carol, the Cherry-Tree Carol, the Carnal and the Crane) are not repeated. Three traditional pieces that Sandys does not print, The Holly and the Ivy and two wassailing songs, come from Henry Husk's Songs of the Nativity (c. 1868), and the dates of the carols themselves range from the fifteenth century to the eighteenth.
