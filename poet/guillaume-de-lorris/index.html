Guillaume de Lorris and Jean de Meun

Guillaume fl. c. 1230; Jean c. 1240–c. 1305

Guillaume de Lorris wrote the first part of the Roman de la Rose, about four thousand lines of octosyllabic couplets composed around 1230 and left unfinished. Nothing certain is known of him beyond what the poem and its continuator say: that he died before completing it. The work is a dream allegory in which a young man enters a walled garden and woos a rosebud, with love's stages and obstacles personified as figures such as Fair Welcome, Danger and Jealousy.

Jean de Meun, who came from Meung-sur-Loire, took up the poem some forty years later and extended it to more than twenty thousand lines. He turned it into an encyclopaedic debate on love, nature, reason, fortune and society, and also translated Boethius and Vegetius. The combined Roman became one of the most widely read poems of the later Middle Ages, and an English version of its first part, the Romaunt of the Rose, is traditionally linked to Chaucer.
