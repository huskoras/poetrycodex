Upanishadic Seers

c. 7th–5th century BCE (traditional; uncertain)

The Upanishads are the philosophical texts that close the Vedic corpus, which is why they are also called Vedanta, the end of the Veda. They record teaching on Brahman, the ultimate reality, and on the Atman, the Self, in dialogue and in verse. The three given here, the Isa, Kena and Katha, are among the oldest and most quoted of the principal Upanishads. They are attributed to no individual author: tradition ascribes them to anonymous seers (rishis) and to the schools of the Vedas that handed them down, and modern scholars place them at about the seventh to the fifth century BCE, with no firm dates.

The Isa, which takes its name from its opening word, is a short text of eighteen verses attached to the White Yajurveda. The Kena asks by whom the mind and senses are moved, and answers in a parable of the gods who cannot so much as burn a blade of straw before the unknown spirit. The Katha, in two chapters of three parts each, tells how the boy Nachiketas is given to Death and wins from him three boons and the teaching of the Self. The archive gives the Sanskrit text as transcribed on Wikisource, and the English translation of Swami Paramananda (1919), from Project Gutenberg, with the translator's commentary left out.
