Nature

Thanatopsis

by William Cullen Bryant 1794–1878

Nature

To him who in the love of Nature holds
Communion with her visible forms, she speaks
A various language; for his gayer hours
She has a voice of gladness, and a smile
And eloquence of beauty, and she glides
Into his darker musings, with a mild
And healing sympathy, that steals away
Their sharpness, e're he is aware. When thoughts
Of the last bitter hour come like a blight
Over thy spirit, and sad images
Of the stern agony, and shroud, and pall,
And breathless darkness, and the narrow house,
Make thee to shudder, and grow sick at heart;--
Go forth, under the open sky, and list
To Nature's teachings, while from all around--
Earth and her waters, and the depths of air,--
Comes a still voice--Yet a few days, and thee
The all-beholding sun shall see no more
In all his course; nor yet in the cold ground,
Where thy pale form was laid, with many tears,
Nor in the embrace of ocean, shall exist
Thy image. Earth, that nourished thee, shall claim
Thy growth, to be resolved to earth again,
And, lost each human trace, surrendering up
Thine individual being, shalt thou go
To mix for ever with the elements,
To be a brother to the insensible rock
And to the sluggish clod, which the rude swain
Turns with his share, and treads upon. The oak
Shall send his roots abroad, and pierce thy mould.
Yet not to thine eternal resting-place
Shalt thou retire alone--nor couldst thou wish
Couch more magnificent. Thou shalt lie down
With patriarchs of the infant world--with kings,
The powerful of the earth--the wise, the good,
Fair forms, and hoary seers of ages past,
All in one mighty sepulchre.--The hills
Rock-ribbed and ancient as the sun,--the vales
Stretching in pensive quietness between;
The venerable woods--rivers that move
In majesty, and the complaining brooks
That make the meadows green; and, poured round all,
Old ocean's gray and melancholy waste,--
Are but the solemn decorations all
Of the great tomb of man. The golden sun,
The planets, all the infinite host of heaven,
Are shining on the sad abodes of death,
Through the still lapse of ages. All that tread
The globe are but a handful to the tribes
That slumber in its bosom.--Take the wings
Of morning--and the Barcan desert pierce,
Or lose thyself in the continuous woods
Where rolls the Oregon, and hears no sound,
Save his own dashings--yet--the dead are there:
And millions in those solitudes, since first
The flight of years began, have laid them down
In their last sleep--the dead reign there alone.
So shalt thou rest---and what, if thou withdraw
Unheeded by the living, and no friend
Take note of thy departure? All that breathe
Will share thy destiny. The gay will laugh
When thou art gone, the solemn brood of care
Plod on, and each one as before will chase
His favourite phantom; yet all these shall leave
Their mirth and their employments, and shall come,
And make their bed with thee. As the long train
Of ages glide away, the sons of men,
The youth in life's green spring, and he who goes
In the full strength of years, matron, and maid,
And the sweet babe, and the gray-headed man,--
Shall one by one be gathered to thy side,
By those, who in their turn shall follow them.
So live, that when thy summons comes to join
The innumerable caravan, that moves
To that mysterious realm, where each shall take
His chamber in the silent halls of death,
Thou go not like the quarry-slave at night,
Scourged to his dungeon, but, sustained and soothed
By an unfaltering trust, approach thy grave,
Like one who wraps the drapery of his couch
About him, and lies down to pleasant dreams.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Thanatopsis: title and date l. 1

    'Thanatopsis' joins Greek thanatos, death, and opsis, a seeing: a 'view of' or 'meditation upon' death. The date is uncertain and Bryant himself could not recall it. His friend and later son-in-law Parke Godwin placed it in 1811, when Bryant was seventeen and had just left Williams College; later scholars, weighing the manuscripts, prefer c. 1813-15. His father, Peter Bryant, found the manuscript and sent it, unknown to his son, to the North American Review, which printed it in September 1817. Bryant revised it for his Poems of 1821, the text kept ever after and the one printed here. The poem is often credited with giving American poetry its first serious transatlantic hearing.

    Parke Godwin, A Biography of William Cullen Bryant (New York, 1883), vol. 1; 'Thanatopsis', Wikipedia (summary of composition and publication history, with references to Godwin, Bigelow and Trent); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism); W. C. Bryant, 'Thanatopsis', North American Review 5 (September 1817) [first printing, without the opening and closing frames]

  2. The 1821 frame l. 2

    The 1817 text began with what is now the voice of Nature ('Yet a few days, and thee / The all-beholding sun shall see no more') and ended with 'And make their bed with thee'. For the 1821 Poems Bryant added the Wordsworthian introduction, which makes Nature a teacher who 'speaks / A various language', and the closing exhortation from 'As the long train' onward. Critics read the revision differently: McLean sees it giving the poem the shape of a Puritan sermon (text, doctrine, application), while others note that the frame softens a bleak, almost stoic core with a gentler, more consoling tone. Students should notice the seam: the frame speaks of 'him' and Nature in the third person, then turns to 'thee'.

    W. C. Bryant, 'Thanatopsis', North American Review 5 (September 1817) [first printing, without the opening and closing frames]; W. C. Bryant, Poems (Cambridge, Mass., 1821) [revised text with new opening and conclusion]; Albert F. McLean, Jr., 'Bryant's "Thanatopsis": A Sermon in Stone', American Literature 31 (1960); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  3. Nature as teacher l. 3

    Bryant had read Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (1798), and the frame echoes 'Tintern Abbey', where nature is the 'anchor' and 'guardian' of the poet's moral being and can lighten 'the heavy and the weary weight' of the world. Here Nature answers the 'gayer hours' with gladness and glides into 'darker musings' with 'healing sympathy'. 'Various' carries its older sense, 'varied, diverse'. The idea that Nature 'speaks' prepares for the long speech that follows; scholars disagree whether that speech is truly Nature's voice or the poet's own reflection, since the opening also uses the language of eighteenth-century associationist psychology. Ferguson argues that the label 'American Wordsworth' obscures the professional and intellectual contexts of Bryant's early nature poetry.

    William Wordsworth, "Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey", Lyrical Ballads (1798); Robert A. Ferguson, 'William Cullen Bryant: The Creative Context of the Poet', New England Quarterly 53 (1980); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  4. e're l. 8

    'Ere' (Old English aer) means 'before'. Printers in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries sometimes wrote it 'e'er' or 'e're', confusing it with the contraction of 'ever'. The archive's text keeps this period spelling. The sense is that Nature's sympathy removes the sharpness of dark thoughts before the sufferer notices: consolation works by stealth, a motif that anticipates the poem's final image of falling asleep.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ere'; Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'ere'

  5. the narrow house l. 12

    The list 'agony, and shroud, and pall, / And breathless darkness, and the narrow house' runs through the deathbed, the winding-sheet, the cloth over the coffin, the darkness of the tomb and the grave itself. These are the stock images of the eighteenth-century 'graveyard school', which Bryant read closely: Robert Blair's The Grave (1743), Edward Young's Night Thoughts, Beilby Porteus's Death and Gray's Elegy. Bryant summons them only to set them aside: the reader is told to leave such 'sad images' and 'Go forth, under the open sky'. Critics note that the poem thus turns from churchyard gloom to the open landscape for its consolation.

    Robert Blair, The Grave (1743); Charles H. Brown, William Cullen Bryant (New York, 1971); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism); 'Thanatopsis', Wikipedia (summary of composition and publication history, with references to Godwin, Bigelow and Trent)

  6. list l. 14

    'List' (from Old English hlystan) is a poetic form of 'listen', still common in verse of Bryant's time (compare the Ghost in Hamlet, 'List, list, O list!'). The imperative 'Go forth' turns the poem from description into instruction, and the rest of the poem is cast as the lesson heard 'under the open sky'. The shift from the third-person 'him' of the opening to 'thee' here makes the reader the pupil.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'list, v.'; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.5.22

  7. a still voice l. 17

    In 1 Kings 19:11-12 God is not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire but in 'a still small voice'. Bryant borrows the biblical solemnity but gives the voice to Nature, and what it teaches is not salvation but dissolution: 'Yet a few days, and thee / The all-beholding sun shall see no more'. This is where the 1817 poem began. The absence of any Christian afterlife in the speech that follows troubled some early readers, and critics still debate whether the poem is deist, stoic, or a Christian sermon with its doctrine left implicit.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Kings 19:12; Albert F. McLean, Jr., 'Bryant's "Thanatopsis": A Sermon in Stone', American Literature 31 (1960); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  8. resolved to earth again l. 23

    'Resolved' here means 'dissolved, broken down into component parts'. Earth 'that nourished thee' reclaims 'Thy growth'. The passage echoes Genesis 3:19 ('for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return'), but where Genesis speaks of punishment, Bryant speaks of natural recycling: the self loses 'each human trace' and its 'individual being' and mixes 'for ever with the elements'. Many readers take this frank acceptance of extinction to be the poem's boldest move; the consolation offered is company and continuity, not personal survival.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'resolve, v.'; 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  9. clod; rude swain; share l. 28

    'Clod' is a lump of earth; 'sluggish' stresses its inertness. The 'rude swain' is an unpolished rustic, the conventional figure of pastoral poetry (Latin-derived 'rude', 'rough, untaught'; 'swain' from Old Norse sveinn, 'boy, servant'). 'Share' is the ploughshare, the blade that cuts the furrow. The humbling point is that the body, once proud, becomes the soil a peasant 'treads upon'. 'Insensible' means 'without sensation'. The verse here is a fine example of Bryant's weighty monosyllables and run-on lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swain, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'share, n.1'; Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'clod'

  10. mould l. 30

    In older English 'mould' means friable soil and, by extension, the earth of the grave and the dust of the body ('earth to earth'). A second sense, 'form, shape', hovers here: the oak's roots will pierce the shape the body has become. The oak, a traditional emblem of strength and long life, grows by feeding on the dead, an image of nature's cycle that closes the first movement of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mould, n.1'; Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'mould'

  11. patriarchs ... kings l. 34

    The 'patriarchs of the infant world' are the long-lived fathers of Genesis (Adam to Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob), the earliest generations. The thought that in death one rests with kings echoes Job 3:13-14, where Job wishes he had died and lay 'at rest, With kings and counsellors of the earth'. The classical counterpart is Lucretius' argument in De Rerum Natura book 3 that kings, generals, poets and philosophers have all died before us, so it is unreasonable to complain. Bryant joins both traditions: death is not lonely but universal.

    Bible (KJV), Job 3:14; Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 3.1024-52 (the great and good of the past have all died); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  12. one mighty sepulchre l. 37

    A sepulchre is a tomb. Bryant expands the 'narrow house' into a cosmic one: hills 'Rock-ribbed and ancient as the sun', 'pensive' valleys, woods, rivers, brooks and 'Old ocean's gray and melancholy waste' are 'but the solemn decorations all / Of the great tomb of man'. The sublime landscape, normally a source of uplift, becomes a monument. The passage shows Bryant's skill with the catalogue and with the pathetic fallacy ('complaining brooks', 'pensive quietness'). Critics often single out these lines as the poem's grandest, and as an early American attempt to make native scenery carry philosophical weight.

    'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism); Charles H. Brown, William Cullen Bryant (New York, 1971); W. P. Trent et al., eds., The Cambridge History of American Literature, vol. 1 (1917), chapter on Bryant

  13. host of heaven l. 46

    'The host of heaven' is the biblical phrase for the stars (Deuteronomy 4:19; Jeremiah 33:22: 'the host of heaven cannot be numbered'). Bryant enlarges the tomb from earth to cosmos: sun and planets shine 'on the sad abodes of death, / Through the still lapse of ages'. 'Lapse' is the gliding passage of time (Latin lapsus, 'a slipping'). The scale prepares for the startling arithmetic that follows: the living are 'but a handful' compared with the 'tribes' of the dead.

    Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 4:19; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 33:22; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lapse, n.'

  14. a handful to the tribes l. 49

    'All that tread / The globe are but a handful to the tribes / That slumber in its bosom'. The idea that the dead vastly outnumber the living (the Latin idiom abiit ad plures, 'he has gone to join the majority', used by Petronius' Trimalchio) gives the consolation its weight: to die is to join the greater company. The word 'tribes' lends the dead a biblical, communal identity, and 'slumber' begins the metaphor of death as sleep that the final lines complete.

    Petronius, Satyricon 42 (abiit ad plures); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  15. Take the wings / Of morning l. 50

    Psalm 139:9-10: 'If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; Even there shall thy hand lead me'. The psalmist's point is that God is everywhere; Bryant's is that death is everywhere: travel to the remotest desert or forest, 'yet--the dead are there'. The substitution of the dead for God is typical of the poem's quiet secularising of biblical language, a point made by critics who read it as deist.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 139:9; Albert F. McLean, Jr., 'Bryant's "Thanatopsis": A Sermon in Stone', American Literature 31 (1960); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  16. the Barcan desert l. 51

    Barca was a Greek city in Cyrenaica, in what is now eastern Libya, whose foundation and fortunes Herodotus relates; in early-modern geography the whole region, with its desert, was called Barca. Bryant pairs this Old World wilderness with a New World one, the forests of the Oregon, to show that the most solitary places on both sides of the globe are full of the dead. 'Pierce' means 'penetrate, travel into'.

    Herodotus, Histories 4.160-67 (the founding and fortunes of Barca in Libya); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 11th ed. (1911), s.v. 'Barca'

  17. the Oregon l. 53

    In 1817 the Pacific Northwest was remote, almost unmapped country. The name 'Oregon' for the great 'River of the West' first appears in the explorer Jonathan Carver's Travels through the Interior Parts of North America (1778); the river is the one named Columbia by Robert Gray in 1792. It is often said that Bryant's widely read lines helped make the name familiar. The image of a river that 'hears no sound, / Save his own dashings' captures a wilderness so empty that only nature hears itself, yet even there 'the dead reign there alone' (the Native American dead, unnamed).

    Jonathan Carver, Travels through the Interior Parts of North America (London, 1778)

  18. All that breathe / Will share thy destiny l. 60

    Nature answers the fear of being forgotten ('no friend / Take note of thy departure') with a reminder that everyone will follow. Each person chases 'His favourite phantom', a moralist's phrase for the illusory goals of life. 'Make their bed with thee' recalls Job 17:13 ('I have made my bed in the darkness'), and 'gathered to thy side' echoes the Genesis formula for the patriarchs' deaths, 'gathered to his people' (Genesis 25:8). The 1817 poem ended at 'And make their bed with thee'; the catalogue of the ages ('the youth in life's green spring ... the gray-headed man') was added in 1821.

    Bible (KJV), Job 17:13; Bible (KJV), Genesis 25:8; W. C. Bryant, 'Thanatopsis', North American Review 5 (September 1817) [first printing, without the opening and closing frames]; W. C. Bryant, Poems (Cambridge, Mass., 1821) [revised text with new opening and conclusion]

  19. So live ... l. 73

    The final paragraph, added in 1821, is the poem's 'application', in McLean's sermon terms: a doctrine about death becomes a rule for life. Critics are divided about what the 'unfaltering trust' rests on. Christian readers find an implied faith; others note that the poem never names God or immortality and read the trust as stoic acceptance of the natural order, or as faith in Nature itself. The ambiguity may be deliberate, allowing a young poet from a Calvinist household with a Unitarian-leaning father to write a consolation acceptable to both.

    Albert F. McLean, Jr., 'Bryant's "Thanatopsis": A Sermon in Stone', American Literature 31 (1960); 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism); Frank Gado, William Cullen Bryant: An American Voice (2006); W. C. Bryant, Poems (Cambridge, Mass., 1821) [revised text with new opening and conclusion]

  20. The innumerable caravan l. 74

    A caravan is a company of travellers crossing a desert together (from Persian karwan). The image recalls the Barcan desert earlier and turns death from solitary extinction into a shared journey. 'Innumerable' looks back to 'the tribes / That slumber'. The 'chamber in the silent halls of death' converts the grave into a room in a great house, preparing for the closing image of a sleeper retiring to bed. Such images make the dead a society, the poem's answer to the fear of loneliness.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'caravan, n.'; 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  21. the quarry-slave l. 77

    Quarrying was the proverbial work of slaves and convicts in antiquity (the Athenian prisoners in the Syracusan quarries in Thucydides 7.86-87 are the classic case). The image contrasts compulsion and terror with willing rest: the slave is 'Scourged to his dungeon', while the good man approaches his grave 'sustained and soothed'. The image is usually read morally, as a contrast between enforced and freely accepted death.

    Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 7.86-87; 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  22. lies down to pleasant dreams l. 81

    The 'drapery of his couch' is the bed-curtains or coverlet; 'couch' recalls the earlier 'Couch more magnificent' of the grave. The simile of death as sleep is ancient (Homer's Sleep and Death are twin brothers, Iliad 16.672; Paul speaks of the dead as 'them which are asleep', 1 Thessalonians 4:13). 'Pleasant dreams', however, is cautious: it promises peace, not resurrection. The line is among the most quoted in American poetry and was long memorised by schoolchildren; its calm cadence, closing an unrhymed blank-verse poem, gives an effect of resolution without asserting any doctrine.

    Homer, Iliad 16.672 (Sleep and Death as twins); Bible (KJV), 1 Thessalonians 4:13; 'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism)

  23. Blank verse and sentence l. 58

    'Thanatopsis' is written in blank verse, the measure of Milton, of Young's Night Thoughts and Cowper's The Task (which Bryant knew), and of Wordsworth's 'Tintern Abbey'. Bryant varies the iambic line with trochees and spondees, heavy pauses (marked in this text by long dashes, as in 'So shalt thou rest---') and frequent enjambment, so that sense runs across line-ends in long periodic sentences. Critics have praised the maturity of the verse for so young a poet. The form suits a poem that is at once sermon, landscape meditation and elegy.

    'Thanatopsis', Poetry for Students (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com (survey of criticism); William Cowper, The Task (1785)
