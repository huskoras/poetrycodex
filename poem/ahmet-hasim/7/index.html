Love

Bülbül

by Ahmet Haşim c. 1885–1933

From Piyale (1926). Original Turkish text.

LoveNature

Bir gamlı hazanın seherinde
Israra ne hâcet yine, bülbül!
Bil, kalbimizin bahçelerinde
Can verdi senin söylediğin gül!

Savrulmada gül şimdi havada
Gün doğmada bir başka ziyâda...

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The title means 'The Nightingale'. On an autumn dawn the poet tells the nightingale that there is no use singing on, because the rose it sang of has already died in our hearts.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Bülbül ('The Nightingale') and the rose title; l. 2

    In Persian and Ottoman lyric the nightingale (bülbül) is the lover, singing endlessly to the rose (gül), the beloved, who is beautiful, thorny and indifferent; the pairing (gül ü bülbül) is so common that it stands for the whole tradition, and in Sufi reading the rose can be divine beauty. Haşim's short poem, from Piyâle (1926), keeps the cast but changes the season to autumn and tells the bird that its song is pointless, because the rose it sang of has died in our hearts. Aysel Basci reads the poem as a farewell to classical poetry itself: the nightingale is the old style, the dead rose its exhausted subject. Basci also links it, more loosely, to the end of the Ottoman Empire and the founding of the Republic a few years before Piyâle; Haşim does not say so, and the poem is undated in the archive, so this is critical inference.

    Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. 1 (London, 1900), introductory chapters on prosody and on the conventions of the lyric; Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  2. Metre and rhyme ll. 1–6

    The lines scan — — ∪ / ∪ — — ∪ / ∪ — —: 'Bir gam-lı ha-zâ-nın se-he-rin-de'. The first quatrain rhymes abab (seherinde / bülbül / bahçelerinde / gül), so that the rhyme words themselves bring the nightingale and the rose together; the closing couplet rhymes cc (havada / ziyâda) and moves from the dying garden to the open air and a new light. The brevity is part of the meaning: Haşim reduces the vast nightingale-and-rose tradition to six lines.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. 1 (London, 1900), introductory chapters on prosody and on the conventions of the lyric; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı, Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction (prosody, imagery)

  3. gam, hazan (grief; autumn) l. 1

    Hazan, from Persian khazān, means autumn and especially the fall of leaves; in divan poetry it is the enemy of the rose garden, the opposite of spring (bahar), the season of the nightingale's song. Gam (Arabic ghamm) is grief, sorrow. A 'grieving autumn' is therefore exactly the wrong time for the nightingale, whose song belongs to spring and the opening rose. Haşim's choice of season tells the reader at once that the classical scene has outlived itself.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  4. seher (dawn, the hour before daybreak) l. 1

    Seher (Arabic saḥar) is the time just before daybreak. In classical poetry the nightingale sings at seher, and the hour is also associated with the lover's sleeplessness and with mystical prayer and weeping. Placing seher inside a 'sorrowful autumn' joins two moments of transition, the end of night and the end of the year. Dawn and dusk are Haşim's favourite hours, when outlines dissolve and colour dominates.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  5. ısrar, hâcet (insistence; need) l. 2

    Israr (Arabic iṣrār) is insistence or persistence; hâcet (Arabic ḥāja) is need. 'Ne hâcet' is a classical formula meaning 'what need is there?', 'there is no point'. The nightingale's song in divan poetry is precisely an endless, insistent lament; to call it mere insistence makes it sound tiresome and outdated. The vocative 'bülbül!' with its exclamation mark gives the line the tone of a gentle rebuke.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  6. 'In the gardens of our heart' l. 3

    The garden (bağ, gülşen, bahçe) is the setting of the nightingale's song and, in mystical reading, an image of paradise or of the heart where divine beauty blooms. Haşim internalises the garden: the rose lives or dies in 'the gardens of our heart'. The plural 'our' extends the loss from one lover to a whole community or generation, which supports the readings that see the poem as a statement about a culture, not only about a private love.

    Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  7. 'The rose you sang of has died' l. 4

    Can (Persian jān) is life or soul; 'can vermek' means to die, literally to surrender one's soul. The phrase personifies the rose, as the tradition always did, but the tradition's rose is eternally desired and never dies; Haşim declares its death. 'Senin söylediğin gül', 'the rose that you sang (of)', makes the rose a product of the song: the nightingale's object exists in its music, and that music no longer moves the heart. The bluntness of the line is the turn of the poem.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  8. savrulmada (is being scattered) l. 5

    Savrulmak is to be blown about or scattered by the wind; the form 'savrulmada' (locative of the verbal noun, like modern -makta) gives a continuous present favoured in literary Turkish of Haşim's time: 'is in the course of being scattered'. The same form returns in 'doğmada' in the last line, binding the couplet. The dead rose does not simply vanish; its petals drift in the air, a final image of dispersal. Haşim's poems often end in such moving, unsettled pictures.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989)

  9. 'Gün doğmada bir başka ziyâda' l. 6

    Ziyâ is Arabic ḍiyāʾ, brightness or light. The last line says that day is rising 'in another light', and its tone is open. It can be the indifferent sunrise that ignores the nightingale's mourning, as in Haşim's other poems where the sun is a cold power; or it can be the dawn of something new. Basci reads it historically, as the passage from Eastern to Western literary forms and, more loosely, from the Ottoman Empire to the Republic. The careful reader should note that the poem itself gives no names; the historical reading is a critical inference.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books; M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989)
