Nature

Bir Günün Sonunda Arzu

by Ahmet Haşim c. 1885–1933

From Piyale (1926). Original Turkish text.

NatureTime & Brevity

Yorgun gözümün halkalarında
Güller gibi fecr oldu nümâyân,
Güller gibi... sonsuz iri güller,
Güller ki kamıştan daha nalân
Gün doğdu yazık arkalarından!

Altın kulelerden yine kuşlar,
Tekrârını ömrün eder i'lân,
Kuşlar mıdır onlar ki her akşam,
Âlemlerimizden sefer eyler?

Akşam, yine akşam, yine akşam,
Bir sırma kemerdir suya baksam;
Üstünde sema kavs-i mutalsam!
Akşam, yine akşam, yine akşam,
Göllerde bu dem bir kamış olsam!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The title means 'Desire at the End of a Day'. The refrain 'Akşam, yine akşam, yine akşam' ('Evening, again evening, again evening') leads to the closing wish to be a reed in the lakes at this hour.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Bir Günün Sonunda Arzu ('Desire at the End of a Day') title; l. 1

    The poem appeared in Dergâh in 1921, the review of the young Istanbul writers gathered round the national struggle, and was later placed in Haşim's second book, Piyâle (1926). Readers and journalists complained that it was too dark to understand, and humour papers parodied it. Haşim answered first in an essay, 'Şiirde Mâna ve Vuzuh' (meaning and clarity), which was later reissued as the famous preface to Piyâle under the title 'Şiir Hakkında Bazı Mülâhazalar'. There he argues that the language of poetry lies between speech and music, nearer to music, and that poems are to be felt rather than paraphrased. The poem therefore stands at the start of the Turkish idea of 'pure poetry'. It is a symbolist evening piece built of three moments: dawn seen through tired eyes, birds at sunset, and a refrain of evening that ends in a wish. Students should read it as a sequence of images and sounds rather than as an argument.

    M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989); Ahmet Haşim, 'Şiir Hakkında Bazı Mülâhazalar', preface to Piyâle (1926); Abdülhak Şinasi Hisar, Ahmed Hâşim: Şiiri ve Hayatı (1963), reviewed by Fevziye Tansel, Belleten 27/106 (1963)

  2. Aruz metre: mef'ûlü mefâîlü feûlün throughout (e.g. l. 8)

    Although he was a modern symbolist, Haşim kept the Persian-Arabic quantitative metre, aruz, which measures syllables as long or short rather than counting them. Every line of this poem scans as mef'ûlü mefâîlü feûlün (— — ∪ / ∪ — — ∪ / ∪ — —), a short form of the hezec metre, ten syllables to the line: 'Yor-gun gö-zü-mün hal-ka-la-rın-da'. Closed syllables and syllables with long vowels (as in nümâyân, nalân) count as long. The refrain 'Akşam, yine akşam, yine akşam' fits the pattern perfectly, so that the repeated word falls on the heavy positions and gives the stanza its swaying, incantatory beat. The choice matters: the poem's music, which Haşim valued above plain sense, is carried by a metre inherited from divan poetry, used here for a new, impressionist subject.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. 1 (London, 1900), introductory chapters on prosody and on the conventions of the lyric; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı, Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction (prosody, imagery); M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989)

  3. halka (ring, circle) l. 1

    Halka is a ring or circle; 'gözün halkaları' are the shadows under or round the eyes of someone who has not slept. The poem thus opens inside the body of a weary watcher: dawn is not seen in the sky so much as in the rings of his own fatigue. This inward placing of landscape is typical of Haşim, whose scenes of water, light and sky are always coloured by mood. The image also anticipates the circular shapes that follow: the roses, the golden towers, the girdle and the bow of the last stanza.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989)

  4. fecr, nümâyân (dawn; visible, appearing) l. 2

    Fecr is the Arabic fajr, the first light before sunrise (also the name of the dawn prayer); Haşim's own early circle called itself Fecr-i Âtî, 'Dawn of the Future'. Nümâyân is a Persian present participle, 'appearing, manifest', from the verb nimūdan, to show. The phrase 'oldu nümâyân' (became manifest) is classical poetic syntax, with the participle placed for rhyme at the end of the line, where it chimes with nalân and i'lân. The dawn arrives 'like roses', the first of several flower comparisons that turn light into colour.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  5. Güller gibi ('like roses') ll. 2–3

    In the divan tradition the rose (gül) is the beloved's face and the object of the nightingale's lament. Haşim borrows the flower but strips it of its allegory: the roses here are the coloured clouds and light of sunrise, 'endless, huge'. The repetition 'Güller gibi... güller... Güller ki' imitates the slow spreading of the light and makes the image almost hypnotic. At the same time, the next line gives these roses a voice of grief, so the old association of the rose with love-sorrow survives under the impressionist surface.

    Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  6. nalân (lamenting, moaning) l. 4

    Nalân is the Persian participle of nālīdan, 'to moan, lament'. The comparison with the reed (kamış) recalls the most famous opening in Persian literature, Rūmī's Mesnevî, where the reed flute (ney) cut from its reed-bed laments its separation, a figure of the soul exiled from God and widely known in Ottoman culture through the Mevlevi order. Haşim does not name Rūmī, and the allusion should be treated as a resonance rather than a quotation; but it prepares the poem's last line, where the speaker longs to become a reed himself. The synaesthesia, roses that weep more than a reed sings, is typical of the symbolist mingling of colour and sound.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Jalālu'ddīn Rūmī, The Mathnawí, ed. and trans. R. A. Nicholson, Book I, opening lines ('The Song of the Reed'); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  7. 'Gün doğdu yazık arkalarından' l. 5

    The line is compressed and has puzzled readers. The most natural reading is that the sun rose 'behind' or 'after' the roses of dawn, and that this is a pity (yazık): the delicate half-light that the tired eye loves is destroyed by the brutal clarity of day. Haşim's poetry consistently prefers dusk, dawn and night to noon; the sun is often a hostile power in his work. Another reading takes 'arkalarından' with the eyes of line 1, so that the day rises behind the watcher's weariness. Either way the first stanza ends in regret, and the poem then leaps to the end of the day, as the title promises.

    M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  8. Altın kuleler ('golden towers') l. 6

    The golden towers are most simply towers and minarets turned gold by the setting sun, an image that fits Istanbul's skyline, though the poem gives no place-name. Gold (altın, zer) is one of Haşim's key colours, the colour of the dream-country in his 'O Belde'. The birds 'announce the repetition of life' (Tekrârını ömrün eder i'lân): their evening flight is a daily ritual, and each return reminds the speaker that time turns in circles while he grows older. The Arabic words tekrâr (repetition) and i'lân (announcement) keep the elevated register.

    M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  9. âlem, sefer eylemek (world; to journey) ll. 8–9

    Âlem (Arabic ʿālam) is 'world'; the plural possessive 'âlemlerimiz' suggests the separate worlds of human beings, or the realm of the living. Sefer (Arabic safar) is a journey; 'sefer eylemek', with the old auxiliary eylemek (to do, make), is the classical way of saying 'to travel'. The question gives the birds a symbolic role: they leave our world each night, perhaps for the beyond. Basci reads the stanza as the speaker's envy of creatures who can depart; this envy prepares the wish of the last line.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  10. Refrain: 'Akşam, yine akşam, yine akşam' ll. 10, 13

    The final stanza is built like a small incantation. Its first and fourth lines are the same refrain, and all five lines rhyme on -am (akşam, baksam, mutalsam, akşam, olsam), a monorhyme that imitates the closing in of night. Repetition (tekrir) is the poem's main device: it turns the common word akşam into something ritual and almost magical, which suits the 'talismanic' bow of line 12. Haşim was called the poet of evening, and in his work dusk is the hour when the visible world dissolves into colour and dream. The verb forms 'baksam' and 'olsam' (if I looked; if only I were) shift the stanza from description into wish.

    M. Orhan Okay, 'Ahmed Hâşim', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 2 (1989); Ahmet Haşim, 'Şiir Hakkında Bazı Mülâhazalar', preface to Piyâle (1926); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  11. sırma kemer (gold-thread girdle or arch) l. 11

    Sırma is gold or silver thread used in embroidery; kemer (from Persian kamar, 'waist') means both a belt or girdle and an arch. The double sense is exploited: the band of light reflected on the water is a gold-embroidered belt, and, with the sky's curve above it in the next line, it also completes an arch. Water is Haşim's favourite mirror; his first book is called Göl Saatleri, 'Lake Hours'. The conditional 'suya baksam' (if I were to look at the water) shows that the scene depends on the observer's gaze.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books

  12. kavs-i mutalsam ('enchanted bow / arc') l. 12

    Kavs (Arabic qaws) is a bow and, by extension, any arc, including the vault of the sky or the rainbow (kavs-i kuzah). Mutalsam is an Arabic participle built on tılsım, 'talisman', itself borrowed through Arabic from Greek telesma; it means 'enchanted, protected or sealed by a charm'. The Persian izafet -i links the two words in the classical manner. The sky's arch over the gold band of water becomes a magic bow. Basci suggests that the bow prepares the final wish: the reed the speaker longs to be is straight like an arrow ready to leave the earth. That reading is attractive but not the only one; the image may simply crown the scene with mystery.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  13. dem (moment, breath) l. 14

    Dem comes from Persian dam, 'breath', and so 'the time of a breath', a moment. In divan poetry 'bu dem' and 'dem-i vuslat' (the moment of union) are common, and dem also suggests the breath of the ney player and the moment of mystical rapture. Here it pins the wish to the precise instant of dusk: not always, but now, at this evening hour, the speaker wishes to be a reed. The word links the poem's modern impressionism with the vocabulary of the older tradition.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  14. 'Göllerde bu dem bir kamış olsam!' l. 14

    The line was ridiculed by contemporaries; A review of Abdülhak Şinasi Hisar's biography (Fevziye Tansel, Belleten, 1963) recounts a gathering at which the line was recited and dismissed as a dandy's verse. Readings differ. One sees a longing to dissolve into nature, to become a thing without consciousness at the hour when the world turns to colour. Another, following the earlier comparison with the reed's lament, links the reed to the mystical ney and hence to longing for an origin. Basci reads the reed as an arrow on the sky's bow, a wish to leave the earth. His preface argues that poetry should stay open to varied readings and lie closer to music than to statement, which suits an image that resists a single reading. The modest object, a reed among many, ends the grand evening in quietness.

    Abdülhak Şinasi Hisar, Ahmed Hâşim: Şiiri ve Hayatı (1963), reviewed by Fevziye Tansel, Belleten 27/106 (1963); Ahmet Haşim, 'Şiir Hakkında Bazı Mülâhazalar', preface to Piyâle (1926); Aysel K. Basci, 'Ahmet Haşim and His Poems', The Bosphorus Review of Books; Jalālu'ddīn Rūmī, The Mathnawí, ed. and trans. R. A. Nicholson, Book I, opening lines ('The Song of the Reed')
