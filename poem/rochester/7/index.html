The Mind

Upon Nothing

by John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester 1647–1680

From Works (1718)

The Mind

Nothing! thou elder Brother ev'n to Shade,
Thou hadst a Being e'er the World was made,
And (well fix'd) art alone, of ending not afraid.

E'er Time and Place were, Time and Place were not,
When primitive Nothing something straight begot,
Then all proceeded from the great united--What.

Something the gen'ral Attribute of all,
Sever'd from thee, it's sole Original,
Into thy boundless self must undistinguish'd fall.

Yet something did thy mighty Pow'r command,
And from thy fruitful Emptiness's Hand,
Snatch'd Men, Beasts, Birds, Fire, Air, and Land.

Matter, the wicked'st Off-spring of thy Race,
By Form assisted, flew from thy Embrace,
And rebel Light obscur'd thy rev'rend dusky Face.

With Form and Matter, Time, and Place did join,
Body, thy Foe, with thee did Leagues combine
To spoil thy peaceful Realm, and ruin all thy Line.

But turn-coat Time assists the Foe in vain,
And, brib'd by thee, assists thy short-liv'd Reign.
And to thy hungry Womb drives back thy Slaves again.

Tho' Mysteries are barr'd from Laick Eyes,
And the Divine alone, with Warrant, pries
Into thy Bosom, where the Truth in private lies.

Yet this of thee the Wise may freely say,
Thou from the Virtuous nothing tak'st away,
And to be part with thee the Wicked wisely pray.

Great Negative, how vainly wou'd the Wise
Enquire, define, distinguish, teach, devise?
Didst thou not stand to point their dull Philosophies.

Is, or is not, the two great Ends of Fate,
And, true or false, the Subject of Debate,
That perfect, or destroy, the vast Designs of Fate.

When they have rack'd the Politician's Breast,
Within thy Bosom most securely rest,
And, when reduc'd to thee, are least unsafe and best.

But, Nothing, why does Something still permit,
That sacred Monarchs should at Council sit,
With Persons highly thought at best for nothing fit.

Whilst weighty Something modestly abstains,
From Princes Coffers, and from Statesmens Brains,
And nothing there like stately Nothing reigns.

Nothing, who dwell'st with Fools in grave Disguise,
For whom they rev'rend Shapes and Forms devise,
Lawn Sleeves, and Furs, and Gowns, when they like thee look wise.

French Truth, Dutch Prowess, British Policy,
Hibernian Learning, Scotch Civility,
Spaniards Dispatch, Danes Wit, are mainly seen in thee.

The Great Man's Gratitude to his best Friend,
King's Promises, Whores Vows tow'rds thee they bend,
Flow swiftly into thee, and in thee ever end.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Upon Nothing: date, genre, tradition l. 1

    The poem was first printed as a London broadside in 1679 and then in Poems on Several Occasions ('Antwerp', 1680); the archive follows the 1718 Works. It belongs to the Renaissance tradition of the paradoxical encomium, the mock-praise of an unworthy subject, like Erasmus's Praise of Folly. Johnson, who called it the strongest effort of Rochester's muse, noted a Latin precedent, Jean Passerat's 'Nihil', and observed that the poem's wit depends on treating 'Nothing' positively, as an agent, while sometimes slipping into the merely negative sense. Modern critics read it as at once a parody of the creation story, a sceptical metaphysical poem and, in its last stanzas, a political satire.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Rochester', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81); Bodleian Library, Digital Miscellanies Index, 'Nothing thou elder brother even to shade' (printing history); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982)

  2. elder Brother ev'n to Shade l. 1

    In Genesis 1:2 darkness precedes light; Rochester makes Nothing older still, the first-born of all. Johnson wondered whether the line alludes to Wowerus's De Umbra, a Latin treatise in praise of shade which claims that shadow was prior to all things ('Omnibus umbra prior'); if so, Rochester trumps one paradoxical encomium with another. 'Thou hadst a Being e'er the World was made' parodies the language used of God or of Wisdom, who was 'set up from everlasting ... or ever the earth was' (Proverbs 8:23).

    Samuel Johnson, 'Rochester', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81); Genesis 1:2; Proverbs 8:23 (KJV); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'

  3. Triplets with alexandrine close st. 1

    Each stanza is a triplet on one rhyme, two pentameters followed by an alexandrine ('And (well fix'd) art alone, of ending not afraid'). Triplets were unusual for a sustained serious poem in an age dominated by the heroic couplet, and the long third line gives each stanza a weighty, epigrammatic close, often the place of the sting. The form gives the mock-encomium a mock-solemn, hymn-like weight. 'Of ending not afraid' makes the point: Nothing alone cannot end.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968); Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973)

  4. primitive Nothing something straight begot l. 5

    'Primitive' means original, first (Latin primus). Christian doctrine held that God created the world from nothing; Lucretius, whose De Rerum Natura Rochester knew and partly translated, insisted that 'nothing is ever born from nothing by divine power' (1.150). Rochester's poem plays the two against each other: Nothing itself 'begot' Something, as if Nothing were the creator, which is either a blasphemous parody of Genesis or a sly reduction of creation to absurdity. Critics differ on how seriously to take the poem's flirtation with materialist scepticism.

    Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 1.150, 'nullam rem e nihilo gigni divinitus umquam'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'

  5. the great united--What l. 6

    The dash and the bare pronoun turn a gap in language into a joke: the first thing that came from Nothing is simply 'the great united-What'. The phrase suggests the primeval chaos in which everything was mixed and undistinguished, and parodies the scholastic quiddity (Latin quidditas, 'whatness', the essence of a thing), a term already mocked in the period as empty jargon. Editors note that 'Then all proceeded' makes this What the source of all particulars, a mock-philosophical genealogy.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; OED, 'quiddity, n.'; David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968)

  6. must undistinguish'd fall ll. 7-9

    'Something' is the 'gen'ral Attribute' shared by all existing things; Nothing is its 'sole Original' (only origin). The stanza predicts the end: all must fall back, 'undistinguish'd', into Nothing's 'boundless self'. Barbara Everett makes this cycle of all things returning to Nothing central to her reading of the poem as a grim metaphysical joke. The cosmology also recalls the Lucretian dissolution of compounds and the Christian end of the world, without committing to either.

    Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973)

  7. Matter ... By Form assisted ll. 13-14

    In the scholastic version of Aristotle, every body consists of matter (potential, shapeless stuff) and form (what makes it a particular thing). Rochester turns this theory into a family drama: Matter, 'the wicked'st Off-spring', escapes from Nothing's embrace with Form's help, and in the next stanza Time, Place and Body join a league against Nothing's 'peaceful Realm'. The political vocabulary of rebellion, leagues and realms anticipates the satire of the final stanzas.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968)

  8. rebel Light obscur'd thy rev'rend dusky Face l. 15

    In Genesis 1:3 God's first act is to create light, dividing it from darkness. Here Light is a rebel, and it 'obscures' (darkens) Nothing's 'rev'rend dusky Face', a witty reversal in which brightness eclipses darkness. The language of rebellion may suggest Milton's Satan, the light-bearer who revolts, though Paradise Lost (1667) is not a demonstrable source. The joke is that the whole created order appears as an act of disobedience against the original, venerable Nothing.

    Genesis 1:3-4 (KJV); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982)

  9. turn-coat Time l. 19

    A 'turncoat' is one who changes sides, a word given sharp point by the Civil Wars and the Restoration, when many changed allegiance. Time appears to assist Something (the Foe), but since all things in time perish, it actually serves Nothing, driving its 'Slaves' back into Nothing's 'hungry Womb'. The political metaphor makes metaphysics into a story of faction and bribery, preparing the satire on courts and councils later.

    OED, 'turncoat, n.'; Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973)

  10. thy hungry Womb l. 21

    A womb should give birth, not devour. Rochester joins the two functions of origin and end in one image, as Job joins them: 'Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither' (Job 1:21). The womb-tomb rhyme was a Renaissance commonplace. Commentators point out that the paradox sums up the poem's cosmology: what came from Nothing returns to it.

    Job 1:21 (KJV); Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'

  11. Laick Eyes; the Divine ll. 22-3

    'Laic' (from Latin laicus, Greek laikos, 'of the people') means belonging to the laity (OED, laic adj.). 'The Divine' is a clergyman or theologian, who alone 'with Warrant' (authority) may inspect Nothing's bosom 'where the Truth in private lies'. The stanza satirises clerical claims to a monopoly of religious mysteries, with the insinuation that what the divines find, when they pry, is nothing. Rochester's deathbed conversion under Gilbert Burnet (1680) came after the poem was in circulation.

    OED, 'laic, adj. and n.'; 'divine, n.'; Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973)

  12. the Virtuous ... the Wicked ll. 25-7

    The stanza turns on Johnson's distinction between the negative and positive senses of 'nothing'. 'Thou from the Virtuous nothing tak'st away' means both that Nothing does not harm the good and, cynically, that the virtuous have nothing worth taking. The wicked, facing damnation, 'wisely pray' to be part of Nothing, that is, to be annihilated after death rather than punished. The idea of preferring annihilation to judgment had libertine and Epicurean associations.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Rochester', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81); Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'

  13. Great Negative l. 28

    The address gives Nothing the dignity of a divine name. In negative (apophatic) theology God is approached by denials: he is not finite, not mortal, not knowable. Rochester's 'Great Negative' turns that tradition on its head by making the negation itself the deity. The stanza then mocks the busy verbs of philosophy, 'Enquire, define, distinguish, teach, devise', which would be vain without Nothing.

    Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982); Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973)

  14. to point their dull Philosophies l. 30

    'Point' could mean to punctuate a text or to mark its end with a full stop, and also to direct or give a point (sharpness) to (OED, point v.). The sense seems to be that without Nothing to put a stop to them, philosophers' inquiries would never end, or would have no point; their conclusions are always, in the end, nothing. 'Dull' sharpens the joke: Nothing gives point to what has none.

    OED, 'point, v.'; Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968)

  15. Is, or is not ... Ends of Fate ll. 31-3

    'Is, or is not' recalls Aristotle's principle of contradiction (a thing cannot both be and not be) and the Hamlet-like choice between being and not being. These are 'the two great Ends of Fate', and true and false the matter of all debate. In the 1718 text printed here l. 33 repeats 'Fate' as its rhyme, an identical rhyme; whether it is authorial or a printer's slip is a matter for the critical editions (Love, Vieth). The 'vast Designs' that these alternatives 'perfect, or destroy' lead into the political satire that follows.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968); Aristotle, Metaphysics 4.3, 1005b19-20

  16. sacred Monarchs should at Council sit l. 38

    The final stanzas turn from cosmology to politics. Kings sit 'at Council' (the Privy Council) with persons fit 'at best for nothing'. 'Sacred Monarchs' is pointed: Charles II's sacred kingship is set beside the worthlessness of his advisers, and readers in 1679, the year of the Exclusion Crisis and the fall of Danby, would have had candidates in mind. In the next stanza 'weighty Something' (substance, money, sense) 'modestly abstains' from princes' coffers and statesmen's brains, alluding to the court's notorious debts.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968)

  17. Lawn Sleeves, and Furs, and Gowns l. 45

    Lawn is fine linen; the white lawn sleeves of a bishop's rochet made 'lawn sleeves' a byword for bishops (OED, lawn n.). Furs were worn by judges and aldermen, and gowns by lawyers, clergy and university men. Nothing dwells with fools 'in grave Disguise', and these 'rev'rend Shapes and Forms', the outward signs of authority, are what make them 'like thee look wise'. 'Forms' puns on the philosophical 'Form' of stanza 5.

    OED, 'lawn, n.1'; Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; David M. Vieth, ed., The Complete Poems of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Yale, 1968)

  18. French Truth, Dutch Prowess, British Policy ll. 46-8

    An ironic list in the manner of the period's national stereotypes: French honesty, Dutch courage, Irish learning, Scottish civility, Spanish speed, Danish wit are all, the poem implies, nothing. French bad faith was a commonplace, and England had fought the Dutch three times since 1652. Rochester includes 'British Policy' (statecraft) in the list, so the satire comes home to Charles II's government and its secret dealings with France. 'Mainly' means chiefly, greatly.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); OED, 'policy, n.1'

  19. King's Promises, Whores Vows l. 50

    The great man's gratitude to his friend, the King's promises and the whore's vows all 'bend' towards Nothing and 'in thee ever end'. Placing 'King's Promises' beside 'Whores Vows' was daring at a court where Charles II's mistresses were powerful, and where Rochester himself had been banished more than once for satire. The last word 'end' brings the poem round to its first stanza, where Nothing alone was 'of ending not afraid': the poem itself ends by ending in Nothing.

    Harold Love, ed., The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester (Oxford, 1999), text and commentary on 'Upon Nothing'; Dustin H. Griffin, Satires Against Man: The Poems of Rochester (Berkeley, 1973); Barbara Everett, 'The Sense of Nothing', in Jeremy Treglown, ed., Spirit of Wit: Reconsiderations of Rochester (Oxford, 1982)
