Religion

Jordan.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

WHo sayes that fictions onely and false hair
Become a verse? Is there in truth no beauti…
Is all good structure in a winding stair?
May no lines passe, except they do their dutie
Not to a true, but painted chair?

Is it no verse, except enchanted groves
And sudden arbours shadow course-spunne lines?
Must purling streams refresh a lovers loves?
Must all be vail'd, while he that reades, divines,
Catching the sense at two removes?

Shepherds are honest people; let them sing:
Riddle who list, for me, and pull for Prime:
I envie no mans nightingale or spring;
Nor let them punish me with losse of ryme,
Who plainly say, My God, My King.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title: Jordan title, l. 1

    The title names no subject in the poem, and readers explain it in several ways. Endicott notes the crossing of the Jordan into the Promised Land (Joshua 3; Deuteronomy 27), which Christians read as a type of baptism and redemption. Jesus was baptised in the Jordan (Matthew 3:13). The river is also a sacred alternative to Helicon and the Muses' springs of classical poetry, and some readers recall Naaman, who was told to wash in the Jordan rather than the grander rivers of Damascus (2 Kings 5:10-14). Herbert wrote a second 'Jordan' poem; the two are numbered (I) and (II) by modern editors.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Jordan (I)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1; Bible (KJV), Joshua 3:17; Bible (KJV), 2 Kings 5:10-14; F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  2. fictions onely and false hair l. 1

    Wigs and hairpieces were fashionable; 'false hair' was a standard image of cosmetic falsehood (compare Shakespeare's Sonnet 68 on the 'golden tresses of the dead' worn by the living). The question challenges the Renaissance defence of poetry as feigning, set out in Sidney's Defence of Poesy, in which the poet's excellence lies in invention. Herbert asks whether truth can be beautiful too.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954)

  3. Become a verse l. 2

    'Become' means suit or befit: the sense is the one in which clothes 'become' a person. Poetry is pictured as a lady whose toilette is in question, preparing for the 'painted chair' and the cosmetic imagery of the lines that follow. The verb thus starts the poem's sustained comparison of poetry to a dressed-up person. The question is rhetorical in form but genuinely polemical: Herbert is arguing against a widespread assumption.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'become'

  4. beautie l. 2

    The line should read 'Become a verse? Is there in truth no beautie?'. The rhyme with 'dutie' in l. 4 confirms the full form. The question is the poem's thesis: whether truth, rather than fiction, can be the source of beauty in verse. Readers should restore the word mentally when reading this text. Its loss here also removes the question mark that ends the line.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  5. Is all good structure in a winding stair? l. 3

    A winding stair is a structure that conceals its end, a figure for the involved syntax and obscure conceits of fashionable verse. Some readers hear an echo of Francis Bacon, whom Herbert knew (Bacon dedicated his Certaine Psalmes of 1625 to him), in the essay 'Of Great Place': 'All rising to great place is by a winding stair'. That would give the image a sense of courtly indirection and ambition. Herbert's own poem goes up a straight stair to its plain last line.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem; Francis Bacon, 'Of Great Place', in Essays (1625)

  6. Not to a true, but painted chair l. 5

    The contrast is between a 'true' chair and a 'painted' one, as a painted face is false compared with a natural one. 'Dutie' in l. 4 is the bow or obeisance made to a chair of state, even when empty. A 'painted chair' may be a stage throne or a painting of one, and readers have taken the true chair to be God's throne. The contrast is between devotional and secular praise.

    Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem

  7. Stanza form ll. 1-5, all stanzas

    Each stanza has four lines of five stresses and a closing line of four, which cuts the stanza off briefly and pointedly. The first two stanzas consist entirely of rhetorical questions; the third shifts to statements and ends on the plainest possible line, 'Who plainly say, My God, My King'.

    Helen Vendler, The Poetry of George Herbert (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1975); N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Jordan (I)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), rhyme-scheme field

  8. enchanted groves / And sudden arbours ll. 6-7

    Enchanted groves and arbours that appear 'sudden'ly belong to chivalric romance and pastoral, especially Spenser (the Bower of Bliss) and Tasso. Herbert asks whether a poem must hide its 'course-spunne' substance under such scenery. 'Shadow' is both a verb (to shade) and a hint that such ornament obscures. The settings themselves are a kind of costume for thin matter. Herbert's challenge is aimed at a whole literary fashion, not at single poets, and the plural forms ('groves', 'arbours', 'streams') suggest stock furniture.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954); F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  9. course-spunne lines l. 7

    'Course' is the usual early spelling of 'coarse'. Lines of verse are imagined as threads or fabric: coarse-spun cloth is cheap and rough, and the ornate settings 'shadow' (hide) its poor quality. The pun on 'lines' (of verse, of thread) continues the poem's imagery of dress. The claim is that ornament compensates for poverty of substance. Herbert's own poem, by contrast, wears its plainness openly in the final stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coarse'

  10. Must purling streams refresh a lovers loves? l. 8

    'Purling' means flowing with a soft murmur, a favourite word of pastoral verse. Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 15 begins by mocking poets who search for every 'purling spring' flowing from Parnassus; Herbert turns a similar criticism against love poetry in the name of sacred verse. The repeated 'lovers loves' gently mocks the circularity of such verse.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954); Sir Philip Sidney, Astrophil and Stella (1591), sonnet 15

  11. Must all be vail'd, while he that reades, divines l. 9

    'Vail'd' is 'veiled' (the spellings were interchangeable). The reader who 'divines' guesses or interprets hidden meaning, as in allegory or riddling conceits. Compare 'Jordan (II)', in which Herbert concedes that his own verse was once open to the charge of elaborate indirection.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  12. Catching the sense at two removes l. 10

    In allegory, the literal story stands for a meaning that the reader must recover at one or more removes. The phrase may also recall Plato's argument in Republic 10 that art imitates appearances, which themselves imitate reality, so that poetry stands at a third remove from truth. Herbert's alternative is direct statement: the 'sense' given at no remove.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954); Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem

  13. Shepherds are honest people; let them sing l. 11

    Endicott glosses 'shepherds' as poets, following the pastoral convention (Spenser's Shepheardes Calender, Sidney's Arcadia). The line is generous rather than scornful: let them sing as they will. Many readers also hear the biblical shepherds who heard the angels' song at the Nativity (Luke 2:8-20), and the Psalmist David, himself a shepherd, whose plain praise of God is Herbert's model. 'Honest' thus means plain and sincere.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Jordan (I)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 11; Bible (KJV), Luke 2:8

  14. Riddle who list, for me, and pull for Prime l. 12

    'Who list' means whoever wishes; 'for me' means for all I care. Primero was a fashionable card game, and 'prime' a strong hand in it; Endicott glosses 'pull for Prime' as drawing for the winning card. Poets who chase clever effects are like gamblers.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Jordan (I)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 12

  15. I envie no mans nightingale or spring l. 13

    The nightingale is the classic emblem of the poet's song; the spring may be the season or the fountain of the Muses, and the pair has been glossed as sweetness of expression and fullness of matter. The line renounces competition, setting aside the ambition that the poem has satirised. The tone is quiet rather than bitter. Herbert does not condemn other poets' sweetness and matter; he simply declines to compete for them, which prepares for the final request.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  16. Nor let them punish me with losse of ryme l. 14

    Herbert asks the fashionable poets not to deny that plain devotional verse is real poetry. 'Losse of ryme' may also play on the proverbial pairing of rhyme and reason. Compare Herbert's 'A True Hymne', which argues that God values sincere desire even where the verse is scant. The poem itself, of course, rhymes perfectly. The irony is deliberate.

    Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem

  17. My God, My King l. 15

    The phrase recalls Psalm 5:2 ('my King, and my God'), Psalm 84:3 and Psalm 145:1 ('I will extol thee, my God, O king'). Herbert's 'Antiphon (I)' celebrates the same phrase and that it recurs in 'The Elixir' ('Teach me, my God and King'). The ending enacts the poem's argument: after two stanzas of elaborate questions, it closes on four monosyllables of direct address. Readers note the irony that so artful a poem argues for plainness.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 145:1; Bible (KJV), Psalm 5:2; Helen Vendler, The Poetry of George Herbert (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1975)
