Religion

Prayer.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

PRayer the Churches banquet, Angels age,
Gods breath in man returning to his birth,
The soul in paraphrase, heart in pilgrimage,
…he Christian plummet sounding heav'n and earth;

…gine against th' Almightie, sinners towre,
Reversed thunder, Christ-side-piercing spear,
The six-daies world-transposing in an houre,
A kinde of tune, which all things heare and fear,

Softnesse, and peace, and joy, and love, and blisse,
Exalted Manna, gladnesse of the best,
Heaven in ordinarie, man well drest,
The milkie way, the bird of Paradise,

Church-bels beyond the starres heard, the souls bloud,
The land of spices; something understood.

Study notes by the editors

  1. a sonnet without a main verb ll. 1-14

    The poem is a single unfinished sentence: more than two dozen images in apposition to 'Prayer', with no main clause. Wilcox calls it a sonnet without a main verb, which attempts to describe the almost indescribable. Palmer notes that it is found in the Williams manuscript (so relatively early) and that, like eleven of Herbert's seventeen sonnets, it departs from the strict Shakespearean form in the third quatrain, which here rhymes effe ('blisse / best / drest / Paradise'). The catalogue technique recalls litanies and the many biblical names for God; it implies that no single definition is adequate.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81

  2. the Churches banquet l. 1

    Palmer glosses the phrase as 'what the Church feeds on'. Wilcox stresses that the image presents prayer as communal celebration, in contrast with the individual 'heart in pilgrimage' of l. 3. A banquet implies abundance and fellowship, and in a poem from The Temple the Lord's Supper is never far off. The feasting image returns in 'Exalted Manna' (l. 10) and in 'Heaven in ordinarie' (l. 11), where 'ordinary' may suggest a meal.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  3. Angels age l. 1

    Palmer reads 'Angels age' as 'Prayer is as old as the angels', that is, it existed before man. 'Age' can also mean the lifetime or condition of a being, so the phrase may describe the angels' eternal life as unending worship (as in the song of the seraphim, Isaiah 6:3). Either way, prayer links the human worshipper with the heavenly host.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 6:3

  4. Gods breath in man returning to his birth l. 2

    In Genesis 2:7 God breathes into man's nostrils the breath of life. Herbert imagines prayer as that same breath travelling back to God, its 'birth' or origin. Prayer thus returns to God the breath he gave. Wilcox describes the line as a kind of home-coming. The image also touches on the Holy Spirit, who in Paul 'maketh intercession for us' (Romans 8:26), so that true prayer is God praying in us.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:7; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  5. The soul in paraphrase l. 3

    A paraphrase is a restatement of a text in other, often fuller, words; in Herbert's day verse paraphrases of the Psalms were a major devotional genre. Palmer glosses 'in paraphrase' as 'in epitome': as a man prays, so is he. The two readings pull in different directions (expansion versus summary), but both make prayer an expression or translation of the inner self. The literary metaphor fits a poet who prays through writing.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  6. heart in pilgrimage l. 3

    Palmer glosses: moving toward its goal. The pilgrimage was a central image of Christian life as a journey toward the heavenly city (Hebrews 11:13-16, where believers are 'strangers and pilgrims on the earth'). Wilcox reads it as an individual journey of love, balancing the communal 'banquet' of l. 1. The image of movement upward and outward also prepares the vertical metaphors that follow (plummet, tower, reversed thunder).

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; Bible (KJV), Hebrews 11:13

  7. The Christian plummet sounding heav'n and earth l. 4

    A plumb-line or sounding-lead is dropped to test the depth of water. Prayer is the Christian's instrument for measuring the distance between earth and heaven, sounding both. The image is paradoxical, since a plummet drops downward while prayer rises; Herbert makes it reach both ways.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  8. Engine against th' Almightie l. 5

    An 'engine' in the period is any contrivance, especially a military machine such as a battering-ram or catapult. Palmer explains that prayer wrests from God for our aid the power that would otherwise be turned against us. The paradox of besieging God recalls Matthew 11:12, 'the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by force'. Wilcox notes that the line also allows that prayer can express anger towards God, a frequent note in The Temple.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:12

  9. sinners towre l. 5

    Palmer reads the tower as a place of both refuge and attack, citing Psalm 18:2, where God is the believer's 'high tower'; Proverbs 18:10 similarly calls the name of the Lord 'a strong tower'. In the siege context of 'Engine', it may also be a moving siege-tower. Some readers hear an echo of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11), the proud human attempt to reach heaven, here redeemed: prayer is the one tower sinners may legitimately build toward God.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Bible (KJV), Psalm 18:2; Bible (KJV), Proverbs 18:10

  10. Reversed thunder l. 6

    Palmer explains: thunder works from heaven to earth, prayer from earth to heaven; the former overwhelms, the latter preserves. In Scripture thunder is often God's voice (Psalm 29:3; Job 37:4-5). Herbert's oxymoron gives human prayer the same awesome force, directed back at its source. Herbert's 'The Storm' has a similar idea, with sighs and prayers as a tempest assailing heaven.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Bible (KJV), Psalm 29:3

  11. Christ-side-piercing spear l. 6

    In John 19:34 a soldier pierces the side of the dead Christ, and blood and water flow out, traditionally read as the sacraments of Eucharist and Baptism. Herbert's compound makes prayer a weapon that wounds Christ, which may suggest both that sin-laden prayer reopens his wounds and that prayer, like the spear, releases the flow of grace from his side. The ambivalence is typical: the same act is aggression and access. Herbert's poem 'The Bag' also turns on the wound in Christ's side.

    Bible (KJV), John 19:34; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81

  12. The six-daies world-transposing in an houre l. 7

    Palmer offers two readings. Prayer upsets in an hour, as if by magic, the order God took six days to establish (Genesis 1); or the 'six-daies world' is the ordinary working week, transfigured by an hour of prayer, as on Sunday. 'Transposing' may also be musical, moving a piece into another key, which leads naturally into 'A kinde of tune' (l. 8).

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:31

  13. A kinde of tune, which all things heare and fear l. 8

    Herbert, an accomplished musician, often figures devotion as music. A tune that 'all things heare and fear' combines harmony with awe; 'kinde of' admits that the metaphor is approximate, a note of modesty that anticipates the ending. The idea that the whole cosmos responds to sacred music draws on the ancient notion of cosmic harmony and on Revelation 5:13, where every creature in heaven and earth joins in praise. Wilcox's reading that the poem is about the limits of language is pointed up by this tentative phrasing.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; Bible (KJV), Revelation 5:13

  14. Softnesse, and peace, and joy, and love, and blisse l. 9

    After the military and cosmic images of ll. 5-8, the third quatrain changes tone completely. The polysyndeton slows the line, enacting the calm it names. The list recalls Paul's fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22: love, joy, peace, gentleness). The shift marks the poem's internal turn, comparable to a sonnet's volta, from prayer as struggle to prayer as fulfilment.

    Bible (KJV), Galatians 5:22; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81

  15. Exalted Manna l. 10

    Manna was the miraculous food God sent the Israelites in the desert (Exodus 16:14-15). In John 6:31-35 Jesus contrasts it with 'the true bread from heaven', himself. 'Exalted' means raised up, refined, perhaps also sublimated as in alchemy. Prayer is thus spiritual food of the highest kind, extending the banquet image of l. 1. 'Gladnesse of the best' that follows suggests the joy of the best people, or the best kind of joy.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 16:15; Bible (KJV), John 6:31-35; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81

  16. Heaven in ordinarie l. 11

    Palmer glosses the phrase as heaven in common life: prayer brings heaven into the everyday. 'Ordinary' had several period senses that critics bring to bear: a public eating-house or meal at a fixed price (continuing the banquet image), and the phrase 'in ordinary' used of a regular royal servant (as 'physician in ordinary'), suggesting heaven in regular attendance on the soul. Wilcox reads the line as presenting prayer as the meeting-place of believer and God, a reciprocal relationship. The richness lies in combining the sacred and the commonplace.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  17. man well drest l. 11

    Palmer contrasts the phrase with a passage in 'The Church-Porch' about vanity in dress. The likely biblical background is the wedding garment of Matthew 22:11-12 and the Pauline idea of 'putting on' Christ (Galatians 3:27), so that the praying person is dressed in grace. Wilcox pairs the phrase with 'Heaven in ordinarie': heaven comes down and man is made fit to meet it.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; Bible (KJV), Matthew 22:11-12

  18. The milkie way l. 12

    The Milky Way was known in classical tradition as a road of the gods (Ovid, Metamorphoses I.168-71, where it leads to Jupiter's palace). Prayer becomes the shining road from earth to heaven. 'Milk' also recalls the 'sincere milk of the word' by which believers grow (1 Peter 2:2), so the feeding imagery persists. The image belongs to the run of exotic, luminous figures that Wilcox finds ultimately insufficient to define prayer.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 2:2; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  19. the bird of Paradise l. 12

    Skins of birds of paradise reached Europe from the East Indies with their feet removed, giving rise to the belief, widely accepted in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, that the birds had no feet and lived perpetually aloft. As an image of prayer, the bird suggests something heavenly that does not rest on earth. Its name also points to Paradise itself. Wilcox notes that even such exotic vocabulary proves insufficient, since prayer must be experienced, not evoked.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; Edward Worth Library, Dublin, 'Birds of Paradise' (exhibition on early modern natural history: the legless legend)

  20. Church-bels beyond the starres heard l. 13

    The image is reversible: either the bells of the earthly church ring out beyond the stars into heaven, or the believer in prayer hears bells from beyond the stars. Wilcox describes it as both familiar and otherworldly, the most ordinary parish sound heard at a cosmic distance. The line is crowded with heavy stresses, as if its reach strained the pentameter. The sound image also answers the 'tune' of l. 8, and in an English parish the bell was the ordinary summons to common prayer, so the everyday call to worship is imagined as carrying all the way to God.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81

  21. the souls bloud l. 13

    Blood was understood as the vital principle ('the life of the flesh is in the blood', Leviticus 17:11). Prayer is thus the soul's lifeblood. Coming after the 'Christ-side-piercing spear' of l. 6, the phrase may also recall the blood flowing from Christ's side, the sacramental source of the soul's life. Herbert compresses these associations into a three-word phrase. The image is the most bodily of the poem's definitions, placed just before the airy 'land of spices', and it insists that prayer is not an ornament of the spiritual life but the circulation on which that life depends.

    Bible (KJV), Leviticus 17:11; Bible (KJV), John 19:34

  22. The land of spices l. 14

    In Herbert's age the 'spice lands' of the East Indies were objects of commercial fascination, voyages and wealth. Biblically, spices are associated with the beloved in the Song of Songs (8:14, 'the mountains of spices') and with incense, which Psalm 141:2 compares to prayer ('Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense'). Prayer is both a far country of rich fragrance and the incense that rises to God. Wilcox includes the phrase among the exotic images that ultimately fall short.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 141:2; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 8:14; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016

  23. something understood l. 14

    After the dazzling metaphors the poem ends with two plain words. Palmer reads them as 'prayer finds in the world an intelligible order'. Wilcox emphasises the deliberate plainness and its open-endedness: the believer understands, and is understood by God, in a mystery that words cannot fully express; she notes that the poem does not close but opens. 'Understood' may also mean 'implied, not expressed', so that prayer's meaning lies beyond its words. The ending thus enacts the poem's sense of the limits of language.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Prayer (I)', Cambridge University Press blog, 16 May 2016; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), vol. II, commentary on 'Prayer' [I], pp. 180-81; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:12
