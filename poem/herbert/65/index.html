Vertue.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

SWeet day, so cool, so calm, so bright,
The bridall of the earth and skie:
The dew shall weep thy fall to night;
For thou must die.

Sweet rose, whose hue angrie and brave
Bids the rash gazer wipe his eye:
Thy root is ever in its grave,
And thou must die.

Sweet spring, full of sweet dayes and roses,
A box where sweets compacted lie;
My musick shows ye have your closes,
And all must die.

Onely a sweet and vertuous soul,
Like season'd timber, never gives;
But though the whole world turn to coal,
Then chiefly lives.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Vertue (title and reception) title; l. 1

    The title is spelled 'Vertue' in The Temple (1633). The word means moral goodness but keeps the older sense of Latin virtus, strength or power, which suits the last stanza's image of tough, seasoned timber. Palmer notes that the poem was rewritten by Bishop Horne and by John Wesley, who made it into a popular hymn. Izaak Walton quotes it entire in The Compleat Angler (1653), where Piscator recites what 'holy Mr. Herbert' says of such days and flowers after a rain shower; this made it one of Herbert's best-known lyrics. Helen Wilcox calls it one of the most perfect lyrics in English.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Izaak Walton, The Compleat Angler (1653), ch. V (Piscator quotes 'Vertue' in full); Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  2. Sweet ... so cool, so calm, so bright l. 1

    The anaphora on 'Sweet' (day, rose, spring) structures the poem: three apostrophes to transient beauties, each ending in death, before the fourth stanza turns to 'a sweet and vertuous soul'. Herbert's 'Sunday' opens in a similar way ('O Day most calm, most bright'). The tripled 'so' and the monosyllables of l. 1 produce a slow, balanced, almost liturgical cadence. 'Sweet' is the poem's key word, occurring repeatedly; Wilcox observes that the last stanza brings it into union with the title word, so that virtue is revealed as the true sweetness.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  3. bridall l. 2

    'Bridall' means a wedding or wedding feast (from Old English bryd-ealu, 'bride-ale'). The calm, bright day seems to unite earth and heaven in harmony. The image also prepares the irony of the following lines: the dew will mourn the day's death 'to night', so the wedding becomes a funeral within the stanza. The marriage of heaven and earth is a traditional figure (it lies behind Psalm 19's sun coming forth 'as a bridegroom'), but Herbert uses it lightly, as the setting for a lament.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:4-5; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37

  4. The dew shall weep thy fall l. 3

    The pathetic fallacy turns the natural fall of dew at evening into mourning. 'Fall' plays on the descent of evening ('nightfall') and on death, and for a devotional poet it can hardly avoid the further echo of the Fall of man, through which death entered the world (Romans 5:12). 'To night' means 'tonight', this very evening, stressing how short the interval is between bridal and funeral.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 5:12; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  5. angrie and brave l. 5

    'Angry' could describe something red and inflamed, as a sore or a blush is; applied to the rose it suggests a fierce, burning crimson. 'Brave' here has its common early modern sense of fine, splendid, gaudy, not courageous. Palmer explains the next line: a colour that bright makes the eyes blink or water, so the 'rash gazer' must wipe them. Some readers also detect tears in the wiping, anticipating the rose's death, so the line combines brilliance and mourning. The rose is the traditional emblem of transient beauty in carpe diem poetry.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  6. rash gazer l. 6

    'Rash' means hasty or imprudent: the gazer looks too boldly at too bright a thing. Palmer's note on the line explains that a very bright colour makes the eye blink. There may also be a moral hint, since fixing one's eyes on the beauty of creatures is itself rash for a Christian, a theme that links this poem to 'The Pulley'. The wiping of the eye may also suggest tears of grief.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  7. Thy root is ever in its grave l. 7

    A memento mori folded into botany: the rose grows from soil, and soil is where the dead are buried, so its root is permanently 'in its grave'. Wilcox points out that each sweet thing in the first three stanzas contains the seed of its own mortality. Palmer observes that the possessive 'its' was still uncommon in Herbert's English, and records that the Bodleian manuscript reads 'his' here.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016; Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19

  8. A box where sweets compacted lie l. 10

    'Sweets' here are perfumes or sweet-smelling substances, and a box of them is a container of scents; 'compacted' means pressed closely together. Spring is the season that contains the day and the rose of the earlier stanzas, so the third stanza gathers the first two into one figure. But a box is also enclosed and finite, and it can suggest a coffin, so the containing image again carries death within it. Wilcox observes of the poem's sweet things generally that they are full of potential yet carry the seeds of their own mortality.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37

  9. closes l. 11

    Palmer glosses 'close' as the technical term for a musical cadence and cites Milton's 'heavenly close' in the Nativity Ode. Herbert, a skilled lutenist, says his 'musick', meaning both his poetry and actual music, demonstrates that all sweet things come to a cadence and end. Every stanza of the poem is itself a phrase with a close, the falling short line. Wilcox likewise hears the poet's 'musick' as a melody that contains its own ending.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; John Milton, 'On the Morning of Christ's Nativity' (1629), ('each heavenly close'), cited by Palmer; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  10. refrain: must die ll. 4, 8, 12

    The short dimeter refrain falls like a knell after three tetrameters. Its slight variations widen the scope: the day dies, the rose dies, then 'all must die'. Wilcox notes that this pattern leads the reader to expect a conventional carpe diem ending urging us to enjoy pleasures while we can; the final stanza instead turns to the one thing that does not die. The last line answers the refrain with its opposite, 'Then chiefly lives', making the reversal audible in the form itself.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37

  11. a sweet and vertuous soul l. 13

    'Onely' marks the volta. After three stanzas on beautiful perishable things, Herbert names a sweetness of a different order: the soul made good by grace. Time is destructive in the first three stanzas, while in the last it leaves virtue firm. The word 'sweet' carries over, but its meaning changes from sensuous pleasure to moral and spiritual excellence. Wilcox describes the turn as unexpected and typical of Herbert's rhetorical skill.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  12. season'd timber ... gives l. 14

    Timber that has been seasoned, dried slowly so that it will not shrink or warp, is strong and durable. Palmer glosses 'gives' as 'yields, bends, gives way', citing a sixteenth-century example in the New English Dictionary (now the OED). The homely image of carpentry contrasts with the delicate beauties of the earlier stanzas. 'Season'd' also echoes the seasons of stanza 3: the soul has been tempered by time instead of destroyed by it. Seasoned wood also burns well, which adds an edge of tension to the next line's world turning to coal.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016

  13. the whole world turn to coal l. 15

    'Coal' here means glowing embers or cinders. The line points to 2 Peter 3:10, where the heavens pass away and the earth and its works are burned up on the day of the Lord. The cosmic scale contrasts with the miniature world of day, rose and spring. Whereas the earlier refrains declare that all must die, this line imagines the end of everything, and yet the virtuous soul 'then chiefly lives'.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'Vertue', vol. III, pp. 334-37; Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:10

  14. Then chiefly lives l. 16

    The final line inverts the refrain: instead of 'must die', 'chiefly lives', meaning lives most of all, or principally. The soul's true life begins when the created world ends, at the resurrection and judgment. Wilcox notes that the poem thus ends as a celebration of what lives instead of a lament for what dies. The adverb 'chiefly' is understated, typical of Herbert's plain, exact diction at moments of greatest weight.

    Helen Wilcox (editor of The English Poems of George Herbert, CUP 2007), 'A poem a day by George Herbert: Vertue', Cambridge University Press blog, May 2016; Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:13
