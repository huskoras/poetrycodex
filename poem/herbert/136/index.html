Nature

The Flower.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Nature

HOw fresh, O Lord, how sweet and clean
Are thy returns! ev'n as the flowers in spring;
To which, besides their own demean,
The late-past frosts tributes of pleasure bring.
Grief melts away
Like snow in May,
As if there were no such cold thing.

Who would have thought my shrivel'd heart
Could have recover'd greennesse? It was gone
Quite under ground; as flowers depart
To see their mother-root, when they have blown;
Where they together
AH the hard weather,
Dead to the world, keep house unknown.

These are thy wonders, Lord of power,
Killing and quickning, bringing down to hell
And up to heaven in an houre;
Making a chiming of a passing-bell,
We say amisse,
This or that is:
Thy word is all, if we could spell.

O that I once past changing were,
Fast in thy Paradise, where no flower can wither!
Many a spring I shoot up fair,
Offring at heav'n, growing and groning thither:
Nor doth my flower
Want a spring-showre,
My sinnes and I joining together:

But while I grow in a straight line,
Still upwards bent, as if heav'n were mine own,
Thy anger comes, and I decline:
What frost to that? what pole is not the zone,
Where all things burn,
When thou dost turn,
And the least frown of thine is shown?

And now in age I bud again,
After so many deaths I live and write;
I once more smell the dew and rain,
And relish versing: O my onely light,
It cannot be
That I am he
On whom thy tempests fell all night.

These are thy wonders, Lord of love,
To make us see we are but flowers that glide:
Which when we once can finde and prove,
Thou hast a garden for us, where to bide.
Who would be more,
Swelling through store,
Forfeit their Paradise by their pride.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its date l. 1

    'The Flower' is not in the Williams manuscript, the early collection of Herbert's poems, and so is usually thought to belong to his last years at Bemerton (1630-3); l. 36, 'now in age', is often taken to support a late date. Its subject is the fluctuation of spiritual life, compared with the life-cycle of a perennial plant, with biblical roots in Job 14:2, Psalm 103:15 and Isaiah 40:6. Coleridge, in his notes on The Temple, called it 'a delicious poem'.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), notes to the poem; F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem; Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem; S. T. Coleridge, marginal notes on The Temple, in The Temple by George Herbert (London: Pickering, 1838)

  2. Stanza form ll. 5-7, all stanzas

    Each stanza alternates longer lines (four and five stresses) in the first four lines, then drops to a pair of two-stress lines before a final line of four stresses. Readers have often felt that the stanza's shape enacts the poem's subject, a shrinking down and a regrowth; Vendler discusses the poem at length.

    Helen Vendler, The Poetry of George Herbert (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1975)

  3. thy returns l. 2

    The poem treats grace as experienced in time: God seems to withdraw and to come back. The plural 'returns' implies that this has happened many times, a theme the poem develops ('Many a spring', 'After so many deaths'). Readers have related the experience to Herbert's illness and to the alternation of spiritual dryness and consolation described in devotional writing.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954)

  4. demean l. 3

    Grosart took 'demean' as a form of 'demesne', domain or estate, so that the flowers have their own possessions to which the frosts add a tribute; 'demeanour', carriage or bearing, is the other possibility, so that flowers give pleasure by their own show and also by contrast with the recent frost (compare Psalm 90:15). Both senses were current. The political language of 'tributes' slightly favours the estate reading.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'demean'

  5. The late-past frosts tributes of pleasure bring l. 4

    The frosts are like conquered subjects paying tribute: their memory increases the joy of spring. The thought is that pain past enhances present happiness. The phrase is compressed: the subject is 'frosts', the object 'tributes of pleasure', and the verb comes last. Its clogged movement contrasts with the light, short lines that follow, where grief melts 'Like snow in May'.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  6. my shrivel'd heart ... greennesse ll. 8-9

    'Greennesse' is the sign of life in a plant and, figuratively, of spiritual vitality. The question 'Who would have thought ...?' expresses surprised delight. The image of the heart as a garden recurs in Herbert, and here he extends it into the life of a bulb or perennial that dies back to its root and returns. The verb 'recover'd' carries a medical sense too, of returning health.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954)

  7. as flowers depart / To see their mother-root ll. 10-11

    Herbert's exact botanical observation: after a plant has 'blown' (flowered), its life withdraws underground to the rootstock. The expression 'depart / To see their mother-root' turns the process into a visit home. The root is also implicitly the soul's hidden life in God.

    Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem

  8. blown l. 11

    From the verb 'blow', to blossom, now rare in this sense. The sense survives in 'full-blown'. Flowers go back to the root only after flowering. The point is that the flower's withdrawal is not a failure but the natural sequel to flowering: the plant has done its work, and now rests. This affects how the speaker interprets his own spiritual seasons.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blow, v.2'

  9. All the hard weather l. 13

    The 1633 text and modern editions read 'All the hard weather': through the whole winter the flowers and their root keep house together underground. The 'AH' here is almost certainly an error in this transcription (a misread 'All'), not an exclamation. Read it as 'All'. The whole clause means that the flowers and root shelter together through winter. The error matters, because an 'Ah' would add a sigh the poem does not contain.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  10. Dead to the world, keep house unknown l. 14

    'Dead to the world' has both a literal and a spiritual sense, recalling Colossians 3:3: 'ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God'. The domestic phrase 'keep house' suggests quiet, sheltered life, not death. The image lets the poet reinterpret the times of desolation as periods of hidden growth. 'Unknown' means unseen by others and perhaps unknown even to the plant itself.

    Bible (KJV), Colossians 3:3; Helen Vendler, The Poetry of George Herbert (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1975)

  11. Killing and quickning, bringing down to hell / And up to heaven ll. 16-17

    The line closely follows 1 Samuel 2:6 (and Deuteronomy 32:39, 'I kill, and I make alive'). 'Quickning' means bringing to life (as in 'the quick and the dead'). Compare Wisdom 16:13: 'thou hast power of life and death: thou leadest to the gates of hell, and bringest up again'. Herbert adds the startling speed: 'in an houre'. 'Lord of power' here pairs with 'Lord of love' in l. 43.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Samuel 2:6; Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 32:39; Bible (Apocrypha, KJV), Wisdom 16:13

  12. Making a chiming of a passing-bell l. 18

    The passing-bell was tolled when someone was dying or had died, to ask prayers for the departing soul. The line turns the death-knell into a chime, as of a joyful peal. 'Chiming' suggests the music of bells rung in changes for celebration. The transformation of one into the other figures God's reversal of death into life. The bell-ringing image fits a poet who was also a parish priest.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'passing-bell'

  13. Thy word is all, if we could spell l. 21

    'Spell' means to read out or interpret (OED): God's word explains everything, if only we could read it. Human judgements, 'This or that is', are mistaken because they fix on the present state; God's word alone is the whole truth. The image of spelling is of children laboriously reading letter by letter, suggesting human slowness before divine meaning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spell, v.'

  14. O that I once past changing were l. 22

    The stanza's longing reverses the earlier joy: recovery is welcome, but the cycle of loss and return is painful. Paradise is imagined as a garden of unfading flowers, recalling 1 Peter 1:4, 'an inheritance incorruptible ... that fadeth not away'. The second line ('Fast in thy Paradise, where no flower can wither!') runs a syllable past the stanza's usual pentameter, enacting the desire to reach beyond time.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 1:4

  15. Offring at heav'n, growing and groning thither l. 25

    'Offring' means aiming or attempting, lifting itself toward. 'Groning' recalls Romans 8:22-23, where the whole creation 'groaneth and travaileth' and we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for redemption. The near-identical words make spiritual effort and natural growth the same act. Herbert loved such near-puns, as in 'Paradise'. The two words differ by one sound, and the line thus contains, in miniature, the poem's claim that growth and suffering are one process.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 8:22-23

  16. Nor doth my flower / Want a spring-showre, / My sinnes and I joining together ll. 26-28

    'Joining together' means joining to produce tears. The flower does not lack watering, because sin and the sinner together make the showers of contrition. The idea that tears of repentance nourish the soul is a devotional commonplace; Herbert gives it a gentle irony, since sin is itself a source of the growth. The shower is thus both penance and nourishment.

    Helen Wilcox, ed., The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to the poem

  17. while I grow in a straight line, / Still upwards bent ll. 29-30

    The upright growth that looks like virtue is revealed as pride: the soul treats heaven as its possession. God's frown then brings a frost worse than any natural cold. The poem thus moralises the cycle it described: the withdrawals of grace correct presumption. The final stanza draws the lesson explicitly in 'Forfeit their Paradise by their pride'. The geometry is pointed: a straight line upward implies no dependence.

    Helen Vendler, The Poetry of George Herbert (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1975); Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954)

  18. what pole is not the zone, / Where all things burn ll. 32-33

    The 'zone' is the torrid zone, the hot belt of the earth around the equator in ancient geography. The sense is that the coldest experience on earth is warm compared with God's chilling frown. The extremes of cold and heat are gathered into one paradox: God's anger is a frost colder than the pole, which makes the pole seem burning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'zone'

  19. And now in age I bud again l. 36

    'In age' has been taken as evidence that the poem was written late. Herbert died at thirty-nine, so 'age' is relative, perhaps meaning the later part of life or the frailty of sickness. 'Bud again' may also recall Aaron's rod, which budded as a sign of God's choice (Numbers 17:8), and Job 14:7-9, where a cut tree 'will sprout again' at the scent of water, which matches 'I once more smell the dew and rain'.

    Bible (KJV), Job 14:7-9

  20. And relish versing l. 39

    'Relish' means to taste with pleasure, linking writing with the senses of smell and taste in the preceding line. The plain, domestic word is more telling than a dignified one such as 'love' would be. The return of poetry is part of the return of life: 'I live and write'.

    F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on the poem

  21. O my onely light l. 39

    The address may echo Psalm 27:1 and John 8:12 ('I am the light of the world'). It turns the stanza from description to astonishment: 'It cannot be / That I am he / On whom thy tempests fell all night.' The night of storms and the light of morning complete the poem's weather imagery. 'Onely' stresses that God is the sole source, and the line, unusually, breaks mid-way with a colon before the address.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 27:1; Bible (KJV), John 8:12

  22. we are but flowers that glide l. 44

    The biblical background is Job 14:2, man 'cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow', and Psalm 103:15-16. 'Glide' suggests silent, almost imperceptible passing, the smooth movement of time. 'Which' in the next line refers to this knowledge: once we can find and prove (test by experience) our transience, God has a garden where we may stay.

    Bible (KJV), Job 14:2; Bible (KJV), Psalm 103:15-16

  23. Who would be more, / Swelling through store ll. 47-49

    'Store' is one of Herbert's favourite words, meaning abundance or plenty; the sense here is 'proud through prosperity'. Compare Shakespeare's Sonnet 64, 'Increasing store with loss and loss with store'. The last line recalls the Fall in Genesis 3, where the desire to be 'as gods' cost Adam and Eve the first garden. The poem ends by setting humility, accepting that we are 'but flowers', against the presumption described in stanza five.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:5
