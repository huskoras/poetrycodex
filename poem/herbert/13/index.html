Religion

Easter wings.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

My tender age in sorrow did beginne▪
And still with sicknesses and shame
Thou didst so punish sinne,
That I became
Most thinne.
With thee
Let me combine,
And feel this day thy victorie:
For, if I imp my wing on thine,
Affliction shall advance the flight in me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. My tender age l. 1

    Stanza one narrated the race's history; stanza two repeats the pattern autobiographically. Herbert lost his father in 1596, when he was about three and was troubled by poor health (probably consumption) through much of his life, dying at 39 in 1633; Walton's Life records his ill health. The line also has a general scriptural sense: man is 'born unto trouble' (Job 5:7) and conceived in sin (Psalm 51:5), so personal suffering mirrors inherited guilt.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Izaak Walton, The Life of Mr. George Herbert (1670); Bible (KJV), Psalm 51:5; Job 5:7

  2. sicknesses and shame ll. 2-3

    Herbert frequently treats illness as divine discipline: the 'Affliction' poems in The Temple explore it at length. The idea is biblical, 'whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth' (Hebrews 12:6). The pairing of sickness and shame joins body and conscience; the line is answered at the stanza's end by the transformed word 'Affliction', which now 'advances' rather than punishes. The rhyme 'sinne' / 'thinne' links moral cause and bodily effect in the narrowing wing.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 12:6; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  3. Most thinne l. 5

    'Thinne' is literal (emaciated by sickness) and figurative (spiritually depleted), and it describes the line itself, which is as thin as the printed wing can be. The exact parallel with 'Most poore' in the first stanza shows that the individual life recapitulates the Fall. Summers notes that the shape is not decoration: the reader sees the diminution before reading it.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'thin'

  4. combine l. 7

    'Combine' here means to unite closely, a sense already current in Herbert's day. It corresponds to 'O let me rise' in stanza one but is more intimate: rising follows from union. The doctrine is the Pauline union with Christ in death and resurrection (Romans 6:5; Galatians 2:20, 'I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'combine'; Bible (KJV), Galatians 2:20

  5. imp l. 9

    To 'imp' was to splice feathers into a hawk's broken or moulted wing to restore its flight; the word comes from Old English impian, to graft (originally of plants). Herbert's conceit makes Christ's wing the source of the new feathers: the speaker's broken wing flies because it is joined to Christ's. The technical term would be familiar to readers of the hawking manuals and of Shakespeare ('Imp out our drooping country's broken wing', Richard II, Act 2, scene 1). It completes the poem's turn from the visual shape of wings to their use.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'imp, v.'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  6. Affliction shall advance l. 10

    The last line answers the last line of stanza one ('Then shall the fall further the flight in me'): 'Affliction' takes the place of 'the fall', and 'advance' of 'further'. What punished in line 3 now propels. Herbert uses 'affliction' with weight throughout The Temple, where five poems bear the title. The thought is Pauline: 'our light affliction ... worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory' (2 Corinthians 4:17).

    Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 4:17; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)

  7. victorie l. 8

    As in stanza one, the source is 1 Corinthians 15:57: 'thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ'. Here the speaker asks to 'feel' that victory, moving from celebration (sing) to experience (feel). The shift from plural to singular narrows the cosmic triumph to one life, as the whole second stanza narrows history to autobiography.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:57; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem
