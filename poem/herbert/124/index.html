Religion

The Collar.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

I Struck the board, and cry'd, No more.
I will abroad.
What? shall I ever sigh and pine?
My lines and life are free; free as the rode,
Loose as the winde, as large as store.
Shall I be still in suit?
Have I no harvest but a thorn
To let me bloud, and not restore
What I have lost with cordiall fruit?
Sure there was wine
Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn
Before my tears did drown it.
Is the yeare onely lost to me?
Have I no bayes to crown it?
No flowers, no garlands gay? all blasted?
All wasted?
Not so, my heart: but there is fruit,
And thou hast hands.
Recover all thy sigh-blown age
On double pleasures: leave thy cold dispute
Of what is fit, and not forsake thy cage,
Thy rope of sands,
Which pettie thoughts have made, and made to thee
Good cable, to enforce and draw,
And be thy law,
While thou didst wink and wouldst not see.
Away; take heed:
I will abroad.
Call in thy deaths head there: tie up thy fears.
He that forbears
To suit and serve his need,
Deserves his load.
But as I rav'd and grew more fierce and wilde,
At every word,
Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe:
And I reply'd, My Lord.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Collar: title title; l. 1

    A collar is the band worn by a servant or a harnessed beast, an image of restraint and discipline (compare 'to slip the collar'); the clerical collar is a later association and should not be pressed. Editors also hear 'choler', the angry humour that drives the speech, and possibly 'caller', the voice of the last lines. Herbert, ordained priest in 1630, wrote much of The Temple about the tension between service and freedom. Editors gloss the title as discipline and note the punning possibilities; the 'caller' reading is the least certain.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'collar'

  2. board l. 1

    'Board' was the ordinary word for a table, especially one set for meals ('bed and board'). The Book of Common Prayer calls the altar 'the Lord's Table', and 'board' could also name it, so the gesture may be a priest's rebellion against his sacramental ministry. Critics divide between the domestic sense and a eucharistic reading, which is supported by the wine, corn and 'cordiall fruit' that follow.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'board, n.'

  3. abroad l. 2

    'Abroad' here means out, at large, away from one's dwelling, with no sense of foreign travel. The verb of motion is omitted, as often in Elizabethan usage ('I will away'). The phrase recurs at line 28, framing the tirade. Its restless energy is matched by the poem's irregular line lengths and scattered rhymes, which Summers reads as deliberately mimicking disorder.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abroad'; Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'

  4. rode (road) l. 4

    The speaker claims his 'lines' (verses, but also his lot in life, as in Psalm 16:6 'The lines are fallen unto me in pleasant places') are as free as the highway. 'Rode' is a seventeenth-century spelling of 'road'. A possible ironic overtone of 'rood', the cross, has been suggested, but the primary sense is the open road. 'Store' in line 5 means abundance, as in 'Easter wings'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'road'; Bible (KJV), Psalm 16:6; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  5. in suit l. 6

    To be 'in suit' was to be a suitor, petitioning a patron for favour. Herbert knew the court world well: he was University Orator at Cambridge and had hopes of secular advancement before turning to the Church. The word returns at line 31 ('To suit and serve his need'), where it means to satisfy or provide for. 'Still' means always.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'suit, n.'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Amy Charles, A Life of George Herbert (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1977)

  6. thorn l. 7

    The rebel complains that his only harvest is a thorn that makes him bleed. The image recalls Genesis 3:18 ('Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee') and, beneath the speaker's awareness, the crown of thorns (Matthew 27:29). Dramatic irony runs throughout: the speaker uses sacramental images (blood, wine, corn) while rejecting the faith that gives them meaning.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:18; Matthew 27:29; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)

  7. cordiall l. 9

    A 'cordial' was a medicine or drink that stimulated the heart; 'cordial fruit' would restore the blood lost to the thorn. Herbert's medical vocabulary is precise. The word anticipates 'my heart' at line 17, where the speaker addresses his own heart. The complaint is that the thorn wounds without healing: it lets blood (a medical term for bleeding a patient) but does not restore what is lost. Readers alert to the poem's eucharistic undertones hear in 'cordiall fruit' the wine of communion, the fruit of the vine (Matthew 26:29), which the rebel cannot recognise as offered to him.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cordial'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  8. wine and corn ll. 10-12

    Corn (grain) and wine are biblical signs of plenty (Genesis 27:28; Psalm 4:7) and the materials of the Lord's Supper. The rebel believes his own grief has spoiled them; the reader recognises that they are offered to him in the sacrament. The paired conceit of sighs drying and tears drowning is a characteristic Herbert wit. The tense is telling: 'there was' wine and corn, as though plenty belonged only to the past. Wilcox and others note that the poem's whole vocabulary of harvest, fruit and wine can be read twice, once as the rebel's worldly appetite and once as the sacramental gift he overlooks.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 4:7; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  9. bayes l. 14

    The bay (laurel) crowned poets and triumphant generals, as in the myth of Apollo and Daphne. The rebel asks whether the year brings him no honours, no 'garlands gay', only blasting (blighting). 'Blasted' is the agricultural term for crops withered by wind or disease. 'Is the yeare onely lost to me?' means: am I the only one for whom the year yields nothing? The harvest calendar of the passage (corn, wine, fruit, flowers) sets the speaker's sense of a wasted life against nature's seasonal return. Herbert, who had given up the prospect of a public career, may be glancing at the poetic and civic laurels he renounced; the poem invites but does not require that biographical reading.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bay, n.'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  10. inner voice ll. 17-18

    From here a second voice (or the same voice in a cajoling mode) urges the heart to grasp the pleasures within reach ('there is fruit, / And thou hast hands'). The fruit recalls the forbidden fruit of Eden, so the temptation re-enacts the Fall. The poem lets the reader share the rebel's energies before they are undercut.

    Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'

  11. cold dispute l. 20

    'Dispute' means argument or deliberation, here the scholastic or casuistical weighing of duty. Calling it 'cold' sets reason against the passion (choler) of the speaker. Herbert's own training at Cambridge, where disputation was the method of education, gives the word bite. The speaker urges his heart to stop arguing about 'what is fit' (what is proper or permitted) and to recover lost time with 'double pleasures', making up twice over for the years spent sighing. The adjective 'sigh-blown' (l. 19) means blown away or withered by sighs, as a flower is blasted by wind.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dispute, n.'; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  12. rope of sands l. 22

    'To make a rope of sand' was proverbial for a futile or impossible task, and a rope of sand is a bond that cannot truly bind. The rebel says that 'pettie thoughts' have made this flimsy cord seem like 'Good cable' and law. The cage, rope and cable keep up the title's image of a collar or tether, and the irony is that the speaker's sense of freedom is the true delusion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rope, n.' (phrase 'rope of sand'); F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  13. wink l. 26

    In early modern English 'wink' commonly means to close the eyes, not to blink one eye. The line charges the heart with having consented to its own bondage by refusing to look. It gives an ironic echo of the biblical theme of those who 'have eyes, and see not' (Jeremiah 5:21), turned around by the rebel. The rebel's argument is that his scruples are self-imposed: petty thoughts made a rope of sand seem like a strong cable and a law, while he consented by looking away. The reader may reverse the charge, since it is the rebel who fails to see the relation of child and father that the ending reveals.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wink, v.'; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 5:21

  14. death's head l. 29

    A death's head (a skull, or a ring or emblem showing one) was a common reminder of mortality. Devotional practice encouraged meditation on death; the rebel dismisses it, along with his 'fears'. 'Call in' is the language of recalling a proclamation or coin from circulation. The rebel sees such devotional props as instruments of fear. The imperative tone ('Away; take heed', 'tie up thy fears') imitates a man giving orders, while the irregular lines grow shorter and more breathless as the outburst reaches its height.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'death's head'; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  15. suit and serve ll. 30-32

    The sentence is a mock proverb: he that forbears (refrains) from gratifying his needs deserves his load. 'Suit' now means to fit or provide for. The words 'serve' and 'load' point ironically to Christ's sayings, 'my yoke is easy, and my burden is light' (Matthew 11:30), which the reader hears behind the speaker's grievance. The vocabulary of service and burden is, without the speaker knowing it, the vocabulary of discipleship, which the last line, 'My Lord', will accept.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:28-30; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  16. disorder and resolution ll. 33-36

    Summers showed that the poem's apparent chaos is controlled: line lengths vary from two to five feet and rhymes are delayed or unpredictable, yet nearly every line rhymes somewhere. Only in the final four lines does a regular alternating rhyme (wilde/word/Childe/Lord) appear, so that the form is 'collared' at the moment the speaker submits. This is one of the best-known examples of Herbert's form enacting his meaning.

    Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  17. Childe / My Lord ll. 35-36

    The tirade is cut short by a voice heard (or thought heard: 'Me thoughts') calling 'Childe'. The address recalls Paul's teaching that believers receive 'the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father' (Romans 8:15) and the call of the boy Samuel (1 Samuel 3). The reply 'My Lord' accepts the yoke the poem began by resisting. Critics stress that God argues nothing; love simply calls, and the reader, too, is caught up short.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 8:15; 1 Samuel 3:1-10; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983); Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem
