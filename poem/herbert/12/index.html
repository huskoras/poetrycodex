Religion

Easter wings.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

Lord, who createdst man in wealth and store,
Though foolishly he lost the same,
Decaying more and more,
Till he became
Most poore:
With thee
O let me rise
As larks, harmoniously,
And sing this day thy victories:
Then shall the fall further the flight in me▪

Study notes by the editors

  1. Easter wings: a pattern poem title; ll. 1-10

    In The Temple (1633) the two stanzas of 'Easter wings' were printed turned sideways on facing pages, each with its own title, so that the lines appear as outspread wings when the book is turned; the archive gives each stanza as a separate entry. The device descends from the Greek pattern poems (technopaegnia) of the Greek Anthology, among them a 'Wings' by Simmias of Rhodes, revived in the Renaissance; Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie (1589) had already discussed shaped verse ('geometrical figures') in English. Herbert makes the shape carry the argument: lines shrink as man decays to 'Most poore' and expand as the speaker prays to rise. Summers reads such poems as 'hieroglyphs', where visual form is itself a statement of meaning.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'; 'Easter Wings', Wikipedia (summary of the 1633 layout and the Simmias pattern-poem tradition; checked against Hutchinson)

  2. wealth and store l. 1

    'Wealth' keeps its older sense of welfare or prosperity in general (compare 'commonwealth'), and 'store' means abundance, a plentiful supply. The phrase recalls Genesis 1-2, where man is made in God's image and placed in a garden furnished with every tree 'good for food'. The line's length, the longest in the stanza, visually enacts this original fullness before the contraction that follows.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wealth'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'store'; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26-31; 2:8-9

  3. the Fall l. 2

    The 'same' is the 'wealth and store' of line 1. Herbert compresses the story of Genesis 3 into a single line; the adverb 'foolishly' points to the sapiential tradition in which sin is folly. Lines 2-5 then shorten progressively ('Decaying more and more'), so that the page shows humanity's inherited decline. The poem has a Pauline frame: as in Adam all die, so in Christ shall all be made alive (1 Corinthians 15:22), the chapter that also supplies 'victory'.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3; 1 Corinthians 15:22; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  4. Most poore l. 5

    Line 5 is the stanza's waist, where the printed shape is thinnest; the 1633 layout makes the poverty visible. It is matched in the second stanza by 'Most thinne', so the two stanzas move in parallel from general (mankind) to personal (the speaker's body). After this point, beginning 'With thee', the lines lengthen again: union with Christ reverses the decline. Herbert may also be thinking of Christ's own self-emptying (2 Corinthians 8:9: 'though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor').

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Joseph H. Summers, George Herbert: His Religion and Art (London: Chatto & Windus, 1954), ch. 'The Poem as Hieroglyph'; Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 8:9

  5. With thee l. 6

    The second half of each stanza begins with 'With thee', marking the hinge of the shape and of the theology. Herbert's Protestant emphasis falls on grace: the speaker rises only by being joined to Christ's resurrection (compare Romans 6:4-5, 'planted together in the likeness of his death ... also in the likeness of his resurrection'). Strier stresses Herbert's insistence that human merit contributes nothing to salvation, a theme this small phrase carries.

    Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983); Bible (KJV), Romans 6:4-5

  6. larks l. 8

    The skylark sings while climbing steeply and was a conventional image of the soul's or the worshipper's ascent at dawn (compare Shakespeare's Sonnet 29, 'Like to the lark at break of day arising ... sings hymns at heaven's gate'). 'Harmoniously' joins the speaker's song to the bird's, and it also suggests concord between the soul and God restored at Easter. Easter was traditionally associated with sunrise and early song, the day of the resurrection 'very early in the morning' (Mark 16:2).

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Bible (KJV), Mark 16:2

  7. victories l. 9

    The word is drawn from 1 Corinthians 15:54-57 ('O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? ... thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ'), a chapter read at the Burial of the Dead in the Book of Common Prayer and central to Easter. The plural in stanza one ('victories', over sin and death) becomes the singular 'victorie' in stanza two, applied to the speaker's own life. 'This day' fixes the poem on Easter Day itself.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:54-57; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  8. fortunate fall l. 10

    The line distils the ancient idea of the 'fortunate fall' (felix culpa), sung in the Easter Vigil hymn Exsultet: 'O happy fault, which merited so great a Redeemer'. Because man fell, Christ rose, and those joined to him rise higher than unfallen Adam. Alliteration on f binds fall, further and flight, and the line returns to full length, the outspread tip of the wing. Critics differ on how far Herbert endorses the paradox: editors commonly gloss it as felix culpa, while others stress that the speaker's rise is wholly Christ's work, not a reward for falling.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Arthur O. Lovejoy, 'Milton and the Paradox of the Fortunate Fall', ELH 4 (1937)
