Love

Love.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Love

LOve bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back,
Guiltie of dust and sinne.
But quick-ey'd Love, observing me grow slack
From my first entrance in,
Drew nearer to me, sweetly questioning,
If I lack'd any thing.

A guest, I answer'd, worthy to be here:
Love said, you shall be he.
I the unkinde, ungratefull? Ah my deare,
I cannot look on thee.
Love took my hand, and smiling did reply,
Who made the eyes but I?

Truth Lord, but I have marr'd them: let my shame
Go where it doth deserve.
And know you not, sayes Love, who bore the blame?
My deare, then I will serve.
You must sit down, sayes Love, and taste my meat:
So I did sit and eat.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Love (III): place in The Temple title; l. 1

    In The Temple (1633) this is the third poem titled 'Love' and the final poem of 'The Church', after the 'last things' sequence. It is followed only by the words 'FINIS. Glory be to God on high, and on earth peace, good will towards men' (Luke 2:14). The welcome is therefore read both as the soul's reception into heaven and as the welcome to the communion table on earth. The poem became famous in the twentieth century partly through Simone Weil, who said it played a part in her religious experience.

    F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)

  2. dust and sinne l. 2

    'Dust' recalls Genesis 2:7 and 3:19 ('dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return') and suggests the soiled feet of a traveller entering a house. The soul hesitates at the threshold like an unworthy guest; the paradox is that it feels guilty even of being made of dust, its creaturely nature. Strier notes that the speaker's sense of unworthiness is itself the obstacle that Love must overcome: humility, pressed too far, becomes a refusal of grace. The poem's opening thus sets up a contest of courtesy rather than of power.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  3. quick-ey'd Love l. 3

    'Quick' means alert, alive. Love notices the guest growing 'slack' (hesitant, drawing back) from the moment of entrance and comes forward. Commentators compare the father in Luke 15:20 who sees the prodigal 'a great way off' and runs to him. Love is personified as Christ; Strier emphasises that the initiative is wholly Love's. 'Slack' carries a sense of loosening or slowing; the soul's hesitation is physical as well as moral. 'Drew nearer' answers 'drew back' in line 1, a symmetry that sums up the poem's movement: as the guest retreats, the host approaches.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 15:20; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)

  4. lack'd l. 6

    The courtesy is the polite phrase of hospitality ('What do you lack?' was a shopkeeper's or tapster's cry). Behind it lies Psalm 23 ('I shall not want ... Thou preparest a table before me'), which Herbert paraphrased in 'The 23d Psalme'. The poem's dialogue is a drama of courteous manners, where the guest's excessive humility becomes a form of refusal.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 23; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  5. A guest ... worthy l. 7

    In Matthew 22:1-14 the king's feast is open to all, but a guest without a wedding garment is cast out; the soul fears it is such a guest. Love's answer, 'you shall be he', makes the soul worthy by declaring it so, an image of grace. The exchange also recalls the Prayer Book's Prayer of Humble Access before communion: 'We do not presume to come to this thy Table, O merciful Lord, trusting in our own righteousness, but in thy manifold and great mercies'.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 22:1-14; Book of Common Prayer (1559/1604), Order for the Administration of the Lord's Supper, Prayer of Humble Access; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  6. Who made the eyes l. 12

    The soul protests 'I cannot look on thee' (as Moses could not look on God's face, Exodus 33:20). Love answers wittily, taking the hand and reminding the soul that he is Creator of its sight ('He that formed the eye, shall he not see?', Psalm 94:9). Herbert lets wordplay carry doctrine: creation precedes and answers guilt. The gesture of taking the hand and smiling is the first physical contact in the poem and shows Love's courtesy as tender rather than stern. The question is rhetorical: it reminds the soul that the eyes it thinks unworthy were designed to see their Maker.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 94:9; Exodus 33:20; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  7. marr'd l. 13

    'Truth Lord' is a concessive formula ('True, Lord'), echoing the Syrophoenician woman's 'Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs' (Matthew 15:27, KJV). 'Marr'd' means damaged or defaced. The soul asks that its shame 'go where it doth deserve', that is, to punishment, still trying to refuse the gift. The scriptural echo is apt: the woman in Matthew 15 also argues from her unworthiness, and is praised for her faith. Herbert's soul, by contrast, still wants to be punished, and Love must answer once more.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 15:27; Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem

  8. who bore the blame l. 15

    Love's question is the poem's theological centre: the soul's guilt has already been borne by Christ ('Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree', 1 Peter 2:24; 'he was wounded for our transgressions', Isaiah 53:5). The doctrine of substitutionary atonement leaves no room for the soul's attempt to pay for its own shame. Love's tone is gentle, a question rather than a rebuke ('And know you not ...?'). The soul's reply 'My deare' shows that it has begun to accept the intimacy offered.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 2:24; Isaiah 53:5; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)

  9. I will serve l. 16

    Unable to deny its forgiveness, the soul offers to serve, a last attempt to earn its place. Strier reads the refusal as Herbert's firmest statement that grace cannot be repaid by works. The scene inverts social hierarchy: the Lord himself serves, as in Luke 12:37 ('he shall gird himself, and make them to sit down to meat, and will come forth and serve them') and John 13 (the washing of feet).

    Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983); Bible (KJV), Luke 12:37; John 13:4-15

  10. meat l. 17

    In early modern English 'meat' meant any food. 'My meat' may be both the food Love provides and Love's own body, given in the Lord's Supper ('my flesh is meat indeed', John 6:55). The eschatological feast of Luke 12:37 and the Marriage Supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9) stand behind the final scene. Editors read the ending on both levels at once: the soul's admission to heaven and the communicant's reception of the sacrament. 'Taste' recalls Psalm 34:8, 'O taste and see that the Lord is good'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'meat'; Bible (KJV), John 6:55; Revelation 19:9; F. E. Hutchinson (ed.), The Works of George Herbert (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1941), commentary on this poem

  11. the last line l. 18

    Each stanza alternates long (pentameter) and short (trimeter) lines; the last short line is wholly monosyllabic. After the soul's protests, the poem ends with narration, not speech: the soul stops arguing and receives. Critics often contrast the guest's elaborate courtesy with this simplicity, which enacts the passivity of faith. Wilcox notes the plainness of the final line after the courtly fencing of the dialogue. Each stanza (rhyming ababcc) closes with a couplet, and this last couplet ('meat' / 'eat') rhymes the gift with its reception.

    Helen Wilcox (ed.), The English Poems of George Herbert (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), notes to this poem; Richard Strier, Love Known: Theology and Experience in George Herbert's Poetry (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983)
