The Pulley.

by George Herbert 1593–1633

Religion

WHen God at first made man,
Having a glasse of blessings standing by;
Let us (said he) poure on him all we can:
Let the worlds riches, which dispersed lie,
Contract into a span.

So strength first made a way;
Then beautie flow'd, then wisdome, honour, pleasure:
When almost all was out, God made a stay,
Perceiving that alone of all his treasure
Rest in the bottome lay.

For if I should (said he)
Bestow this jewell also on my creature,
He would adore my gifts in stead of me,
And rest in Nature, not the God of Nature,
So both should losers be.

Yet let him keep the rest,
But keep them with repining restlesnesse:
Let him be rich and wearie, that at least,
If goodnesse leade him not, yet wearinesse
May tosse him to my breast.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Pulley (title and occasion) title; l. 1

    The title names a simple machine that lifts a load by a rope running over a wheel; the image never appears in the poem itself, so the reader must work out that the 'pulley' is the restlessness that hauls humanity upward to God (l. 20). The poem retells the creation of man as a little myth, with God speaking in his own voice. Palmer records that it is not found in the Williams manuscript, the early collection of Herbert's poems, which suggests it belongs to his later years; he also quotes J. Churton Collins's claim that it is the one Herbert poem free of his usual faults. Herbert names his poems after objects elsewhere too ('The Collar', 'The Altar', 'The Bag'), leaving the title to do interpretive work the text withholds.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26-27

  2. Let us (said he) l. 3

    The phrase recalls the divine deliberation of Genesis 1:26, where God speaks in the plural before making humankind; Christian exegesis traditionally read the plural as a hint of the Trinity. Herbert turns the solemn biblical moment into something domestic and almost conversational, with God thinking aloud over his supplies like a host at table. The parenthetical '(said he)' recurs at l. 11, framing God's two speeches: the first is pure generosity ('all we can'), the second a reasoned second thought. The device lets the poem present a theological argument as a story with a speaking character.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  3. glasse of blessings l. 2

    'Glasse' here is a drinking vessel or flask, from which the gifts are 'poured' (l. 3) and which is nearly empty when 'almost all was out' (l. 8). Commentators commonly compare the myth of Pandora in Hesiod, where a jar releases evils into the world and Hope alone stays inside under the rim. Herbert inverts the story: the vessel holds blessings, and the one thing left at the bottom is Rest, retained deliberately rather than trapped by accident. The parallel is not signalled in the poem, so it should be treated as a likely resonance rather than a declared source; the classical story would have been familiar to any Cambridge-educated reader such as Herbert, who was University Orator.

    Hesiod, Works and Days, ll. 90-105 (Pandora's jar), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (1914); G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  4. span l. 5

    A span was the distance from thumb-tip to little-finger tip of a spread hand, about nine inches, and so came to mean any small compass. God wants the riches 'dispersed' across the world to be gathered into the little frame of a human being, a version of the Renaissance commonplace that man is a microcosm, an epitome of the whole creation (Herbert develops it at length in his poem 'Man'). The word also carries a hint of brevity: the Psalmist says God has made his days 'as an handbreadth' (Prayer Book: 'a span long'), so the creature who contains the world's wealth is also short-lived. The short closing line, only three stresses long, itself enacts the contraction.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 39:5; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  5. strength ... beautie ... wisdome, honour, pleasure ll. 6-7

    Strength comes first and opens a passage; then beauty, wisdom, honour and pleasure 'flow'. The verbs keep the image of liquid poured from the glass. Palmer notes that Herbert lists such worldly goods, under slightly different names, in several other poems (e.g. 'The World', 'The Pearl', 'The Quip'), where they usually figure as rivals to God. Here they are genuinely blessings, freely given; the danger lies not in the gifts themselves but in man's tendency to rest in them (l. 14). The line is metrically crowded, with an extra syllable, as if the gifts were tumbling over each other.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  6. made a stay l. 8

    To 'make a stay' is to halt or pause; God stops pouring just before the vessel is empty. 'Stay' could also mean a support or a curb, so the phrase quietly suggests that God's pause is itself a kind of restraint built into human nature. The word belongs to a cluster of terms for stopping and staying still (stay, rest, keep) that the poem sets against movement (made a way, flow'd, tosse). The poem's drama turns on this moment of divine hesitation, which leads into the reasoned speech of stanza 3.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Bible (KJV), Psalm 39:5

  7. Rest in the bottome lay l. 10

    The withheld gift is 'Rest', both repose and peace of soul. Its position 'in the bottome' completes the image of a nearly emptied vessel and is the clearest point of contact with Hesiod's Pandora, where Hope stays inside the jar when its lid is replaced. Herbert's reversal is theological: rest is kept back not out of malice but because it properly belongs to God, the only true resting-place of the soul. The line looks forward to the punning play on 'rest' in stanzas 3 and 4.

    Hesiod, Works and Days, ll. 90-105 (Pandora's jar), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (1914); Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. E. B. Pusey

  8. jewell l. 12

    The vocabulary shifts from liquid to treasure ('all his treasure', l. 9; 'jewell', l. 12; 'rich', l. 18). Calling rest a jewel marks it as the most valuable of the gifts, worth more than strength, beauty or wisdom. In Herbert's period 'jewel' could be used of anything highly prized, especially a spiritual good. The economic language also prepares the paradox of l. 18, where man is to be 'rich and wearie'.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:19-21

  9. rest in Nature, not the God of Nature l. 14

    God's fear is that a fully satisfied creature would 'adore my gifts in stead of me' and find contentment in the natural world. The antithesis echoes Paul's charge that idolaters 'worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator' (Romans 1:25). The chiasmus-like repetition of 'Nature' sharpens the distinction between the world and its maker. The consequence, 'So both should losers be' (l. 15), is striking: God would lose man's love, and man would lose God, who is his real good. Herbert makes God's motive relational rather than punitive.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 1:25; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  10. second divine speech l. 11

    Stanzas 1 and 3 each open a speech by God marked with the same parenthesis, while stanza 2 is narrative and stanza 4 continues the second speech as a decree ('Yet let him keep the rest'). The shape is that of a fable: an action, a pause, a reason, and a resolution. This dramatic structure is typical of Herbert's 'parable' poems such as 'Redemption' and 'The World', in which an abstract doctrine becomes a short narrative.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26

  11. the rest ... restlesnesse ll. 16-17

    In l. 16 'the rest' means 'all the other gifts', but it cannot be read without the capitalised 'Rest' of l. 10 and 'restlesnesse' of l. 17. The wordplay compresses the poem's argument: man may keep the remainder of God's blessings, but must keep them without rest. Palmer, who thought Herbert used very few puns, singles this one out; modern readers tend to see wordplay as central to Herbert's method. The play also recalls Augustine's opening prayer in the Confessions, that the heart is restless until it rests in God, which Palmer gives as the poem's subject.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. E. B. Pusey

  12. repining l. 17

    To repine is to feel or express discontent, to complain peevishly. Herbert's phrase makes the restlessness active and querulous: human beings will possess every good and still grumble. The alliteration on 'r' links the word to 'rest' and 'rich', drawing the stanza's key terms together. The dissatisfaction is presented as part of God's design rather than a flaw in it, a providential irritant.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49; Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. E. B. Pusey

  13. rich and wearie ll. 18-19

    God allows man to be wealthy but tired, and the closing lines frankly admit that weariness may succeed where virtue fails ('If goodnesse leade him not, yet wearinesse'). The pairing of 'goodnesse' and 'wearinesse' in l. 19, with the same ending, sets the two motives side by side. Herbert's realism here is characteristic: grace can work through boredom and fatigue as well as through love. The line recalls Christ's invitation to those who 'labour and are heavy laden' (Matthew 11:28).

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:28-29; G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49

  14. tosse him to my breast l. 20

    The last line completes the pulley image: restlessness is the force that lifts the weight. 'Tosse' is a deliberately rough verb, as if man were flung rather than led, yet the destination is intimate, God's 'breast', recalling the beloved disciple lying on Jesus' breast (John 13:23-25) and Christ's promise 'I will give you rest' (Matthew 11:28). Palmer identifies the controlling idea as Augustine's 'our heart is restless until it finds rest in Thee'. Rest, withheld at creation, is thus restored at the end, but only in God.

    Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. E. B. Pusey; Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:28; Bible (KJV), John 13:23-25

  15. stanza form st. 1-4

    Each stanza rhymes ababa and is shaped by length: a three-stress opening line, three five-stress lines, and a three-stress close (e.g. 'Contract into a span', 'So both should losers be'). The short final lines act as epigrammatic conclusions. Palmer describes the metre as 'unique' in Herbert, who rarely repeats a stanza form exactly. The returning 'a' rhyme gives each stanza a closed, circular feel, appropriate to a poem about returning to the source.

    G. H. Palmer, ed., The English Works of George Herbert (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1905), commentary on 'The Pulley', vol. III, pp. 148-49
