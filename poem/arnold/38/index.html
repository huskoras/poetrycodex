The Forsaken Merman

by Matthew Arnold 1822–1888

From Narrative Poems

Mythology & Folklore

Come, dear children, let us away;
Down and away below!
Now my brothers call from the bay,
Now the great winds shoreward blow,
Now the salt tides seaward flow;
Now the wild white horses play,
Champ and chafe and toss in the spray.
Children dear, let us away!
This way, this way!

Call her once before you go--
Call once yet!
In a voice that she will know:
"Margaret! Margaret!"
Children's voices should be dear
(Call once more) to a mother's ear;
Children's voices, wild with pain--
Surely she will come again!
Call her once and come away;
This way, this way!
"Mother dear, we cannot stay!
The wild white horses foam and fret."
Margaret! Margaret!

Come, dear children, come away down;
Call no more!
One last look at the white-wall'd town,
And the little grey church on the windy shore;
Then come down!
She will not come though you call all day;
Come away, come away!

Children dear, was it yesterday
We heard the sweet bells over the bay?
In the caverns where we lay,
Through the surf and through the swell,
The far-off sound of a silver bell?
Sand-strewn caverns, cool and deep,
Where the winds are all asleep;
Where the spent lights quiver and gleam,
Where the salt weed sways in the stream,
Where the sea-beasts, ranged all round,
Feed in the ooze of their pasture-ground;
Where the sea-snakes coil and twine,
Dry their mail and bask in the brine;
Where great whales come sailing by,
Sail and sail, with unshut eye,
Round the world for ever and aye?
When did music come this way?
Children dear, was it yesterday?

Children dear, was it yesterday
(Call yet once) that she went away?
Once she sate with you and me,
On a red gold throne in the heart of the sea,
And the youngest sate on her knee.
She comb'd its bright hair, and she tended it well,
When down swung the sound of a far-off bell.
She sigh'd, she look'd up through the clear green sea;
She said: "I must go, for my kinsfolk pray
In the little grey church on the shore to-day.
'Twill be Easter-time in the world--ah me!
And I lose my poor soul, Merman! here with thee."
I said: "Go up, dear heart, through the waves;
Say thy prayer, and come back to the kind sea-caves!"
She smiled, she went up through the surf in the bay.
Children dear, was it yesterday?

Children dear, were we long alone?
"The sea grows stormy, the little ones moan;
Long prayers," I said, "in the world they say;
Come!" I said: and we rose through the surf in the bay.
We went up the beach, by the sandy down
Where the sea-stocks bloom, to the white-wall'd town;
Through the narrow paved streets, where all was still,
To the little grey church on the windy hill.
From the church came a murmur of folk at their prayers,
But we stood without in the cold blowing airs.
We climb'd on the graves, on the stones worn with rains,
And we gazed up the aisle through the small leaded panes.
She sate by the pillar; we saw her clear:
"Margaret, hist! come quick, we are here!
Dear heart," I said, "we are long alone;
The sea grows stormy, the little ones moan."
But, ah, she gave me never a look,
For her eyes were seal'd to the holy book!
Loud prays the priest; shut stands the door.
Come away, children, call no more!
Come away, come down, call no more!

Down, down, down!
Down to the depths of the sea!
She sits at her wheel in the humming town,
Singing most joyfully.
Hark what she sings: "O joy, O joy,
For the humming street, and the child with its toy!
For the priest, and the bell, and the holy well;
For the wheel where I spun,
And the blessed light of the sun!"
And so she sings her fill,
Singing most joyfully,
Till the spindle drops from her hand,
And the whizzing wheel stands still.
She steals to the window, and looks at the sand,
And over the sand at the sea;
And her eyes are set in a stare;
And anon there breaks a sigh,
And anon there drops a tear,
From a sorrow-clouded eye,
And a heart sorrow-laden,
A long, long sigh;
For the cold strange eyes of a little Mermaiden
And the gleam of her golden hair.

Come away, away children;
Come children, come down!
The hoarse wind blows coldly;
Lights shine in the town.
She will start from her slumber
When gusts shake the door;
She will hear the winds howling,
Will hear the waves roar.
We shall see, while above us
The waves roar and whirl,
A ceiling of amber,
A pavement of pearl.
Singing: "Here came a mortal,
But faithless was she!
And alone dwell for ever
The kings of the sea."

But, children, at midnight,
When soft the winds blow,
When clear falls the moonlight,
When spring tides are low;
When sweet airs come seaward
From heaths starr'd with broom,
And high rocks throw mildly
On the blanch'd sands a gloom;
Up the still, glistening beaches,
Up the creeks we will hie,
Over banks of bright seaweed
The ebb-tide leaves dry.
We will gaze, from the sand-hills,
At the white, sleeping town;
At the church on the hill-side--
And then come back down.
Singing: "There dwells a loved one,
But cruel is she!
She left lonely for ever
The kings of the sea."

Codex Note

Poetical Works of Matthew Arnold (Macmillan, 1891)

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Forsaken Merman: date and source title; l. 1

    Arnold's first collection appeared anonymously as by 'A.' in 1849, and early reviewers found much of the volume obscure and aloof, but this poem was admired, and Swinburne later praised its lyric beauty. The story of a woman who marries a merman, bears him children, then returns to land and church and will not go back, is found across Danish, German and Slavonic folklore. The closest analogue is the Danish ballad 'Agnete og Havmanden', also told in a prose version in J. M. Thiele's Danish folk-tales, where the woman is called Grethe; because Arnold's heroine is Margaret, some have thought Thiele's account his real source. Arnold named no source, and the question has been revisited by Herbert Wright (1918) and Clyde C. Hyder (1980); Hans Christian Andersen and George Borrow have also been suggested as intermediaries. Arnold's change is decisive: the tale is told not by the narrator of a ballad but by the abandoned merman himself, speaking to his children.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Herbert Wright, 'The Source of Matthew Arnold's "Forsaken Merman"', Modern Language Review 13.1 (1918), 90-94; Clyde C. Hyder, "'The Forsaken Merman' and 'The Neckan': Another Look", Studies in English, new series 1.4 (1980), 1-15; Wikipedia, 'Agnete og Havmanden' (the Danish ballad and Thiele's prose version); Wikipedia, 'The Forsaken Merman' (publication, reception, further reading)

  2. The merman as speaker ll. 1-9

    Arnold gives the legend the shape of a dramatic monologue with a silent audience, the merman's children. This throws the reader's sympathy, unusually, toward the pagan sea-world rather than the Christian town. The merman is tender, patient and bewildered rather than demonic; his repeated orders to 'come away' alternate with his inability to stop calling. Critics have read the poem as one of Arnold's early dramatisations of divided allegiance: between a natural, unreflective life (the sea) and a life governed by conscience and religion (the church), with the poet's sympathies notably uncertain. Margaret herself never speaks except through reported words and her song in the town, so the poem's centre is a loss described from outside.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Wikipedia, 'The Forsaken Merman' (publication, reception, further reading)

  3. wild white horses l. 6

    'White horses' for white-capped waves was an established English idiom (recorded in the OED). Arnold revives the dead metaphor by giving the horses verbs proper to animals: they 'Champ and chafe and toss in the spray', and later 'foam and fret' (l. 21). The image belongs to the merman's world, where the sea is a living, restless household rather than a scene. The heavy alliteration and the run of stressed monosyllables imitate the surf, and the horses' impatience presses on the children's calls to their mother: the tide is turning and they cannot wait.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  4. Margaret l. 13

    In the Danish ballad the woman is Agnete (Agnes); Arnold renames her Margaret, a name with strong Christian associations (St Margaret of Antioch, among others) and a domestic English ring. The doubled cry is the poem's first refrain, and it returns at l. 22 and in the merman's 'Margaret, hist!' at the church window (l. 77). Some readers have connected the name with the 'Marguerite' of Arnold's Switzerland lyrics, written around the same time, where another lover is lost across a dividing sea; the connection is suggestive rather than proven, but both poems turn on estrangement that neither party can undo.

    Wikipedia, 'Agnete og Havmanden' (the Danish ballad and Thiele's prose version); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  5. Refrain and irregular metre ll. 30, 47-48, 63

    The poem is written in irregular, mostly four-stress lines with very varied line lengths and rhyme, closer to the free 'Pindaric' lyric than to the strict ballad stanza; a contemporary American reviewer called it a wild, irregular melody. The refrain question 'was it yesterday?' frames the memories of the bells and of Margaret's departure. It may be a real uncertainty: in many sea-legends time passes differently under the water, and the merman cannot tell whether a day or years have gone by. It also expresses grief that keeps the loss always recent. The verse loosens and tightens with emotion, moving from the long, hypnotic catalogue of the caverns to the clipped commands 'Come away, come away!'

    Wikipedia, 'The Forsaken Merman' (publication, reception, further reading); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  6. The sea-caverns ll. 35-46

    The sentence beginning 'Children dear, was it yesterday' runs for sixteen lines before its question mark, carried by repeated 'Where' clauses. The undersea world is calm and unhurried: the winds are 'all asleep', the 'spent lights' (sunlight exhausted by its passage through water) 'quiver and gleam', the sea-beasts graze like cattle in 'the ooze of their pasture-ground'. Everything here is self-contained and cyclical; even the whales sail 'Round the world for ever and aye', echoing ballad diction ('for ever and aye'). Against this enclosed paradise the single intruding sound is the 'silver bell' of the church, which first draws Margaret away. The passage is often admired as the most purely musical in Arnold's verse.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Oxford English Dictionary

  7. with unshut eye l. 44

    Arnold imagines the whales sailing round the world with eyes that never shut, a detail of fancy rather than natural history. The image extends the timelessness of the sea-world: nothing there sleeps, ends, or turns inward. 'The sea-snakes ... Dry their mail and bask in the brine' (ll. 41-2) is a similarly playful paradox, since 'mail' (armour, here scales) can hardly dry in salt water. These touches make the undersea world strange and slightly comic, and they distinguish it from the town, which is ruled by clock-like church time: Easter, prayers, bells.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Oxford English Dictionary

  8. the church bell ll. 30-34, 54

    Church bells heard under the sea are a recurring motif in legends of drowned or enchanted people; here they announce Easter and draw Margaret 'up through the clear green sea'. The bell is 'far-off' and 'silver', beautiful to the merman, who does not grasp what it means. It belongs to the same cluster as 'the priest, and the bell, and the holy well' (l. 91) in Margaret's later song, the apparatus of a parish religion. The poem stages the contrast without simply taking sides: the bell brings Margaret salvation, as she believes, and brings the merman and his children abandonment.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  9. Easter-time; 'lose my poor soul' ll. 58-59

    In folklore the merfolk were often imagined as beings without immortal souls, an idea Arnold could have met in many forms (Hans Christian Andersen's 'The Little Mermaid', 1837, turns on a mermaid's longing for a soul). Margaret's words, 'I lose my poor soul, Merman! here with thee', make the conflict explicitly religious: to stay in the sea is damnation. Easter, the feast of resurrection and the season of annual communion, is the occasion that pulls her back. The phrase 'in the world' marks the land as the realm of human, historical, Christian time, quite distinct from the sea's eternity.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Wikipedia, 'Agnete og Havmanden' (the Danish ballad and Thiele's prose version)

  10. sate l. 50

    'Sate' was a common literary spelling of the past tense 'sat' into the nineteenth century, and Arnold uses it again at l. 76 ('She sate by the pillar'). Together with forms like 'aye', 'hie' (l. 133, 'go quickly') and 'anon' (l. 101, 'presently, at once'), it gives the poem the colouring of an old ballad. The 'red gold throne in the heart of the sea' is equally conventional, the fairy-tale splendour of a sea-king's hall, set against the 'little grey church' of the town.

    Oxford English Dictionary

  11. hist l. 77

    The merman and his children, standing outside the church 'in the cold blowing airs', climb on the graves and peer through the 'small leaded panes'. Their whispered 'hist!' is a lovely touch of shyness: the sea-folk are intruders in sacred space. The scene is the dramatic climax of the poem, since Margaret does not look up: 'her eyes were seal'd to the holy book'. The graveyard setting quietly reminds us of the mortality Margaret has accepted along with her soul.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  12. eyes seal'd to the holy book l. 81

    'Seal'd' suggests both fixed attention and closed sight, a person blind to everything but the page. The line invites different readings. On one view Arnold criticises a narrow religion that seals its followers off from natural affection; Margaret's 'faithless' desertion of her children is the cost of orthodoxy. On another, her steadfastness is heroic and the merman cannot understand it. The next section complicates both readings, since Margaret is not at peace: at her spinning-wheel she sings joyfully but then stares at the sea and weeps. Many critics see here a version of Arnold's lifelong sense of being caught between two worlds, neither fully satisfying.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Wikipedia, 'The Forsaken Merman' (publication, reception, further reading)

  13. Margaret's song in the town ll. 89-93

    The catalogue mirrors the merman's catalogue of the caverns, so the two worlds are given equal lyrical weight. Margaret's joys are social and domestic: 'the humming street', 'the child with its toy', ordinary labour at 'the wheel where I spun'. The 'holy well' recalls the many wells associated with saints in medieval and folk Christianity. Most striking is 'the blessed light of the sun', which the sea-world lacks, since there only 'spent lights' reach. The spinning-wheel has a long literary history as the emblem of a woman's settled, domestic life.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  14. the stare at the sea ll. 100-110

    This passage, imagined by the merman since he cannot see it, gives Margaret an inner life of regret. The 'whizzing wheel stands still' and the spindle drops: her domestic contentment breaks off. Her grief is focused on 'the cold strange eyes of a little Mermaiden' and 'her golden hair', the child she combed in the sea (l. 53). The 'cold strange eyes' suggest that the child already seems alien to her, half of another world. The irregular shortening of lines ('A long, long sigh') slows the verse to match the stillness.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  15. A ceiling of amber, / A pavement of pearl ll. 118-119

    The final sections shift into short, dactylic and anapaestic two-stress lines, giving a lilting, song-like close. While the waves 'roar and whirl' above, the merfolk will have 'A ceiling of amber' over them and 'A pavement of pearl' beneath, a fairy-tale image of riches in which the amber may suggest light glinting through the water. Their song, 'Here came a mortal, / But faithless was she!', turns the story into a legend told against Margaret. In the closing section it becomes 'cruel is she!', a significant hardening of judgment.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  16. sea-stocks; broom ll. 69, 129

    The sea stock (Matthiola sinuata) is a fragrant, purple-flowered plant of sand-dunes and sea-cliffs; Arnold's 'sandy down' is a dune. In the last section (l. 129), 'heaths starr'd with broom' refers to the shrub Cytisus scoparius, whose bright yellow flowers spot the heath like stars. Such precise botanical details anchor the legend in an English or Northern European shoreline, with its 'white-wall'd town' and 'little grey church', rather than in a fairy-tale nowhere.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'

  17. hie l. 133

    The word belongs, like 'sate' and 'anon', to the poem's old-ballad vocabulary. In the final section the merman promises that the family will come up at midnight, at low spring tides, to gaze at the sleeping town, and then 'come back down'. 'Blanch'd sands' (l. 131), sands whitened by the moonlight, and 'a gloom' thrown by the high rocks continue the moonlit, hushed atmosphere. The return visits will be secret and repeated, a ritual of longing.

    Oxford English Dictionary

  18. 'The kings of the sea': the ending ll. 120-123, 140-143

    The two final stanzas end with parallel quatrains. The first: 'Here came a mortal, / But faithless was she! / And alone dwell for ever / The kings of the sea.' The second: 'There dwells a loved one, / But cruel is she! / She left lonely for ever / The kings of the sea.' The small changes ('mortal' to 'loved one', 'faithless' to 'cruel', 'alone' to 'lonely') shift the emphasis from a broken bond to a permanent wound. The merman's dignity ('kings') is preserved, but the poem closes on desolation rather than resolution. Critics continue to differ over whether the poem's sympathy finally lies with the sea or with the church.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), notes to 'The Forsaken Merman'; Wikipedia, 'The Forsaken Merman' (publication, reception, further reading)
