Nature

Dover Beach

by Matthew Arnold 1822–1888

From Lyric Poems

Nature

The sea is calm to-night.
The tide is full, the moon lies fair
Upon the straits;--on the French coast the light
Gleams and is gone; the cliffs of England stand,
Glimmering and vast, out in the tranquil bay.
Come to the window, sweet is the night-air!
Only, from the long line of spray
Where the sea meets the moon-blanch'd land,
Listen! you hear the grating roar
Of pebbles which the waves draw back, and fling,
At their return, up the high strand,
Begin, and cease, and then again begin,
With tremulous cadence slow, and bring
The eternal note of sadness in.

Sophocles long ago
Heard it on the AEgaean, and it brought
Into his mind the turbid ebb and flow
Of human misery; we
Find also in the sound a thought,
Hearing it by this distant northern sea.

The Sea of Faith
Was once, too, at the full, and round earth's shore
Lay like the folds of a bright girdle furl'd.
But now I only hear
Its melancholy, long, withdrawing roar,
Retreating, to the breath
Of the night-wind, down the vast edges drear
And naked shingles of the world.

Ah, love, let us be true
To one another! for the world, which seems
To lie before us like a land of dreams,
So various, so beautiful, so new,
Hath really neither joy, nor love, nor light,
Nor certitude, nor peace, nor help for pain;
And we are here as on a darkling plain
Swept with confused alarms of struggle and flight,
Where ignorant armies clash by night.

Codex Note

Poetical Works of Matthew Arnold (Macmillan, 1891)

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion, date and setting l. 1

    Most editors date the poem to 1851, the year Arnold married Frances Lucy Wightman; the couple stayed at Dover that June and passed through again in the autumn on the way to the Continent. A pencil draft of the first 28 lines survives on the back of a sheet of notes on Empedocles, which Tinker and Lowry described; it ends with a shorthand reference to the closing 'Ah love' passage, so the rest of the poem seems already to have existed. Earlier datings (1849-50) have been proposed, but they rest on the Empedocles notes rather than on any record of a visit to Dover. Arnold held the poem back for sixteen years, printing it only in New Poems (1867), so a lyric of 1851 reached readers after Darwin's Origin (1859) and the Essays and Reviews controversy (1860), which coloured how it was read. The opening sentence is flatly declarative and calm; everything that follows unsettles it.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini)

  2. the French coast ll. 3-4

    From Dover the French coast lies roughly twenty miles away across the Strait (the 'straits' of l. 3). The light that 'Gleams and is gone' is a lighthouse on the French side, and its intermittence is the first hint of the poem's rhythm of appearance and withdrawal, picked up later in the waves that 'Begin, and cease'. The scene is set at a window looking out on the moonlit Channel, with France glimmering and England's cliffs 'Glimmering and vast': two coasts facing each other, an image of the wider European frame in which Arnold, a critic of English insularity, liked to think.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'

  3. the addressee l. 6

    The invitation turns description into dramatic address. The listener never speaks, and is named only in 'Ah, love' (l. 29); readers have generally identified her with Arnold's bride, given the honeymoon setting, though the poem itself keeps her anonymous. The silent woman has drawn later responses: Anthony Hecht's 'The Dover Bitch' (1967) gives her a cynical voice of her own and treats the speaker's gloom as a lover's self-indulgence. Note how the imperative 'Come' is immediately followed by 'Only', which qualifies the beauty just described and moves from sight to sound.

    Anthony Hecht, 'The Dover Bitch: A Criticism of Life' (in The Hard Hours, 1967); 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini); A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'

  4. moon-blanch'd l. 8

    'Blanch' means to make white or pale (French blanc). The compound describes the white chalk and shingle of the Dover shore drained of colour by moonlight, where the 'long line of spray' marks the boundary of sea and land. The pallor anticipates the poem's later emptiness: the beach that looks beautiful by moonlight becomes the 'naked shingles of the world' (l. 28). The elided spelling '-blanch'd' marks the past participle as a single syllable.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blanch'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  5. the grating roar ll. 9-10

    Dover's beach is of shingle (pebbles), not sand, and the undertow drags the stones seaward with a harsh rattling noise, then hurls them back up the 'high strand'. 'Grating' is both literal (the grinding of stones) and evaluative (jarring, unpleasant). The command 'Listen!' marks the poem's decisive shift from the visual calm of the opening to an auditory meditation; from here on the poem is about what is heard, and the sound becomes in turn Sophocles' 'turbid ebb and flow' and the 'melancholy, long, withdrawing roar' of faith.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'

  6. rhythm of the waves ll. 12-13

    The commas break the line into short pulses that imitate the surf's alternation, and the return of 'begin' makes the line circle back on itself. 'Tremulous cadence slow', with its inverted adjective order, slows the line further. The verse paragraph has no regular stanza pattern: line lengths run from three to five stresses and rhymes recur irregularly, an organisation by sound and breath rather than by fixed form. Some critics have called the poem an early experiment towards free verse; others hear in its four irregular paragraphs the movements of a Pindaric ode.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini)

  7. The eternal note of sadness l. 14

    The 'note' is musical: the waves' cadence becomes a single sustained tone of grief. 'Eternal' matters, because the next paragraph argues that the sadness is not new; Sophocles heard it too. The sadness in this first paragraph is general and timeless, whereas the third paragraph will give the modern speaker a specific, historical cause (the retreat of faith). The poem thus moves from a universal melancholy to a particular Victorian one.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  8. Sophocles ll. 15-18

    Arnold admired Sophocles as the poet who 'saw life steadily, and saw it whole' (his sonnet 'To a Friend', 1849). Many annotators point to the second stasimon of Antigone (582-92), where the chorus compares a house cursed by the gods to the sea-swell driven by Thracian winds, rolling up dark sand and beating on the headlands with a moaning roar. Some also cite Trachiniae (112-21), where Heracles' troubles are likened to waves driven over the sea by the south or north wind, as an alternative; commentators do not agree on which passage Arnold had in mind. Either way the point is the continuity of tragic perception: the same sea-sound brought misery to the Greek mind on the Aegean.

    Sophocles, Antigone 582-592 (second stasimon), tr. R. C. Jebb (1891); Sophocles, Trachiniae 112-121, tr. R. C. Jebb (1892); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text and notes

  9. turbid l. 17

    'Turbid' describes water clouded with stirred-up sediment, and figuratively a confused, disordered state. The tidal 'ebb and flow' is transferred to human misery, which recedes and returns as constantly as the tide. The word recalls the dark sand rolled up from the sea-bed in the Antigone simile. The phrase also prepares the central figure of the third paragraph, where faith too has a tide and is now at the ebb.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'turbid'; Sophocles, Antigone 582-592 (second stasimon), tr. R. C. Jebb (1891); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  10. this distant northern sea l. 20

    The contrast between Greek south and modern north recurs in Arnold's criticism, which repeatedly measures Victorian England against Hellenic culture. The speaker and his companion 'Find also in the sound a thought', so the modern listener does not merely repeat Sophocles; he draws a different thought from the same sound, which the next paragraph supplies. 'Distant' implies distance in both space and time from the source of Western tragic wisdom.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  11. The Sea of Faith ll. 21-23

    Faith was 'once, too, at the full', like the tide in l. 2, encircling the world. The loss Arnold describes is cultural rather than personal conversion or apostasy: by mid-century German biblical criticism (Strauss's Life of Jesus, translated by George Eliot in 1846) and geology (Lyell's Principles, 1830-33) had made literal belief harder for educated Victorians. Arnold elsewhere hoped poetry and culture might do some of the work religion had done. The metaphor is carefully chosen: a tide that ebbs also returns, but the poem gives no sign of a coming flood, and its sound is only 'withdrawing'. Readers should note that the poem predates Darwin's Origin (1859), though its publication followed it.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini)

  12. the folds of a bright girdle furl'd l. 23

    A girdle is a belt or sash, and the image is of the sea (and faith) wrapped closely round the shore of the world, its folds bright. Commentators have puzzled over 'furl'd': a furled sail or flag is rolled up and put away, which would suggest something folded and inactive rather than spread out. Some read it simply as 'folded close', gathered and tight about the earth, giving security; others hear an ironic anticipation of the ebb. The grammar is also loose, since it is the Sea that 'Lay like the folds'. Either way, the image of an enclosing, ornamental band contrasts with the exposure of the 'naked shingles' a few lines later.

    C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Dover Beach'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'furl'

  13. withdrawing roar l. 25

    The 'grating roar' of l. 9 returns, now explicitly interpreted. The string of adjectives and the long vowels make the line itself feel slow and withdrawing. 'But now I only hear' insists that the speaker experiences faith only as its departure, an absence perceived as a fading sound. The 'breath / Of the night-wind' that accompanies it continues the elegiac personification.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  14. shingles ll. 27-28

    'Shingle' (often plural) is the loose waterworn gravel of a beach like Dover's. 'Drear' (archaic and poetic for 'dreary') and 'naked' make the receding of faith an exposure: what was clothed in the 'bright girdle' is now stripped. 'Edges' suggests the world reduced to its limits and margins. The paragraph ends on 'world', which the final paragraph immediately takes up in 'the world, which seems / To lie before us'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shingle'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'drear'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'

  15. Ah, love, let us be true ll. 29-30

    'True' means faithful, constant; the word takes the place left by 'Faith'. Critics differ on how consoling this is. Some read the turn as the poem's answer, human love as the one remaining value; others note that the appeal rests on the world's lack of 'love' (l. 33), so that fidelity is a defensive alliance against a hostile world rather than a solution. The tone of the line, with its sigh 'Ah', is closer to plea than to assurance.

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini); Anthony Hecht, 'The Dover Bitch: A Criticism of Life' (in The Hard Hours, 1967)

  16. a land of dreams ll. 30-32

    The verb 'seems' sets up the contrast with 'Hath really' (l. 33). The three-part line 'So various, so beautiful, so new' gives the world's apparent promise, and may recall Adam and Eve at the close of Paradise Lost, with 'The World' 'all before them' (12.646), though the poem does not mark the echo. The 'dreams' are also the young married couple's hopes, now subjected to the poem's sober reckoning.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'

  17. certitude ll. 33-34

    'Certitude' (subjective certainty, sure conviction) is the word that links the private and the intellectual crisis: the age's doubt is precisely the loss of certitude in belief. The repeated 'nor' builds a negative catalogue, the rhetorical inverse of the 'So various, so beautiful, so new' of l. 32. 'Light' recalls the light that 'Gleams and is gone' on the French coast, and prepares the 'darkling plain'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'certitude'; A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'

  18. darkling l. 35

    'Darkling' is an old adverb ('in the dark') also used as an adjective. Arnold's readers would hear Milton's nightingale that 'Sings darkling' (Paradise Lost 3.38-40) and Keats's 'Darkling I listen' in 'Ode to a Nightingale'. Arnold reverses those precedents: there the darkness surrounds a solitary singer, here it covers a battlefield. A. Dwight Culler treats the 'darkling plain' as Arnold's central image of the modern condition. Hardy's 'The Darkling Thrush' (1900) borrows the word, probably with Arnold in mind.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (1674) 3.37-40 ('the wakeful Bird / Sings darkling'); John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), l. 51 ('Darkling I listen'); A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'darkling'

  19. ignorant armies clash by night ll. 36-37

    In Thucydides 7.44 the Athenian assault on Epipolae turns to chaos in the moonlight: the troops could see shapes but could not tell whether they were friends or enemies, and some fought their own side. Arnold's father, Thomas Arnold, had edited Thucydides, and the passage is almost universally accepted as the source; other contemporary battles have also been proposed. 'Ignorant' is the key word: the combatants do not know what they fight for or against, which turns military confusion into an image of an age without certitude, its quarrels (religious, political, intellectual) conducted blindly. 'Confused alarms' (calls to arms) continues the same scene.

    Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 7.44, tr. Richard Crawley (1874); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1965; 2nd edn ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini)

  20. Form of the poem ll. 1-37

    The poem has 37 lines in paragraphs of 14, 6, 8 and 9 lines. Lines range from three to five stresses, and rhymes (night/light, stand/land/strand) recur without a scheme, so that the poem's music follows the rise and fall of the sea rather than a stanza. Critics have described it variously as proto-free verse and as a loosely Pindaric ode whose strophe and antistrophe end in catastrophe. 'Alarms' here means calls to arms (Italian all'arme).

    A. Dwight Culler, Imaginative Reason: The Poetry of Matthew Arnold (Yale UP, 1966), on 'Dover Beach'; 'Dover Beach', Wikipedia (summarising Allott, Tinker and Lowry, Culler, Pratt, Collini); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alarm'
