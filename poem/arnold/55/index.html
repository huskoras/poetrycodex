Love

Switzerland: 5. To Marguerite--Continued

by Matthew Arnold 1822–1888

From Lyric Poems

Love

Yes! in the sea of life enisled,
With echoing straits between us thrown,
Dotting the shoreless watery wild,
We mortal millions live alone.
The islands feel the enclasping flow,
And then their endless bounds they know.

But when the moon their hollows lights,
And they are swept by balms of spring,
And in their glens, on starry nights,
The nightingales divinely sing;
And lovely notes, from shore to shore,
Across the sounds and channels pour--

Oh! then a longing like despair
Is to their farthest caverns sent;
For surely once, they feel, we were
Parts of a single continent!
Now round us spreads the watery plain--
Oh might our marges meet again!

Who order'd, that their longing's fire
Should be, as soon as kindled, cool'd?
Who renders vain their deep desire?--
God, a God their severance ruled!
And bade betwixt their shores to be
The unplumb'd, salt, estranging sea.

Codex Note

Poetical Works of Matthew Arnold (Macmillan, 1891)

Study notes by the editors

  1. To Marguerite--Continued: title and publication l. 1

    The poem first appeared in Empedocles on Etna, and Other Poems (1852) under the title 'To Marguerite, in Returning a Volume of the Letters of Ortis', referring to Ugo Foscolo's novel of despairing love, Ultime lettere di Jacopo Ortis (1802). Arnold changed its title more than once and moved it among the lyrics later grouped as 'Switzerland'; in the 1857 volume it was printed as a second part of 'Isolation', and by 1869 it had its present title, signalling that it continues the preceding poem, 'Isolation. To Marguerite'. 'Yes!' at its opening answers that poem's conclusion that the lover must live alone.

    H. Kerpneck, ed., 'To Marguerite: Continued', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line note; 'To Marguerite--Continued' (Matthew Arnold), EBSCO Research Starters; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  2. Who was Marguerite? l. 4

    Arnold's letters to A. H. Clough of 1848-49 are the main biographical evidence, and the 'Switzerland' poems describe a love affair, set in Switzerland, that ended in separation. Biographers long sought the original of 'Marguerite'. She has often been identified with Mary Claude, whom Arnold knew through the Lake District, though the identification is contested. Some critics have questioned whether Marguerite existed at all, reading the sequence as a poetic construction. This poem, at any rate, generalises the lovers' separation into a statement about all human beings.

    Park Honan, Matthew Arnold: A Life (Weidenfeld and Nicolson / McGraw-Hill, 1981); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'Matthew Arnold', The Poetry Archive (biographical note)

  3. enisled l. 1

    The verb 'enisle' means to make into an island, and hence to isolate. The opening line establishes the poem's controlling metaphor: human beings are islands, and the 'sea of life' that seems to connect them actually divides them. The rare, slightly archaic word gives a formal elevation to the opening, after the conversational 'Yes!'. 'Isle' and 'isolate' are etymologically distinct (Latin insula lies behind both 'isle' and, via Italian isolato, 'isolate'), but Arnold's word draws them together in sense.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. enisle, v.; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  4. With echoing straits between us thrown l. 2

    Straits are narrow sea-channels between land masses. That they are 'echoing' suggests that sound crosses them, but only as reverberation; voices reach across the gap without bridging it. 'Thrown' implies that someone or something has cast these channels between us, preparing for the poem's last stanza, where the question 'Who order'd' this is asked. The image anticipates the third stanza's 'lovely notes, from shore to shore'.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite: Continued', Wikipedia

  5. the shoreless watery wild l. 3

    'Wild' used as a noun (a wilderness, an uncultivated waste) is a poetic usage. The sea is 'shoreless' because it seems to have no limit: islands dot it but do not bound it. The phrase gives the human condition a cosmic scale, millions of small islands scattered across an endless ocean. The sound, with its repeated w and its open vowels, imitates the expanse.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. wild, n.; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  6. Six-line stanza and the shift from we to they l. 5

    Each stanza has six eight-syllable lines rhymed ababcc, closing on a couplet that gives a sense of finality. The first stanza states the human situation ('We mortal millions'), but from line 5 Arnold speaks of 'The islands', which 'feel' and 'know': the metaphor is extended into an allegory with its own feelings. In the third stanza the pronoun 'we' returns ('For surely once, they feel, we were'), fusing islands and people. The movement between the two levels shows how fully the human longing has been projected onto the natural image.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite: Continued', Wikipedia

  7. The islands feel the enclasping flow, / And then their endless bounds they know l. 6

    'Enclasping' suggests an embrace, but the sea's embrace confines. The islands become aware of their boundaries, which are 'endless' in that they surround each island completely and can never be crossed. The oxymoron captures Arnold's double sense of the self: wholly enclosed and also aware of being so. The couplet ends the first stanza with a bleak recognition, and the rhyme 'flow / know' joins the physical sea to the islands' consciousness of it.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite: Continued', Wikipedia

  8. balms of spring l. 8

    A balm is a sweet-smelling ointment or resin that soothes or heals, and by extension any soft, fragrant air. In the second stanza the scene changes from the cold sea to a spring night of moonlight, sweet air and birdsong. The beauty does not console; instead it awakens the longing of the third stanza. The romantic setting of moon, stars and nightingales belongs to the tradition of love poetry, which Arnold invokes only to show its limits.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. balm, n.; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  9. The nightingales divinely sing l. 10

    The nightingale is the traditional bird of love-poetry and of poetic inspiration, from the Ovidian myth of Philomela to Keats's 'Ode to a Nightingale'. Arnold himself wrote 'Philomela' (1853). Here the birds' song travels 'from shore to shore' across the 'sounds and channels', the only thing that crosses the water. 'Sounds' is a pun: inlets of the sea and the sounds of music. Beauty and art connect the isolated islands, but only enough to make them feel their isolation more painfully.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite: Continued', Wikipedia

  10. a longing like despair l. 13

    This is the emotional centre of the poem. The longing is 'sent' to the 'farthest caverns' of the islands, the deepest recesses of the self. That it is 'like despair' marks it as a desire that knows it cannot be fulfilled. Arnold's poetry frequently stages such moments in which natural beauty awakens a sense of loss rather than consolation, as in 'Dover Beach'. The Romantic sublime, in which nature lifts the soul, gives way to a Victorian melancholy.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite--Continued' (Matthew Arnold), EBSCO Research Starters

  11. Parts of a single continent! l. 16

    The islands 'feel', without knowing, that they once formed a single land mass. Behind the line lies John Donne's Meditation XVII in Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624): 'No man is an Iland, intire of it selfe; every man is a peece of the Continent'. Donne affirms the unity of mankind; Arnold reverses him, making unity a lost past and isolation the present condition. Some readers also recall Aristophanes' myth in Plato's Symposium, where lovers are halves of a once-whole being, longing to be reunited. The speaker's 'surely' expresses faith in a lost union that the last stanza will deny can be restored.

    John Donne, Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624), Meditation XVII; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  12. marges l. 18

    'Marge' (from French marge, Latin margo) is an archaic and poetic word for a margin or border, especially a shore. The wish that 'our marges meet again' completes the third stanza with the islands' (and the lovers') longing for reunion. The alliteration of m makes the line musical and pleading. 'Watery plain', in the line before, is a stock poetic phrase for the sea.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. marge, n.; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes

  13. Who order'd ... Who renders vain l. 19

    The poem turns from description to theodicy. The 'longing's fire' that the beauty of the spring night kindles is immediately 'cool'd', an image of fire and water that echoes the sea surrounding the islands. The rhetorical questions demand an agent: someone must have willed this separation. The answer in the next line is ambiguous and austere. The movement from lament to accusation is characteristic of Arnold's mid-century poetry of religious doubt.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; 'To Marguerite--Continued' (Matthew Arnold), EBSCO Research Starters

  14. A God, a God their severance ruled! l. 22

    The shift of article is pointed. The answer to 'Who order'd' is 'a God', an undefined power, rather than the Christian God who, in Christian belief, unites humanity in love. The line has been read as fatalistic, close to the classical idea of a divine power that set fixed boundaries between lands. This text prints 'God, a God', with the first word as an exclamation, while other editions read 'A God, a God'; the difference changes the stress but not the sense. The word 'severance' (division, cutting off) gives a final, legal-sounding decision.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes; Horace, Odes I.3.21-24 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); H. Kerpneck, ed., 'To Marguerite: Continued', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line note

  15. The unplumb'd, salt, estranging sea l. 24

    The final line, with its three slow adjectives, is one of the most celebrated in Victorian poetry. 'Unplumb'd' means unfathomed, too deep to be measured with a plumb-line; 'salt' is a Homeric epithet of the sea, and suggests bitterness and tears; 'estranging' means making strangers of those it separates. Behind it lies Horace, Odes I.3.21-23: 'nequiquam deus abscidit / prudens Oceano dissociabili / terras' ('in vain has a prudent god severed the lands by the estranging Ocean'). Conington's 1863 translation even renders the phrase 'the estranging main'. Arnold, a classicist, turns Horace's poem on the impiety of seafaring into a statement of human isolation decreed by a god.

    Horace, Odes I.3.21-24 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); Horace, Odes I.3, trans. John Conington (1863), Perseids text; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold (Longman, 1965; 2nd edn, ed. Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes
