Death & Dying

Thyrsis

by Matthew Arnold 1822–1888

From Elegiac Poems

Death & Dying

How changed is here each spot man makes or fills!
In the two Hinkseys nothing keeps the same;
The village street its haunted mansion lacks,
And from the sign is gone Sibylla's name,
And from the roofs the twisted chimney-stacks--
Are ye too changed, ye hills?
See, 'tis no foot of unfamiliar men
To-night from Oxford up your pathway strays!
Here came I often, often, in old days----
Thyrsis and I; we still had Thyrsis then.

Runs it not here, the track by Childsworth Farm,
Past the high wood, to where the elm-tree crowns
The hill behind whose ridge the sunset flames?
The signal-elm, that looks on Ilsley Downs,
The Vale, the three lone weirs, the youthful Thames?--
This winter-eve is warm,
Humid the air! leafless, yet soft as spring,
The tender purple spray on copse and briers!
And that sweet city with her dreaming spires,
She needs not June for beauty's heightening,

Lovely all times she lies, lovely to-night!--
Only, methinks, some loss of habit's power
Befalls me wandering through this upland dim.
Once pass'd I blindfold here, at any hour;
Now seldom come I, since I came with him.
That single elm-tree bright
Against the west--I miss it! is it gone?
We prized it dearly; while it stood, we said,
Our friend, the Gipsy-Scholar, was not dead;
While the tree lived, he in these fields lived on.

Too rare, too rare, grow now my visits here,
But once I knew each field, each flower, each stick;
And with the country-folk acquaintance made
By barn in threshing-time, by new-built rick.
Here, too, our shepherd-pipes we first assay'd.
Ah me! this many a year
My pipe is lost, my shepherd's holiday!
Needs must I lose them, needs with heavy heart
Into the world and wave of men depart;
But Thyrsis of his own will went away.

It irk'd him to be here, he could not rest.
He loved each simple joy the country yields,
He loved his mates; but yet he could not keep,
For that a shadow lour'd on the fields,
Here with the shepherds and the silly sheep.
Some life of men unblest
He knew, which made him droop, and fill'd his head.
He went; his piping took a troubled sound
Of storms that rage outside our happy ground;
He could not wait their passing, he is dead.

So, some tempestuous morn in early June,
When the year's primal burst of bloom is o'er,
Before the roses and the longest day--
When garden-walks and all the grassy floor
With blossoms red and white of fallen May
And chestnut-flowers are strewn--
So have I heard the cuckoo's parting cry,
From the wet field, through the vext garden-trees,
Come with the volleying rain and tossing breeze:
The bloom is gone, and with the bloom go I!

Too quick despairer, wherefore wilt thou go?
Soon will the high Midsummer pomps come on,
Soon will the musk carnations break and swell,
Soon shall we have gold-dusted snapdragon,
Sweet-William with his homely cottage-smell,
And stocks in fragrant blow;
Roses that down the alleys shine afar,
And open, jasmine-muffled lattices,
And groups under the dreaming garden-trees,
And the full moon, and the white evening-star.

He hearkens not! light comer, he is flown!
What matters it? next year he will return,
And we shall have him in the sweet spring-days,
With whitening hedges, and uncrumpling fern,
And blue-bells trembling by the forest-ways,
And scent of hay new-mown.
But Thyrsis never more we swains shall see;
See him come back, and cut a smoother reed,
And blow a strain the world at last shall heed--
For Time, not Corydon, hath conquer'd thee!

Alack, for Corydon no rival now!--
But when Sicilian shepherds lost a mate,
Some good survivor with his flute would go,
Piping a ditty sad for Bion's fate;
And cross the unpermitted ferry's flow,
And relax Pluto's brow,
And make leap up with joy the beauteous head
Of Proserpine, among whose crowned hair
Are flowers first open'd on Sicilian air,
And flute his friend, like Orpheus, from the dead.

O easy access to the hearer's grace
When Dorian shepherds sang to Proserpine!
For she herself had trod Sicilian fields,
She knew the Dorian water's gush divine,
She knew each lily white which Enna yields,
Each rose with blushing face;
She loved the Dorian pipe, the Dorian strain.
But ah, of our poor Thames she never heard!
Her foot the Cumner cowslips never stirr'd;
And we should tease her with our plaint in vain!

Well! wind-dispersed and vain the words will be,
Yet, Thyrsis, let me give my grief its hour
In the old haunt, and find our tree-topp'd hill!
Who, if not I, for questing here hath power?
I know the wood which hides the daffodil,
I know the Fyfield tree,
I know what white, what purple fritillaries
The grassy harvest of the river-fields,
Above by Ensham, down by Sandford, yields,
And what sedged brooks are Thames's tributaries;

I know these slopes; who knows them if not I?--
But many a dingle on the loved hill-side,
With thorns once studded, old, white-blossom'd trees,
Where thick the cowslips grew, and far descried
High tower'd the spikes of purple orchises,
Hath since our day put by
The coronals of that forgotten time;
Down each green bank hath gone the ploughboy's team,
And only in the hidden brookside gleam
Primroses, orphans of the flowery prime.

Where is the girl, who by the boatman's door,
Above the locks, above the boating throng,
Unmoor'd our skiff when through the Wytham flats,
Red loosestrife and blond meadow-sweet among
And darting swallows and light water-gnats,
We track'd the shy Thames shore?
Where are the mowers, who, as the tiny swell
Of our boat passing heaved the river-grass,
Stood with suspended scythe to see us pass?--
They all are gone, and thou art gone as well!

Yes, thou art gone! and round me too the night
In ever-nearing circle weaves her shade.
I see her veil draw soft across the day,
I feel her slowly chilling breath invade
The cheek grown thin, the brown hair sprent with grey;
I feel her finger light
Laid pausefully upon life's headlong train;--
The foot less prompt to meet the morning dew,
The heart less bounding at emotion new,
And hope, once crush'd, less quick to spring again.

And long the way appears, which seem'd so short
To the less practised eye of sanguine youth;
And high the mountain-tops, in cloudy air,
The mountain-tops where is the throne of Truth,
Tops in life's morning-sun so bright and bare!
Unbreachable the fort
Of the long-batter'd world uplifts its wall;
And strange and vain the earthly turmoil grows,
And near and real the charm of thy repose,
And night as welcome as a friend would fall.

But hush! the upland hath a sudden loss
Of quiet!--Look, adown the dusk hill-side,
A troop of Oxford hunters going home,
As in old days, jovial and talking, ride!
From hunting with the Berkshire hounds they come.
Quick! let me fly, and cross
Into yon farther field!--'Tis done; and see,
Back'd by the sunset, which doth glorify
The orange and pale violet evening-sky,
Bare on its lonely ridge, the Tree! the Tree!

I take the omen! Eve lets down her veil,
The white fog creeps from bush to bush about,
The west unflushes, the high stars grow bright,
And in the scatter'd farms the lights come out.
I cannot reach the signal-tree to-night,
Yet, happy omen, hail!
Hear it from thy broad lucent Arno-vale
(For there thine earth-forgetting eyelids keep
The morningless and unawakening sleep
Under the flowery oleanders pale),

Hear it, O Thyrsis, still our tree is there!--
Ah, vain! These English fields, this upland dim,
These brambles pale with mist engarlanded,
That lone, sky-pointing tree, are not for him;
To a boon southern country he is fled,
And now in happier air,
Wandering with the great Mother's train divine
(And purer or more subtle soul than thee,
I trow, the mighty Mother doth not see)
Within a folding of the Apennine,

Thou hearest the immortal chants of old!--
Putting his sickle to the perilous grain
In the hot cornfield of the Phrygian king,
For thee the Lityerses-song again
Young Daphnis with his silver voice doth sing;
Sings his Sicilian fold,
His sheep, his hapless love, his blinded eyes--
And how a call celestial round him rang,
And heavenward from the fountain-brink he sprang,
And all the marvel of the golden skies.

There thou art gone, and me thou leavest here
Sole in these fields! yet will I not despair.
Despair I will not, while I yet descry
Neath the mild canopy of English air
That lonely tree against the western sky.
Still, still these slopes, 'tis clear,
Our Gipsy-Scholar haunts, outliving thee!
Fields where soft sheep from cages pull the hay,
Woods with anemonies in flower till May,
Know him a wanderer still; then why not me?

A fugitive and gracious light he seeks,
Shy to illumine; and I seek it too.
This does not come with houses or with gold,
With place, with honour, and a flattering crew;
'Tis not in the world's market bought and sold--
But the smooth-slipping weeks
Drop by, and leave its seeker still untired;
Out of the heed of mortals he is gone,
He wends unfollow'd, he must house alone;
Yet on he fares, by his own heart inspired.

Thou too, O Thyrsis, on like quest wast bound;
Thou wanderedst with me for a little hour!
Men gave thee nothing; but this happy quest,
If men esteem'd thee feeble, gave thee power,
If men procured thee trouble, gave thee rest.
And this rude Cumner ground,
Its fir-topped Hurst, its farms, its quiet fields,
Here cam'st thou in thy jocund youthful time,
Here was thine height of strength, thy golden prime!
And still the haunt beloved a virtue yields.

What though the music of thy rustic flute
Kept not for long its happy, country tone;
Lost it too soon, and learnt a stormy note
Of men contention-tost, of men who groan,
Which task'd thy pipe too sore, and tired thy throat--
It fail'd, and thou wast mute!
Yet hadst thou alway visions of our light,
And long with men of care thou couldst not stay,
And soon thy foot resumed its wandering way,
Left human haunt, and on alone till night.

Too rare, too rare, grow now my visits here!
'Mid city-noise, not, as with thee of yore,
Thyrsis! in reach of sheep-bells is my home.
--Then through the great town's harsh, heart-wearying roar,
Let in thy voice a whisper often come,
To chase fatigue and fear:
_Why faintest thou? I wander'd till I died.
Roam on! The light we sought is shining still.
Dost thou ask proof? Our tree yet crowns the hill,
Our Scholar travels yet the loved hill-side._

Codex Note

Poetical Works of Matthew Arnold (Macmillan, 1891)

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Thyrsis': occasion and genre st. 1, l. 1

    Arnold subtitled the poem 'A Monody, to commemorate the author's friend, Arthur Hugh Clough, who died at Florence, 1861'. Clough died on 13 November 1861. The poem returns to the Cumnor hills near Oxford, the landscape of Arnold's 'The Scholar-Gipsy' (1853), and walks there on a winter evening. It belongs to the line of pastoral elegy from Theocritus and Bion through Milton's 'Lycidas' and Shelley's 'Adonais'. Critics have often noted how little of Clough the man appears in the poem, and still debate how far it mourns Clough and how far Arnold's own lost youth and poetic gift.

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  2. The stanza st. 1, l. 6

    Arnold reuses the stanza of 'The Scholar-Gipsy', itself adapted from the stanza of Keats's odes. The ten lines are iambic pentameter except the sixth, a short line of three stresses ('Are ye too changed, ye hills?'). The short line acts as a hinge or sigh in mid-stanza. The rhymes interlace (fills / same / lacks / name / stacks / hills; men / strays / days / then). The return of the same stanza ties 'Thyrsis' to the earlier poem: it is literally a revisiting.

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  3. the two Hinkseys and Sibylla st. 1, l. 4

    The walk starts from Oxford over the Hinkseys toward Cumnor. The details of change are exact and local: a 'haunted mansion' gone from the village street, twisted chimney-stacks gone, and a woman's name gone from an inn sign. Sibylla was evidently the name of the inn's keeper, painted on its sign. The opening line sets the poem's argument: what man 'makes or fills' changes, so the question 'Are ye too changed, ye hills?' asks whether nature too has failed. The poet's return is only to find loss.

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; 'Hinksey', Wikipedia

  4. Childsworth Farm, Ilsley Downs, the Vale st. 2, l. 11

    'Childsworth' is Arnold's form of Chilswell Farm, near the top of Boars Hill. From the ridge the view runs southwest to the Berkshire Downs around East Ilsley and over the Vale of the White Horse. The 'three lone weirs' and 'youthful Thames' are on the upper Thames near Oxford, 'youthful' in contrast to the broad lower river. The exact topography is part of the elegy's method: the friends' shared knowledge of every field and path becomes a form of memory.

    Geograph photo caption on the 'signal elm' near Chilswell Farm (identifies Childsworth as Chilswell); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  5. 'that sweet city with her dreaming spires' st. 2, l. 19

    The phrase made Oxford 'the city of dreaming spires'. Seen from the winter hills, the towers rise from the mist of the Thames valley. 'Dreaming' gives the city the meditative, half-asleep life of a place of study, and links it with the poem's language of reverie. Arnold elsewhere (preface to Essays in Criticism, 1865) praised Oxford as 'whispering from her towers the last enchantments of the Middle Age'. The next lines insist that she is lovely even in winter: 'She needs not June for beauty's heightening'.

    Matthew Arnold, Essays in Criticism (1865), Preface (the 'last enchantments of the Middle Age'); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  6. the signal-elm st. 2, l. 14

    Arnold makes the single tree a sacred sign of their youth. 'While it stood, we said, / Our friend, the Gipsy-Scholar, was not dead' (st. 3). At first he cannot see it and fears it is gone. Its rediscovery, 'Bare on its lonely ridge, the Tree! the Tree!' (st. 16), is the poem's turning point. Local tradition pointed to a tree near Chilswell; one later caption says the dead tree shown as its remains was in fact an oak. Philip J. Stewart (1981) used weather records, maps and evidence of tree growth to tie the tree to an experience of Arnold's in November 1863. The tree is the elegy's version of the traditional pastoral token.

    Philip J. Stewart, 'Matthew Arnold's Elm', Nineteenth-Century Prose (1981); Geograph photo caption on the 'signal elm' near Chilswell Farm (identifies Childsworth as Chilswell); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  7. the Gipsy-Scholar st. 3, l. 29

    Joseph Glanvill's The Vanity of Dogmatizing (1661) tells of an Oxford scholar who left the university for poverty and joined a band of gipsies to learn their arts, intending to give the world an account when he had mastered them. Arnold's 1853 poem made him an immortal wanderer haunting these same hills, seeking a light beyond modern 'sick hurry'. 'Thyrsis' calls him 'our friend' and closes with him: 'Our Scholar travels yet the loved hill-side'. The two poems are companions, written in the same stanza.

    Joseph Glanvill, The Vanity of Dogmatizing (1661); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  8. shepherd-pipes and the lost pipe st. 4, l. 35

    In pastoral elegy the poets are shepherds and their songs are played on pipes. 'Assay'd' means tried. Arnold's lament 'My pipe is lost, my shepherd's holiday!' refers to his own career. He had become a school inspector (from 1851) and Oxford Professor of Poetry (1857-67), and was turning increasingly to criticism. Having lost the pipe 'needs must' (of necessity), he went 'into the world and wave of men'. The contrast follows: 'But Thyrsis of his own will went away'.

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  9. 'of his own will went away' st. 4, l. 40

    Fellows had to subscribe to the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles. Clough, troubled by religious doubt, resigned his fellowship and tutorship at Oriel College in 1848; he later became principal of University Hall, London (1849). Stanza 5 makes this a pastoral restlessness: 'It irk'd him to be here, he could not rest'. A 'shadow lour'd' (darkened) on the fields. 'Some life of men unblest / He knew' is Clough's sense of social suffering and religious unrest. 'Silly sheep' keeps the old sense of 'silly': innocent, helpless (compare Milton's 'silly thoughts' in the Nativity Ode).

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'silly, adj.'

  10. the cuckoo's parting cry st. 6, l. 57

    Cuckoos fall silent and leave in midsummer. Arnold hears the 'parting cry' as if the bird were saying 'The bloom is gone, and with the bloom go I!' He chides it as a 'Too quick despairer' and lists the high summer flowers it will miss (carnations, snapdragon, sweet-william, stocks, roses, jasmine). The contrast then turns: the bird 'next year ... will return'. That turn draws on the Lament for Bion, where mallows and garden herbs revive each year but men sleep 'a long, endless, unawakening sleep'.

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; [Moschus], Lament for Bion, trans. Andrew Lang, Theocritus, Bion and Moschus (1880); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  11. Thyrsis and Corydon st. 8, l. 80

    Virgil's Eclogue 7 ends with the judge's verdict: 'I remember how Thyrsis was beaten and strove in vain; from then on Corydon has been Corydon for us' (7.69-70). Arnold adopts the names, himself as Corydon and Clough as Thyrsis. The two names come ultimately from Theocritus (Thyrsis sings the lament for Daphnis in Idyll 1). The line courteously reverses the contest: Clough's death was not defeat by a rival poet but by Time. The next stanza laments 'for Corydon no rival now'.

    Virgil, Eclogue 7.69-70; H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Theocritus, Idyll 1 (Thyrsis' song of Daphnis)

  12. the Sicilian shepherds and Bion st. 9, l. 82

    Sicily was the home of pastoral (Theocritus, and traditionally Bion and Moschus). The Lament for Bion, formerly attributed to Moschus, imagines its poet going down to Pluto and singing to Persephone, who as a Sicilian girl would know the Dorian songs and let Bion return. The 'unpermitted ferry' is Charon's boat over the Styx, which living men may not cross. Arnold follows this closely, with 'flute his friend, like Orpheus, from the dead': Orpheus's music won Eurydice back from Hades. Arnold's turn in the next stanza is that this hope fails in the Thames valley.

    [Moschus], Lament for Bion, trans. Andrew Lang, Theocritus, Bion and Moschus (1880); H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  13. Proserpine and Enna st. 9, l. 88

    Ovid tells how Pluto (Dis) seized Proserpina as she picked violets and lilies near Enna in Sicily (Metamorphoses 5.385-424). Milton's Paradise Lost 4.268-72 speaks of 'that fair field / Of Enna, where Proserpin gathering flowers / Herself a fairer flower by gloomy Dis / Was gathered'. Arnold's 'lily white which Enna yields' and 'flowers first open'd on Sicilian air' draw on this tradition. Because she was herself Sicilian, the Dorian shepherds' songs reached her easily ('O easy access to the hearer's grace').

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.385-424; John Milton, Paradise Lost 4.268-72; H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis

  14. Dorian st. 10, l. 97

    Syracuse and other Sicilian cities were founded largely by Dorian Greeks (Syracuse from Corinth). Theocritus's bucolic poems use a Doric literary dialect, and 'Doric' became a byword for pastoral simplicity. Milton's 'Lycidas' ends with the swain singing his 'Doric lay'. The insistent repetition (Dorian shepherds, Dorian water, Dorian pipe, Dorian strain) builds the classical world that is lost in the next lines: 'But ah, of our poor Thames she never heard!'

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; John Milton, 'Lycidas', l. 189

  15. Cumner, Fyfield, Ensham, Sandford st. 10, l. 99

    The English place-names answer the Sicilian ones. Cumnor lies west of Oxford on the hills of the poem. Fyfield, further west, had a famous elm also mentioned in 'The Scholar-Gipsy'. Eynsham and Sandford lie up- and down-stream of Oxford, so 'Above ... down' measures the whole river. Arnold's spelling 'Ensham' follows older usage. The famous fritillaries (snake's-head lilies, white and purple) grow in the Thames water-meadows. The catalogue asserts the poet's local knowledge: 'Who, if not I, for questing here hath power?'

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'; Matthew Arnold, 'The Scholar-Gipsy' (1853), st. 9 (the Fyfield elm)

  16. the Wytham flats and the girl at the boatman's door st. 13, l. 123

    Wytham's meadows lie along the Thames upstream of Oxford. The stanza remembers rowing past red loosestrife and meadow-sweet, the girl who untied their skiff, and the mowers who stopped 'with suspended scythe to see us pass'. It is ubi sunt: 'Where is ... ? Where are ... ?', the classical and medieval lament form asking where the past has gone. The answer comes flatly: 'They all are gone, and thou art gone as well!'

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  17. night and age, 'sprent with grey' st. 14, l. 135

    'Sprent' is the old past participle of 'sprenge', to sprinkle; the OED records it as archaic. Night weaves her shade 'in ever-nearing circle' and lays her 'finger light / ... pausefully upon life's headlong train'. The list of symptoms is personal: less eager feet, less bounding heart, hope 'less quick to spring again'. Arnold was 43 in 1866. Stanza 15 turns the mood philosophical: the 'throne of Truth' on the mountain-tops seems further, the world's fort 'unbreachable', and death 'as welcome as a friend'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sprent, adj.'; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  18. the mountain-tops and the throne of Truth st. 15, l. 144

    The landscape becomes allegory. 'Sanguine youth' (hopeful, from the sanguine humour) misjudged distances. The 'fort / Of the long-batter'd world', besieged by reformers, will not fall. This reflects the shared hopes of Arnold and Clough in the 1840s, the revolutionary year of 1848 and its aftermath. The temptation here, to welcome death as rest, is answered by the omen of the tree.

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  19. 'Bare on its lonely ridge, the Tree! the Tree!' st. 16, l. 160

    The exclamation has an epic ring: compare the Greek soldiers' 'Thalatta! Thalatta!' ('The sea! The sea!') in Xenophon's Anabasis (4.7.24), the cry of men who have found the way home. 'I take the omen!' begins the next stanza. Though he 'cannot reach the signal-tree to-night', the tree's survival means the Gipsy-Scholar 'was not dead'. This hope is what the elegy has to offer instead of a Christian resurrection, a feature critics stress when comparing 'Thyrsis' with 'Lycidas'.

    Xenophon, Anabasis 4.7.24; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  20. the Arno-vale and the 'unawakening sleep' st. 17, l. 169

    Clough is buried in the English (Protestant) Cemetery in Florence. The oleander, a Mediterranean flowering shrub, marks the southern grave against the English 'brambles pale with mist'. The phrase 'morningless and unawakening sleep' recalls the Lament for Bion, where men, unlike the garden plants, sleep a long, endless, unwakening sleep. 'Earth-forgetting eyelids' adds the idea of Lethe, oblivion. The pastoral convention would bring the mourned shepherd back to the fields; Arnold insists instead on the distance between the Italian grave and the English hills.

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; [Moschus], Lament for Bion, trans. Andrew Lang, Theocritus, Bion and Moschus (1880); Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  21. the great Mother st. 18, l. 177

    The Great Mother of antiquity was Cybele, the Phrygian goddess, often fused with Demeter or Rhea. The Phrygian setting of the next stanza (the 'Phrygian king' Lityerses) supports the link. The phrase is often glossed simply as Nature. Clough's soul now wanders in a 'boon southern country' (boon = bountiful, kindly) among the Apennines that fold round Florence. The praise in the parenthesis, 'purer or more subtle soul than thee ... the mighty Mother doth not see', is Arnold's most direct tribute.

    H. Kerpneck, notes to 'Thyrsis', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), including Arnold's own note on Daphnis; Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'

  22. Lityerses and Daphnis st. 19, l. 184

    Arnold's note explains that Daphnis followed his mistress Piplea to Phrygia, where King Lityerses made strangers compete with him in reaping and killed those he beat. Hercules arrived, won the contest and killed Lityerses. The 'Lityerses-song' was an ancient reapers' song. Arnold adds another tradition: Daphnis, bound by a jealous nymph's oath, loved a princess and was blinded; his father Mercury raised him to heaven, and a fountain sprang where he rose. Hence 'his blinded eyes', 'the fountain-brink' and 'the golden skies'. Daphnis's lament and death are the subject of Theocritus's first Idyll. Clough hears in the afterlife the original pastoral songs.

  23. 'A fugitive and gracious light' st. 21, l. 201

    The 'light' is never defined: truth, joy, or spiritual insight beyond dogma. It is 'shy to illumine', so it shows itself rarely. The Scholar 'wends unfollow'd' and 'must house alone'. Arnold's 'and I seek it too' marks the turn from mourning to consolation. Stanza 22 extends it to Clough: 'Thou too, O Thyrsis, on like quest wast bound'. Men gave him nothing, but the quest gave him power and rest. The 'fir-topped Hurst' is Cumnor Hurst, a wooded hill.

    Kenneth Allott and Miriam Allott, eds., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, 2nd ed. (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1979), headnote and notes to 'Thyrsis'; C. B. Tinker and H. F. Lowry, The Poetry of Matthew Arnold: A Commentary (Oxford UP, 1940), on 'Thyrsis'

  24. 'a stormy note / Of men contention-tost' st. 23, l. 223

    Arnold says his friend's pipe lost its 'happy, country tone', 'learnt a stormy note' of men 'contention-tost', and failed: 'thou wast mute!' He means Clough's turn to the controversies of faith and society. Many modern readers think Arnold underrated Clough's ironic, experimental work (The Bothie, 1848; Amours de Voyage, 1858; the posthumous Dipsychus). They see the elegy's judgement as reflecting Arnold's own taste and his own fear for his poetic career.

  25. the closing words of Thyrsis st. 24, l. 237

    Pastoral elegies often end with a turn to consolation, as 'Lycidas' ends with the dead friend in heaven. Arnold's version is secular: an imagined whisper amid 'the great town's harsh, heart-wearying roar' (Arnold now lived in London). Thyrsis repeats the poem's symbols as proof: 'The light we sought is shining still', 'Our tree yet crowns the hill', 'Our Scholar travels yet'. The italics mark another voice, but it is Arnold's own wish speaking. That ambiguity is central to modern readings of the poem.
