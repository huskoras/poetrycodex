Nature

The Scholar-Gipsy

by Matthew Arnold 1822–1888

From Elegiac Poems

Nature

Go, for they call you, shepherd, from the hill;
Go, shepherd, and untie the wattled cotes!
No longer leave thy wistful flock unfed,
Nor let thy bawling fellows rack their throats,
Nor the cropp'd herbage shoot another head.
But when the fields are still,
And the tired men and dogs all gone to rest,
And only the white sheep are sometimes seen
Cross and recross the strips of moon-blanch'd green,
Come, shepherd, and again begin the quest!

Here, where the reaper was at work of late--
In this high field's dark corner, where he leaves
His coat, his basket, and his earthen cruse,
And in the sun all morning binds the sheaves,
Then here, at noon, comes back his stores to use--
Here will I sit and wait,
While to my ear from uplands far away
The bleating of the folded flocks is borne,
With distant cries of reapers in the corn--
All the live murmur of a summer's day.

Screen'd is this nook o'er the high, half-reap'd field,
And here till sun-down, shepherd! will I be.
Through the thick corn the scarlet poppies peep,
And round green roots and yellowing stalks I see
Pale pink convolvulus in tendrils creep;
And air-swept lindens yield
Their scent, and rustle down their perfumed showers
Of bloom on the bent grass where I am laid,
And bower me from the August sun with shade;
And the eye travels down to Oxford's towers.

And near me on the grass lies Glanvil's book--
Come, let me read the oft-read tale again!
The story of the Oxford scholar poor,
Of pregnant parts and quick inventive brain,
Who, tired of knocking at preferment's door,
One summer-morn forsook
His friends, and went to learn the gipsy-lore,
And roam'd the world with that wild brotherhood,
And came, as most men deem'd, to little good,
But came to Oxford and his friends no more.

But once, years after, in the country-lanes,
Two scholars, whom at college erst he knew,
Met him, and of his way of life enquired;
Whereat he answer'd, that the gipsy-crew,
His mates, had arts to rule as they desired
The workings of men's brains,
And they can bind them to what thoughts they will.
"And I," he said, "the secret of their art,
When fully learn'd, will to the world impart;
But it needs heaven-sent moments for this skill."

This said, he left them, and return'd no more.--
But rumours hung about the country-side,
That the lost Scholar long was seen to stray,
Seen by rare glimpses, pensive and tongue-tied,
In hat of antique shape, and cloak of grey,
The same the gipsies wore.
Shepherds had met him on the Hurst in spring;
At some lone alehouse in the Berkshire moors,
On the warm ingle-bench, the smock-frock'd boors
Had found him seated at their entering,

But, 'mid their drink and clatter, he would fly.
And I myself seem half to know thy looks,
And put the shepherds, wanderer! on thy trace;
And boys who in lone wheatfields scare the rooks
I ask if thou hast pass'd their quiet place;
Or in my boat I lie
Moor'd to the cool bank in the summer-heats,
'Mid wide grass meadows which the sunshine fills,
And watch the warm, green-muffled Cumner hills,
And wonder if thou haunt'st their shy retreats.

For most, I know, thou lov'st retired ground!
Thee at the ferry Oxford riders blithe,
Returning home on summer-nights, have met
Crossing the stripling Thames at Bab-lock-hithe,
Trailing in the cool stream thy fingers wet,
As the punt's rope chops round;
And leaning backward in a pensive dream,
And fostering in thy lap a heap of flowers
Pluck'd in shy fields and distant Wychwood bowers,
And thine eyes resting on the moonlit stream.

And then they land, and thou art seen no more!--
Maidens, who from the distant hamlets come
To dance around the Fyfield elm in May,
Oft through the darkening fields have seen thee roam,
Or cross a stile into the public way.
Oft thou hast given them store
Of flowers--the frail-leaf'd, white anemony,
Dark bluebells drench'd with dews of summer eves,
And purple orchises with spotted leaves--
But none hath words she can report of thee.

And, above Godstow Bridge, when hay-time's here
In June, and many a scythe in sunshine flames,
Men who through those wide fields of breezy grass
Where black-wing'd swallows haunt the glittering Thames,
To bathe in the abandon'd lasher pass,
Have often pass'd thee near
Sitting upon the river bank o'ergrown;
Mark'd thine outlandish garb, thy figure spare,
Thy dark vague eyes, and soft abstracted air--
But, when they came from bathing, thou wast gone!

At some lone homestead in the Cumner hills,
Where at her open door the housewife darns,
Thou hast been seen, or hanging on a gate
To watch the threshers in the mossy barns.
Children, who early range these slopes and late
For cresses from the rills,
Have known thee eying, all an April-day,
The springing pastures and the feeding kine;
And mark'd thee, when the stars come out and shine,
Through the long dewy grass move slow away.

In autumn, on the skirts of Bagley Wood--
Where most the gipsies by the turf-edged way
Pitch their smoked tents, and every bush you see
With scarlet patches tagg'd and shreds of grey,
Above the forest-ground called Thessaly--
The blackbird, picking food,
Sees thee, nor stops his meal, nor fears at all;
So often has he known thee past him stray,
Rapt, twirling in thy hand a wither'd spray,
And waiting for the spark from heaven to fall.

And once, in winter, on the causeway chill
Where home through flooded fields foot-travellers go,
Have I not pass'd thee on the wooden bridge,
Wrapt in thy cloak and battling with the snow,
Thy face tow'rd Hinksey and its wintry ridge?
And thou hast climb'd the hill,
And gain'd the white brow of the Cumner range;
Turn'd once to watch, while thick the snowflakes fall,
The line of festal light in Christ-Church hall--
Then sought thy straw in some sequester'd grange.

But what--I dream! Two hundred years are flown
Since first thy story ran through Oxford halls,
And the grave Glanvil did the tale inscribe
That thou wert wander'd from the studious walls
To learn strange arts, and join a gipsy-tribe;
And thou from earth art gone
Long since, and in some quiet churchyard laid--
Some country-nook, where o'er thy unknown grave
Tall grasses and white flowering nettles wave,
Under a dark, red-fruited yew-tree's shade.

--No, no, thou hast not felt the lapse of hours!
For what wears out the life of mortal men?
'Tis that from change to change their being rolls;
'Tis that repeated shocks, again, again,
Exhaust the energy of strongest souls
And numb the elastic powers.
Till having used our nerves with bliss and teen,
And tired upon a thousand schemes our wit,
To the just-pausing Genius we remit
Our worn-out life, and are--what we have been.

Thou hast not lived, why should'st thou perish, so?
Thou hadst one aim, one business, one desire;
Else wert thou long since number'd with the dead!
Else hadst thou spent, like other men, thy fire!
The generations of thy peers are fled,
And we ourselves shall go;
But thou possessest an immortal lot,
And we imagine thee exempt from age
And living as thou liv'st on Glanvil's page,
Because thou hadst--what we, alas! have not.

For early didst thou leave the world, with powers
Fresh, undiverted to the world without,
Firm to their mark, not spent on other things;
Free from the sick fatigue, the languid doubt,
Which much to have tried, in much been baffled, brings.
O life unlike to ours!
Who fluctuate idly without term or scope,
Of whom each strives, nor knows for what he strives,
And each half lives a hundred different lives;
Who wait like thee, but not, like thee, in hope.

Thou waitest for the spark from heaven! and we,
Light half-believers of our casual creeds,
Who never deeply felt, nor clearly will'd,
Whose insight never has borne fruit in deeds,
Whose vague resolves never have been fulfill'd;
For whom each year we see
Breeds new beginnings, disappointments new;
Who hesitate and falter life away,
And lose to-morrow the ground won to-day--
Ah! do not we, wanderer! await it too?

Yes, we await it!--but it still delays,
And then we suffer! and amongst us one,
Who most has suffer'd, takes dejectedly
His seat upon the intellectual throne;
And all his store of sad experience he
Lays bare of wretched days;
Tells us his misery's birth and growth and signs,
And how the dying spark of hope was fed,
And how the breast was soothed, and how the head,
And all his hourly varied anodynes.

This for our wisest! and we others pine,
And wish the long unhappy dream would end,
And waive all claim to bliss, and try to bear;
With close-lipp'd patience for our only friend,
Sad patience, too near neighbour to despair--
But none has hope like thine!
Thou through the fields and through the woods dost stray,
Roaming the country-side, a truant boy,
Nursing thy project in unclouded joy,
And every doubt long blown by time away.

O born in days when wits were fresh and clear,
And life ran gaily as the sparkling Thames;
Before this strange disease of modern life,
With its sick hurry, its divided aims,
Its heads o'ertax'd, its palsied hearts, was rife--
Fly hence, our contact fear!
Still fly, plunge deeper in the bowering wood!
Averse, as Dido did with gesture stern
From her false friend's approach in Hades turn,
Wave us away, and keep thy solitude!

Still nursing the unconquerable hope,
Still clutching the inviolable shade,
With a free, onward impulse brushing through,
By night, the silver'd branches of the glade--
Far on the forest-skirts, where none pursue,
On some mild pastoral slope
Emerge, and resting on the moonlit pales
Freshen thy flowers as in former years
With dew, or listen with enchanted ears,
From the dark dingles, to the nightingales!

But fly our paths, our feverish contact fly!
For strong the infection of our mental strife,
Which, though it gives no bliss, yet spoils for rest;
And we should win thee from thy own fair life,
Like us distracted, and like us unblest.
Soon, soon thy cheer would die,
Thy hopes grow timorous, and unfix'd thy powers,
And thy clear aims be cross and shifting made;
And then thy glad perennial youth would fade,
Fade, and grow old at last, and die like ours.

Then fly our greetings, fly our speech and smiles!
--As some grave Tyrian trader, from the sea,
Descried at sunrise an emerging prow
Lifting the cool-hair'd creepers stealthily,
The fringes of a southward-facing brow
Among the AEgaean isles;
And saw the merry Grecian coaster come,
Freighted with amber grapes, and Chian wine,
Green, bursting figs, and tunnies steep'd in brine--
And knew the intruders on his ancient home,

The young light-hearted masters of the waves--
And snatch'd his rudder, and shook out more sail;
And day and night held on indignantly
O'er the blue Midland waters with the gale,
Betwixt the Syrtes and soft Sicily,
To where the Atlantic raves
Outside the western straits; and unbent sails
There, where down cloudy cliffs, through sheets of foam,
Shy traffickers, the dark Iberians come;
And on the beach undid his corded bales.

Codex Note

Poetical Works of Matthew Arnold (Macmillan, 1891)

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening: the shepherd ll. 1-10

    Published in Arnold's Poems (1853), the poem opens in the convention of classical pastoral (Theocritus, Virgil's Eclogues), addressing an unnamed shepherd. The shepherd is told to go and tend his flock by day and come back at evening to 'begin the quest' again, the quest being the search for the Scholar-Gipsy. The shepherd is a pastoral convention rather than a portrait, though Arnold later told his brother that the poem was meant to keep alive memories of walks in the Cumnor hills with Clough and others; his elegy for Clough, 'Thyrsis' (1866), is a companion poem set in the same landscape. The shepherd is addressed only in the first three stanzas, and the speaker continues alone.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  2. The ten-line stanza st. 1

    Each stanza has ten lines rhyming abcbcadeed: pentameters except the sixth line, a trimeter (here 'But when the fields are still'). The form is widely recognised as a variation on the ten-line stanza Keats used in his great odes of 1819, especially 'Ode to a Nightingale', which also has a short line (its eighth). Arnold's choice links the poem with Keats's mood of longing to escape from the 'fever and the fret' of the world, and the nightingales of stanza 22 (l. 220) make the debt explicit. Arnold used the same stanza, slightly varied, for 'Thyrsis'.

    Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), Poetry Foundation text

  3. wattled cotes; rack; cropp'd herbage ll. 2-5

    A 'cote' is a shelter or pen for animals (as in 'dovecote'), and 'wattled' means made of wattle, stakes interwoven with twigs, the traditional material of sheep hurdles. The shepherd should untie the folds and let the flock out. 'Bawling fellows rack their throats' means his fellow shepherds strain their voices calling him ('rack' as in torture, to stretch). 'Nor the cropp'd herbage shoot another head' means: do not leave the grazed grass time to send up new growth, i.e. graze the flock at once. The diction is deliberately rustic and precise, setting the pastoral scene with real agricultural detail.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cote, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wattle, n.1'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  4. earthen cruse l. 13

    A 'cruse' is a small earthenware pot or jar for liquids. The word was archaic by Arnold's day and chiefly biblical, as in the widow of Zarephath's 'a little oil in a cruse' (1 Kings 17:12). The reaper leaves his coat, basket and drinking jug in a corner of the field, to return at noon. The archaic word lends the harvest scene a timeless, almost scriptural simplicity, preparing for the Scholar-Gipsy's world of 'two hundred years' before.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cruse, n.'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Kings 17:12

  5. Glanvil's book ll. 31-40

    Arnold printed a passage from Glanvill as a headnote to the poem. In chapter 20 of The Vanity of Dogmatizing (1661), the philosopher Joseph Glanvill told of 'a Lad in the University of Oxford' of 'very pregnant and ready parts' who, lacking 'preferment', left for poverty and joined 'a company of Vagabond Gypsies', learning their secret of binding others' thoughts 'by the power of Imagination'. Glanvill used the story to argue that apparently impossible things may be possible. Arnold borrows its phrasing ('pregnant parts', 'preferment's door') and its scene of two old acquaintances meeting the wanderer. The 'two hundred years' of line 131 measures the gap between Glanvill and Arnold. In 1929 Marjorie Hope Nicolson suggested the story's original was the alchemist F. M. van Helmont.

    Joseph Glanvill, The Vanity of Dogmatizing (London, 1661), ch. 20 (EEBO-TCP text A42833); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception)

  6. arts to rule ... heaven-sent moments ll. 47-50

    In Glanvill the gipsies 'could do wonders by the power of Imagination', binding others' thoughts; the scholar intends, once he has 'compassed the whole secret', to 'give the world an account' of it. Arnold keeps the plot but changes its emphasis: the secret cannot be learned by method, only in 'heaven-sent moments'. This prepares the poem's central symbol of waiting for 'the spark from heaven' (ll. 120, 171). Critics have read the art as poetic inspiration, as religious faith, or as the integrated mind Arnold felt his age had lost; the poem never says what it finally is.

    Joseph Glanvill, The Vanity of Dogmatizing (London, 1661), ch. 20 (EEBO-TCP text A42833); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception)

  7. the Hurst; the Berkshire moors ll. 57-58

    The Hurst is Cumnor Hurst, a hill about three miles west of Oxford, in the area Arnold and Clough walked as young men. Before the county boundary changes of 1974 the country south and west of the Thames at Oxford lay in Berkshire, hence 'the Berkshire moors' for the higher open ground around Cumnor. The precise local geography is part of the poem's appeal: it is often described as a topographical poem of Oxford's countryside, and many of the places can still be walked.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Wikipedia, 'Cumnor Hurst'; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception)

  8. ingle-bench; smock-frock'd boors l. 59

    An 'ingle' is a fire on a hearth, or the fireside itself (from Scottish Gaelic aingeal, 'fire'), and an ingle-bench is a seat in the chimney-corner. A 'smock-frock' was the loose linen over-garment, often embroidered ('smocked'), worn by English farm labourers. 'Boors' here simply means peasants or rustics (Dutch boer, 'farmer'), not yet with the full modern sense of rudeness. The scene of rustic drinkers coming upon the silent scholar, who then flees, is one of many sightings that build his elusive legend.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ingle, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'smock-frock, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'boor, n.'

  9. Cumner hills l. 69

    Cumnor is a village on the high ground west of Oxford; the 'Cumner hills' or 'Cumner range' (l. 127) form the skyline seen from the Thames meadows. Arnold's spelling 'Cumner' reflects older usage. The phrase 'green-muffled' captures how the hills look softened by summer foliage. The same hills reappear in 'Thyrsis', where Arnold returns after Clough's death to find the landscape changed. The scholar is imagined haunting their 'shy retreats', places where one may be alone.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Matthew Arnold, 'Thyrsis' (1866), Poetry Foundation text

  10. Bab-lock-hithe; Wychwood ll. 74-79

    Bablock Hythe ('hithe' = landing place) is a crossing of the Thames between Cumnor and Stanton Harcourt where a ferry, drawn by a rope, has run for centuries; 'the punt's rope chops round' describes the swing of the ferry-rope in the current. The 'stripling Thames' is the young, narrow upper river. Wychwood, an ancient royal forest north-west of Oxford, gives the 'distant ... bowers' where the scholar picks flowers. The picture of him trailing his fingers in the water with a lap full of flowers recalls pastoral figures and, for some readers, Ophelia.

    Wikipedia, 'Bablock Hythe'; Wikipedia, 'Wychwood'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  11. the Fyfield elm in May l. 83

    Fyfield is a village about eight miles south-west of Oxford, then in Berkshire. Arnold's maidens dance around its elm on May Day, a traditional English festival of spring; 'Thyrsis' also mentions 'the Fyfield tree'. The flowers the scholar gives them (wood anemone, bluebells, purple orchis) are plants of the local woods and meadows in spring. The maidens can 'report' nothing of what he said, reinforcing his silence and elusiveness.

    Wikipedia, 'Fyfield, Oxfordshire'; Matthew Arnold, 'Thyrsis' (1866), Poetry Foundation text; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  12. Godstow Bridge; lasher ll. 91-95

    Godstow, on the Thames north-west of Oxford beside Port Meadow, has the ruins of a medieval Benedictine nunnery, burial place of Rosamund Clifford, mistress of Henry II. 'Lasher' is a local Thames word for a weir, the water rushing over it, or the pool beneath, a favourite bathing place; 'abandon'd' suggests one no longer in use. The June haymaking, with 'many a scythe in sunshine flames', and the bathers crossing the meadows give a vivid picture of the river's summer life, from which the scholar vanishes.

    Wikipedia, 'Godstow'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lasher, n.'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  13. Bagley Wood; Thessaly ll. 111-115

    Bagley Wood lies south of Oxford towards Abingdon and was long a camping ground for travellers. The gipsies' tents are 'smoked' and the bushes hung with their washing, 'scarlet patches' and 'shreds of grey'. The 'forest-ground called Thessaly' is evidently a local field- or place-name; the poem's own 'called' marks it as such. Its Greek associations are apt: Thessaly was famous in classical literature as the home of witches and magic (as in Lucan's Pharsalia, book 6, and Apuleius's Metamorphoses), and so suits the gipsies' secret arts.

    Wikipedia, 'Bagley Wood'; Lucan, Pharsalia (Bellum Civile), book 6, on the Thessalian witches (Latin Library); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  14. the spark from heaven ll. 120, 171

    The scholar is pictured 'waiting for the spark from heaven to fall', and the phrase returns at line 171 ('Thou waitest for the spark from heaven! and we ...'). It develops the 'heaven-sent moments' of line 50. The spark may be read as poetic inspiration, as religious revelation, as the moment in which the gipsies' secret becomes clear, or more generally as the unifying insight Arnold felt modern life lacked. The image draws on the Promethean fire stolen from heaven and on Pentecostal tongues of fire (Acts 2:3). The scholar waits 'in hope'; the moderns, the poem says, also wait but without hope.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Acts 2:3; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception)

  15. Hinksey; Christ-Church hall ll. 121-130

    North and South Hinksey are villages just west of Oxford, reached across flood meadows by a raised causeway and wooden bridges, which the speaker imagines crossing in winter. From the 'white brow' of the snow-covered Cumnor ridge the scholar looks back to see the 'line of festal light' in the hall of Christ Church, Oxford's largest college, lit for an evening feast. The image sets the scholar outside the warmth and society of the university he renounced, and the 'sequester'd grange' (secluded farm building) where he sleeps on straw underlines his poverty and solitude.

    Wikipedia, 'North Hinksey'; Wikipedia, 'Christ Church, Oxford' (Great Hall); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  16. teen l. 147

    'Teen' (Old English tēona, 'injury, harm, grief') was archaic by Arnold's time, surviving in poetic usage from Spenser and Shakespeare (e.g. Miranda's 'the teen that I have turn'd you to', The Tempest 1.2). Paired with 'bliss', it sums up the emotional extremes that 'use' or wear out human nerves. The passage (ll. 141-150) explains why the scholar cannot have died: what kills people is not time but repeated 'shocks' that exhaust their energy, and he has avoided these by devoting himself to one aim.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'teen, n.1'; William Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2.64 (MIT Complete Works); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  17. the just-pausing Genius l. 149

    In Roman religion a person's genius was the guardian spirit born with him, governing his life and character; Horace calls it 'the companion who rules our natal star' (Epistles 2.2.187-189). Arnold imagines this Genius as 'just-pausing', waiting for the moment when we hand back ('remit') our worn-out life, at which point we are 'what we have been', that is, nothing: dead, or merely part of the past. The classical personification gives a stoic, impersonal account of death, contrasting with the poem's later 'immortal lot' of the scholar.

    Horace, Epistles 2.2.187-189 (Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genius, n.'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  18. Light half-believers of our casual creeds ll. 171-175

    The moderns are 'half-believers', holding their 'casual creeds' (religious or philosophical) lightly, never deeply feeling or clearly willing, with 'insight' that 'never has borne fruit in deeds'. The passage is one of the best-known statements of Victorian religious doubt, comparable to Arnold's 'Dover Beach' and Clough's poems. The contrast is with the scholar's 'one aim, one business, one desire' (l. 152). Arnold himself wrote to Clough in November 1853 that the poem awakened a 'pleasing melancholy' but did not 'animate' readers, a criticism of exactly this mood.

    Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Victorian Web, Adam Roberts on Arnold (Arnold to Clough, 30 November 1853)

  19. one, who most has suffer'd ... intellectual throne ll. 182-190

    Among the moderns 'one, who most has suffer'd' becomes the age's chief thinker and 'lays bare' his misery: its 'birth and growth and signs' and the 'anodynes' (pain-killers) that soothe it. Critics have long debated who is meant. A common view identifies Tennyson, whose In Memoriam (1850) chronicled his grief for Arthur Hallam in a long series of lyrics; Isaac Gruber has recently read the poem's medical language as a criticism of In Memoriam. Others suggest Goethe, whom Arnold revered as a sage. The phrase 'This for our wisest!' (l. 191) is sardonic whichever is meant.

    Isaac Gruber, 'The Final Simile of The Scholar-Gipsy', Essays in Criticism 74.1 (2024), doi:10.1093/escrit/cgae004; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'anodyne, n.'

  20. this strange disease of modern life ll. 203-205

    The phrase defines the poem's central contrast between the scholar, 'born in days when wits were fresh and clear', and the Victorian present. Its symptoms are listed: 'sick hurry', 'divided aims', 'heads o'ertax'd', 'palsied hearts'. The medical imagery continues in 'infection' and 'feverish contact' (ll. 221-222), so that the scholar must flee as one flees contagion. The diagnosis anticipates Arnold's later prose criticism of his age (Culture and Anarchy, 1869). F. R. Leavis found its charm to be one of relaxation; others see the diagnosis as its lasting strength.

    Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  21. Averse, as Dido did ll. 208-210

    In Virgil's Aeneid, book 6, Aeneas meets the shade of Dido, the Carthaginian queen who killed herself after he abandoned her. He pleads, but she keeps her eyes fixed on the ground, 'turned away' (aversa, 6.469), and flees to the shade of her first husband Sychaeus. Arnold's 'Averse' translates Virgil's word, and 'her false friend' is Aeneas. The scholar should turn from the moderns as Dido turned from her betrayer. The allusion also prepares the Carthaginian (Tyrian) note of the closing simile, since Dido was a Tyrian princess.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.450-476 (Latin Library); Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  22. the nightingales l. 220

    The scholar is imagined at night listening 'with enchanted ears, / From the dark dingles' ('dingles' = deep wooded hollows) to the nightingales. The bird, traditionally linked to poetry and to the myth of Philomela, here recalls Keats's ode, in which the speaker wishes to 'fade away' with the bird and forget 'The weariness, the fever, and the fret' of human life. Arnold's 'feverish contact' in the very next line echoes that 'fever'. The scholar has already achieved what Keats's speaker only longed for.

    John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), Poetry Foundation text; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dingle, n.'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'

  23. the Tyrian trader simile ll. 231-250

    The poem ends with an extended Homeric simile. A 'grave' Tyrian (Phoenician) trader sees a 'merry Grecian coaster' arriving among the Aegean isles with luxury goods, recognises 'the intruders on his ancient home', and sails the length of the Mediterranean to trade with the 'dark Iberians' of Spain. The scholar's flight from moderns is thus compared to the older, graver civilisation withdrawing before a lighter, newer one. Critics disagree about the fit: the scholar, not the moderns, seems the old trader, yet the 'light-hearted' Greeks hardly resemble the 'feverish' Victorians. Isaac Gruber has proposed a link with Tennyson; others read the simile as an image of the poet's own withdrawal into art.

    Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Isaac Gruber, 'The Final Simile of The Scholar-Gipsy', Essays in Criticism 74.1 (2024), doi:10.1093/escrit/cgae004; Wikipedia, 'The Scholar Gipsy' (publication, sources, Arnold's letter to Clough of November 1853, critical reception)

  24. Grecian coaster; Chian wine; tunnies ll. 237-239

    A 'coaster' is a vessel that trades along coasts. Chios, an Aegean island off Asia Minor, produced one of the most famous wines of the ancient world. 'Tunnies' are tuna, salted or pickled in brine, a staple trade item of the Greek world. The rich sensuous list of goods characterises the Greeks as 'young light-hearted masters of the waves', in contrast to the 'grave' Tyrian. Historically the Phoenicians had been the dominant seafarers of the Mediterranean before Greek colonisation spread from the eighth century BC.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tunny, n.'; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'; Herodotus, Histories 1.1 (Phoenician traders), Perseus

  25. Midland waters; Syrtes; western straits; Iberians ll. 243-250

    'Midland waters' translates the Latin mare mediterraneum, the sea 'in the middle of the land'. The Syrtes are two shallow, treacherous gulfs on the North African coast (modern Libya and Tunisia), feared by ancient sailors; Acts 27:17 refers to fears of running onto these 'quicksands'. The 'western straits' are the Strait of Gibraltar, beyond which the Atlantic 'raves'. The 'shy traffickers, the dark Iberians' and the merchant who 'undid his corded bales' on the beach recall Herodotus's account (4.196) of the Carthaginians' 'silent trade' beyond the Pillars of Heracles, where goods were laid on the shore and exchanged without speech. The silent trade suits the scholar's silence.

    Herodotus, Histories 4.196 (silent trade), Perseus; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Acts 27:17; Kenneth Allott, ed., The Poems of Matthew Arnold, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1965; 2nd ed. by Miriam Allott, 1979), headnote and notes to 'The Scholar-Gipsy'
