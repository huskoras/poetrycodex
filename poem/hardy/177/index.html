Nature

Hap

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Wessex Poems and Other Verses

Nature

IF but some vengeful god would call to me
From up the sky, and laugh: “Thou suffering thing,
Know that thy sorrow is my ecstasy,
That thy love’s loss is my hate’s profiting!”

Then would I bear, and clench myself, and die,
Steeled by the sense of ire unmerited;
Half-eased in that a Powerfuller than I
Had willed and meted me the tears I shed.

But not so. How arrives it joy lies slain,
And why unblooms the best hope ever sown?
—Crass Casualty obstructs the sun and rain,
And dicing Time for gladness casts a moan . . .
These purblind Doomsters had as readily strown
Blisses about my pilgrimage as pain.

1866.

“IN VISION I ROAMED”
TO —

IN vision I roamed the flashing Firmament,
So fierce in blazon that the Night waxed wan,
As though with an awed sense of such ostent;
And as I thought my spirit ranged on and on

In footless traverse through ghast heights of sky,
To the last chambers of the monstrous Dome,
Where stars the brightest here to darkness die:
Then, any spot on our own Earth seemed Home!

And the sick grief that you were far away
Grew pleasant thankfulness that you were near?
Who might have been, set on some outstep sphere,
Less than a Want to me, as day by day
I lived unware, uncaring all that lay
Locked in that Universe taciturn and drear.

1866.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hap: title and date title; l. 1

    'Hap' (from Old Norse happ, luck) gives 'happen', 'happy' and 'mishap'; the title names the poem's real subject, blind chance. Hardy dated it 1866, when he was a young architect in London who had lost his religious faith partly through reading Darwin's Origin of Species (1859) and the theological controversies of the 1860s. He kept such early poems unpublished for three decades, collecting them in his first volume of verse, Wessex Poems (1898), after giving up novel-writing. (In the archive's text this poem is followed by 'In Vision I Roamed', also dated 1866.)

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes; Oxford English Dictionary; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature

  2. the vengeful god ll. 1-4

    The octave is a hypothetical: 'If but' (if only) some god would announce from the sky that the speaker's sorrow is his 'ecstasy' and the speaker's 'love's loss' is the god's 'hate's profiting'. The god sounds like the mocking gods of Greek tragedy or the 'President of the Immortals' who has 'sport' with Tess at the end of Tess of the d'Urbervilles (1891), itself echoing Aeschylus. 'Thou suffering thing' reduces the speaker to an object of sport, recalling Gloucester's 'As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods' (King Lear 4.1.36).

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes; Shakespeare, King Lear 4.1.36

  3. 'bear, and clench myself, and die' ll. 5-6

    The verbs describe a stoic endurance: to 'bear' and to 'clench myself' (tense the body, grit the teeth). 'Ire unmerited' means anger not deserved. The idea is that injustice is easier to endure than meaninglessness, since it leaves the victim a moral position. The rhetorical shape (If ... Then ... But) gives the sonnet the form of a syllogism that collapses in the sestet.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature

  4. Powerfuller l. 7

    Hardy often formed irregular words (comparatives like 'Powerfuller', negatives like 'unblooms'), part of what critics call his deliberately awkward, 'unpoetic' style. Capitalised, 'a Powerfuller' becomes a quasi-name for a deity known only by its superior strength. 'Meted' (l. 8) means allotted, measured out (as in Matthew 7:2, 'with what measure ye mete'): a god would at least have measured the tears deliberately.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes; Bible (KJV), Matthew 7:2

  5. 'But not so': the volta l. 9

    The turn at the start of the sestet is abrupt and colloquial. Having imagined meaning in suffering, the speaker rejects the possibility, and asks two questions in the old poetic manner of inversion: 'How arrives it joy lies slain, / And why unblooms the best hope ever sown?' (how does it happen that joy is killed?). The answer is impersonal: chance and time, not a will.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes

  6. unblooms l. 10

    'Unbloom' is a characteristic Hardy negative formation; the word is very rare outside Hardy. The image is agricultural: hope 'sown' like seed should come up in flower, but instead it 'unblooms', a process of reversal. The farming imagery continues in 'sun and rain', the conditions of growth, which 'Crass Casualty' obstructs. The negative prefix turns a natural process backward, as if the universe were actively un-making what was planted.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes

  7. Crass Casualty l. 11

    'Casualty' derives from Latin casus, chance or fall, and before its modern sense of accident victim it meant chance or an accidental event. 'Crass' (Latin crassus, thick) suggests stupidity and insensibility rather than malice. The capitalised abstraction is a parody of an allegorical god. Hardy's philosophy, later developed in The Dynasts, of an unconscious 'Immanent Will' driving the universe, is in germ here.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes

  8. dicing Time l. 12

    'Casts' is the verb for throwing dice. The line is condensed: Time, rolling dice, throws grief instead of joy, by luck rather than intent. The image recalls the Roman soldiers who cast lots for Christ's garments (Matthew 27:35), and the long tradition of Fortune as a gambler. The ellipsis that follows ('moan . . .') lets the line trail off.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 27:35; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature

  9. purblind Doomsters l. 13

    'Doomster' (also 'dempster', 'deemster') was an old word for a judge, and in Scotland the officer who pronounced sentence; it survives in the Isle of Man's 'Deemsters'. 'Purblind' originally meant wholly blind but came to mean partly blind, obtuse. Chance and Time are judges who cannot see whom they sentence. They 'had as readily strown / Blisses about my pilgrimage as pain': they would just as easily have scattered blessings. That is the bitterness of the close.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, 5 vols (Clarendon Press, 1982-95), vol. 1, notes; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  10. 'my pilgrimage' l. 14

    The image of life as a pilgrimage runs from Hebrews 11:13 ('strangers and pilgrims on the earth') and Psalm 119:54 to Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress (1678). Hardy keeps the word but removes its destination and guiding providence: the path is strewn haphazardly with 'Blisses' or 'pain'. 'Strown' is an old past participle of 'strew'. The final word 'pain' answers 'slain' in line 9, closing the sestet's rhyme frame on suffering.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 11:13; Oxford English Dictionary

  11. Form: a hybrid sonnet ll. 1-14

    The first two quatrains rhyme abab cdcd as in a Shakespearean sonnet and are printed as separate stanzas; the closing six lines (slain, sown, rain, moan, strown, pain) rhyme efeffe, forming a sestet with an Italian-style volta at line 9. Hardy thus fuses the two traditions, using the octave/sestet division for the argument's reversal. The heavily stressed, monosyllabic lines and awkward inversions are typical of his early verse.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Hap"', Interesting Literature; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes
