Nature

During Wind and Rain

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses

Nature

THEY sing their dearest songs—
He, she, all of them—yea,
Treble and tenor and bass,
And one to play;
With the candles mooning each face . . .
Ah, no; the years O!
How the sick leaves reel down in throngs!

They clear the creeping moss—
Elders and juniors—aye,
Making the pathways neat
And the garden gay;
And they build a shady seat . . .
Ah, no; the years, the years;
See, the white storm-birds wing across!

They are blithely breakfasting all—
Men and maidens—yea,
Under the summer tree,
With a glimpse of the bay,
While pet fowl come to the knee . . .
Ah, no; the years O!
And the rotten rose is ript from the wall.

They change to a high new house,
He, she, all of them—aye,
Clocks and carpets and chairs
On the lawn all day,
And brightest things that are theirs . . .
Ah, no; the years, the years;
Down their carved names the rain-drop ploughs.

Study notes by the editors

  1. During Wind and Rain: occasion title; l. 1

    Emma Lavinia Gifford, whom Hardy met in Cornwall in 1870, died at Max Gate in November 1912 after years of estrangement. Among her papers Hardy found a manuscript memoir of her early life, later published as Some Recollections. The four scenes of this poem, a family singing, gardening, breakfasting out of doors and moving house, are often read as images of the Gifford household in Emma's girlhood in Plymouth, a world Hardy never saw and knew only through her words; the link to her recollections is an interpretation, not something Hardy states. Dennis Taylor's 1975 article is a detailed study of the poem and of how it fits Hardy's poetic development. The poem is therefore an elegy at two removes: for Emma, and for the vanished family life she remembered. It was collected in Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses (1917).

    Dennis Taylor, 'Hardy's Poetic Development: The Case of "During Wind and Rain"', Colby Library Quarterly 11.1 (March 1975); Emma Hardy, Some Recollections, ed. Evelyn Hardy and Robert Gittings (Oxford University Press, 1961); Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford University Press, 2004)

  2. Title: 'wind and rain' title; l. 27

    Feste's song at the end of Twelfth Night moves a single life through its stages, from 'a little tiny boy' to age, against a refrain of weather that never changes: 'For the rain it raineth every day'. Hardy's title sets each of his happy scenes against the same steady weather of time, and the rain returns in the poem's last line to wear away the carved names. Hardy is not known to have pointed to the echo himself, so it is best treated as a suggestion; but the shared pattern of a song, a refrain and the passing years makes it apt. The word 'During' also matters: the scenes do not come before the storm but are lived inside it.

    Shakespeare, Twelfth Night 5.1 (Feste's song), Folger Shakespeare text; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009)

  3. Treble, tenor and bass l. 3

    Treble, tenor and bass are the voice parts of part-song or hymn singing; with 'one to play' (l. 4), presumably at the piano, the scene is a Victorian family evening. Hardy's father and grandfather had been church musicians at Stinsford, and music recurs in his poems as the sign of shared, now lost, community. The list form ('He, she, all of them', 'Treble and tenor and bass') makes the household a harmonious whole, and the same device returns in each stanza ('Elders and juniors', 'Men and maidens', 'Clocks and carpets and chairs'), so that the catalogue of people becomes a catalogue of things by the end.

    Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford University Press, 2004); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  4. yea / aye ll. 2, 9, 16, 23

    'Yea' (yes, indeed) and 'aye' (yes) are old affirmative words; here they work less as meaning than as song-like intensifiers, completing the second line of each stanza in the manner of a ballad or carol. Their regular alternation (yea, aye, yea, aye) mirrors the alternation of the two refrains ('the years O!' and 'the years, the years'). 'Aye' can also be heard in its old sense of 'always', a quiet irony in a poem about how nothing lasts. The OED records both as emphatic affirmative words.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009)

  5. mooning l. 5

    The verb is characteristic of Hardy's habit of coining or reviving compact words. The candles 'moon' the singers' faces, lighting them so that each looks pale, round and luminous like a small moon. The image is tender, but moons wane and candles burn down; the light that makes the faces beautiful already measures time. The ellipsis that follows (' . . .') marks the point at which the remembered scene breaks off and the speaker's present intrudes. The verb is unusual in this transferred sense, giving the line a striking compression.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  6. Refrain: 'Ah, no; the years O!' ll. 6, 13, 20, 27

    Each stanza builds a scene in the present tense and then is cut off by an ellipsis and a sigh. The refrain alternates between 'the years O!' (stanzas 1 and 3) and 'the years, the years' (stanzas 2 and 4), one of the poem's many exact symmetries. The 'O' gives the line the ring of a folk-song refrain, as if the speaker could only lament rather than argue. 'Ah, no' is a self-correction: the speaker realises that the scene is past and cannot be held. The refrain is the hinge between two presents, the remembered present of the family and the actual present of decay. It is also the one line in each stanza that does not rhyme.

    Dennis Taylor, 'Hardy's Poetic Development: The Case of "During Wind and Rain"', Colby Library Quarterly 11.1 (March 1975); Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "During Wind and Rain"', Interesting Literature (2016)

  7. Stanza form st. 1-4

    Each stanza rhymes a b c b c, then the unrhymed refrain, then a final line rhyming with the first (songs/throngs, moss/across, all/wall, house/ploughs). Lines 1 to 5 are short and lilting, with the fourth line, like this one, the shortest; the final line is longer and is set further out to the left, so it reads as an outside voice of weather breaking into the song. The return of the first rhyme in the last line encloses the happy scene inside its own ruin. The strict repetition of the pattern is itself expressive: time comes round in each stanza, as the seasons do, and always ends the same way.

    Dennis Taylor, 'Hardy's Poetic Development: The Case of "During Wind and Rain"', Colby Library Quarterly 11.1 (March 1975); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  8. 'the sick leaves reel down in throngs' l. 7

    This is the first of four final lines of natural destruction: falling leaves, storm-birds, a torn-down rose, rain on gravestones. The sequence darkens by degrees, ending at the grave itself. The words are carefully human: leaves are 'sick', they 'reel' as if drunk or faint, and they come in 'throngs', a crowd that grimly mirrors the family gathered round the singers. The exclamation ('How the sick leaves reel') belongs to the speaker's own moment, set against the remembered scene.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "During Wind and Rain"', Interesting Literature (2016)

  9. creeping moss l. 8

    In the scene the household tidies the garden, scraping off moss to make 'the pathways neat'. 'Creeping' gives the moss the slow persistence of time, which the family can clear only for a season. Moss is a traditional sign of age and of untended graves, and the stanza's building and neatening will be undone by the end of the poem, when the carved names are left to the rain. 'Gay' (l. 11) has its older sense of bright and cheerful.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009)

  10. shady seat l. 12

    The family builds a shady seat for future summers, an act of confidence in time to come. The phrase looks forward to the 'summer tree' of the next stanza and to a future the speaker knows did not last. Like the other domestic details, it gives the reader the sense of an ordinary, secure Victorian middle-class home, which makes the interruption of the refrain all the more abrupt.

    Emma Hardy, Some Recollections, ed. Evelyn Hardy and Robert Gittings (Oxford University Press, 1961); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  11. storm-birds l. 14

    A 'storm-bird' is a bird whose appearance is thought to forecast storm; the OED applies the term especially to the petrel. The sea-bird image sits easily with the coastal setting suggested by 'the bay' in the next stanza. In the poem's sequence of final lines they are omens, the weather of time arriving over the garden the family has just made neat. The command 'See' pulls the reader into the speaker's present moment.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009)

  12. blithely breakfasting l. 15

    'Blithe' is an old word for joyful and light-hearted, now mainly literary. The scene of breakfast under a tree, with 'pet fowl' coming up to the knee, is the most relaxed of the four, the high summer of the household's happiness. The alliteration (blithely, breakfasting) and the pairing 'Men and maidens' keep the song-like lilt of the earlier stanzas, so that this is the scene the refrain interrupts most cruelly.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  13. the bay l. 18

    Emma Gifford was born in Plymouth in 1840 and grew up there; around 1860, when the family's finances declined, they moved to a cheaper house near Bodmin in Cornwall. The 'glimpse of the bay' places the scene by the sea, which ties it to Plymouth and to the sea-birds of the previous stanza. The detail is small but exact, and may well be a remembered particular from Emma's account of her childhood, though the poem does not name the place.

    'Emma Gifford', Wikipedia; Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford University Press, 2004); Emma Hardy, Some Recollections, ed. Evelyn Hardy and Robert Gittings (Oxford University Press, 1961)

  14. pet fowl l. 19

    The tame birds coming to the diners' knees contrast with the wild 'white storm-birds' of the previous stanza: one is a sign of a safe, enclosed home, the other of weather and the open sea. The phrase shows how precisely Hardy fills each scene with particular, almost photographic detail, as though he were looking at an old family picture, and the fowl, like the family, belong to a vanished moment.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  15. 'the rotten rose is ript from the wall' l. 21

    The rose has long stood for brief beauty in English lyric (as in Herrick's 'Gather ye rose-buds while ye may'). Here it is already 'rotten' before the wind tears it down. The harsh alliteration of r-sounds (rotten, rose, ript) makes this the most violent of the poem's final lines, and 'ript', an older form of the past participle, gives it a hard, abrupt sound. The passive voice leaves the agent unnamed: it is the wind, or time itself.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "During Wind and Rain"', Interesting Literature (2016)

  16. 'a high new house' l. 22

    On the literal level the family moves house and sets out its furniture on the lawn, a scene that readers sometimes connect with the Giffords' changes of home. Some readers take 'high new house' as an image of death, either a heavenly mansion (compare John 14:2, 'In my Father's house are many mansions') or the narrow 'house' of the grave. Others object that Hardy, who did not hold an orthodox faith, would hardly offer a consoling image of heaven, and that the irony lies rather in the gap between the family's pride in its new house and the gravestones of the last line. The poem keeps both senses open.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "During Wind and Rain"', Interesting Literature (2016); John 14:2 (KJV); Dennis Taylor, 'Hardy's Poetic Development: The Case of "During Wind and Rain"', Colby Library Quarterly 11.1 (March 1975)

  17. Clocks and carpets and chairs l. 24

    The furniture is carried out 'On the lawn all day', the odd, exposed moment of a house removal. The catalogue repeats the pattern of the earlier lists of people (He, she; Elders and juniors; Men and maidens), so that persons are now replaced by possessions, and 'brightest things that are theirs' are what remain. The first item is 'Clocks': the instruments that measure the years lead the procession.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems, Longman Annotated Texts (1993; 2nd edn 2009); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision

  18. 'Down their carved names the rain-drop ploughs' l. 28

    The poem is usually read as ending where all its scenes were heading, at the graves, since the 'carved names' suggest gravestones. The single rain-drop 'ploughs' down the carved letters: a tiny force that, repeated over years, erodes stone, as the refrain's 'years' erode the family. 'Ploughs' turns the gravestone into a field furrowed by time, and the line rhymes back to 'house' in the stanza's first line, binding the new house to the grave. The present tense places the speaker, and the reader, in the rain now, after all the people are dead.

    Dennis Taylor, 'Hardy's Poetic Development: The Case of "During Wind and Rain"', Colby Library Quarterly 11.1 (March 1975); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "During Wind and Rain"', Interesting Literature (2016); Thomas Hardy, The Complete Poetical Works, ed. Samuel Hynes, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), Moments of Vision
