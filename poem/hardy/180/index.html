Nature

Neutral Tones

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Wessex Poems and Other Verses

Nature

WE stood by a pond that winter day,
And the sun was white, as though chidden of God,
And a few leaves lay on the starving sod,
—They had fallen from an ash, and were gray.

Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove
Over tedious riddles solved years ago;
And some words played between us to and fro—
On which lost the more by our love.

The smile on your mouth was the deadest thing
Alive enough to have strength to die;
And a grin of bitterness swept thereby
Like an ominous bird a-wing . . .

Since then, keen lessons that love deceives,
And wrings with wrong, have shaped to me
Your face, and the God-curst sun, and a tree,
And a pond edged with grayish leaves.

1867.

[Picture: Sketch of church with person outside wall]

SHE
AT HIS FUNERAL

THEY bear him to his resting-place—
In slow procession sweeping by;
I follow at a stranger’s space;
His kindred they, his sweetheart I.
Unchanged my gown of garish dye,
Though sable-sad is their attire;
But they stand round with griefless eye,
Whilst my regret consumes like fire!

187–.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Neutral Tones: date and publication l. 1

    Hardy dated the poem 1867, when he was a young architect's assistant writing poetry that magazines rejected. It remained unpublished until his first collection, Wessex Poems and Other Verses (1898), which he illustrated with his own drawings (hence the bracketed note of a sketch in this text). It is widely considered the finest of his early poems. The woman addressed is not identified, and attempts to link the poem to a particular early love remain speculation. Its date makes it a remarkable anticipation of the bleak, plain style of Hardy's later verse.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); Thomas Hardy, Wessex Poems and Other Verses (1898), Project Gutenberg text; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  2. Title and colour: 'Neutral Tones' l. 4

    'Neutral tint' and 'neutral tones' were watercolourists' terms for greys and dull mixtures without strong hue, and Hardy, trained as an architect and a capable draughtsman, knew this vocabulary. The poem's palette is accordingly white (the sun), gray (the leaves), and the implied browns of the 'starving sod'. 'Neutral' also suggests emotional deadness: neither love nor open hatred, but exhausted indifference. The title therefore announces both the scene's visual monotony and the emotional state of the couple.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  3. the sun was white l. 2

    In winter the sun can appear as a pale white disc, giving light without warmth. Hardy's choice of 'white' rather than gold or red removes the sun's usual associations with life and joy. The line also inverts the conventional pathetic fallacy: rather than nature sympathising with lovers, it seems to share in the punishment of the scene. The flat, mostly monosyllabic diction of the opening line sets the plain, unemphatic tone of the whole poem.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  4. as though chidden of God l. 2

    'Chide' means to scold or reprove, and 'chidden' is its archaic past participle; 'of' in the sense 'by' is also old-fashioned. The sun has been rebuked by God, a simile that hints at a universe under divine disfavour rather than benevolence. It is the first of two references to God in the poem: by the last stanza the sun has become 'God-curst', an escalation from reprimand to curse that measures how the speaker's bitterness has hardened over time.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. chide, v.; 'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior)

  5. the starving sod l. 3

    'Sod' is the surface of the ground with grass growing on it. 'Starving' personifies the earth as underfed and suffering, matching the deprivation of the relationship. The alliteration of s across 'sun', 'starving' and 'sod' links sky and ground in a single bleak picture. The few leaves lying on it suggest that the year, like the love, has spent itself.

    James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants; 'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior)

  6. They had fallen from an ash l. 4

    The ash is a common English tree, among the last to come into leaf and the first to drop its leaves. Hardy's specificity is typical of his observant naturalism. Some readers hear a secondary suggestion in the word: ashes are what remains after a fire, and the love here has burnt itself out. Whether intended or not, the grey colour of the leaves supports the association, and the tree returns in the final stanza as part of the remembered scene.

    James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants; 'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior)

  7. Over tedious riddles solved years ago l. 6

    The simile says that she looks at him without interest, as one glances over old puzzles whose answers are already known: he is no longer a mystery to her. 'Tedious' sharpens the insult. The line exists in two versions: this text, following the 1898 Wessex Poems, reads 'riddles solved years ago', while many modern printings give 'riddles of years ago'; check a critical edition for which reading is Hardy's later one. The variant changes the sense slightly, from riddles already solved (nothing left to discover) to riddles from long ago (stale). Editors such as Gibson record Hardy's revisions in the textual apparatus.

    Thomas Hardy, Wessex Poems and Other Verses (1898), Project Gutenberg text; 'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  8. On which lost the more by our love l. 8

    The compressed syntax means: some words passed between us about which of us had lost more because of our love. The love is spoken of as a loss rather than a gain, and the conversation is a weary contest of grievances, 'played' back and forth like a game. Some readers take 'played' to suggest triviality or even a cruel game. The line ends the second stanza on an irregular, stumbling rhythm.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  9. The smile on your mouth was the deadest thing / Alive enough l. 9

    The oxymoron is the poem's most memorable conceit. The smile is dead yet 'Alive enough to have strength to die': it is so near death that its only remaining vitality is the power to expire. The effect is of a gesture of affection emptied of meaning, persisting only as a mechanical movement. Commentators have often singled out this line for the way it compresses the poem's theme, the death of love while the lovers still live, into a single image.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  10. Like an ominous bird a-wing l. 12

    Birds were traditionally read as omens (as in Roman augury), and a bird of ill omen, such as a raven or crow, foretells misfortune. The grin of bitterness flits across the smile as such a bird crosses the sky, an ominous sign of what is to come. 'A-wing' uses the archaic prefix a- (from Old English on) as in 'afoot' or 'asleep'. The stanza ends with an ellipsis in the original, as if the omen remains unspoken.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. a-, prefix

  11. Since then, keen lessons that love deceives l. 13

    The first three stanzas describe a single winter day; the fourth reflects from later years ('Since then'). Subsequent 'keen' (sharp, painful) lessons that love deceives and 'wrings with wrong' have caused the remembered scene to come back to him whenever love fails. The syntax is that these lessons 'have shaped to me' her face, the sun, the tree and the pond: the image has become a permanent emblem of disillusion. The poem is circular, returning to the pond and leaves of the opening. The alliteration of 'wrings with wrong' mimics a twisting pain.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  12. the God-curst sun l. 15

    Hardy repeats the elements of the first stanza in a list (face, sun, tree, pond), but the sun now carries a compound epithet that intensifies the earlier simile. Being 'curst' by God is far stronger than being 'chidden'. The change suggests how bitterness has grown over the years, and the hyphenated compound is a characteristic feature of Hardy's diction. The phrase also points toward Hardy's lifelong scepticism about divine benevolence, though here it remains part of a personal, not a philosophical, statement.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  13. And a pond edged with grayish leaves l. 16

    Each stanza has four lines rhymed abba, with a mainly four-beat line that mixes iambs and anapaests, and an indented fourth line. The envelope rhyme encloses each stanza, and the poem as a whole is enclosed by the return to the pond and leaves. The slight change from 'gray' to 'grayish' adds vagueness, as of a memory blurred by time. Critics often note the poem's circular structure as an image of the lover's inability to escape the moment of disillusion.

    'Neutral Tones', Wikipedia (citing J. Persoon and C. Senior); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants
