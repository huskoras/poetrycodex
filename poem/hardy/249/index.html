The Ruined Maid

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Poems of the Past and the Present

History & Politics

“O ’Melia, my dear, this does everything crown!
Who could have supposed I should meet you in Town?
And whence such fair garments, such prosperi-ty?”—
“O didn’t you know I’d been ruined?” said she.

—“You left us in tatters, without shoes or socks,
Tired of digging potatoes, and spudding up docks;
And now you’ve gay bracelets and bright feathers three!”—
“Yes: that’s how we dress when we’re ruined,” said she.

—“At home in the barton you said ‘thee’ and ‘thou,’
And ‘thik oon,’ and ‘theäs oon,’ and ‘t’other’; but now
Your talking quite fits ’ee for high compa-ny!”—
“Some polish is gained with one’s ruin,” said she.

—“Your hands were like paws then, your face blue and bleak,
But now I’m bewitched by your delicate cheek,
And your little gloves fit as on any la-dy!”—
“We never do work when we’re ruined,” said she.

—“You used to call home-life a hag-ridden dream,
And you’d sigh, and you’d sock; but at present you seem
To know not of megrims or melancho-ly!”—
“True. There’s an advantage in ruin,” said she.

—“I wish I had feathers, a fine sweeping gown,
And a delicate face, and could strut about Town!”—
“My dear—a raw country girl, such as you be,
Isn’t equal to that. You ain’t ruined,” said she.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date st. 1; dateline

    Hardy wrote the poem in 1866 while he was working in London as an assistant to the architect Arthur Blomfield and lodging at Westbourne Park Villas, Paddington; it was not published until Poems of the Past and the Present (1901). 'Melia is short for Amelia. A girl just up from the country meets her old companion in 'Town' (London) and marvels at her fine clothes; 'Melia explains each change by her 'ruin', the Victorian word for a woman's loss of chastity outside marriage. The poem turns the solemn literature of the 'fallen woman' (from Victorian sermons to Rossetti's 'Jenny') into satire: ruin has brought comfort, idleness and polish.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'; The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text

  2. ruined st. 1, l. 4

    In Victorian usage a 'ruined' woman was one seduced or living as a mistress, who had thereby, in respectable opinion, lost her prospects of marriage and her place in society; the OED records this sense of ruin from the eighteenth century. Hardy exploits the gap between the moral word and the material facts: the 'ruined' maid is the one whose life has visibly improved, while the virtuous girl is still in rags. The irony anticipates Hardy's later quarrel with conventional sexual morality in Tess of the d'Urbervilles (1891), subtitled 'A Pure Woman'. 'Melia's replies keep returning to the word, as 'ruined' or 'ruin', in all six stanzas.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'

  3. Wrenched rhymes: 'prosperi-ty', 'compa-ny' sts. 1, 3, 4, 5

    The poem is in rhymed couplets of anapestic tetrameter (four strong beats, mostly with two light syllables between them: 'Who could HAVE sup-POSED I should MEET you in TOWN?'). The third line of each stanza ends on a polysyllable split by a hyphen, 'prosperi-ty', 'compa-ny', 'la-dy', 'melancho-ly', so that its final syllable takes a stress and rhymes with the refrain's 'she'. The wrenched stress is a ballad and music-hall effect, and it mimics the country speaker's awkward, over-careful pronunciation of the grand words she uses for her friend's new life. The jaunty metre itself undercuts any tragic reading of ruin.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'; The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text

  4. spudding up docks st. 2, l. 6

    A spud is a small, narrow-bladed spade or weeding chisel, and to 'spud' is to dig out weeds with it; docks are the broad-leaved weeds (Rumex) that infest pasture. The Victorian Web glosses the phrase as digging up weeds with a chisel-headed spade. Together with 'digging potatoes' and going 'without shoes or socks', the phrase sums up the life of a female field labourer in Dorset, the kind of work Tess does at Flintcomb-Ash in Hardy's novel. The contrast with 'gay bracelets and bright feathers three' is the poem's whole argument in miniature.

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'

  5. bright feathers three st. 2, l. 7

    Large ostrich feathers on a hat or bonnet were high fashion for women in the 1860s; three plumes was a lavish display. The detail, like the bracelets, the 'fair garments' and the 'little gloves', belongs to the dress of a lady, but on a former farm girl it would be read by any worldly observer as the sign of a mistress maintained by a wealthy man. The innocent speaker reads the signs literally and envies them, and in the last stanza she wishes for 'feathers, a fine sweeping gown'. 'Melia's reply, 'that's how we dress when we're ruined', is a knowing joke at the expense of moral language.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'

  6. barton st. 3, l. 9

    From Old English beretun ('barley enclosure', i.e. threshing-floor or grange), a barton in south-western dialect is a farmyard or the home farm of an estate. Barnes's Dorset glossary and the Victorian Web both gloss it as farmyard or barn. Hardy uses the word throughout the Wessex novels (for instance in Far from the Madding Crowd). It fixes 'Melia's origins in the working farm.

    Oxford English Dictionary; William Barnes, A Glossary of the Dorset Dialect (Dorchester, 1863; enlarged 1886); The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text

  7. 'thee' and 'thou', 'thik oon', 'the's oon', 't'other' st. 3, ll. 9-10

    In the Dorset dialect recorded by Hardy's friend William Barnes, 'thik' means 'that' (so 'thik oon' is 'that one'), the form printed here as 'the’s oon' (Hardy's 'theäs oon', garbled in the archive text) represents 'this one', and 't'other' is 'the other'. Rural speakers also kept the old familiar pronouns 'thee' and 'thou', long gone from polite speech. The country girl notes that 'Melia now talks in a way that fits 'ee (you) for high company; the joke is that her own phrasing ('fits 'ee') is itself dialect. Speech marks class, and ruin has given 'Melia the accent of a lady.

    William Barnes, A Glossary of the Dorset Dialect (Dorchester, 1863; enlarged 1886); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'

  8. hands like paws, face blue and bleak st. 4, l. 13

    The country girl's memories are blunt and physical. Field work in the cold left hands red, chapped and clumsy, 'like paws', and faces 'blue and bleak' (pinched with cold). Against this she sets 'Melia's 'delicate cheek' and gloves that fit as on any lady. The point, made with comic exaggeration, is economic: the respectable life available to a poor rural woman was hard manual labour, and the 'fallen' life offered comfort. 'Melia's reply, 'We never do work when we're ruined', states the bargain plainly.

    J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'

  9. hag-ridden dream st. 5, l. 17

    To be 'hag-ridden' was originally to be ridden by a hag or witch in one's sleep, the folk explanation of nightmare (the 'mare' of nightmare is a goblin that sits on the sleeper). By extension the word means oppressed or harassed. 'Melia once called home life a 'hag-ridden dream', a waking nightmare of drudgery. Hardy, a collector of Dorset superstitions, uses such beliefs throughout his fiction.

    Oxford English Dictionary; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'

  10. sock; megrims st. 5, ll. 18-19

    Barnes's Dorset glossary defines 'sock' as to sigh with a loudish sound. 'Megrims', from French migraine and ultimately Greek hemikrania ('half the head'), meant a one-sided headache and then, figuratively, low spirits or whims, and Victorian Web notes gloss it along these lines. The country girl recalls 'Melia sighing and moping at home, and now sees in her no trace of 'melancho-ly'. 'Melia agrees: 'There's an advantage in ruin.'

    William Barnes, A Glossary of the Dorset Dialect (Dorchester, 1863; enlarged 1886); Oxford English Dictionary; The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text

  11. Refrain: '... said she' last line of each stanza

    The poem is built as a dialogue in which the country girl speaks three lines and 'Melia answers in the fourth, always closing with 'said she'. The refrain gives the poem the shape of a folk ballad or music-hall song, and the variation within it ('ruined', 'ruin', 'ruin', 'ruined') creates the comedy of repetition. 'Melia's answers grow more knowing as the poem goes on, from surprise ('O didn't you know') to a frank statement of advantage.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'; J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'; The Victorian Web, 'The Ruined Maid', annotated text

  12. 'You ain't ruined' st. 6, ll. 23-24

    In the last stanza the country girl wishes for 'Melia's finery and the freedom to 'strut about Town'. 'Melia's answer is condescending, a raw country girl 'isn't equal to that', but its grammar betrays her: 'such as you be' and 'You ain't ruined' are rustic, not ladylike. Critics read the slip in different ways: as a final comic deflation of 'Melia's pretensions; as a hint that the 'polish' gained with ruin is thin and her position precarious; or as a sign that the two women remain sisters under the skin. The poem refuses to moralise either way, and Hardy leaves the reader to weigh the costs of 'ruin' that 'Melia never mentions.

    J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1970), 'The Ruined Maid'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), notes to 'The Ruined Maid'
