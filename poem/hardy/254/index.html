The Self-unseeing

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Poems of the Past and the Present

Love

HERE is the ancient floor,
Footworn and hollowed and thin,
Here was the former door
Where the dead feet walked in.

She sat here in her chair,
Smiling into the fire;
He who played stood there,
Bowing it higher and higher.

Childlike, I danced in a dream;
Blessings emblazoned that day
Everything glowed with a gleam;
Yet we were looking away!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Self-Unseeing: title and setting title; l. 1

    The poem first appeared in Poems of the Past and the Present (1901). Its setting is widely taken to be the parlour of the cottage at Higher Bockhampton where Hardy was born and grew up; the house survives, and visitors are still shown the room where the boy danced. The title is a characteristic Hardy compound: the 'self-unseeing' are people who cannot see themselves, and in particular cannot see their own happiness while it is happening. The poem thus moves from a present visit (the stressed 'HERE', repeated as 'Here' and 'here') to a remembered scene and finally to a judgment on that scene. Its theme, the failure to recognise joy until it is past, recurs across Hardy's poems of memory, most fully in the 1912-13 elegies for his first wife.

    Thomas Hardy, Poems of the Past and the Present (London: Macmillan, 1901; Project Gutenberg text); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature; David Sutton, 'Week 621: The Self-Unseeing, by Thomas Hardy' (2024)

  2. Footworn and hollowed and thin l. 2

    The floor is the first witness. 'Footworn' is a plain compound in Hardy's manner, and the run of 'and ... and' (polysyndeton) slows the line as if the eye were moving across the surface. A floor 'hollowed and thin' records the weight of years; it also anticipates the 'dead feet' of l. 4, so that the wearing of the stone and the passing of the people are the same process seen twice. Hardy, a trained architect who restored churches, was alert to how buildings keep traces of use, and the poem treats the room almost as an archaeological site. The effect is quiet: the material survives while the people who wore it do not.

    David Sutton, 'Week 621: The Self-Unseeing, by Thomas Hardy' (2024); Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004)

  3. the dead feet walked in ll. 3-4

    'Here was the former door' implies that the doorway the speaker remembers is no longer the entrance, so that even the fabric of the room has altered. The phrase 'the dead feet' is a bold transferred epithet: the feet were living then, but the speaker can only name them by what their owners have since become. By the time of publication Hardy's father, the fiddler of the second stanza, had died (1892); his mother Jemima lived until 1904, so the line is best read as the speaker's general sense of a vanished household rather than a strict roll-call. The image prepares the move from present to past in stanza 2, where the dead are suddenly restored to life.

    Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature

  4. She ... He who played ll. 5-7

    The second stanza restores the remembered figures, but only as pronouns, 'She' and 'He'. Most readers take them as the poet's parents: Jemima Hardy by the fire and Thomas Hardy senior, a builder who was also a keen and capable country musician, playing for the household. The Hardy Society's life notes the father's passion for music and the son's early fiddling at local dances. Leaving them unnamed keeps the scene both intimate and typical, a family evening anyone might have known. The present tense of the first stanza ('Here is') has now given way to the past ('sat', 'stood'), and the shift is part of the poem's argument about where happiness can be seen from.

    The Thomas Hardy Society, 'Life of Thomas Hardy'; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature; David Sutton, 'Week 621: The Self-Unseeing, by Thomas Hardy' (2024)

  5. Bowing it higher and higher l. 8

    To 'bow' here is to draw the bow across the strings. The pronoun 'it' has no clear antecedent: it must mean the tune or the music, which the player drives 'higher and higher', in pitch, in tempo, or in the excitement of a country dance as it accelerates. The repetition imitates the climbing phrase. Hardy's father played the jigs, reels and hornpipes of Dorset dance tradition, and Hardy himself fiddled at local gatherings as a boy. The line is the most animated in the poem, and its energy is what the last line will declare went unnoticed.

    The Thomas Hardy Society, 'Life of Thomas Hardy'; Florence Emily Hardy [largely Hardy himself], The Early Life of Thomas Hardy, 1840-1891 (London: Macmillan, 1928), on his childhood response to his father's dance tunes; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  6. Childlike, I danced in a dream l. 9

    The Early Life (1928), issued under Florence Hardy's name but largely written by Hardy himself, describes the child as intensely sensitive to music: among his father's many dance tunes a few always moved him to tears, which he tried to hide. The poem's 'danced in a dream' catches that absorbed, half-conscious state. 'Childlike' is subtly double: it describes the child as he was, but also the speaker's present judgment that such unreflecting joy belongs to childhood. The dream is the condition of happiness and also the reason it was not seen; the dancer cannot watch himself dance.

    Florence Emily Hardy [largely Hardy himself], The Early Life of Thomas Hardy, 1840-1891 (London: Macmillan, 1928), on his childhood response to his father's dance tunes; The Thomas Hardy Society, 'Life of Thomas Hardy'; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature

  7. Blessings emblazoned that day l. 10

    To 'emblazon' (from 'blazon', the heraldic description or painting of a coat of arms) is to adorn with bright devices, and by extension to set forth splendidly. The day was, as it were, painted over with blessings. The line has no finite verb: it may be read as 'Blessings [were] emblazoned [on] that day', or as an absolute phrase hanging between the dancing and the glow. The archive text, like many printings, gives no comma after 'day', which leaves the syntax open and lets the line run into the next. Tearle notes the stanza's heavy alliteration ('Blessings ... day', 'glowed ... gleam'), which makes the last line, without it, fall flat by contrast.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1976), notes to the poem

  8. Yet we were looking away! l. 12

    The final line explains the title. 'Everything glowed', but the family was 'looking away': at the fire, at the dance, at the future, anywhere but at its own present good fortune. The pronoun widens from 'I' to 'we', so the failure is shared. The exclamation mark gives a rare note of anguish to an otherwise restrained poem. Readers have long compared D. H. Lawrence's 'Piano', another memory of childhood music in a parlour. Hardy's point is not simply nostalgia but the idea that happiness and self-consciousness exclude each other: the moment could be lived or seen, not both. The present visit of stanza 1 is the 'seeing' that comes too late.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature; David Sutton, 'Week 621: The Self-Unseeing, by Thomas Hardy' (2024); Thomas Hardy, Poems of the Past and the Present (London: Macmillan, 1901; Project Gutenberg text)

  9. Metre and structure st. 1-3

    Each stanza has four lines of three stresses, with a mixture of iambic and anapaestic feet that gives a light, dance-like movement suited to the subject. The rhyme is alternate (floor/door, thin/in). The structure is a three-step argument: stanza 1 is the present ('HERE is ... Here was'), stanza 2 the remembered scene, stanza 3 the child's experience and then the verdict. The pointing words 'Here ... here' anchor the poem in a place the speaker can touch, which makes the absence of the people more acute. The compression is typical of Hardy's best short lyrics: twelve lines carry a whole family history.

    James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1976), notes to the poem; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Self-Unseeing"', Interesting Literature
