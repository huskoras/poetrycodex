Nature

Afterwards

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses

Nature

WHEN the Present has latched its postern behind my tremulous stay,
And the May month flaps its glad green leaves like wings,
Delicate-filmed as new-spun silk, will the neighbours say,
“He was a man who used to notice such things”?

If it be in the dusk when, like an eyelid’s soundless blink,
The dewfall-hawk comes crossing the shades to alight
Upon the wind-warped upland thorn, a gazer may think,
“To him this must have been a familiar sight.”

If I pass during some nocturnal blackness, mothy and warm,
When the hedgehog travels furtively over the lawn,
One may say, “He strove that such innocent creatures should come to no
harm,
But he could do little for them; and now he is gone”?

If, when hearing that I have been stilled at last, they stand at the
door,
Watching the full-starred heavens that winter sees,
Will this thought rise on those who will meet my face no more,
“He was one who had an eye for such mysteries”?

And will any say when my bell of quittance is heard in the gloom,
And a crossing breeze cuts a pause in its outrollings,
Till they rise again, as they were a new bell’s boom,
“He hears it not now, but used to notice such things”?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and placing st. 1

    Hardy placed 'Afterwards' at the end of Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses (1917), so it reads as a farewell to that book and, by anticipation, to his life (he lived until 1928). Each stanza imagines the moment of his death in a different season or setting, and asks what the neighbours will say. The answer he hopes for is modest: not that he was great, but that he 'used to notice such things'. The poem is both an elegy for himself and a statement of what he valued as a poet, close observation of the natural world.

    Thomas Hardy, Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses (Macmillan, 1917), Project Gutenberg text; James Gibson (ed.), The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976), 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'

  2. Postern l. 1

    A postern is a small rear or private gate, especially in a castle or town wall. Death is not a dramatic departure through a main gate. The Present 'latches' its back gate quietly behind him, as one closes a garden gate. The personified 'Present' implies that the dead pass out of time. 'My tremulous stay' describes his life as a brief, unsteady sojourn, and 'tremulous' may also suggest the trembling of age.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'postern, n.'; The Victorian Web, 'Afterwards' with notes; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'

  3. Form: long lines and questions st. 1–5

    The stanzas alternate long and slightly shorter lines, with a loose, mostly triple rhythm that sounds like speech. Each stanza follows the same pattern: a situation ('When', 'If'), a scene from nature, and an imagined comment in quotation marks, often in question form. Hardy's habit of compounding words is visible throughout (Delicate-filmed, new-spun, dewfall-hawk, wind-warped, full-starred). The form's patience and repetition match the poem's subject, a lifetime's habit of attention.

    Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'

  4. May's leaves like wings ll. 2–3

    The month of May is personified as a bird, flapping 'its glad green leaves like wings'. The leaves are 'Delicate-filmed as new-spun silk', as thin and translucent as fresh silk. The image is closely observed: young beech or lime leaves do look filmy and crumpled as they open. The contrast between spring renewal and the speaker's death is gentle. Life continues regardless, and the poet hopes only to be remembered as one who saw it.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; The Victorian Web, 'Afterwards' with notes

  5. 'He used to notice such things' l. 4

    The poem's most famous line sets out Hardy's modest ideal of the poet as an observer. Its key words return in the final stanza ('used to notice such things'), framing the poem. In between come variations: 'a familiar sight', 'He strove that such innocent creatures should come to no harm', 'an eye for such mysteries'. The phrasing is plain and conversational, as neighbours would talk. The hesitant question marks suggest Hardy's uncertainty whether anyone will remember him at all.

    James Gibson (ed.), The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976), 'Afterwards'; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'

  6. Eyelid's soundless blink l. 5

    'Like an eyelid's soundless blink' describes how suddenly and silently the dusk passes. The simile is one of Hardy's most delicate. It also connects evening with eyes, observation and death. The poet who 'had an eye' for things will soon close his for good. The swoop of the dewfall-hawk in the same sentence is as silent as the blink. Hardy's verse often ties seeing to survival in this way.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; The Victorian Web, 'Afterwards' with notes

  7. Dewfall-hawk l. 6

    The 'dewfall-hawk' is not glossed in the standard online texts, and Hardy does not name a species. It is usually taken to be the nightjar (Caprimulgus europaeus), a crepuscular bird of heaths and commons such as the Dorset heathland of Hardy's Egdon, which flies at dusk and catches moths on the wing. 'Nighthawk' was an old local English name for the nightjar, and the silent, hawk-like glide fits the poem's picture. Its moment of crossing 'the shades to alight / Upon the wind-warped upland thorn' is exactly the sort of passing natural event the poem wants remembered. The identification is an inference, not Hardy's own statement.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dew-fall, n.'

  8. Wind-warped thorn l. 7

    Hawthorns on exposed downland grow leaning away from the wind, shaped by years of weather. Hardy's compound 'wind-warped' compresses that history. The image recurs in his work as a figure of endurance under hardship. For the gazer who sees the hawk alight on it, 'To him this must have been a familiar sight', and so the dead man's knowledge becomes part of the landscape.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; James Gibson (ed.), The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976), 'Afterwards'

  9. Hedgehog l. 10

    On a warm, 'mothy' night the hedgehog 'travels furtively over the lawn', a small, defenceless creature. The neighbours' imagined comment, 'He strove that such innocent creatures should come to no harm, / But he could do little for them', reflects Hardy's lifelong concern for animal welfare, visible in poems such as 'The Blinded Bird' and in his fiction. 'Could do little' is characteristically modest and sad. The long line spills over into a short 'harm', as the archive's layout shows.

    James Gibson (ed.), The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976), 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'

  10. Starry winter heavens l. 14

    In the fourth stanza news of his death reaches people who stand at the door, 'Watching the full-starred heavens that winter sees'. Hardy was fascinated by astronomy, most fully in Two on a Tower (1882), and many of his poems set human lives against vast indifferent space. 'An eye for such mysteries' extends his observation from the hedgerow to the universe, while keeping the same plain, wondering tone.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'

  11. Bell of quittance l. 17

    'Quittance' is a release or acquittal, especially discharge from a debt or obligation. The 'bell of quittance' is therefore the church bell tolled for a death, imagined as announcing that his account with life is closed. Hardy grew up with village church bells, and the knell rung for a parishioner is part of the rural world his poems record. The phrase is archaic and dignified, but the scene remains local and humble: 'heard in the gloom' by neighbours.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quittance, n.'; The Victorian Web, 'Afterwards' with notes; Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'

  12. Breeze and bell ll. 18–19

    The detail is acoustic: a crosswind blows the sound away so the bell seems to pause, and when it returns it sounds like a different bell. It is exactly the kind of small phenomenon the poet 'used to notice'. In the poem's last irony he cannot notice this one, because it marks his own death. 'Outrollings' is a Hardyan compound for the bell's waves of sound.

    Tim Armstrong (ed.), Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes on 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'

  13. The last line l. 20

    The last line joins the poem's two themes, death ('He hears it not now') and the habit of attention ('but used to notice such things'). It is phrased as a question about whether 'any' will say this, which keeps the poem's tone of hopeful uncertainty. Critics have admired the stanza for its restraint. Hardy imagines being remembered not through his novels or fame but in the small sensory world of the parish, and makes this poem his own 'bell of quittance'.

    The Victorian Web, 'Afterwards' with notes; James Gibson (ed.), The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976), 'Afterwards'; Samuel Hynes (ed.), The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), notes on 'Afterwards'
