Channel Firing

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries, with Miscellaneous Pieces

History & Politics

THAT night your great guns, unawares,
Shook all our coffins as we lay,
And broke the chancel window-squares,
We thought it was the Judgment-day

And sat upright. While drearisome
Arose the howl of wakened hounds:
The mouse let fall the altar-crumb,
The worms drew back into the mounds,

The glebe cow drooled. Till God called, “No;
It’s gunnery practice out at sea
Just as before you went below;
The world is as it used to be:

“All nations striving strong to make
Red war yet redder. Mad as hatters
They do no more for Christés sake
Than you who are helpless in such matters.

“That this is not the judgment-hour
For some of them’s a blessed thing,
For if it were they’d have to scour
Hell’s floor for so much threatening . . .

“Ha, ha. It will be warmer when
I blow the trumpet (if indeed
I ever do; for you are men,
And rest eternal sorely need).”

So down we lay again. “I wonder,
Will the world ever saner be,”
Said one, “than when He sent us under
In our indifferent century!”

And many a skeleton shook his head.
“Instead of preaching forty year,”
My neighbour Parson Thirdly said,
“I wish I had stuck to pipes and beer.”

Again the guns disturbed the hour,
Roaring their readiness to avenge,
As far inland as Stourton Tower,
And Camelot, and starlit Stonehenge.

April 1914.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion title, l. 1

    The title names firing practice by warships in the English Channel, a regular sound on the Dorset coast near the naval base at Portland. Hardy dated the poem April 1914, at the height of Anglo-German naval rivalry; it was published in May 1914 and collected in Satires of Circumstance (1914), and after August it read as uncannily prophetic. 'Your great guns' addresses the living (and the nation) directly from the grave: the speaker is one of the dead. The poem's mix of ghost-story, comic dialogue and apocalyptic satire is characteristic of Hardy's churchyard poems.

    'Channel Firing', annotated text, ibiblio (note on date and Anglo-German naval rivalry); Thomas Hardy, Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries (London: Macmillan, 1914), Project Gutenberg text; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  2. Metre and stanza st. 1–9

    The poem uses cross-rhymed tetrameter quatrains, a measure of hymns and ballads, with frequent enjambment between stanzas ('We thought it was the Judgment-day / And sat upright') which creates a conversational, anecdotal flow. Hardy enjoys comic and feminine rhymes ('hatters / matters', 'wonder / under'), a bathetic register that suits the dead men's grumbling. The last stanza returns to the opening sound of guns, framing the poem and giving it the solemn close the comedy has deferred.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; Thomas Hardy, Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries (London: Macmillan, 1914), Project Gutenberg text

  3. chancel window-squares l. 3

    The chancel (from Latin cancelli, the lattice screen that once divided it from the nave) is the part of the church containing the altar and choir. 'Window-squares' is Hardy's plain, almost rustic compound for panes, here shattered by the concussion of the guns. The detail roots the poem in a real parish church near the coast and sets up the stanza's sacred props, altar-crumb and glebe cow, which are all disturbed together.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  4. Judgment-day l. 4

    Christian tradition holds that at the end of the world a trumpet will sound and the dead will be raised to be judged: 'the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised' (1 Corinthians 15:52; cf. 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Revelation 20:12–13). The dead sit 'upright' in their coffins expecting resurrection, a grotesque and comic picture. The poem later returns to the trumpet in God's own mouth (st. 6), and to the 'judgment-hour', so this mistaken apocalypse frames the whole poem's question about whether any judgement will ever come.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; Bible (KJV), 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  5. drearisome l. 5

    'Drearisome' adds the suffix -some to 'dreary', as in dialect words like 'wearisome' and 'lonesome'; the OED records it as rare. Hardy liked such homely coinages and regionalisms, which ground his metaphysical poems in rural speech. Here it describes the howl of dogs woken by the gunfire, a traditional omen of death; the creatures' reactions (hounds, mouse, worms, cow) form a comic catalogue of the parish disturbed.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  6. the altar-crumb l. 7

    The 'altar-crumb' is a fragment of bread left from Holy Communion, which in Christian belief represents or becomes the body of Christ. The mouse nibbling it is a homely detail of a country church, but it also suggests the neglect or debasement of the sacrament in a 'striving' world. The line's companion, 'The worms drew back into the mounds', inverts the usual image of worms feeding on the buried dead: here even they retreat in fear of the supposed Judgement.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; The Book of Common Prayer (1662), The Order for the Administration of the Lord's Supper

  7. glebe cow l. 9

    'Glebe' (Latin gleba, clod, soil) is the portion of land assigned to a parish clergyman as part of his benefice, which he could farm or let; a churchyard and adjoining glebe were often grazed. The cow 'drooled', slavering in alarm or stupidity, a bathetic end to the catalogue of creatures before God's voice cuts in. The word reminds us that the parish is also an economic unit, and anticipates Parson Thirdly's rueful view of his own career.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  8. God's speech ll. 9–24

    Hardy, an agnostic, often dramatised a God or a cosmic power who is indifferent, forgetful or ironic (compare 'God-Forgotten' and 'New Year's Eve'). Here God speaks in colloquial idiom ('Mad as hatters', 'Ha, ha'), assures the dead that the noise is only gunnery practice and that 'the world is as it used to be'. His speech, running over four stanzas, condemns nations that 'make / Red war yet redder', yet also casts doubt on his own promises. The irreverence shocked some readers but is central to the poem's satire on Christian nations preparing for war.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; Thomas Hardy, Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries (London: Macmillan, 1914), Project Gutenberg text

  9. Mad as hatters l. 14

    'As mad as a hatter' was a common nineteenth-century simile, often linked to the nervous effects of mercury used in felt-hat making, and popularised by the Hatter of Lewis Carroll's Alice books (1865). Placing this colloquialism in the mouth of God undercuts the solemnity expected of the scene. The rhyme 'hatters / matters' and the pun on 'for Christ's sake' (an oath, and literally 'on Christ's behalf') sharpen the point: the warring nations, nominally Christian, do nothing for Christ.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  10. scour Hell's floor ll. 19–20

    'Scour' means scrub clean, and the image of the belligerent nations doomed to scour Hell's floor is a grotesque domestic punishment, more comic than Dantean. The line break after 'scour' creates a little suspense. The trailing ellipsis after 'threatening' allows God's speech to pause before the joke of 'warmer', which plays on Hell's fire. Some readers find the humour defuses the satire; others see the jocularity as Hardy's way of conveying a deeper bitterness.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  11. the trumpet (if indeed / I ever do) ll. 22–23

    This is the theological sting of the poem. The 'last trump' (1 Corinthians 15:52) is the signal for the general resurrection; God here suggests he may never blow it, because men 'rest eternal sorely need'. 'Rest eternal' echoes the liturgy for the dead ('Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine', 'Grant them eternal rest, O Lord'). The joke is double-edged: eternal rest is a mercy for exhausted humanity, but it also implies there will be no resurrection and no final justice, a view in keeping with Hardy's agnosticism.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  12. indifferent century l. 28

    One of the dead wonders whether the world will ever be 'saner' than in the century when 'He sent us under' (killed and buried us). 'Indifferent' can mean mediocre, neither good nor bad, or unconcerned, morally and spiritually lukewarm. Its ambiguity matters: if the dead lived in an 'indifferent' age, the present age of arms races is worse rather than better, undermining any Victorian faith in progress. The line also hints at the cosmic indifference that runs through Hardy's verse.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  13. Parson Thirdly l. 31

    Parson Thirdly is the parson of Weatherbury in Far from the Madding Crowd (1874), set in Hardy's fictional Wessex, where he is a minor, poor and unassuming figure. The name evokes the formal divisions of a long Victorian sermon ('thirdly, my brethren'). His regret that he did not stick 'to pipes and beer' instead of 'preaching forty year' is the poem's comic climax: even the clergy doubt the value of their ministry in a world that never changes. 'Forty year' (the dialect uninflected plural) may glance at the Israelites' forty years in the wilderness (Psalm 95:10).

    Thomas Hardy, Far from the Madding Crowd (1874), chs. 8, 33, 42, 51 and 54 (Parson Thirdly), Project Gutenberg text; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; Bible (KJV), Psalm 95:10

  14. readiness to avenge l. 34

    The final stanza abandons comedy. The alliterative 'Roaring their readiness' makes the guns animal and eager, and 'to avenge' implies grievances waiting to be paid back, a fitting account of the alliance system and naval arms race of the pre-war years. The phrase gains force from the date: the First World War broke out in August 1914, and the poem's later readers could not hear these lines without that knowledge.

    'Channel Firing', annotated text, ibiblio (note on date and Anglo-German naval rivalry); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  15. Stourton Tower l. 35

    Stourton Tower (King Alfred's Tower) is a tall triangular brick folly on the Stourhead estate on the Somerset–Wiltshire border, built for the banker Henry Hoare II in the 1760s–70s to mark the place where Alfred the Great is said to have raised his standard against the Danes. The final lines trace the sound of the guns travelling inland and backward in time: from a monument to a war hero, to the legendary Camelot, to prehistoric Stonehenge. Every site recalls an earlier phase of England's history of war and power, suggesting the long continuity of human conflict.

    Atlas Obscura, 'King Alfred's Tower' (the Stourhead folly); Danny Howell, 'Attracting Tourists to Stourton Tower (Alfred's Tower) in the 1890s'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  16. Camelot … Stonehenge l. 36

    Camelot, the court of King Arthur, was identified by the Tudor antiquary John Leland with South Cadbury hill-fort in Somerset, within the region Hardy evokes. Stonehenge, the Neolithic and Bronze Age stone circle in Wiltshire, appears in Tess of the d'Urbervilles as Tess's last refuge. 'Starlit' gives the poem's last image a stillness and timelessness that contrasts with the roaring guns. The receding sequence (Georgian folly, Arthurian legend, prehistory) suggests that war is as old as human civilisation, and that the guns' echo spreads through all of English history.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; English Heritage, 'History of Stonehenge'; 'Channel Firing', annotated text, ibiblio (note on date and Anglo-German naval rivalry)
