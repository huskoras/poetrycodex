The Oxen

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Moments of Vision and Miscellaneous Verses

Religion

CHRISTMAS EVE, and twelve of the clock.
“Now they are all on their knees,”
An elder said as we sat in a flock
By the embers in hearthside ease.

We pictured the meek mild creatures where
They dwelt in their strawy pen,
Nor did it occur to one of us there
To doubt they were kneeling then.

So fair a fancy few would weave
In these years! Yet, I feel,
If someone said on Christmas Eve,
“Come; see the oxen kneel

“In the lonely barton by yonder coomb
Our childhood used to know,”
I should go with him in the gloom,
Hoping it might be so.

1915.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and publication l. 1

    Hardy published the poem in The Times on 24 December 1915, the second Christmas of the war, on a page that also carried an editorial on 'The Second War Christmas'. He revised it for Moments of Vision (1917), where it is dated 1915. The poem recalls a childhood scene by the fire when an elder told the gathered company that the cattle were kneeling at midnight to honour Christ's birth. 'Twelve of the clock' is an older form of 'twelve o'clock', suiting the remembered past.

    The Victorian Web, 'The Oxen' (text from The Times, 24 Dec. 1915, checked against Hynes vol. 2); The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), 'The Oxen' and notes

  2. the kneeling oxen ll. 2-8

    This belief was widespread in rural England and Europe; Francis Kilvert recorded an old man's claim to have seen the oxen kneel, and Ruth Firor's Folkways in Thomas Hardy (1931) discusses it. Hardy had used the same story in Tess of the d'Urbervilles (ch. 17), in a comic anecdote about William Dewy. Behind it lie the ox and ass of the Nativity tradition, from Isaiah 1:3 ('The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib') and the apocryphal Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew, where the animals adore the child in the manger.

    The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'; Thomas Hardy, Tess of the d'Urbervilles (1891), ch. 17, Project Gutenberg; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 1:3

  3. in a flock l. 3

    'Flock' is an unexpected word for people sitting round a fire; it suggests the children's closeness and innocence, and also the pastoral image of the faithful as a flock under a shepherd (John 10). The 'elder' who speaks may be simply an older person, but the word also carries a church sense (an elder of a congregation). 'Hearthside ease' completes the cosy scene of rural community in which the belief was shared.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Bible (KJV), John 10:16

  4. meek mild creatures l. 5

    Charles Wesley's hymn 'Gentle Jesus, meek and mild' (1742) was among the best known children's hymns of the nineteenth century, and 'meek' also recalls the Beatitude 'Blessed are the meek' (Matthew 5:5). Applying these words to the cattle in their 'strawy pen' makes them participants in the Nativity's humility. The children 'pictured' the creatures: the scene is imagined, not seen, and 'Nor did it occur to one of us there / To doubt they were kneeling then'.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 5:5; The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'

  5. 'So fair a fancy few would weave' l. 9

    In The Times the line read 'So fair a fancy few believe', and Hardy revised it to 'would weave' for Moments of Vision. The change shifts the emphasis from belief to imagination: modern people cannot even make such fictions, let alone credit them. 'Fancy' had a Romantic sense (lighter than 'imagination', in Coleridge's distinction) of playful invention. The alliteration ('fair', 'fancy', 'few') makes the line itself a small weaving.

    The Victorian Web, 'The Oxen' (text from The Times, 24 Dec. 1915, checked against Hynes vol. 2); The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), 'The Oxen' and notes

  6. 'In these years!' l. 10

    The line has a double reference. In 1915 'these years' were the years of the Great War, which Hardy, a pessimist with strong humanitarian feelings, found appalling. More broadly they are the decades after Darwin and the Higher Criticism of the Bible, when many educated people, Hardy among them, lost the faith they grew up with. The stanza turns on 'Yet, I feel', marking the poem's sudden admission of a feeling that survives disbelief.

    The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), 'The Oxen' and notes

  7. barton l. 13

    From Old English bere-tun, barley enclosure, 'barton' was used in Dorset and several other southern and western counties for a farmyard or the outbuildings of a farm, often with cattle sheds. Hardy uses it frequently in the Wessex novels. One scholar (J. O. Bailey) connected the scene with a farm near Hardy's birthplace in Higher Bockhampton, Stinsford parish. The dialect word places the hoped-for miracle in a remembered, specific Wessex.

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'

  8. coomb l. 13

    'Coomb' (spelt also 'combe', 'comb'; the Times text has 'comb') derives from an Old English word of Celtic origin, related to Welsh cwm. It is common in West Country place-names. 'Yonder', an old demonstrative meaning 'over there', is spoken by the imagined companion, as though the place were still visible: 'Our childhood used to know' puts the farmyard in the speaker's past.

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'

  9. 'Hoping it might be so' l. 16

    The final stanza is a conditional: if someone invited him, he would go to see the oxen kneel. The 'gloom' is literal darkness and the obscurity of doubt. The last line is among the most quoted in Hardy, a precise statement of the agnostic's longing for faith. Critics differ on its weight: for some it is a gentle nostalgia; others stress a deeper religious yearning; the 1918 Athenaeum called the poem a Christmas reverie.

    The Victorian Web, 'Image, Allusion, Voice, Dialect, and Irony in Thomas Hardy's "The Oxen" and the Poem's Original Publication Context'; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), 'The Oxen' and notes

  10. Form: hymn or ballad metre sts. 1-4

    The poem uses common measure, the alternating tetrameter and trimeter of English hymns and ballads, so the form itself recalls the church and the folk tradition the poem regrets. The rhythm is loosened, with extra unstressed syllables, and the speech-like run-on from stanza 3 to stanza 4 (quotation marks opening a new stanza) lets the imagined invitation spill across the break. The simplicity of the form contains a complicated feeling.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Clarendon Press, 1984), 'The Oxen' and notes; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes
