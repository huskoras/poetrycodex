Nature

The Darkling Thrush

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Poems of the Past and the Present

Nature

I LEANT upon a coppice gate
When Frost was spectre-gray,
And Winter’s dregs made desolate
The weakening eye of day.
The tangled bine-stems scored the sky
Like strings from broken lyres,
And all mankind that haunted nigh
Had sought their household fires.

The land’s sharp features seemed to be
The Century’s corpse outleant,
His crypt the cloudy canopy,
The wind his death-lament.
The ancient pulse of germ and birth
Was shrunken hard and dry,
And every spirit upon earth
Seemed fervourless as I.

At once a voice outburst among
The bleak twigs overhead
In a full-hearted evensong
Of joy illimited;
An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small,
In blast-beruffled plume,
Had chosen thus to fling his soul
Upon the growing gloom.

So little cause for carollings
Of such ecstatic sound
Was written on terrestrial things
Afar or nigh around,
That I could think there trembled through
His happy good-night air
Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew
And I was unaware.

December 1900.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and publication l. 1

    The poem appeared in The Graphic on 29 December 1900 under the title 'By the Century's Deathbed', and again in The Times on 1 January 1901, before Hardy collected it as 'The Darkling Thrush' in Poems of the Past and the Present (dated 1902 on its title page). The manuscript has '1899' struck out and '1900' written in: the nineteenth century strictly ended on 31 December 1900, though many had celebrated its end a year earlier. 'Darkling' (in the dark, at dusk) recalls Keats's nightingale and the 'darkling plain' of Arnold's 'Dover Beach'. Hardy was sixty at the time, and the elderly speaker is close to him.

    The Victorian Web, 'The Darkling Thrush' (text, publication history, manuscript); 'The Darkling Thrush', Wikipedia (publication history; suggested sources incl. Vaughan, Keats, Shelley); James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes

  2. coppice l. 1

    Coppicing, the periodic cutting of hazel, ash or oak to the base so that it throws up new shoots, was a staple of the rural economy Hardy wrote about (compare the woodland trades of The Woodlanders). The word thus brings with it the idea of growth renewed after cutting, which the poem, at least at first, denies. Leaning on a gate is a characteristic posture of the rustic observer in Hardy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coppice'; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  3. Frost was spectre-gray l. 2

    The capitalised 'Frost' and 'Winter' are personifications, in a style that recalls eighteenth-century and Romantic poetry. 'Spectre-gray' introduces the ghostly vocabulary that culminates in the century's 'corpse'. The spelling 'gray' is Hardy's. The landscape is presented as already haunted before the speaker meditates on the century's death, and the gray of frost anticipates the 'gloom' into which the thrush will sing. Hardy's adjectives throughout the first stanza (desolate, weakening, tangled, broken) build a cumulative sense of exhaustion that the bird's song will interrupt.

    James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spectre'

  4. Winter's dregs l. 3

    Dregs are the grounds left in a vessel after the liquid is drunk, and figuratively the worst or most worthless part. The image suggests a cup drained to the bottom, an end-of-the-line exhaustion that fits a dying century and the end of a December day. The dregs make 'desolate' the 'weakening eye of day', the sun low on the horizon.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dregs'; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  5. eye of day l. 4

    Calling the sun the eye of day is an old poetic figure (compare Shakespeare's 'the eye of heaven' in Sonnet 18). Here the eye is 'weakening', both as daylight fades at the dead end of the year, and the image of failing sight anticipates the speaker's own limited vision at the end: he is 'unaware'. The image also continues the personification of the season and the day.

    Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, l. 5 ('the eye of heaven'); James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes

  6. bine-stems l. 5

    'Bine' is a dialect variant of 'bind', used of the twining stems of climbers, as in 'hop-bine' and 'woodbine'. In midwinter only the tangled dead stems remain. 'Scored' means marked with lines or scratches, and also, with the lyre image that follows, written out as music: a score with nothing to play. The dead stems belong to the same world of plants regrowing after cutting as the coppice, here without visible renewal.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bine'; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  7. strings from broken lyres l. 6

    The lyre is the traditional emblem of lyric poetry; the Romantics favoured its cousin the Aeolian harp, played by the wind, as an image of the poet's inspired responsiveness (Coleridge's 'The Eolian Harp'; Shelley's 'Make me thy lyre' in 'Ode to the West Wind'). Hardy's lyres are broken: the winter wind finds no instrument to play, and the Romantic idea of nature inspiring song seems exhausted. The thrush will shortly contradict this.

    P. B. Shelley, 'Ode to the West Wind' (1820), l. 57 ('Make me thy lyre'); S. T. Coleridge, 'The Eolian Harp' (1795/6); Charles Lock, "'The Darkling Thrush' and the Habit of Singing", Essays in Criticism 36.2 (1986), 121-41

  8. Stanza form ll. 7-8

    Each stanza doubles the common metre of hymns and ballads (8.6.8.6), with alternate rhymes. The form's familiarity from church hymnody suits a poem that borrows religious vocabulary ('evensong', 'carollings', 'blessed Hope') while doubting the faith it once expressed. The first stanza closes by withdrawing all humanity indoors ('all mankind that haunted nigh'), leaving the speaker alone with the landscape; 'haunted' continues the ghostly diction.

    Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes; James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes

  9. the Century's corpse outleant l. 10

    'Outleant' (leant out, stretched out) is a rare compound of the kind Hardy favoured, and may be his own coinage. It suggests a corpse laid out for burial, and also a body leaning or projecting out over the land. The sharp features of the land are the corpse's features, emaciated in death. The image explains the original title, 'By the Century's Deathbed'. Readers differ on how far the dead century stands for a particular age (Victorian confidence, or the faith of earlier generations) or simply for time's passing.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Clarendon Press, 1982), notes; James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'outlean'

  10. crypt ... canopy ... death-lament ll. 11-12

    A crypt is an underground vault, usually beneath a church; a canopy is the cloth or structure held over a bier, throne or altar. The sky is thus both tomb and funeral pall, and the wind supplies the dirge. The whole scene is a secular funeral at which no one prays. The religious setting of a crypt anticipates the 'evensong' of the third stanza, where a service is finally sung.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'crypt'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'canopy'; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  11. germ l. 13

    'Germ' (Latin germen, sprout) means the seed, bud or embryo; the 'pulse of germ and birth' is the rhythmic renewal of life in nature. Hardy had read Darwin closely, and the phrase gives biological life a pulse like a heartbeat, now 'shrunken hard and dry' like a dried seed. The image anticipates by contrast the 'aged thrush', in whom some life persists.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'germ'; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  12. fervourless l. 16

    'Fervour' (from Latin fervere, to boil) means intensity of feeling, and especially religious ardour. The line reveals that the desolation of the first two stanzas is partly the speaker's own; the landscape 'seemed' to be dead, and every spirit 'Seemed' as cold as he. That admission prepares for the surprise of the next stanza. The word also prepares the contrast with the 'ecstatic' bird.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fervour'; Charles Lock, "'The Darkling Thrush' and the Habit of Singing", Essays in Criticism 36.2 (1986), 121-41

  13. evensong l. 19

    Evensong is the popular name for the Anglican Order for Evening Prayer. Hardy, who loved church music and had once considered ordination, gives the bird a liturgical role in a world from which the human worshippers have retired to their 'household fires'. 'Full-hearted' recalls Shelley's skylark pouring its 'full heart' in song. 'Illimited' (unlimited) is an older, more Latinate form.

    Book of Common Prayer (1662), 'The Order for Evening Prayer'; P. B. Shelley, 'To a Skylark' (1820), ll. 1-5; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'evensong'

  14. An aged thrush l. 21

    J. O. Bailey identified the bird as a mistle thrush, known for singing from treetops in winter weather. Unlike the 'immortal' nightingale of Keats or the skylark of Shelley, Hardy's bird is old, thin and storm-blown ('blast-beruffled'), so the Romantic singing bird is reimagined in the image of the poet himself, sixty in 1900. The adjectives emphasise how little physical cause the bird has for joy.

    J. O. Bailey, The Poetry of Thomas Hardy: A Handbook and Commentary (U of North Carolina P, 1970), p. 166 (as cited by the Victorian Web); The Victorian Web, 'The Darkling Thrush' (text, publication history, manuscript); John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), l. 51 ('Darkling I listen')

  15. fling his soul / Upon the growing gloom ll. 23-24

    'Had chosen' attributes deliberate choice to the bird, as if its song were an act of defiance or faith. 'Fling' is reckless, wholehearted; 'soul' implies more than animal noise. The alliteration of 'growing gloom' marks the darkness against which the song is set, and returns to the idea of the darkling bird. The phrase contrasts with the passive humanity of stanza one, who 'had sought their household fires'.

    Charles Lock, "'The Darkling Thrush' and the Habit of Singing", Essays in Criticism 36.2 (1986), 121-41; Tim Armstrong, ed., Thomas Hardy: Selected Poems (Longman Annotated Texts, 1993), notes

  16. carollings l. 25

    A carol was originally a ring-dance with song, and then a joyous song, especially one celebrating Christmas. The poem was published four days after Christmas, and the seasonal association adds to the religious vocabulary through which Hardy describes the bird. The speaker can see no 'cause' for such joy 'written on terrestrial things', that is, legible in the earthly world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'carol'; James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes

  17. terrestrial l. 27

    'Terrestrial' (Latin terra, earth) carries a traditional contrast with the celestial (compare 1 Corinthians 15:40, 'There are also celestial bodies, and bodies terrestrial'). By locating the absence of cause in terrestrial things, the speaker leaves open, without asserting, the possibility of a cause beyond them. The Latinate word is also set against the plain monosyllables around it, a typical Hardyan mixture of learned and homely diction, and it raises the register at the moment the speaker reasons about causes. 'Afar or nigh around' insists that he has looked everywhere and found nothing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'terrestrial'; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:40

  18. Some blessed Hope l. 31

    Titus 2:13 speaks of 'looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearing of the great God'; Hope is also one of the three theological virtues (1 Corinthians 13:13). Hardy's 'Some' makes the Hope vague and conjectural, and 'I could think' makes it a possibility the speaker entertains rather than a belief. Some critics read the close as a cautious opening towards hope at the century's turn; others stress its irony, since the hope is attributed to a bird, whose song may be no more than instinct. Charles Lock has argued that the bird's singing may be habit rather than message.

    Bible (KJV), Titus 2:13; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:13; Charles Lock, "'The Darkling Thrush' and the Habit of Singing", Essays in Criticism 36.2 (1986), 121-41

  19. And I was unaware l. 32

    The ending is quietly balanced. 'Whereof he knew / And I was unaware' grants the bird a knowledge the speaker lacks, but only within the hypothetical 'I could think'. The last word places the poet among the uncomprehending, consistent with the honest agnosticism of much of Hardy's verse. The poem stops short of conversion and of denial. Many readers find the poem's honesty in that refusal.

    Charles Lock, "'The Darkling Thrush' and the Habit of Singing", Essays in Criticism 36.2 (1986), 121-41; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 1 (Clarendon Press, 1982), notes

  20. December 1900 subscription

    Hardy usually dated his poems, and here the date is part of the meaning: the 'Century's corpse' is the nineteenth century, which ended strictly at the close of 1900. The manuscript's deletion of '1899' (the year many celebrated as the century's end) has led some to suggest the poem was drafted then and redated for the true turn of the century.

    The Victorian Web, 'The Darkling Thrush' (text, publication history, manuscript); 'The Darkling Thrush', Wikipedia (publication history; suggested sources incl. Vaughan, Keats, Shelley); James Gibson, ed., Thomas Hardy: The Complete Poems (Macmillan, 1976), 'The Darkling Thrush' and textual notes
