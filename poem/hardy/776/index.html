Time & Brevity

The Dead Drummer

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Poems of the Past and the Present

Time & Brevity

I

THEY throw in Drummer Hodge, to rest
Uncoffined—just as found:
His landmark is a kopje-crest
That breaks the veldt around;
And foreign constellations west
Each night above his mound.

II

Young Hodge the Drummer never knew—
Fresh from his Wessex home—
The meaning of the broad Karoo,
The Bush, the dusty loam,
And why uprose to nightly view
Strange stars amid the gloam.

III

Yet portion of that unknown plain
Will Hodge for ever be;
His homely Northern breast and brain
Grow up a Southern tree.
And strange-eyed constellations reign
His stars eternally.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and the Boer War title; st. I, l. 1

    The poem appeared in the weekly Literature on 25 November 1899, only weeks after the Second Boer War (1899-1902) broke out in October, under the title 'The Dead Drummer', which this archive keeps. Hardy retitled it 'Drummer Hodge' when he collected it among the 'War Poems' of Poems of the Past and the Present (1901), and revised the text between versions. The opening verb sets the tone: the burial party 'throw in' the body, a phrase without ceremony. Against the patriotic verse that greeted the war, Hardy offers a single unnamed casualty and a question about where, and to what, such a death belongs.

    The Victorian Web, 'Drummer Hodge' (text and glosses, after Daiches and Charvat); University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature

  2. Hodge st. II, l. 1

    From the Middle Ages 'Hodge', a familiar form of the name Roger, served as a generic name for the rustic labourer, and by Hardy's time it carried an urban sneer at the slow-witted yokel. Hardy disliked the stereotype: in his essay 'The Dorsetshire Labourer' (Longman's Magazine, 1883) he argued that the imagined 'Hodge' hid real, various individuals. Giving the drummer this name does two things at once. It makes him an Everyman of the English countryside, any village boy; and it quietly rebukes the readers who would use the name dismissively, since the poem grants him a cosmic resting place. Note that the archive's title omits the name, which Hardy added only in 1901.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'; Michael Millgate, Thomas Hardy: A Biography Revisited (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004)

  3. Drummer st. I, l. 1

    Regimental drummers were frequently young, and the drummer boy was a favourite figure of Victorian military sentiment. The St Andrews 'Hardy and the Boer War' project suggests that Hardy cast Hodge as a drummer to puncture that romance, setting him against conventional models such as Kipling's story 'The Drums of the Fore and Aft' (1888), in which two drummer boys rally a fleeing regiment and die heroically. Hardy's drummer does nothing heroic in the poem; he is simply found dead and buried. The adjective 'Young' in stanza II and 'Fresh from his Wessex home' underline his youth and inexperience.

    University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature

  4. Uncoffined st. I, l. 2

    'Uncoffined' is a stark negative coinage in Hardy's manner (compare his many 'un-' words, as in 'The Self-Unseeing'). Soldiers killed in the field were commonly buried where they fell, wrapped if at all in a blanket. The phrase 'just as found' adds that no one prepared the body. For a Wessex labourer, burial would normally mean a churchyard among his own people; the poem pointedly denies him that. Tearle observes the contrast with conventional burial. The deprivation is then reversed in the last stanza, where the earth itself takes him in.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  5. kopje-crest st. I, l. 3

    'Kopje' (pronounced roughly 'koppie') is Afrikaans/Dutch for a small hill, literally 'little head'. Such outcrops rise from the flat veld and became tactically important in the war. 'Landmark' here keeps its old sense of a boundary marker or sign of position: in place of a headstone, Hodge's grave is identified only by the shape of the land. Hardy deliberately fills the first two stanzas with the foreign words that British readers were then meeting in newspaper dispatches (kopje, veldt, Karoo), so that the strangeness Hodge felt is felt in the reader's mouth.

    The Victorian Web, 'Drummer Hodge' (text and glosses, after Daiches and Charvat); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature

  6. veldt st. I, l. 4

    From the Dutch 'veld', field, the word denotes the open, largely treeless grassland of southern Africa. The spelling with -dt follows older Dutch orthography and was standard in English writing around 1900. The kopje 'breaks' the veldt, interrupting its flatness, an image of a single feature in an empty space that matches the single body in a foreign land. The Victorian Web gloss calls it a Boer term for prairie.

    The Victorian Web, 'Drummer Hodge' (text and glosses, after Daiches and Charvat); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  7. foreign constellations west st. I, ll. 5-6

    The syntax is compressed. Most readers take 'west' as a verb: the foreign constellations 'west each night', that is, wheel across the sky and set westward above his grave. The OED records 'west' as a verb in this sense of moving towards the west, used especially of heavenly bodies, and Tearle paraphrases the stars as moving west each night. An alternative reading treats 'west' as an adverb ('in the west') with a verb understood, which is grammatically awkward. Either way the stars are 'foreign': in the southern hemisphere Hodge lies under constellations, such as the Southern Cross, that he never saw at home. The theme returns in each stanza's final couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1976), notes to the poem

  8. Wessex st. II, l. 2

    Hardy revived the name of the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of the West Saxons for the region of his novels and poems, roughly Dorset and the neighbouring counties. Calling Hodge's home 'Wessex' makes him kin to the rural figures of Hardy's fiction and to the poet himself. 'Fresh from' stresses how little time has passed between village and battlefield. The contrast of the familiar 'Wessex home' with the strange 'Karoo' and 'Bush' structures the whole stanza, which is a list of what he 'never knew'.

    The Thomas Hardy Society, 'Life of Thomas Hardy'; University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'

  9. Karoo; the Bush st. II, ll. 3-4

    The Great Karoo is a high, dry plateau in what was then Cape Colony; the name is usually derived from a Khoekhoe (Khoikhoi) word for a dry or hard place. 'The Bush' is the colonial term for wild, uncultivated land covered in scrub. Hodge 'never knew / The meaning' of these places: he did not understand the land, or the war fought over it, or why he was there. The phrase 'the dusty loam' brings in a homely English farming word, 'loam' (a fertile soil), and roughens it with 'dusty', so even the soil is not what he knew.

    The Victorian Web, 'Drummer Hodge' (text and glosses, after Daiches and Charvat); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  10. gloam st. II, l. 6

    'Gloam' is a rare poetic word for twilight, formed from 'gloaming' (Old English glomung). The OED records it as a nineteenth-century back-formation. Hardy liked such archaic or dialect-tinged words, and the rhyme with 'home' and 'loam' binds the strange sky to the lost home. The 'Strange stars' of this line answer the 'foreign constellations' of stanza I and prepare the 'strange-eyed constellations' of the close, so that each stanza ends under alien stars.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature

  11. portion of that unknown plain st. III, ll. 1-2

    The last stanza turns from what Hodge did not know to what he will become: 'portion of' the plain 'for ever'. The idea that the dead are rolled into the earth's processes recalls Wordsworth's 'A slumber did my spirit seal', and it anticipates, by some fifteen years, Rupert Brooke's 'The Soldier' (1914), in which an English body makes a 'corner of a foreign field' forever England. Tearle makes the Brooke comparison. Hardy's version runs the other way from Brooke's: the foreign field does not become English; the English boy becomes African. There is a bleak consolation in this union with the land, but no patriotism.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'

  12. Grow up a Southern tree st. III, ll. 3-4

    The antithesis 'Northern ... Southern' compresses the poem's geography. 'Homely' here has the older sense of belonging to home, plain and domestic. 'Breast and brain', feeling and thought, will 'Grow up' as a tree, the matter of his body feeding the plant. The image has a long ancestry in elegy (the dead becoming flowers, as in Ovid's metamorphoses), but Hardy keeps it literal and unconsoling: this is chemistry rather than resurrection. The verb 'Grow up' also recalls that Hodge was 'Young' and will now never grow up in the ordinary sense.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1976), notes to the poem

  13. strange-eyed constellations reign st. III, ll. 5-6

    The final couplet completes the star motif ('foreign', 'Strange', now 'strange-eyed'). The compound personifies the constellations as watchers, and 'reign' gives them sovereignty, perhaps ironically echoing the imperial power that sent Hodge to Africa. Yet the stars are 'His stars eternally': possession is granted only in death. The poem refuses any statement about the war's justice; its judgment lies in the gap between a farm boy's narrow life and the huge, indifferent cosmos in which he now lies, a characteristic Hardy perspective.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "Drummer Hodge"', Interesting Literature; University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'

  14. Stanza and structure st. I-III

    Each numbered stanza has six lines alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter, rhyming ababab, a ballad-like measure stretched by one extra pair. The structure is strict: stanza I, burial; II, ignorance; III, transformation, each ending with the stars. The plain, regular form suits a common soldier's elegy, and Hardy's Roman numerals give it the slightly formal air of a monument. The St Andrews project notes the consistent rhyme and metre. The last line shortens into a cadence of finality.

    University of St Andrews, 'Hardy and the Boer War': 'A Practical Look at Drummer Hodge'; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1976), notes to the poem
