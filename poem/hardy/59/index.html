The Voice

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries, with Miscellaneous Pieces

History & Politics

WOMAN much missed, how you call to me, call to me,
Saying that now you are not as you were
When you had changed from the one who was all to me,
But as at first, when our day was fair.

Can it be you that I hear? Let me view you, then,
Standing as when I drew near to the town
Where you would wait for me: yes, as I knew you then,
Even to the original air-blue gown!

Or is it only the breeze, in its listlessness
Travelling across the wet mead to me here,
You being ever consigned to existlessness,
Heard no more again far or near?

Thus I; faltering forward,
Leaves around me falling,
Wind oozing thin through the thorn from norward
And the woman calling.

December 1912.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'The Voice': Emma Hardy and the Poems of 1912-13 l. 1

    Emma Lavinia Gifford, whom Hardy met in Cornwall in 1870 and married in 1874, died suddenly at Max Gate, Dorchester, on 27 November 1912. The marriage had long been estranged, and her death released in Hardy a flood of remorseful love poems, gathered as 'Poems of 1912-13' in Satires of Circumstance (1914), under the epigraph 'Veteris vestigia flammae' ('traces of the old flame'), Dido's words in Aeneid IV.23. This text reproduces the 1914 first edition, with the date 'December 1912'. According to Claire Tomalin, the first draft addressed the presence as a 'weird' woman, a more sinister tone later dropped.

    Ian Lancashire, ed., 'The Voice', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Claire Tomalin, introduction to Thomas Hardy, Unexpected Elegies: Poems of 1912-13 and Other Poems about Emma (Persea, 2010); Virgil, Aeneid IV.23 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library)

  2. Dactylic metre and triple rhymes l. 1

    The first three stanzas use a four-beat line built largely of dactyls (a stressed syllable followed by two unstressed), as in 'WOMan much MISSED, how you CALL to me, CALL to me'. The odd lines end with triple rhymes ('call to me / all to me', 'view you, then / knew you then', 'listlessness / existlessness'), which echo like a call returning. The repetition 'call to me, call to me' imitates the persistence of the voice. Critics have called the rhythm a kind of dance, which makes the collapse of the metre in the last stanza all the more affecting.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  3. changed from the one who was all to me l. 3

    The voice says that she is not 'as you were', that is, as she had become during the long cold years of the marriage, when she had 'changed from the one who was all to me'. Instead she is 'as at first, when our day was fair', the woman of the Cornish courtship of 1870. The lines quietly acknowledge the estrangement while imagining it undone. Whether the change was hers alone, or partly his, the poem does not say; other poems of 1912-13 show Hardy's guilt at his own neglect.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Claire Tomalin, introduction to Thomas Hardy, Unexpected Elegies: Poems of 1912-13 and Other Poems about Emma (Persea, 2010)

  4. when our day was fair l. 4

    In March 1870 Hardy, then a young architect, travelled to St Juliot in north Cornwall to survey the dilapidated parish church for restoration; Emma Gifford was living at the rectory with her sister, the rector's wife. Their courtship over the following years, among the cliffs and valleys near Boscastle, became the lost paradise of the 'Poems of 1912-13', several of which Hardy wrote after revisiting the places in March 1913. 'Fair' carries both the weather and the beauty of that time.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Claire Tomalin, introduction to Thomas Hardy, Unexpected Elegies: Poems of 1912-13 and Other Poems about Emma (Persea, 2010); James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  5. Can it be you that I hear? Let me view you, then l. 5

    The second stanza begins with doubt ('Can it be you that I hear?') and passes immediately to desire. The voice is heard but not seen; the speaker asks for a vision to confirm it. Robert Langbaum has described the poem's experience as purely auditory, placeless and timeless, which the wish to 'view' attempts to fix in place and time. The triple rhyme 'view you, then / knew you then' ties seeing now to knowing then.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017)

  6. Standing as when I drew near to the town / Where you would wait for me l. 6

    During the courtship Hardy made the long journey from Dorset to Cornwall several times, and Emma would wait for him on his arrival. The poem does not name the town, and readers should be cautious about identifying it; the point is the image of the young woman waiting expectantly for her lover. The memory reverses the later marriage, in which she waited in vain for his attention. The precise detail, the waiting, the approach, the gown, gives the vision a photographic clarity.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Claire Tomalin, introduction to Thomas Hardy, Unexpected Elegies: Poems of 1912-13 and Other Poems about Emma (Persea, 2010)

  7. Even to the original air-blue gown! l. 8

    The speaker wants to see her exactly as she was, 'even to' the very gown. 'Air-blue' is Hardy's compound for a pale sky-blue, and A. Banerjee notes that it replaced an earlier 'hat and gown', giving the figure a more airy, benign ghostliness. 'Original' has several senses: the first gown, the genuine one, and the gown of her original self before she changed. The exclamation mark registers the intensity of the wish.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017)

  8. Or is it only the breeze, in its listlessness l. 9

    The third stanza introduces a rational explanation: the call may be the sound of a tired breeze. 'Listlessness' means a lack of energy or interest, and it transfers to the wind the speaker's own exhausted state. The meadow ('mead', an old poetic word for meadow) is wet with the rain of a December day, the season of Emma's death. The rhyme 'listlessness / existlessness' binds the wind's apathy to her non-existence.

    Ian Lancashire, ed., 'The Voice', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017)

  9. mead l. 10

    'Mead' (Old English maed) is a meadow or grassland, often used in poetry and preserved in English place-names. Its rustic, archaic flavour fits Hardy's Dorset landscape around Max Gate, and its wetness establishes the late-autumn damp that runs through the last stanza's falling leaves and oozing wind. The breeze 'Travelling across the wet mead to me here' also gives the sound a direction and a distance, as if the voice were approaching across open ground.

    Ian Lancashire, ed., 'The Voice', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. mead, n.2

  10. You being ever consigned to existlessness l. 11

    This line was later revised. The first edition (1914), followed here, reads 'consigned to existlessness', a stark coinage meaning non-existence; from the Collected Poems onward Hardy printed 'dissolved to wan wistlessness', where 'wistless' (the OED cites Southey, 1796) means unknowing, inattentive. Critics weigh the versions differently. Oliver Tearle prefers the alliteration of the revision and notes that it makes death a loss of awareness rather than of existence; John Lucas argues that Hardy could hardly speak of the 'existlessness' of a woman whose presence the poem feels, though he concedes 'wan' is dull. The variant thus bears directly on whether the voice is real.

    Ian Lancashire, ed., 'The Voice', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017); John Lucas, 'Letter from John Lucas', PN Review; James Gibson, ed., The Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (Macmillan, 1976; New Wessex edn), text and variants

  11. Thus I; faltering forward l. 13

    The final stanza changes form: the lines shorten to three stresses, the rhythm becomes uneven, and the rhymes become feminine two-syllable ones ('forward / norward', 'falling / calling'). 'Thus I' is an abrupt, almost formal turn to narrative, as if the speech has run out. 'Faltering forward' describes his unsteady walk but also the poem's own stumbling movement. Robert Langbaum, agreeing with Donald Davie's estimate of the poem, argues that this break in symmetry makes it a major Hardy poem: form enacts the collapse of the illusion.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017)

  12. Wind oozing thin through the thorn from norward l. 15

    'Norward' is a contracted form of 'northward', here meaning from the north. 'Oozing' is an unexpected verb for wind, suggesting a slow, thin seepage like water through cracks, rather than a blowing. The thorn (hawthorn) is a recurrent tree in Hardy's poetry, associated with bleakness and endurance. The falling leaves place the scene in autumn or early winter, contrasting with the summer gown of the second stanza and suggesting the death of the year. The RPO notes record that the punctuation of this line differs between the 1914 edition and the Collected Poems.

    Ian Lancashire, ed., 'The Voice', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin

  13. And the woman calling l. 16

    The final line returns to the first word, 'Woman', but now in the third person and without an answer. The participle 'calling' leaves the action open, unconcluded. The speaker has raised the possibility that it is only the breeze, but the ending does not settle the question: the call continues, whether as ghost, wind or memory. Readers have seen in this unresolved close the essence of Hardy's elegiac art, which neither believes nor disbelieves in the presence of the dead.

    A. Banerjee, 'The Voice', The Victorian Web (2024), citing Robert Langbaum and Claire Tomalin; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Thomas Hardy's "The Voice"', Interesting Literature (2017)
