The Convergence of the Twain

by Thomas Hardy 1840–1928

From Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries, with Miscellaneous Pieces

Living

(Lines on the loss of the “Titanic”)

I

IN a solitude of the sea
Deep from human vanity,
And the Pride of Life that planned her, stilly couches she.

II

Steel chambers, late the pyres
Of her salamandrine fires,
Cold currents thrid, and turn to rhythmic tidal lyres.

III

Over the mirrors meant
To glass the opulent
The sea-worm crawls—grotesque, slimed, dumb, indifferent.

IV

Jewels in joy designed
To ravish the sensuous mind
Lie lightless, all their sparkles bleared and black and blind.

V

Dim moon-eyed fishes near
Gaze at the gilded gear
And query: “What does this vaingloriousness down here?” . . .

VI

Well: while was fashioning
This creature of cleaving wing,
The Immanent Will that stirs and urges everything

VII

Prepared a sinister mate
For her—so gaily great—
A Shape of Ice, for the time far and dissociate.

VIII

And as the smart ship grew
In stature, grace, and hue,
In shadowy silent distance grew the Iceberg too.

IX

Alien they seemed to be:
No mortal eye could see
The intimate welding of their later history,

X

Or sign that they were bent
By paths coincident
On being anon twin halves of one august event,

XI

Till the Spinner of the Years
Said “Now!” And each one hears,
And consummation comes, and jars two hemispheres.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion title

    The White Star liner Titanic, advertised as practically unsinkable, struck an iceberg on her maiden voyage and sank on 15 April 1912 with the loss of some 1,500 lives. Hardy dated his poem 24 April 1912, and it was first printed in the souvenir programme of the 'Dramatic and Operatic Matinée in Aid of the Titanic Disaster Fund' at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, on 14 May 1912, in a shorter early form of thirty lines (the final text has thirty-three), under the headnote 'Improvised on the Loss of the Titanic'. It was revised and collected in Satires of Circumstance (1914). For a charity occasion the poem is strikingly cold: it names no victim and offers no consolation, and critics have long debated whether it is a sermon on vanity or a detached cosmic fable (see the 'Immanent Will' note).

    '“The Convergence of the Twain”: Thomas Hardy and Popular Sentiment', CPRW essay on the poem's first printing; Royal Museums Greenwich archive: photocopy of Hardy's manuscript of 'The Convergence of the Twain'; Thomas Hardy, Satires of Circumstance, Lyrics and Reveries (London: Macmillan, 1914), Project Gutenberg text; James Gibson, ed., The Variorum Edition of the Complete Poems of Thomas Hardy (London: Macmillan, 1979), text and variants for the poem

  2. Twain (title) and twin halves title; l. 30

    'Twain' (Old English twā / twēgen) survived by 1912 mainly in elevated, biblical and proverbial use; its best-known occurrence is the marriage text 'they twain shall be one flesh' (Matthew 19:5), which the poem's language of 'mate', 'intimate welding' and 'consummation' grimly echoes. 'Convergence' is geometric and scientific: two lines of travel meeting at a point, the paths 'coincident' of st. X. The title therefore yokes a Latinate, almost technical noun to an archaic, ceremonial one, the same blend of registers that marks the whole poem. Its 'twain' returns as 'twin halves': two parts of one whole, recalling the myth in Plato's Symposium of lovers as severed halves seeking reunion, though whether Hardy intended that echo is uncertain.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Bible (KJV), Matthew 19:5; 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  3. Stanza form st. I–XI

    Each stanza rhymes aaa, with two short lines (roughly trimeter) followed by a long line of six stresses. Readers have seen the printed shape as visual mimesis, the long third line as the hull or the submerged bulk of the iceberg, with more below the surface than above. The single sustained rhyme makes each stanza close with a heavy, inevitable click. The poem also divides in two: stanzas I–V show the wreck on the sea-floor; stanzas VI–XI turn back in time to the parallel 'growth' of ship and ice, a structural convergence that mirrors the subject. Some readers have also made something of the odd number of stanzas (eleven), though this is interpretive rather than documented.

    'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  4. the Pride of Life l. 3

    The capitalised phrase quotes the King James Bible: 'For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world' (1 John 2:16). Hardy makes it almost an allegorical person, the vainglory that 'planned her'. The biblical frame governs stanzas I–V, which itemise worldly vanities (luxury fittings, mirrors, jewels) now rendered useless. Note, though, that Hardy does not adopt the Epistle's theology: the agent of retribution later in the poem is not a judging God but the impersonal Immanent Will.

    Bible (KJV), 1 John 2:16; Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  5. stilly couches l. 3

    'Stilly' (adverb, 'silently, calmly') is an old poetic word, familiar from Thomas Moore's 'Oft in the stilly night'. 'Couch' as an intransitive verb means to lie down or lie hidden, used of beasts in a den and of sleepers or the dead. The inverted syntax ('couches she') and the feminine pronoun conventional for ships prepare for the poem's later treatment of the liner as a bride. The calm verb also contrasts ironically with the violence of the sinking, which the poem never describes.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  6. salamandrine fires l. 5

    Ancient natural history held that the salamander could live in, or even quench, fire (Aristotle, Historia animalium 5.19; Pliny, Natural History 10.86). Hardy's adjective makes the stokeholds of the steamship a home of elemental fire, now drowned in its opposite element: the 'pyres' of the boilers are 'late' (former) and cold currents pass through them. The word 'pyres' also hints at funeral fires, a quiet reminder of the dead the poem otherwise leaves unmentioned.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Pliny the Elder, Natural History 10.86 (Bohn numbering; the salamander and fire)

  7. thrid l. 6

    'Thrid' is an old by-form of the verb 'thread', meaning to pass through a narrow or intricate way; the OED records it chiefly in literary use from the sixteenth century on. The chambers once full of fire are now threaded by currents that 'turn to rhythmic tidal lyres', that is, the flowing water plays on the hull's openings like wind on the strings of a lyre (an Aeolian harp). The music image is eerie: the only song for the dead is mechanical and tidal.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  8. mirrors meant to glass the opulent ll. 7–8

    'To glass' (to mirror, reflect an image) is a poetic verb. The first-class saloons were famous for their mirrors and fittings; now 'the sea-worm crawls' over them. The descending string 'grotesque, slimed, dumb, indifferent' moves from the worm's appearance to its moral blankness: 'indifferent' is the key word, for the creature, like the universe of the poem, neither gloats nor pities. The stanza is a version of the vanitas tradition, in which luxury objects are shown overtaken by decay.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  9. bleared l. 12

    To 'blear' is to dim the eyes with rheum or to blur. The jewels, made 'to ravish the sensuous mind' (to transport with delight), lie 'lightless': without light to refract they lose their sparkle. The alliterating triad 'bleared and black and blind' personifies the stones as blinded eyes, preparing for the 'moon-eyed fishes' who gaze back in the next stanza. 'Ravish' carries its older senses of seize and enrapture, with sexual overtones that anticipate the later marriage conceit.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  10. moon-eyed fishes l. 13

    'Moon-eyed' means having large, round, staring eyes (and, in an older sense, dim-sighted). The fishes 'gaze at the gilded gear' (gilt fittings) with uncomprehending curiosity, and Hardy gives them a question in deliberately awkward, abstract diction. The comic incongruity of fish speaking Latinate English is part of the satire: human splendour is meaningless to the creatures of the deep. The stanza ends with a printed ellipsis, marking the turn of the poem from present wreck to past history.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  11. vaingloriousness l. 15

    'Vainglory' (Latin vana gloria, empty glory) is excessive or unwarranted pride in one's achievements, a sin in the medieval scheme closely related to the 'pride of life'. The five-syllable form 'vaingloriousness' is cumbersome on purpose, sitting oddly in the fishes' mouths and making the sentence sound like a moralist's verdict delivered by the wrong speakers. According to the CPRW essay, this fishes' stanza was inserted after the poem's first printing, which ran to thirty lines. Hardy here comes nearest to the sermon on vanity that some critics, such as Donald Davie, find in the poem (Davie reads it as a condemnation of the liner's luxury); others stress that the poem's real subject is cosmic indifference rather than moral punishment.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  12. Well: while was fashioning l. 16

    Stanza VI pivots the poem. After the fishes' question, the speaker answers in a conversational tone ('Well:') and goes back to the period of building in Belfast (the ship was constructed by Harland and Wolff, 1909–12). The syntax is elliptical: 'while [she] was fashioning', or 'while [the Will] was fashioning', the passive-active ambiguity typical of Hardy's crabbed style. The long sentence that begins here runs over three stanzas, the syntax itself enacting the slow, unseen preparation of the meeting. 'Creature of cleaving wing' figures the ship as a bird cutting the air or sea.

    Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  13. The Immanent Will l. 18

    In The Dynasts (1904–08), Hardy's epic-drama of the Napoleonic wars, the 'Immanent Will' is the unconscious motive power within all things, working without purpose, foresight or feeling; it never speaks, while Spirits (including the Spirit of the Years) comment on its workings. 'Immanent' (Latin immanere, to remain within) distinguishes this force from a transcendent God who acts from outside creation. Hardy's philosophy drew on Schopenhauer's Will and on contemporary science. In this poem the Will 'stirs and urges everything', so that the disaster is neither divine punishment nor pure accident but the working-out of an indifferent process. Critics divide over whether this undercuts the moral reading of stanzas I–V or completes it.

    Thomas Hardy, The Dynasts (1904–08), Fore Scene and passim, Project Gutenberg text; Thomas Hardy Society, 'The Dynasts Study Day' (on the Immanent Will and the Spirits); 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)

  14. sinister mate l. 19

    The central conceit of the second half is a perverse wedding. 'Mate' suggests a spouse; 'sinister' means ominous, and in heraldry 'on the left' (Latin sinister), perhaps a hint of a left-handed, ill-omened match. 'So gaily great' gives the ship a bride's festive splendour. The Iceberg grows 'in shadowy silent distance' as the ship grows 'in stature, grace, and hue', like two partners maturing for an arranged union. 'A Shape of Ice' is deliberately vague and capitalised, almost a spectral figure. 'Dissociate' (separated) contrasts with the 'intimate welding' to come.

    'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  15. smart l. 22

    In 1912 'smart' was a fashionable word for stylish, socially elevated (the 'smart set' who sailed first class). It also retains its original senses, brisk and quick, and, as noun and verb, sharp pain. The colloquial adjective brings the social satire of stanzas I–V into the account of the ship's growth. The paired verbs 'grew … grew' set the ship's construction and the iceberg's formation in exact parallel across a single stanza, the structural core of the poem's design.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  16. intimate welding l. 27

    'Welding' (uniting metal pieces by heat and pressure) suits a steel ship, and the Titanic was in fact riveted rather than welded, so the word is figurative. 'Intimate' adds the sense of close personal, even sexual, union. The phrase 'their later history' treats ship and iceberg as characters in a novel whose plots are converging unseen, a typically Hardyan view of coincidence. No 'mortal eye' could see it: only a perspective outside time, like that of the Spirits in The Dynasts, perceives the pattern.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  17. anon … august event l. 30

    'Anon' is an archaism for 'soon' or 'presently'. 'August' (Latin augustus, venerable) gives the collision the dignity of a solemn ceremony, continuing the wedding conceit (a royal or august marriage) while ironically elevating a catastrophe. 'Bent … on' means set upon, determined toward, as if ship and iceberg had intentions; 'paths coincident' uses the geometric sense of coincident (occupying the same place), so that what looks like accident becomes an exact meeting of lines plotted in advance. The irony is that the human builders meant an 'august' maiden voyage, and the Will delivered a different august event.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Samuel Hynes, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Thomas Hardy, vol. 2 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984), text and notes for the poem

  18. the Spinner of the Years l. 31

    In Greek myth the three Moirai (Fates) spin, measure and cut each mortal's thread of life; Clotho, the Spinner, is their best-known emblem. Hardy fuses this image with the Spirit of the Years from The Dynasts, the oldest and most detached of the commenting Spirits. The Spinner's single word 'Now!' is the poem's climax: an act of timing rather than of judgement, which supports the reading that the disaster is fated rather than punitive. The sudden direct speech after so much slow syntax enacts the instant of impact.

    Thomas Hardy, The Dynasts (1904–08), Fore Scene and passim, Project Gutenberg text; 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie); Hesiod, Theogony 217–19, 904–06 (the Moirai: Clotho, Lachesis, Atropos)

  19. consummation … jars two hemispheres l. 33

    'Consummation' completes the marriage conceit: a marriage is legally consummated by sexual union. The word also means completion or end, and it echoes Christ's last word in the Vulgate, 'consummatum est' ('It is finished', John 19:30), and Hamlet's death as 'a consummation devoutly to be wished'. 'Jars two hemispheres' works at several levels: the physical shock of the impact; the news shaking both the Old World and the New, since the ship sailed from Southampton for New York; and perhaps the two halves of a single sphere brought together. Readers disagree whether the ending is a tragic recognition or a sardonic joke at the expense of human pride.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Bible (KJV), John 19:30; 'The Convergence of the Twain', Wikipedia (summarising Childs, Baldick and Davie)
