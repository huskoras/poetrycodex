Religion

Splendidis longum valedico Nugis

by Sir Philip Sidney 1554–1586

From The Oxford Book of English Verse (1900), ed. A. T. Quiller-Couch

Religion

Leave me, O Love, which reachest but to dust,
And thou, my mind, aspire to higher things!
Grow rich in that which never taketh rust:
Whatever fades, but fading pleasure brings.
Draw in thy beams, and humble all thy might
To that sweet yoke where lasting freedoms be;
Which breaks the clouds and opens forth the light
That doth both shine and give us sight to see.
O take fast hold! let that light be thy guide
In this small course which birth draws out to death,
And think how evil becometh him to slide
Who seeketh Heaven, and comes of heavenly breath.
Then farewell, world! thy uttermost I see:
Eternal Love, maintain thy life in me!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Splendidis longum valedico nugis title

    The Latin tag means 'I say a long farewell to glittering trifles'. The poem is Certain Sonnets 32 in Ringler's edition, the last of the miscellaneous sonnets Sidney collected separately from Astrophil and Stella; the group was first printed in the 1598 folio of the Arcadia. Quiller-Couch's Oxford Book of English Verse (1900), the archive's source, uses the Latin phrase as title. Some nineteenth-century readers printed it as if it closed Astrophil and Stella, giving the sequence a moral ending, but Ringler and later editors keep the two apart. The renunciation of love for God is a common Renaissance closing gesture, compare Petrarch's final canzone to the Virgin.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford, 1962), Certain Sonnets 32 and commentary; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), Certain Sonnets 32; The Oxford Book of English Verse, ed. A. T. Quiller-Couch (1900), the text used here

  2. reachest but to dust l. 1

    The love the speaker dismisses is physical, mortal love, whose objects are bodies made of dust and destined to return to it: 'for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19), words echoed in the committal in the burial service of the Book of Common Prayer ('earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust'). The opening sets the poem's governing contrast between what perishes (dust, rust, fading) and what lasts (lasting freedom, light, heaven).

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Book of Common Prayer (1559), Order for the Burial of the Dead; Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, commentary on CS 32

  3. never taketh rust l. 3

    The line echoes Matthew 6:19-20: 'Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt ... But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt.' To 'grow rich' in spiritual goods is the Christian paradox of true wealth. The next line, 'Whatever fades, but fading pleasure brings', turns the point into an aphorism: transient things can give only transient joy.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:19-20; Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, commentary on CS 32; Duncan-Jones, ed., Major Works, note on CS 32

  4. Draw in thy beams l. 5

    The address is to the mind of line 2. Renaissance optics and psychology sometimes imagined sight and attention as beams sent out from the eye or mind; the mind is told to recall those beams from the world and to 'humble all thy might' in submission. The image anticipates the true light of lines 7-8, which comes from God rather than from the self. 'Might' here is power, faculty.

    Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, commentary on CS 32; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'beam'

  5. that sweet yoke l. 6

    The 'sweet yoke' recalls Matthew 11:29-30: 'Take my yoke upon you ... For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.' The paradox that this yoke brings 'lasting freedoms' matches the collect for peace in the Book of Common Prayer, which addresses God 'whose service is perfect freedom'. The contrast is with the bondage of earthly love, a commonplace of the love sonnet in which the lover is Love's slave or prisoner.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:29-30; Book of Common Prayer (1559), Morning Prayer, second collect, for peace; Duncan-Jones, ed., Major Works, note on CS 32

  6. opens forth the light ll. 7-8

    The light of God both shines and 'give[s] us sight to see': it is the object of vision and its precondition. The idea is biblical and Platonic at once: compare Psalm 36:9, 'in thy light shall we see light', and John 1:9 on the true light that lights every man. The image contrasts with the mind's own beams, drawn in at line 5. The clouds it breaks are the obscurities of sense and passion.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 36:9; Bible (KJV), John 1:9; Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, commentary on CS 32

  7. this small course l. 10

    The 'course' is a race or path; life is small because short, and birth 'draws it out', extends it, only as far as death. The image of the Christian life as a race is Pauline (1 Corinthians 9:24; 2 Timothy 4:7, 'I have finished my course'). The command 'take fast hold' in line 9 asks the soul to grip the guiding light as a runner or traveller holds to a rope or guide.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Timothy 4:7; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 9:24; Duncan-Jones, ed., Major Works, note on CS 32

  8. evil becometh him to slide l. 11

    'Evil becometh' means 'ill befits', 'is unbecoming to'; to 'slide' is to slip or fall morally, to backslide. The man of heavenly origin and heavenly aim should not fall into earthly love. 'Comes of heavenly breath' alludes to the creation of man in Genesis 2:7, when God 'breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul'. The soul's divine origin is the ground for its divine destination.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'become'; Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, commentary on CS 32

  9. Eternal Love l. 14

    The sonnet is framed by two addresses to Love. Line 1 dismisses earthly Love, which reaches only to dust; line 14 prays to 'Eternal Love', God, who is called love in 1 John 4:8. The repetition makes the poem a conversion of the word itself. 'Farewell, world! thy uttermost I see' means that the speaker has seen the most the world can offer and found it finite. The sonnet is in the English form (abab cdcd efef gg), with the couplet set off as a prayer.

    Bible (KJV), 1 John 4:8; Ringler, ed., Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, CS 32; Duncan-Jones, ed., Major Works, note on CS 32
