Love

Sonnet 38

by Sir Philip Sidney 1554–1586

Love

COme Sleepe, ô Sleepe, the certaine knot of peace,
The bathing place of wits, the balme of woe,
The poore mans wealth, the prysoners release,
The indifferent Iudge betweene the hie and lowe,
With shielde of proofe, shielde me from out the presse
Of these fierce dartes, Dispayre at me doth throw;
O make in me those ciuill warres to cease:
I will good trybute pay if thou doe soe.
Take thou of me smooth pillowes, sweetest bed,
A chamber deafe of noyse, and blinde of light,
A rosie garland, and a wearie head.
And if these things (as being thine in right)
Mooue not thy heauie grace, thou shalt in mee
(Liuelier then els) rare Stellas Image see.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Come Sleep: Astrophil and Stella 39 l. 1

    In modern editions (Ringler, Duncan-Jones) 'Come sleep, O sleep' is sonnet 39 of Astrophil and Stella, Sidney's sequence of 108 sonnets and 11 songs written in the early 1580s and first printed in 1591, after his death. The archive labels it 'Sonnet 38', so students should cite it as AS 39. The poem belongs to a long tradition of addresses to Sleep, going back through Renaissance Italian and French poets to Ovid's Somnus in Metamorphoses 11 and Statius' Silvae 5.4. Astrophil, kept awake by love for Stella, bargains with Sleep as if with a lord, offering tribute in exchange for peace.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39; Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.623-25 (address to Somnus); Statius, Silvae 5.4 ('Crimine quo merui')

  2. the certaine knot of peace ... ll. 1-4

    The quatrain is a list of appositions in the manner of a hymn or invocation, a figure that Ovid's address to Somnus ('rest of all things, gentlest of the gods, peace of the mind') had already modelled. Sleep is the 'knot' that ties peace fast; the 'bathing place of wits', where the mind is refreshed; the 'balme of woe', a healing ointment; the poor man's wealth and the prisoner's release, because in sleep both forget their condition. Shakespeare's Macbeth later gathers similar phrases about sleep that 'knits up the ravell'd sleave of care', and editors often set the two side by side.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.623-25; Shakespeare, Macbeth 2.2 ('Sleep that knits up the ravell'd sleave of care')

  3. wits l. 2

    In Elizabethan usage 'wits' commonly means the mental powers, the faculties of perception, memory and judgement (as in the 'five wits'). The phrase 'bathing place of wits' therefore pictures sleep as a pool where the tired mind is washed and refreshed, not as a resort for clever people. The modern sense of quick humour was available but is not primary here.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wit'; The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39

  4. indifferent Iudge l. 4

    'Indifferent' in its older sense means unbiased, even-handed, not unconcerned. Sleep, like death, is a leveller that makes no distinction between 'the hie and lowe'. The line is metrically long: it can be brought down to ten syllables only with heavy elision ('th'indifferent'), and it is often read as a line of six stresses. 'Iudge' is the printing convention of the period, in which i stands for j. The idea of sleep as a fair judge who gives the beggar the same rest as the king was a commonplace, and it prepares the political vocabulary of the following lines, in which Sleep becomes a ruler to whom tribute is paid.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'indifferent'; The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39

  5. shielde of proofe l. 5

    Armour 'of proof' was armour tested, typically by firing at it, and shown to resist weapons. Astrophil asks Sleep to shield him 'from out the presse', the press or crush of battle, against the 'fierce dartes' that Despair throws at him. The wordplay 'shielde ... shielde' turns noun into verb. The military language prepares for the 'ciuill warres' of line 7 and the 'trybute' of line 8, so that Sleep becomes a protecting overlord.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'proof'; The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39

  6. ciuill warres l. 7

    The civil wars are within Astrophil: the struggle of reason, virtue and duty against desire which runs through the whole sequence (compare AS 4, 'Virtue, alas, now let me take some rest', and AS 71's contest of virtue and desire). The image was charged in Sidney's period, when civil war was the great political fear, and it makes the sleepless lover a divided kingdom. In return for peace he promises 'good trybute', the payment a vassal state made to a protector.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39

  7. Take thou of me smooth pillowes ll. 9-11

    The third quatrain spells out the tribute in comic, concrete detail. A 'chamber deafe of noyse, and blinde of light' gives the room the senses it lacks; the 'rosie garland' may recall the classical association of roses with sleep and with secrecy, and has been read as a token of festivity. The final item, 'a wearie head', is the lover himself. The shift from high allegory to domestic furniture is characteristic of Sidney's wit and keeps the invocation from becoming solemn.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39

  8. heauie grace l. 13

    To 'move' Sleep's 'heavy grace' is to persuade a drowsy lord. 'Heavy' plays on the heaviness of sleep and on the weightiness of a great person's favour; 'grace' is the courtly title used to a duke or prince ('your Grace'). The phrase 'as being thine in right' in the line before means that the offered goods belong to Sleep by right already, so they are hardly a bribe; hence the sweetener of the couplet.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'heavy'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grace'

  9. rare Stellas Image l. 14

    The couplet is the sonnet's turn. If ordinary comforts do not persuade Sleep, Astrophil offers the best thing he has: Stella's image, imprinted in him and 'livelier then els', more vivid than anywhere else. The compliment is double-edged. Critics point out that the very image that would tempt Sleep is what keeps Astrophil awake, so the prayer undoes itself; others read it as a hope that sleep will bring dreams of Stella. 'Liuelier' also suggests 'more lifelike', as of a portrait. Either way, the poem ends in the obsession it sought rest from.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39

  10. Form: a Sidneian sonnet ll. 1-14

    Sidney's sonnets vary their rhyme schemes. This one keeps two rhymes through the octave (peace, release, press, cease; woe, lowe, throw, soe) in alternating order, and then follows with a quatrain and couplet (bed, light, head, right; mee, see). The structure thus combines Italian economy in the octave with an English close. The couplet's punchline is typical of Astrophil and Stella, where wit is saved for the last two lines.

    The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney, ed. William A. Ringler, Jr. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 39; Sir Philip Sidney, The Major Works, ed. Katherine Duncan-Jones (Oxford World's Classics), notes to Astrophil and Stella 39
