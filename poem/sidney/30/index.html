Love

Sonnet 31

by Sir Philip Sidney 1554–1586

Love

WIth how sad steps ô Moone thou clim'st the skyes,
How silently, and with how meane a face,
What may it be, that euen in heauenly place,
That busie Archer his sharpe Arrowes tryes?
Sure if that long with loue acquainted eyes
Can iudge of loue, thou feelst of Louers case,
I reade within thy lookes thy languisht grace.
To mee that feele the like, my state discries.
Then euen of fellowship ô Moone tell me,
Is constant loue deemde there but want of wit?
Are beauties there, as proude as heere there be?
Doe they aboue, loue to be lou'd, and yet
Those Louers scorne, whom that loue doth possesse?
Doe they call vertue there vngratefulnesse?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Astrophil and Stella title; l. 1

    Sidney (1554-1586) probably wrote the 108 sonnets and 11 songs of the sequence in 1581-82. They circulated in manuscript and were printed only after his death, in 1591, which started the fashion for sonnet sequences in the 1590s. Stella is usually linked with Penelope Devereux, who married Lord Rich in 1581, and several sonnets pun on the name 'Rich'. That identification is one reading of the sequence, though, not a key to it. The archive keeps the early spelling ('Moone', 'skyes', u for v), and the capitals 'WI' reproduce the printed drop capital. Sonnet 31 is one of the most admired poems in the sequence.

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'

  2. The weary moon l. 1

    The poem turns astronomy into a love story, attributing human feeling to nature (what Ruskin later called the pathetic fallacy). The moon's slow 'sad steps' and its 'meane' (humble, wan) face are signs of melancholy. The moon is also Diana or Cynthia, goddess of chastity, so it is ironic that Cupid should trouble her. Wordsworth took Sidney's opening line, slightly changed, as the first line of a sonnet in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807). Philip Larkin's 'Sad Steps' (in High Windows, 1974) answers it with modern scepticism.

    Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'; William Wordsworth, Poems, in Two Volumes (1807), sonnet 'With how sad steps, O Moon, thou climb'st the sky'; William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31

  3. meane l. 2

    In early modern English 'mean' could mean humble in rank, poor, or dejected. Used of a face it suggests paleness and lowered eyes, the conventional look of a pining lover. 'How silently' adds the picture of someone who suffers without complaining aloud. The second line repeats the 'how' of the first ('With how sad steps ... How silently ... how meane'), so the octave opens with a run of exclamations.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mean, adj.1'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31

  4. The busy archer l. 4

    Lines 3-4 ask whether Cupid practises ('tryes') his arrows even in 'heavenly place'. 'Busie' means active and meddling. The joke works two ways: Cupid has invaded chaste Diana's realm, and the lover's misery is so universal that the heavens themselves share it. Cupid's arrows come from classical poetry (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1), and Petrarch and his followers made them central to the love lyric.

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-73 (Cupid's two arrows); Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'

  5. acquainted eyes l. 5

    Astrophil sets himself up as an expert in the symptoms of love. 'Case' (l. 6) means both a condition and a legal or medical case. The confident 'Sure' opens an argument from experience that is half mock-logic. The sonnet's wit lies in this mix of tender sympathy and self-regarding cleverness. His claim to be an expert also works against him: a man who knows love so well knows from long experience that it brings misery.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31; William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31

  6. languisht grace l. 7

    'Languish' is the standard word in writing on love-melancholy for wasting away from desire. 'I reade within thy lookes' makes the moon's face a text to be interpreted. Line 8 is compressed: the moon's languishing grace reveals ('discries') a state to the speaker, who feels the same thing. Some readers take 'my state' to mean his own state, so that the moon's face becomes his mirror.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'languish, v.'; William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31

  7. discries l. 8

    One paraphrase is 'to me, who feel the same, your languished grace reveals your condition'. Another is 'to me ... it reveals my own condition', with the moon as his mirror. Both readings support the octave's turn from watching the moon to fellow-feeling with it, and that prepares for 'fellowship' at the start of the sestet. Whichever way it is read, the line makes the speaker's own suffering the key that lets him read the moon.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'descry, v.1'; William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31

  8. Form and volta l. 9

    The rhymes run skyes/face/place/tryes/eyes/case/grace/discries, then me/wit/be/yet/possesse/vngratefulnesse. This sonnet is in iambic pentameter (some of Sidney's are in hexameters), and the mixed rhyme scheme is common in the sequence. The octave sympathizes. In the sestet the speaker asks the moon, for fellowship's sake, whether heaven is like earth, and the questions move from the moon to an indirect accusation of Stella.

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'

  9. of fellowship l. 9

    The questions that follow picture heaven as a court like Elizabeth's, full of proud beauties and despised lovers. When 'constant loue' is 'deemde ... want of wit' (l. 10), faithfulness is mocked as folly, and that is Astrophil's charge against Stella's world. 'Wit' means intelligence or good sense. The courtly world of the sestet values clever, changeable lovers over constant ones, and Astrophil, who is constant, feels he is mocked as a fool for it. The courtly scene also reflects Sidney's own uneasy career at court.

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31

  10. Proud beauties l. 11

    In Petrarchan verse 'proud' means scornful and pleased with oneself; it is the stock fault of the cruel mistress. By asking whether heaven's beauties are 'as proude as heere', Astrophil turns the cosmic conceit into a satire on his own lady. Line 11 is also a comparison of two places, 'there' and 'heere', and the repeated adverbs keep heaven and earth side by side through the sestet. The answer the speaker expects is plain: the heavens are just as cruel as the court, and Stella just as proud.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'

  11. love to be loved, and yet scorn ll. 12-13

    The lines describe a beauty who wants to be admired but despises the people who admire her. She 'loves to be loved' because it feeds her vanity. The line break between 'and yet' and 'Those Louers scorne' acts out the reversal. 'Possesse' suggests both ownership and being mastered: the lover is taken over by love. The paradox is a stock charge against the Petrarchan mistress, but Sidney gives it a sharp, almost logical form.

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31

  12. Virtue and ungratefulness l. 14

    The syntax is ambiguous, and readers have argued over it. The usual reading (Ringler and others) takes 'ungratefulness' as the thing being renamed: in heaven, as on earth, is the lady's refusal to reward love called 'virtue'? Some readers take the words in their plain order instead: do they call virtue (the lover's constancy, or chastity itself) ungratefulness? The first reading sharpens the satire on the lady's chaste disdain. The second shows Astrophil's unease that what he resents in Stella really is virtue, a tension that runs through the whole sequence (compare the last line of Sonnet 71, 'But ah, Desire still cries, give me some food').

    William A. Ringler Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 31; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford Authors, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 31; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 31'
