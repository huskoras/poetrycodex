Love

Sonnet 1

by Sir Philip Sidney 1554–1586

Love

LOuing in trueth, and fayne my loue in verse to show,
That the deere Shee, might take some pleasure of my paine:
Pleasure might cause her reade, reading might make her know,
Knowledge might pittie winne, and pittie grace obtaine.
I sought fit wordes, to paint the blackest face of woe,
Studying inuentions fine, her wittes to entertaine,
Oft turning others leaues, to see if thence would flowe,
Some fresh and fruitfull showre, vpon my Sunne-burnt braine.
But wordes came halting out, wanting inuentions stay,
Inuention Natures childe, fledde Stepdames studies blowes:
And others feete, still seem'de but straungers in my way,
Thus great with Childe to speake, and helplesse in my throwes,
Byting my tongue and penne, beating my selfe for spite:
Foole saide My muse to mee, looke in thy heart and write.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Astrophil and Stella 1 l. 1

    Astrophil and Stella, 108 sonnets and eleven songs, was written in the early 1580s and circulated in manuscript; it was printed after Sidney's death in two unauthorised quartos of 1591 and in the 1598 folio of his works. Astrophil ('star-lover', Greek aster + philos) loves Stella ('star', Latin), widely identified with Penelope Devereux, who married Lord Rich in 1581. The archive's text keeps early printed spelling ('trueth', 'inuentions'). This first sonnet is a programmatic poem about how to write love poetry: it moves from a chain of hopes through failed imitation to the Muse's command to look within. Ringler's edition remains the standard text and commentary.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Poetry Foundation, text of 'Astrophil and Stella 1: Loving in truth, and fain in verse my love to show' (modern spelling, for comparison)

  2. Form: alexandrine sonnet ll. 1-14

    Unlike most sonnets in the sequence, this one is in iambic hexameters (alexandrines), lines of six stresses and twelve syllables, which give it a long, deliberate movement suited to the rhetorical chain of lines 1-4. The octave uses only two rhymes in alternation (abababab), followed by cdcd and a closing couplet ee, a hybrid of Italian and English patterns. The long lines also make metre itself a subject: the poem goes on to complain of 'others feete' and of words that come 'halting'.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Poetry Foundation, text of 'Astrophil and Stella 1: Loving in truth, and fain in verse my love to show' (modern spelling, for comparison)

  3. fayne (fain) l. 1

    'Fain' (OED, fain, adj. and adv.) means gladly, willingly, eager. Many readers hear a pun on 'feign', to pretend or invent, which sets the sincerity of 'Louing in trueth' against the artifice of verse. The pun anticipates the sonnet's tension between truth and fiction, and Sidney's argument in the Defence that the poet 'nothing affirmeth, and therefore never lieth'. The spelling 'fayne' in the archive's text could represent either word.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fain, adj. and adv.'; William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text)

  4. the deere Shee l. 2

    Using 'Shee' as a noun ('the dear she') was an Elizabethan idiom for 'the beloved woman'. The paradox 'pleasure of my paine' is typically Petrarchan: the lover's suffering, made into art, is offered as the lady's entertainment. Stella is not named until later in the sequence; the opening keeps her at a distance, as the object of a rhetorical campaign.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1

  5. Pleasure ... grace: the gradatio ll. 2-4

    Lines 2-4 use the figure the Greeks called climax and the Latins gradatio, in which the final word of one clause becomes the first of the next: pleasure leads to reading, reading to knowing, knowledge to pity, pity to grace. Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie (1589) calls it 'the Marching figure' or 'the clyming figure', since 'Clymax' means a ladder. Sidney uses it to display the lover's calculated hopes as a chain of causes, a piece of reasoning that the rest of the poem shows to be ineffective. 'Grace' means the lady's favour, with an overtone of divine grace.

    George Puttenham, The Arte of English Poesie (1589), Book 3, ch. 19, 'Clymax, or the Marching figure' (Project Gutenberg text); William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1

  6. pittie ... grace l. 4

    In the vocabulary of courtly love, the lady's 'pity' (mercy, compassion) is what the suffering lover hopes to arouse, and her 'grace' is the favour she grants. Both terms are also theological, and Sidney's sequence repeatedly plays on the overlap between devotion to Stella and religious devotion. The progression ends at the highest good the lover can hope for, which makes the collapse of his efforts in the following lines more marked.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grace, n.'

  7. to paint the blackest face of woe l. 5

    'Fit wordes' recalls the rhetorical principle of decorum, the suiting of style to matter. 'Paint' draws on the Renaissance commonplace, from Horace's ut pictura poesis, that poetry is a speaking picture; Sidney's Defence defines poetry as 'a speaking picture'. To paint the 'blackest face' of woe is to represent sorrow in its darkest, most moving form, so as to win pity. The verb also hints at cosmetic artifice, which the sonnet will reject.

    Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text); William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1

  8. inuentions l. 6

    In classical and Renaissance rhetoric, inventio, the first of the five parts of rhetoric, is the discovery of material and arguments (Latin invenire, to find). Here Astrophil studies other writers' ingenious inventions to 'entertain' Stella's wits, that is, imitation of models. At line 10 'Inuention' is personified as Nature's child, contrasting true invention arising from nature with borrowed, studied matter. The word 'fine' suggests elegance with a hint of over-refinement.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'invention, n.'

  9. turning others leaues l. 7

    'Leaves' are pages, and the image suggests a poet thumbing Petrarch and his imitators for material. The botanical sense of 'leaves' then generates the next lines' image of a 'fresh and fruitfull showre' falling on a 'Sunne-burnt braine': books are imagined as trees or clouds that might water a dry mind. The conceit mocks derivative love poetry, a target Sidney also attacks in the Defence.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text)

  10. Sunne-burnt braine l. 8

    The image of a parched brain longing for rain extends the metaphor of fertility; the heat might be that of love, or of the scorching influence of other writers. Since Stella is a star and later sonnets compare her to the sun, some readers see a hint that the beloved herself has dried his invention. In humoral physiology dryness was associated with melancholy and choler, and melancholy was the lover's disease.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1

  11. halting l. 9

    To 'halt' (OED, halt, v.1) is to limp; the word looks forward to 'others feete' in line 11, so that verse with faltering metre becomes a lame walker. The pun on metrical feet and walking was common in Renaissance discussions of verse. 'Wanting inuentions stay' means lacking the support (stay, a prop or brace) that invention would give, so the words cannot stand or walk properly.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'halt, v.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stay, n.2'; William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1

  12. Inuention Natures childe, fledde Stepdames studies blowes l. 10

    Sidney allegorises a debate central to Renaissance poetics, between nature (natural talent, ingenium) and art (learning, study, imitation). Invention is Nature's offspring; Study is a stepmother, proverbially cruel, whose beatings drive the child away. The line does not wholly reject learning (Sidney was a deeply learned poet) but argues that study without native invention produces lifeless verse. It anticipates the Muse's advice to look inward, while also being itself a highly studied figure.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text)

  13. others feete l. 11

    The phrase puns on metrical feet and on footsteps, so that other poets' verses, followed as models, become strangers who block his way. It continues the walking image from 'halting' and connects to the debate over imitation: following another writer's steps produces lines that do not belong to the poet. Ringler and Duncan-Jones gloss the pun in their notes. The image also anticipates Astrophil's later scorn, in sonnets 3, 6 and 15, for poets who borrow Petrarch's or Pindar's manner rather than finding their own.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1

  14. great with Childe ... my throwes l. 12

    The childbirth metaphor for poetic creation is ancient (Plato's Symposium speaks of those pregnant in soul), and Sidney extends the family imagery of line 10. 'Throwes' are throes, the pangs of labour. The image is striking for a male poet and makes poetic composition a bodily ordeal. It sets up the final line, in which the Muse acts as a kind of midwife by telling him where the child is to be found.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'throe, n.'

  15. Byting my tongue and penne l. 13

    The gesture of self-punishment echoes a classical picture of the struggling writer. Persius (Satire 1.106) mocks bad verse that has never made its author pound his desk or taste his bitten nails, and Gildersleeve's commentary compares Horace (Satires 1.10.70), where the true poet scratches his head and gnaws his nails to the quick. Sidney's version turns the gesture into comedy: the poet bites his pen and his tongue, and beats himself 'for spite', that is, in vexation.

    Persius, Satire 1.106 ('nec pluteum caedit nec demorsos sapit unguis'), with Gildersleeve's note comparing Horace, Satires 1.10.70 (ed. B. L. Gildersleeve, 1875, Project Gutenberg); William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1

  16. looke in thy heart and write l. 14

    The Muse's rebuke ('Foole') and her instruction reverse the poem's laborious search: the matter lies within the poet. It is often read as a manifesto of sincerity and connects with the Defence, where Sidney complains that love poets 'coldly ... apply fiery speeches' and lack the 'forcibleness, or energia' of true feeling. But the line has been read more subtly: in Petrarchan convention the beloved's image is engraved in the lover's heart, so to look in his heart is to look at Stella, the true source of invention. Since the line closes a highly artful sonnet, many critics also see irony in its apparent rejection of art. 'Heart' also included thought and understanding in Renaissance usage.

    Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text); William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Neil L. Rudenstine, Sidney's Poetic Development (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1967), on energia and the opening sonnets of Astrophil and Stella; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Sir Philip Sidney (The Oxford Authors; Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), notes to Astrophil and Stella 1

  17. Foole saide My muse to mee l. 14

    The Muses were the nine daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne who inspire the arts. Here the poet's Muse intervenes as a speaking character, interrupting his self-torment with a blunt 'Foole'. The sudden shift from narrative to dramatic speech in the last line gives the sonnet a comic volta in the closing couplet. The archive text's capitalised 'My' and lack of quotation marks reflect early printed punctuation; modern editions punctuate the Muse's words as direct speech.

    William A. Ringler, Jr., ed., The Poems of Sir Philip Sidney (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962), commentary on Astrophil and Stella 1; Poetry Foundation, text of 'Astrophil and Stella 1: Loving in truth, and fain in verse my love to show' (modern spelling, for comparison)
