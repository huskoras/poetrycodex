Love

Idea (1619), Sonnet 61

by Michael Drayton 1563–1631

From Idea (1619)

Love

Since there 's no helpe, Come let vs kisse and part,
Nay, I haue done: You get no more of Me,
And I am glad, yea glad withall my heart,
That thus so cleanly, I my Selfe can free,
Shake hands for euer, Cancell all our Vowes,
And when we meet at any time againe,
Be it not scene in either of our Browes,
That We one iot of former Loue reteyne;
Now at the last gaspe of Loues latest Breath,
When his Pulse fayling, Passion speechlesse lies,
When Faith is kneeling by his bed of Death,
And Innocence is closing vp his Eyes,
Now if thou would'st, when all haue giuen him ouer,
From Death to Life, thou might'st him yet recouer.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its date l. 1

    Drayton's sonnet sequence first came out as Ideas Mirrour (1594) and was reprinted and heavily revised many times; this sonnet is not in the early versions. Martha Foote Crow's edition, based on the 1619 text, states that the famous sixty-first appears for the first time in 1619, so it is the work of a poet in his fifties looking back on the Petrarchan mode rather than a young sonneteer's exercise. The title 'Idea' is Platonic (the ideal form of the beloved); the sequence is often linked to Anne Goodere, daughter of Drayton's patron, but this poem names no one. The archive keeps the old spelling of the 1619 print, with u for v and v for u ('helpe', 'vs', 'haue') and capitalised key nouns. Claims online that the sonnet dates from 1594 confuse the sequence with the poem.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Michael Drayton, Poems (London, 1619), Idea, sonnet 61 (copy-text of Crow's edition)

  2. kiss and part l. 1

    The line sounds like ordinary speech; there is none of the elaborate address that opens most Elizabethan sonnets. A parting kiss was a customary farewell gesture, and here it seals what the speaker presents as a mutual and final settlement. The monosyllables and the strong pause after 'helpe' give the line the tone of someone cutting short an argument. Readers have long admired the way the octave mimics brusque, manly resolve, while the extra emphasis ('yea glad', l. 3) quietly gives that resolve away: a man who protests that he is glad is perhaps not glad. The whole first eight lines work as a performance of indifference that the last six undo.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "part, v."

  3. Nay, I have done l. 2

    'Nay' implies an interlocutor: the poem reads like one side of a conversation already in progress. 'I have done' means 'I have finished (with this, with you)', and 'You get no more of Me' reverses the usual sonnet situation, in which the lover begs for favours; here he refuses to give any more of himself. This dramatic, speaking voice is often compared with Donne's lyrics and with Shakespeare's late sonnets, and it is one reason the poem has seemed modern to many readers.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "do, v. (to have done)"

  4. glad with all my heart l. 3

    'Withall' is the 1619 spelling of 'with all'. The doubled 'glad' can be taken straight (the speaker really is relieved to be free) or as overemphasis that betrays the opposite feeling. Most modern readers take the second view, because the plea of the couplet shows that the freedom of line 4 ('so cleanly, I my Selfe can free') was never wanted. 'Cleanly' means neatly, without mess or residue, and perhaps also with a clear conscience. The tension between bluff octave and pleading sestet is the poem's main effect.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "cleanly, adv."

  5. Shake hands ... cancel all our vows l. 5

    To 'shake hands for ever' is to part on agreed terms, as partners do when dissolving a contract. 'Cancel' comes from Latin cancelli, the lattice of crossed lines drawn through a deed to void it, so the lovers' vows are imagined as documents struck through. The language treats love as a mutual covenant that can be ended by consent, which makes the mythologised deathbed of the sestet all the more striking: what the octave treats as a cancelled contract, the sestet treats as a dying person.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "cancel, v."; Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI

  6. Be it not seen ... in either of our brows l. 7

    The archive text reads 'scene', apparently a transcription slip for 'seene', the 1619 spelling of 'seen'; the sense is 'let it not be seen'. The brow was the conventional place where feeling could be read in a face (compare 'the brow of care'). The speaker is planning their public behaviour: when they meet again, neither should betray 'one jot' of the former love. The concern with appearances suits the poem's social, almost legal framing of the separation.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "brow, n."

  7. one jot l. 8

    Old printing did not distinguish i and j, so 'iot' is 'jot'. The word comes through Latin from Greek iota, the smallest letter of the alphabet, and the idiom was made familiar by Christ's saying that not 'one jot or one tittle' of the law shall pass away. The speaker's claim that no jot of love remains is therefore a claim to complete erasure, which the sestet will reverse.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "jot, n."; Bible (KJV), Matthew 5:18

  8. The turn (line 9) l. 9

    Drayton uses the English (Shakespearean) rhyme scheme, three quatrains and a couplet, but the thought divides like an Italian sonnet, eight lines and six. 'Now' at line 9 marks the shift in tone from the plain speech of the octave to an elevated, slow, ceremonial allegory. A second 'Now' at line 13 marks a further turn, so the poem effectively has two voltas, the last one giving the couplet its reversal. The doubled superlative 'last gaspe of Loues latest Breath' slows the line with heavy stresses, imitating laboured breathing.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "volta, n."

  9. The deathbed of Love ll. 10-12

    The sestet stages a deathbed scene. Love's pulse is failing; Passion lies speechless; Faith kneels in prayer by the bed; Innocence performs the last office of closing the dead man's eyes. These personified abstractions recall the allegorical figures of the morality plays and of emblem books, and they give the private quarrel the dignity of a ritual. Each figure fits the dying relationship: the passion has gone dumb, fidelity can only pray, and innocence presides over the end. Critics often note that the shift from colloquial speech to allegory is itself the argument: the speaker's language rises, betraying how much the love still means.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "passion, n."

  10. given him over l. 13

    To 'give over' a patient was to stop treating him because death was certain. The phrase continues the deathbed metaphor: everyone, Passion, Faith and Innocence included, has given up on Love. 'Would'st' (wouldst) makes the woman the only possible physician. Note the feminine rhymes 'ouer / recouer' (over / recover), whose falling extra syllable gives the closing couplet a soft, imploring cadence after the hard monosyllables of the opening.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "give, v. (give over)"; Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI

  11. The final couplet l. 14

    'From death to life' has a religious ring (a resurrection, a miracle of revival), and only the woman can perform it. The reversal invites different readings: a sincere change of heart forced by the allegory the speaker himself has conjured; a tactical manoeuvre, in which the show of indifference was always a lover's ploy to provoke a response; or a poised irony in which the speaker knows that both moods are true. The conditional 'if thou would'st' leaves the outcome open. Many anthologists have counted it among the best sonnets of the age precisely because so much is compressed into this last turn.

    Martha Foote Crow, ed., Elizabethan Sonnet Cycles: Idea, Fidessa, Chloris (1896), introduction and text of Idea LXI; Bible (KJV), 1 John 3:14 ("passed from death unto life")
