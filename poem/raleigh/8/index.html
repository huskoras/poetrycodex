Love

The Nymph's Reply to the Passionate Shepherd

by Sir Walter Raleigh 1552–1618

From Lyra Elegantiarum, ed. Frederick Locker (London: E. Moxon, 1867)

Love

If all the world and love were young,
And truth in every shepherd's tongue,
Then pretty pleasures might me move,
To live with thee, and be thy love.

Time drives the flocks from field to fold
When rivers rage, and rocks grow cold,
And Philomel becometh dumb;
The rest complain of cares to come.

The flowers do fade, and wanton fields
To wayward winter reckoning yields;
A honey'd tongue, a heart of gall.
Is fancy's spring, but sorrow's fall.

Thy gown, thy shoes, thy beds of roses.
Thy cap, thy kirtle, and thy posies;
Soon break, soon wither, soon forgotten,
In folly ripe, in reason rotten.

Thy belt of straw, and ivy buds.
Thy coral clasps, and amber studs,
All these in me no means can move,
To come to thee, and be thy love.

But could youth last, and love still breed,
Had joys no date, and age no need;
Then these delights my mind might move.
To live with thee, and be thy love.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Text and attribution l. 1

    The reply first appeared, unsigned except for 'Ignoto' (Latin, 'unknown'), in Englands Helicon (1600), immediately after Marlowe's song; one stanza had already been printed in The Passionate Pilgrim (1599). Izaak Walton, in The Compleat Angler (1653), has a milkmaid's mother sing 'an answer to it, which was made by Sir Walter Raleigh in his younger days', and this ascription has generally been accepted. Rudick's historical edition largely sidesteps questions of authenticity, printing poems in the form in which they were first attributed to Ralegh, and the attribution here rests chiefly on Walton's late report. The archive's text is from Locker's Lyra Elegantiarum (1867).

    Englands Helicon (London, 1600), Bodleian 'Digital Miscellanies' records: the Shepherd song signed 'Chr. Marlow', the reply signed 'Ignoto'; Izaak Walton, The Compleat Angler (1653), ch. 2 of the Project Gutenberg text: the milkmaid sings 'that smooth Song which was made by Kit Marlow' and her mother 'an answer to it, which was made by Sir Walter Raleigh in his younger days' (Project Gutenberg); Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), on the poem and its attribution; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Nymph's Reply' (first printed anonymously in Englands Helicon, 1600; ascribed to Ralegh by Walton, 1653)

  2. If ... Then: the conditional frame ll. 1-4

    The first stanza answers Marlowe's 'An if these pleasures may thee move' with its own condition: if the world and love were young, and every shepherd honest, then his pleasures might persuade her. The final stanza returns to the same structure ('But could youth last ... Then these delights my mind might move'). Since neither condition can be met, the polite conditional is a refusal. The same metre (tetrameter couplets) and refrain wording ('To live with thee, and be thy love') imitate Marlowe closely, so the answer is also a parody.

    Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Jack Lynch, ed., 'The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd', copy-text Englands Helicon (1600)

  3. truth in every shepherd's tongue l. 2

    The line questions the sincerity of the lover and also of pastoral poetry, whose shepherds are often poets in disguise. The nymph implies that the shepherd's promises are rhetoric, an echo of Elizabethan suspicions that poets are liars, which Sidney's Defence of Poesie had answered. The idea recurs at line 11 in 'A honey'd tongue, a heart of gall'. The 'tongue' here anticipates the bodily imagery of tongue and heart later in the poem.

    Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Sidney, The Defence of Poesie (pub. 1595), passage on love poetry and 'energia' (Project Gutenberg text)

  4. Time drives the flocks from field to fold l. 5

    A 'fold' is an enclosure where sheep are penned, especially in winter. The line introduces the poem's central argument: the passing of the seasons and of human life, which Marlowe's song excludes. Time is personified as a drover. The alliteration of 'field' and 'fold' gives the line a settled, proverbial ring. Marlowe's shepherds 'feed their flocks' in an eternal present; the reply sets those flocks in motion under the pressure of the year's turning.

    Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  5. rivers rage, and rocks grow cold l. 6

    The line directly answers Marlowe's second stanza ('we will sit upon the rocks ... By shallow rivers'). In winter, rivers flood and the rocks are no place to sit. The nymph's method is to keep the shepherd's images but supply what the pastoral leaves out: weather, time and change. The alliteration on 'r' adds harshness. The verb 'rage' also implies passion out of control, a hint that love too may turn violent or destructive.

    Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  6. Philomel l. 7

    In Ovid's Metamorphoses (book 6), Philomela is raped by her brother-in-law Tereus, who cuts out her tongue; she is later transformed into a bird, in the Latin tradition the nightingale. 'Philomel' became a poetic name for the nightingale, whose song is associated with love and grief. Nightingales stop singing in late summer, so the line means that birdsong ends; but the myth also introduces violence and silencing into the pastoral, a darker reply to the madrigal-singing birds of Marlowe's poem. 'Dumb' means mute.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.412-674 (Tereus, Procne and Philomela), trans. A. S. Kline; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  7. The rest complain of cares to come l. 8

    'Complain' here means to utter a lament (the 'complaint' was a lyric genre). 'The rest' are the other birds or creatures that remain after the nightingale falls silent. Anticipation of future 'cares' is exactly what Marlowe's song suppresses; the nymph's view looks forward. The phrase reverses the shepherd's promise of 'delights', putting worry about the future where he had placed present pleasure. It is also a reminder that in pastoral tradition the shepherd's life was one of seasonal labour as well as song.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'complain, v.'; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  8. wanton fields l. 9

    'Wanton' (OED, wanton, adj.) meant luxuriant or growing profusely, playful, and also lustful or undisciplined. The fields are lush in summer, but the word also hints at the erotic carelessness of the shepherd's proposal. Both kinds of wantonness must give way to winter. The adjective thus prepares the moral turn of the stanza from natural to human seasons: wanton youth must also yield to winter. Shakespeare uses the word in both the botanical and erotic senses.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wanton, adj.'; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  9. To wayward winter reckoning yields l. 10

    'Reckoning' is a financial term, an account or bill to be settled, and 'yields' means both produces and surrenders. The fertile fields must pay their debt to winter. 'Wayward' means perverse, contrary, unpredictable. The line answers Marlowe's 'yields' (l. 4), where landscape yields only pleasure: here it yields to time. Financial metaphors of reckoning were also used of death and the Last Judgement.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reckoning, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wayward, adj.'; Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus

  10. A honey'd tongue, a heart of gall l. 11

    Gall, bile, was proverbially bitter and associated with rancour; honey with sweet, persuasive speech. The contrast of a honeyed tongue and a bitter heart is proverbial and biblical in flavour (compare Proverbs 5:3-4, where the strange woman's lips drop as a honeycomb but her end is bitter as wormwood). In the stanza it describes the course of passion: sweet in the beginning, bitter in the end.

    Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gall, n.1'

  11. fancy's spring, but sorrow's fall l. 12

    'Fancy' in Elizabethan usage meant love, especially fleeting, imaginative infatuation (OED, fancy, n.). The seasonal pun sets 'spring' against 'fall', autumn, and also suggests a moral or sexual fall. 'Fall' evokes autumn (from 'fall of the leaf'). The line makes the seasonal argument into a moral one: love that begins in pleasure ends in grief. Elizabethan readers would also have recognised the commonplace that love is a springtime folly, a theme the reply shares with much moral verse of the period.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fancy, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fall, n.1'; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem

  12. Point-by-point rebuttal ll. 13-18

    The reply mirrors Marlowe's catalogue of gifts: gown, slippers ('shoes'), beds of roses, cap, kirtle, posies, belt of straw and ivy buds, coral clasps and amber studs. Repetition with the possessive 'thy' hands each object back to the giver. The method, typical of the Elizabethan answer-poem, turns the original into a target, and the near-quotation makes the nymph's wit depend on intimate knowledge of the poem she rejects.

    Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; R. S. Forsythe, 'The Passionate Shepherd and English Poetry', PMLA 40 (1925)

  13. Soon break, soon wither, soon forgotten l. 15

    The threefold 'soon' is anaphora, repetition at the beginning of successive clauses, and the three verbs can be heard as matching kinds of gift: made objects break, flowers wither, and the whole affair is forgotten. The feminine rhyme 'forgotten/rotten' slows the line and gives it a falling cadence. The line also answers the shepherd's implied promise of permanence: his gifts, like his 'May-morning', belong to a single season.

    Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus

  14. In folly ripe, in reason rotten l. 16

    The line's parallel antithesis ('folly ripe' / 'reason rotten') uses the image of fruit that ripens and rots. As tokens of folly they are perfect; judged by reason they are already corrupt. It is perhaps the poem's most quoted line, a sententia (moral maxim) in the manner of Elizabethan wisdom literature, and characteristic of the moralising wit associated with Ralegh's poetry.

    Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus

  15. But could youth last, and love still breed l. 21

    The final stanza returns to the conditional of stanza 1. 'Still' means continually, and 'breed' means increase or flourish, with an echo of the flocks' breeding. The condition is impossible, which is the point, but the tone remains wistful: the nymph does not despise pleasure, only its impermanence. Some critics read the poem as a carpe diem argument turned inside out: the passage of time, usually a reason to seize the day, here becomes a reason to refuse.

    Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem; Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), on the poem and its attribution

  16. date ... need l. 22

    'Date' (OED, date, n.2) in Elizabethan English often meant the limit or end of a period, the term of duration, as in 'death's dateless night' in Shakespeare's Sonnet 30. 'Need' is poverty or want, the hardships of old age. Together they summarise the nymph's argument: pleasures end and old age requires provision. The vocabulary of terms and needs continues the financial metaphor of 'reckoning'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'date, n.2'; Agnes M. C. Latham, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1951), notes on the poem
