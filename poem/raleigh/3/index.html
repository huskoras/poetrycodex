Death & Dying

The Conclusion

by Sir Walter Raleigh 1552–1618

Death & Dying

Even such is Time, that takes in trust
Our youth, our joys, our all we have,
And pays us but with earth and dust;
Who in the dark and silent grave,
When we have wander’d all our ways,
Shuts up the story of our days;
But from this earth, this grave, this dust,
My God shall raise me up, I trust.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The occasion: Ralegh's last verses l. 1

    Sir Walter Ralegh was beheaded at Westminster on 29 October 1618, after the failure of his last voyage to Guiana revived a death sentence passed in 1603. Early copies say these lines were written in his Bible the night before, in the Gatehouse prison at Westminster. Editors of Ralegh's poems (Latham, Rudick) record that the first six lines adapt the last stanza of an earlier Ralegh poem ('Nature, that washed her hands in milk', titled in one copy as on his mistress Serena), with the final couplet added. The poem is thus a love poem's conclusion turned into an epitaph. The archive's title 'The Conclusion' is one of several in early copies.

    The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh, ed. Agnes M. C. Latham (London, 1951), notes; Luminarium: 'Even such is time', Sir Walter Ralegh, with source note

  2. takes in trust l. 1

    To 'take in trust' is to receive property to hold for another. The metaphor is financial and legal: Time takes charge of youth, joys and everything we have, and 'pays us but with earth and dust'. Ralegh, who had lived through spectacular wealth and ruin, makes Time a defaulting steward. The word 'trust' returns at the end of the poem in a new, religious sense, faith in God.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trust'; The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999)

  3. earth and dust l. 3

    The payment Time makes is the grave. The phrase echoes God's sentence on Adam, 'dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19), and the committal in the Book of Common Prayer burial service, 'earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the resurrection'. That hope of resurrection is what the final couplet supplies.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Book of Common Prayer (1559), Order for the Burial of the Dead

  4. Shuts up the story of our days l. 6

    When we have 'wander'd all our ways', travelled every road of life, Time 'shuts up' our story, closes it like a book. The image suits a man who had written The History of the World (1614) in the Tower and wrote these lines in a Bible. 'Shut up' also means to imprison or confine, which recalls Ralegh's long imprisonments. The finality of 'shuts up' is the low point of the poem before the turn of the couplet.

    The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shut'

  5. My God shall raise me up, I trust ll. 7-8

    The final couplet, added to the borrowed stanza, overturns it. The repeated 'this earth, this grave, this dust' points to the very things Time paid us with, and turns them into the place of resurrection. The language recalls Job 19:25-26 ('though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God'), read in the burial service. 'I trust' reverses the corrupt 'trust' of line 1: Time betrays trust, God keeps it. Some early copies read 'The Lord will raise me up'.

    Bible (KJV), Job 19:25-26; The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); Luminarium: 'Even such is time', Sir Walter Ralegh, with source note

  6. Form ll. 1-8

    The rhyme scheme is abab cc dd. The first six lines are the inherited stanza, which in the original poem ended with the couplet 'ways/days'. Ralegh's addition repeats the 'dust/trust' rhyme from lines 1 and 3, so that the new couplet answers the opening rhymes directly. The tetrameter gives the poem the plainness of an epitaph. The triple repetition 'this earth, this grave, this dust' gathers the nouns of the first stanza and points to them, as if the speaker were standing at his own grave.

    The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh, ed. Agnes M. C. Latham (London, 1951), notes

  7. Our youth, our joys, our all l. 2

    The line builds by stages to 'our all we have', a phrase that turns the list into a total. The first-person plural makes the poem general, an epitaph for everyone, until the 'My God' and 'me' of the final couplet make it personal. The financial metaphor of trust continues: these are the goods deposited with Time, and the next lines describe how they are repaid. The language of totality, 'our all', makes the loss seem complete before the couplet reverses it.

    The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh, ed. Agnes M. C. Latham (London, 1951), notes

  8. the dark and silent grave l. 4

    The relative clause 'Who in the dark and silent grave ... Shuts up the story' makes Time itself the agent who locks us in the grave. Darkness and silence are the absence of the senses and of speech, the opposite of story and song. The phrase prepares the reversal of the final couplet, in which the same grave becomes the place from which God will raise the speaker.

    The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999); The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh, ed. Agnes M. C. Latham (London, 1951), notes

  9. wander’d all our ways l. 5

    To have 'wander'd all our ways' is to have finished our life's journeys, with a hint of erring or straying. Ralegh, explorer of Virginia and Guiana, had travelled further than most, and the line gains force from the circumstances. The biblical sense of human life as wandering (as in Hebrews 11:13, where the faithful confess 'that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth') lies behind it.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 11:13; The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition, ed. Michael Rudick (Tempe: Renaissance English Text Society, 1999)
