The Lie

by Sir Walter Raleigh 1552–1618

From A Poetical Rhapsody (1602), ed. A. H. Bullen (1890–91)

Social Commentaries

Go, soul, the body's guest,
Upon a thankless arrant;
Fear not to touch the best,
The truth shall be thy warrant:
Go, since I needs must die,
And give the world the lie.

Say to the Court, it glows,
And shines like rotten wood;
Say to the Church, it shows
What's good, and doth no good:
If Church and Court reply.
Then give them both the lie.

Tell Potentates they live
Acting by others' action;
Not loved unless they give,
Not s.trong but by a faction:
If Potentates reply,
Give Potentates the lie.

Tell men of high condition,
That manage the estate,
Their purpose is ambition,
Their practice only hate:
And if they once reply,
Then give them all the lie.

Tell them that brave it most.
They beg for more by spending,
Who in their greatest cost
Seek nothing but commending:
And if they make reply.
Then tell them all they lie.

Tell zeal it wants devotion;
Tell love it is but lust;
Tell time it metes but motion;
Tell flesh it is but dust:
And wish them not reply,
For thou must give the lie.

Tell age it daily wasteth;
Tell honour how it alters;
Tell beauty how she blasteth;
Tell favour how it falters:
And as they shall reply,
Give everyone the lie.

Tell wit how much it wrangles
In tickle points of niceness:
Tell wisdom she entangles
Herself in over-wiseness:
And when they do reply,
Straight give them both the lie.

Tell physic of her boldness;
Tell skill it is pretension;
Tell charity of coldness; /
Tell law it is contention;
And as they do reply.
So give them still the lie.

Tell fortune of her blindness;
Tell nature of decay;
Tell friendship of unkindness;
Tell justice of delay:
And if they will reply,
Then give them all the lie.

Tell arts they have no soundness,
But vary by esteeming;
Tell schools they want profoundness,
And stand too much on seeming:
If arts and schools reply,
Give arts and schools the lie.

Tell faith it's fled the city;
Tell how the country erreth;
Tell, manhood shakes of[f] pity;
Tell, virtue least preferreth:
And if they do reply,
Spare not to give the lie.

So when thou hast, as I
Commanded thee, done blabbing;
Although to give the lie,
Deserves no less than stabbing:
Stab at thee he that will.
No stab the soul can kill!

Codex Note

Anonymous in the Rhapsody; Bullen assigns it to Sir Walter Raleigh, and notes that it was already circulating in manuscript by 1596.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its date and authorship l. 1

    'The Lie' circulated widely in manuscript in the 1590s and provoked several answer poems; it was printed in Davison's 'A Poetical Rhapsody' (1608) without an author's name, and it has long been attributed to Ralegh. A date around 1592, when Ralegh was disgraced and imprisoned for his secret marriage to Elizabeth Throckmorton, is often suggested. Its authorship is not certain: Pierre Lefranc rejected it on grounds of style and theme, while Michael Rudick concluded that either Ralegh wrote it or others fastened it on him to reinforce his reputation for irreverence. A later tradition linking it to the eve of his execution in 1618 cannot be right, since it was circulating in the 1590s. The opening 'Go' adapts the envoy, in which a poet sends off his book.

    'The Lie (poem)', Wikipedia (date, attribution, Lefranc and Rudick); Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999); A. H. Bullen (ed.), Davison's Poetical Rhapsody (1890-91)

  2. The soul as the body's guest l. 1

    The idea of the soul as the body's 'guest' recalls the famous lines attributed to the Roman emperor Hadrian on his deathbed, preserved in the 'Historia Augusta' (Life of Hadrian 25.9): 'animula vagula blandula, / hospes comesque corporis' (little soul, wandering and charming, guest and companion of the body). The image also fits Christian Platonism, in which the soul is a sojourner in the flesh. Here the guest is sent out on a mission, and its immortality ('No stab the soul can kill') becomes the poem's final security.

    Historia Augusta, Life of Hadrian 25.9 (Hadrian's 'Animula vagula blandula'); Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  3. arrant l. 2

    'Arrant' is a variant spelling of 'errand' (OED), a message or mission. The soul is sent as an envoy to deliver unwelcome truths, which will win it no gratitude. 'Fear not to touch the best' means do not be afraid to attack even the highest in rank. The diplomatic language (envoy, errand, 'warrant') makes the poem a kind of embassy to the world.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  4. warrant l. 4

    A warrant was a document authorising an action (an arrest, a search, a journey) or guaranteeing safety. The soul's commission comes not from a monarch but from truth itself, a pointed reversal in a poem attacking court and authority. The phrase also suggests the biblical promise that 'the truth shall make you free' (John 8:32). The soul thus claims a higher authority than any court official could grant.

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Bible (KJV), John 8:32

  5. give the lie l. 6

    To give someone the lie was to tell him to his face that he lied, among the gravest insults in the honour culture of Elizabethan gentlemen; it was expected to lead to a challenge and a duel. Touchstone's mock list of the degrees of the lie in 'As You Like It' (5.4) satirises the elaborate etiquette. The poem's refrain uses the phrase in every stanza, inverting the code: the soul gives the lie to the institutions that ordinarily defend honour. The final stanza acknowledges the cost: giving the lie 'Deserves no less than stabbing'.

    Oxford English Dictionary; William Shakespeare, As You Like It, 5.4 (Folger Shakespeare); 'The Lie (poem)', Wikipedia (date, attribution, Lefranc and Rudick)

  6. Rotten wood l. 8

    Rotting wood infected by certain fungi gives off a faint phosphorescent glow, known as foxfire, which was noted in early natural history. The simile is precise and devastating: the court 'glows' with brilliance, but its light is the luminescence of decay. The Church, in the following lines, 'shows / What's good' (preaches it) but 'doth no good'. Rudick and others note that the attack on the Church has a Puritan edge.

    Oxford English Dictionary; 'The Lie (poem)', Wikipedia (date, attribution, Lefranc and Rudick); Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  7. Church and Court ll. 9-12

    'Shows' means displays, preaches or makes a show of. The Church teaches goodness without practising it. Critics have heard Puritan elements in this attack on a worldly established church, which is one reason Rudick doubted the attribution of the poem to the High Church clergyman Richard Latewar, proposed by Lefranc. Pairing 'Church and Court' sums up the two pillars of Elizabethan authority, both of which receive the lie together.

    'The Lie (poem)', Wikipedia (date, attribution, Lefranc and Rudick); Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  8. Potentates ll. 13-16

    'Potentates' are rulers and great men. They 'live / Acting by others' action': they do nothing themselves but operate through ministers and servants, and perhaps are mere actors playing roles written by others. 'Not loved unless they give' exposes the patronage system, in which loyalty was purchased with favour and office, and their strength depends on 'faction'. The stanza applies, without naming her, to the whole hierarchy under the Queen.

    Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999); Agnes M. C. Latham (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (1929; rev. 1951)

  9. high condition ll. 19-22

    'Men of high condition' are men of high rank, the statesmen who 'manage the estate' (govern the state). Their 'purpose' is ambition and their 'practice' (also intrigue, plotting) only hate. 'Practice' had a common sinister sense of scheming. Readers have often looked for allusions to particular courtiers, such as the Cecils, but the poem names no one. The generality is itself protective: a satire naming no names could circulate more safely.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  10. brave it ll. 25-28

    'Brave' meant splendid or finely dressed, and 'to brave it' meant to swagger or make a display. The courtiers who spend most on show are really begging for more by their spending, since conspicuous display was a way of seeking favour and office; in their 'greatest cost' they seek only 'commending' (praise). The paradox fits a court in which Ralegh himself was famous for his jewelled dress.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  11. Time 'metes but motion' l. 33

    'Metes' is from 'mete', to measure. The line reduces time to Aristotle's definition in the 'Physics' (IV.11), where time is the number or measure of motion. The stanza dismantles abstractions one by one: zeal lacks devotion, love is lust, time merely measures change, flesh is dust ('dust thou art', Genesis 3:19). Each charge is phrased in a compressed half-line, giving the poem its epigrammatic sharpness.

    Aristotle, Physics IV.11 (trans. R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye, MIT Internet Classics Archive); The Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19

  12. tickle; niceness l. 44

    'Tickle' meant insecure, precarious or delicate to handle (OED 'tickle', adj.), and 'niceness' meant excessive precision, fastidious subtlety. Wit 'wrangles' (disputes quarrelsomely) over fine points, and wisdom entangles herself in 'over-wiseness'. The target is the scholastic disputation of the universities and the cleverness of courtly conversation. The pairing of wit and wisdom shows that the poem's scepticism extends to intellect itself, not only to social institutions.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  13. physic l. 49

    The stanza turns to the professions. Physic (medicine) is bold, overconfident in its power to heal; 'skill' (knowledge, expertise) is merely 'pretension' (pretence, claim); charity is cold; and law is 'contention', litigation. The list resembles medieval estates satire, now compressed into sharp accusations. The archive's text keeps a stray slash after 'coldness', an artefact of its source. Each charge is a single short clause, and the cumulative effect is of a world in which every profession betrays its proper end.

    Oxford English Dictionary; A. H. Bullen (ed.), Davison's Poetical Rhapsody (1890-91)

  14. Blind Fortune l. 55

    The goddess Fortuna was often represented blindfold, turning her wheel and giving favour indiscriminately; Cicero calls her blind ('caeca'), and the image was standard in Renaissance emblem books. The stanza then attacks nature (decaying), friendship (unkind, unnatural) and justice (delayed), the last a complaint about Elizabethan courts familiar from Hamlet's 'the law's delay'. Blind Fortune is thus the patroness of a world in which merit goes unrewarded.

    Cicero, De Amicitia 54 ('non enim solum ipsa Fortuna caeca est'); William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1 ('the law's delay')

  15. schools ll. 61-64

    'Arts' (learning, the liberal arts) 'vary by esteeming': their value depends on fashion and opinion. 'Schools' (the universities and the schoolmen) 'want profoundness' (lack depth) and 'stand too much on seeming' (rely on outward appearance). Ralegh himself was later suspected of free-thinking, and the scepticism toward learned authority suited his reputation, though the poem's attitude is also conventional. The scepticism of the stanza anticipates later attacks on scholastic learning by writers such as Francis Bacon.

    Oxford English Dictionary; 'The Lie (poem)', Wikipedia (date, attribution, Lefranc and Rudick)

  16. Faith has fled the city l. 67

    The image of a virtue abandoning the corrupt world recalls Ovid's account of the Iron Age, when the maiden Astraea (Justice), last of the gods, left the blood-soaked earth (Metamorphoses 1.149-50). Elizabethan poets often identified the Queen with Astraea, so the line has a pointed edge. 'Manhood shakes off pity' (the archive marks its editorial '[f]') and 'virtue least preferreth' (virtue is least promoted) complete the picture of a world without trust.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.149-50 (Astraea); Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)

  17. Stanza and refrain each stanza

    Each stanza is six short lines rhyming ababcc, with three stresses to the line. The first four lines list accusations, often with feminine rhymes ('wasteth / blasteth', 'alters / falters'), and the couplet varies the refrain ('Give everyone the lie', 'So give them still the lie'), always on 'reply / lie'. The repeated imperative 'Tell' (anaphora) gives the poem a relentless, catalogue-like movement, building through social institutions to abstractions and back to the soul.

    Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999); Agnes M. C. Latham (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh (1929; rev. 1951)

  18. The final stanza ll. 73-78

    The soul's mission ends with 'blabbing' (indiscreet telling), a self-mocking word. Since giving the lie 'Deserves no less than stabbing', the speaker acknowledges the violence his truth-telling invites, but concludes 'No stab the soul can kill!' The confidence recalls Christ's words, 'fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul' (Matthew 10:28). The ending also explains the poem's frame: the body may die, but the soul, its guest, is beyond the reach of the world it has insulted.

    The Bible (KJV), Matthew 10:28; Michael Rudick (ed.), The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe: ACMRS / RETS, 1999)
