Death & Dying

Lament for the Makers

by William Dunbar c. 1460–c. 1520

Death & Dying

I that in heill was and gladnèss
Am trublit now with great sickness
And feblit with infirmitie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Our plesance here is all vain glory,
This fals world is but transitory,
The flesh is bruckle, the Feynd is slee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

The state of man does change and vary,
Now sound, now sick, now blyth, now sary,
Now dansand mirry, now like to die:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

No state in Erd here standis sicker;
As with the wynd wavis the wicker
So wannis this world’s vanitie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Unto the Death gois all Estatis,
Princis, Prelatis, and Potestatis,
Baith rich and poor of all degree:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He takis the knichtis in to the field
Enarmit under helm and scheild;
Victor he is at all mellie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

That strong unmerciful tyrand
Takis, on the motheris breast sowkand,
The babe full of benignitie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He takis the campion in the stour,
The captain closit in the tour,
The lady in bour full of bewtie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He spairis no lord for his piscence,
Na clerk for his intelligence;
His awful straik may no man flee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Art-magicianis and astrologgis,
Rethoris, logicianis, and theologgis,
Them helpis no conclusionis slee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

In medecine the most practicianis,
Leechis, surrigianis, and physicianis,
Themself from Death may not supplee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

I see that makaris amang the lave
Playis here their padyanis, syne gois to grave;
Sparit is nocht their facultie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He has done petuously devour
The noble Chaucer, of makaris flour,
The Monk of Bury, and Gower, all three:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

The good Sir Hew of Eglintoun,
Ettrick, Heriot, and Wintoun,
He has tane out of this cuntrie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

That scorpion fell has done infeck
Maister John Clerk, and James Afflek,
Fra ballat-making and tragedie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Holland and Barbour he has berevit;
Alas! that he not with us levit
Sir Mungo Lockart of the Lee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Clerk of Tranent eke he has tane,
That made the anteris of Gawaine;
Sir Gilbert Hay endit has he:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He has Blind Harry and Sandy Traill
Slain with his schour of mortal hail,
Quhilk Patrick Johnstoun might nought flee:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He has reft Merseir his endite,
That did in luve so lively write,
So short, so quick, of sentence hie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

He has tane Rowll of Aberdene,
And gentill Rowll of Corstorphine;
Two better fallowis did no man see:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

In Dunfermline he has tane Broun
With Maister Robert Henrysoun;
Sir John the Ross enbrast has he:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

And he has now tane, last of a,
Good gentil Stobo and Quintin Shaw,
Of quhom all wichtis hes pitie:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Good Maister Walter Kennedy
In point of Death lies verily;
Great ruth it were that so suld be:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Sen he has all my brether tane,
He will naught let me live alane;
Of force I man his next prey be:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Since for the Death remeid is none,
Best is that we for Death dispone,
After our death that live may we:--
Timor Mortis conturbat me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its title and date ll. 1-3

    Dunbar's poem was first printed by Walter Chepman and Androw Myllar in Edinburgh in 1508, among the earliest Scottish printed books; the manuscript copies are later. Internal evidence (Walter Kennedy is still alive, but near death) points to about 1505. 'Makar' is the Scots word for a poet, a maker, exactly translating Greek 'poietes'. The opening is personal: 'I that in heill [health] was and gladness / Am trublit now with great sickness / And feblit [enfeebled] with infirmitie'. Whether Dunbar was really ill or adopting a conventional sick-bed persona is uncertain. The poem is a 'danse macabre' (dance of death) that narrows from all estates of society to Dunbar's fellow poets and finally to himself.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  2. Timor mortis conturbat me refrain

    The refrain comes from the liturgy for the dead: in the third nocturn of Matins in the Office of the Dead, the responsory 'Peccantem me quotidie' confesses daily sin without repentance and continues 'timor mortis conturbat me', because in hell there is no redemption. A late medieval reader would know the words from funerals and from the prayers of the Book of Hours. The phrase was a popular refrain in English and Scots poetry; the archive's anonymous Middle English 'In what state that ever I be' uses it too. Repeated after every stanza, it works like a tolling bell and a penitential response, and the fear it names is spiritual as well as physical.

    'Timor mortis conturbat me', Wikipedia (Office of the Dead responsory); Burnet Psalter (University of Aberdeen), f. 276v: Office of the Dead, Matins, third nocturn; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  3. bruckle; slee l. 7

    'Bruckle' (brittle, fragile, easily broken) is a Scots word Dunbar often uses for human life; 'the Feynd' is the devil, and 'slee' (sly) means crafty. With 'This fals world' in the previous line the stanza names the three traditional enemies of the soul, the world, the flesh and the devil, as in the Litany's prayer for deliverance 'from all the deceits of the world, the flesh, and the devil'. 'Vain glory' recalls Ecclesiastes 1:2 ('vanity of vanities'). The stanza sets the moral frame before the catalogue of the dead.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); The Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:2; John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  4. Mutability ll. 9-11

    The third stanza turns the theme of mutability into rhythm: 'Now sound, now sick, now blyth, now sary, / Now dansand mirry, now like to die'. The repeated 'now' (anaphora) imitates the swing of Fortune's wheel, a medieval commonplace descending from Boethius' Consolation of Philosophy. 'Dansand' (dancing), with the Scots present participle in '-and', may hint at the dance of death, the pictorial and poetic tradition in which Death leads people of every rank away.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  5. The wicker in the wind ll. 13-15

    'No state in Erd here standis sicker' means no condition on earth stands secure ('sicker', from Latin 'securus'). The simile compares worldly vanity to the 'wicker', a pliant willow twig, that bends this way and that in the wind; 'wannis' (wanes) suggests diminishing as well as wavering. The image recalls biblical figures of human frailty as reeds and grass (e.g. Matthew 11:7, 'a reed shaken with the wind'). The alliteration of w-sounds ('wynd wavis ... wicker ... wannis ... world') imitates the movement.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); The Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:7; John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  6. All estates: the dance of death ll. 17-19

    'Estatis' are the orders or ranks of society. Dunbar lists princes, prelates (bishops and abbots) and 'Potestatis' (powers, rulers; compare the 'principalities' and 'powers' of Ephesians 6:12), then rich and poor of every degree. This is the logic of the danse macabre, familiar from murals and from Lydgate's English 'Dance of Death' (after the Paris Cemetery of the Innocents), where Death leads pope, emperor, knight, child and labourer in turn. The following stanzas personify Death as a conqueror taking knights, babies, champions, ladies, lords and clerks.

    The Bible (KJV), Ephesians 6:12; 'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  7. mellie l. 23

    Death is imagined as a knight-champion who takes knights from the field 'Enarmit [armed] under helm and scheild' and is 'Victor ... at all mellie', winner in every melee or close combat (Scots 'melle', from Old French 'meslee'). The chivalric diction turns the personified Death into a superior warrior, against whom armour is useless, an idea echoed in stanza 8's 'campion in the stour'.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); Oxford English Dictionary; John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  8. The babe at the breast ll. 25-27

    'That strong unmerciful tyrand' (tyrant) is Death, who takes 'on the motheris breast sowkand [sucking], / The babe full of benignitie [innocence, goodness]'. The image is one of the most affecting in the dance-of-death tradition, where an infant is often shown being taken from its cradle. It sets Death's lack of mercy against the child's 'benignitie'. 'Sowkand' shows the Older Scots present participle ending in '-and'.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); 'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  9. campion; stour; tour; bour ll. 29-31

    'Campion' (champion, fighter) and 'stour' (conflict, battle, also dust and turmoil) are both Older Scots words of romance and chronicle. The stanza then sets the 'captain closit in the tour' (enclosed in the tower, defending a castle) against 'the lady in bour [bower] full of bewtie', so that strength, safety and beauty alike are powerless. The rhyme 'stour / tour / bour' links the three.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  10. The learned professions ll. 37-39

    Having shown that lords and clerks cannot escape ('piscence' is puissance, power; 'straik' a stroke), Dunbar lists the learned: practitioners of magic arts, astrologers, 'Rethoris' (rhetoricians), logicians and theologians. 'Them helpis no conclusionis slee': no subtle conclusions help them. 'Conclusions' were both syllogistic proofs and alchemical or astrological experiments. There is irony in a poet (himself a master of rhetoric) admitting that eloquence and learning cannot save anyone.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  11. Physicians ll. 41-43

    'The most practicianis' are the most skilful practitioners of medicine; 'Leechis' are physicians (from Old English 'laece'), 'surrigianis' surgeons. They 'may not supplee' themselves, cannot rescue or supply aid to themselves (Scots 'suppley', from Latin 'supplere'). The idea recalls the proverb in Luke 4:23, 'Physician, heal thyself'. The stanza ends the list of the professions and prepares the turn to the poets.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); The Bible (KJV), Luke 4:23; John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  12. The turn to the poets ll. 45-47

    With stanza 12 the poem turns to its true subject. 'I see that makaris amang the lave [the rest] / Playis here their padyanis [pageants], syne [then] gois to grave': poets act out their part on the world's stage like players in a pageant, and their 'facultie' (art, gift) is not spared. The theatrical image (the world as a stage) was widespread in medieval and Renaissance moral writing. What follows is the first literary history of Scottish poetry, a roll-call of poets, many of whose works are lost.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); 'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes

  13. Chaucer, the Monk of Bury, Gower ll. 49-51

    Dunbar begins with the three English poets whom Scottish and English writers of the fifteenth century treated as their masters: Geoffrey Chaucer, 'of makaris flour' (the flower of poets); John Lydgate, monk of Bury St Edmunds; and John Gower. The triad was conventional, and Dunbar praises the same three in 'The Goldyn Targe'. 'He has done petuously devour' means Death has piteously devoured them. Opening with the English masters places the Scots poets that follow in a common tradition of 'makaris'.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  14. Sir Hew of Eglintoun l. 53

    Sir Hugh of Eglinton was a fourteenth-century Ayrshire knight (d. 1377) married to a half-sister of Robert II. No poem certainly by him survives. Andrew of Wyntoun mentions a poet called 'Huchoun of the Awle Ryale' (of the royal hall), and since the nineteenth century some have identified Huchoun with Hugh of Eglinton and credited him with alliterative works such as the 'Morte Arthure' or 'The Awntyrs off Arthure'. These identifications are disputed and unproven. Dunbar's list proves only that Hugh was remembered as a poet a century later.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes

  15. Ettrick, Heriot, Wintoun l. 54

    Of the three names in this line only Wintoun can be identified: Andrew of Wyntoun, prior of St Serf's, Lochleven, whose 'Orygynale Cronykil of Scotland' is a verse chronicle from the creation to his own day. Nothing is known of Ettrick or Heriot as poets. 'He has tane out of this cuntrie' (taken from this country) closes the stanza. As with many names in the poem, Dunbar's list is our only evidence that these men wrote at all, which shows how much medieval Scots poetry has been lost.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes

  16. Death as a scorpion ll. 57-59

    Death becomes 'That scorpion fell' (that cruel scorpion), which 'has done infeck' (has infected, poisoned) Master John Clerk and James Afflek, taking them from 'ballat-making and tragedie'. The scorpion's sting recalls 1 Corinthians 15:55 ('O death, where is thy sting?') and the scorpions of Revelation 9:5. Neither Clerk nor Afflek can be identified with certainty (Afflek may be a James Auchinleck); 'ballat' means a poem or song, and 'tragedie' a serious narrative with an unhappy ending.

    The Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:55; 'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue)

  17. Holland and Barbour l. 61

    Richard Holland was the author of 'The Buke of the Howlat' (Book of the Owl), an alliterative allegory of about 1450 written for the Douglas family. John Barbour, archdeacon of Aberdeen, wrote 'The Bruce' (c. 1375), the verse epic of Robert the Bruce and the Wars of Independence, the founding work of Scots literature. 'Berevit' means bereft, taken away. Sir Mungo Lockart of the Lee, whom Dunbar laments in the same stanza, has no known works and may be a knight who died in 1489.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  18. Clerk of Tranent; Gilbert Hay ll. 65-67

    'Anteris' (aventures, adventures) suggests an Arthurian romance about Gawain written by an unnamed 'Clerk of Tranent' (a village east of Edinburgh). Some have tried to link it to surviving alliterative romances such as 'The Awntyrs off Arthure', but this remains speculative. Sir Gilbert Hay (fl. 1456) is better known: he translated works on chivalry and government into Scots prose and is credited with 'The Buik of King Alexander the Conquerour'. 'Endit has he' means Death has ended him.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  19. Blind Harry and the 'schour of mortal hail' ll. 69-71

    Blind Harry (Hary) was the author of 'The Wallace' (c. 1477), a long patriotic poem on William Wallace. Sandy Traill is unidentified. Death has 'slain with his schour of mortal hail' (shower of deadly hail), a battlefield image of arrows or missiles. 'Quhilk Patrick Johnstoun might nought flee' (which Patrick Johnstoun could not escape) is puzzling, since Johnstoun, a courtier with no surviving works, is thought to have been still alive when Dunbar wrote. 'Quh-' is the Scots spelling for 'wh-'.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  20. Merseir ll. 73-75

    'Endite' is the act or art of composing. Mercer (Merseir) is praised for writing 'in luve so lively' (vividly), 'So short, so quick, of sentence hie' (of lofty sense), an unusually precise piece of literary criticism. A few love poems in the Bannatyne Manuscript are attributed to 'Mersar', perhaps the same man. The two Rowlls in the next stanza (of Aberdeen and of Corstorphine) are barely known; one poem survives attributed to a Roull.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  21. Robert Henryson ll. 81-82

    The line 'In Dunfermline he has tane Broun / With Maister Robert Henrysoun' gives one of the few pieces of evidence for Henryson's death and his link with Dunfermline, where he was probably schoolmaster attached to the abbey. 'Maister' indicates a university master's degree. Henryson, author of 'The Testament of Cresseid' (a sequel to Chaucer's Troilus), the 'Morall Fabillis' and 'Orpheus and Eurydice', is now regarded with Dunbar as the greatest of the Older Scots makars. It is striking that Dunbar names him so briefly. Broun is unidentified.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  22. Sir John the Ross; Stobo; Quintin Shaw ll. 83-86

    Sir John Ross appears in 'The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie', the poetic insult contest, as Dunbar's 'commissar' (second); Quintin Shaw, named in the next stanza, served Kennedy in the same role, and one satire by him survives. 'Gentil Stobo' has been identified with John Reid, priest and notary who served as clerk under James II, III and IV. 'Enbrast' means embraced, a grim image of Death's hug. 'Last of a' (last of all) and 'wichtis' (people) are Scots forms.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie', Wikipedia; Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)

  23. Walter Kennedy ll. 89-91

    Walter Kennedy, a Carrick poet and graduate of Glasgow, was Dunbar's opponent in 'The Flyting', and author of 'The Passioun of Crist'. Here he is not yet dead but 'In point of Death lies verily', truly on the point of death; 'Great ruth it were' means it would be a great pity. Because Kennedy is thought to have died c. 1508, the stanza helps date the poem c. 1505-8. The affection shown to an old adversary suggests the Flyting was in part a literary game.

    'Lament for the Makaris', Wikipedia (identifications after Tasioulas 1999 and Bawcutt); 'The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie', Wikipedia; 'Lament for the Makers', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), with notes

  24. The poet's own turn ll. 93-95

    The catalogue ends by turning on the speaker: 'Sen [since] he has all my brether tane [taken all my brothers], / He will naught let me live alane; / Of force [of necessity] I man [must] his next prey be'. 'Brether' expresses a fellowship of poets across Scotland and England. The poem thus returns to the sick 'I' of its opening, and the refrain's fear becomes personal. The ending of the list of makars with himself gives the poem its emotional force.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998); John Conlee (ed.), William Dunbar: The Complete Works (TEAMS Middle English Texts, Kalamazoo, 2004)

  25. The last stanza ll. 97-99

    The closing stanza turns from lament to counsel: since there is no 'remeid' (remedy) against death, it is best that we 'dispone' (prepare, set ourselves in order, from Latin 'disponere') for death, 'After our death that live may we', so that we may live in heaven after dying. This is the orthodox lesson of the art of dying ('ars moriendi'). Yet the poem still ends with the refrain 'Timor Mortis conturbat me', so that consolation and fear stand together. Critics differ on whether the final note is serene or still troubled.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue); 'Timor mortis conturbat me', Wikipedia (Office of the Dead responsory); Priscilla Bawcutt (ed.), The Poems of William Dunbar, 2 vols (Glasgow: ASLS, 1998)
