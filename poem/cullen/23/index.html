Social Commentaries

Heritage

by Countee Cullen 1903–1946

From Color (1925)

Social Commentaries

What is Africa to me:
Copper sun or scarlet sea,
Jungle star or jungle track,
Strong bronzed men, or regal black
Women from whose loins I sprang
When the birds of Eden sang?
One three centuries removed
From the scenes his fathers loved,
Spicy grove, cinnamon tree,
What is Africa to me?

So I lie, who all day long
Want no sound except the song
Sung by wild barbaric birds
Goading massive jungle herds,
Juggernauts of flesh that pass
Trampling tall defiant grass
Where young forest lovers lie,
Plighting troth beneath the sky.
So I lie, who always hear,
Though I cram against my ear
Both my thumbs, and keep them there,
Great drums throbbing through the air.
So I lie, whose fount of pride,
Dear distress, and joy allied,
Is my somber flesh and skin,
With the dark blood dammed within
Like great pulsing tides of wine
That, I fear, must burst the fine
Channels of the chafing net
Where they surge and foam and fret.

Africa? A book one thumbs
Listlessly, till slumber comes.
Unremembered are her bats
Circling through the night, her cats
Crouching in the river reeds,
Stalking gentle flesh that feeds
By the river brink; no more
Does the bugle-throated roar
Cry that monarch claws have leapt
From the scabbards where they slept.
Silver snakes that once a year
Doff the lovely coats you wear,
Seek no covert in your fear
Lest a mortal eye should see;
What’s your nakedness to me?
Here no leprous flowers rear
Fierce corollas in the air;
Here no bodies sleek and wet,
Dripping mingled rain and sweat,
Tread the savage measures of
Jungle boys and girls in love.
What is last year’s snow to me,
Last year’s anything? The tree
Budding yearly must forget
How its past arose or set--
Bough and blossom, flower, fruit,
Even what shy bird with mute
Wonder at her travail there,
Meekly labored in its hair.
One three centuries removed
From the scenes his fathers loved,
Spicy grove, cinnamon tree,
What is Africa to me?

So I lie, who find no peace
Night or day, no slight release
From the unremittant beat
Made by cruel padded feet
Walking through my body’s street.
Up and down they go, and back,
Treading out a jungle track.
So I lie, who never quite
Safely sleep from rain at night--
I can never rest at all
When the rain begins to fall;
Like a soul gone mad with pain
I must match its weird refrain;
Ever must I twist and squirm,
Writhing like a baited worm,
While its primal measures drip
Through my body, crying, “Strip!
Doff this new exuberance.
Come and dance the Lover’s Dance!”
In an old remembered way
Rain works on me night and day.

Quaint, outlandish heathen gods
Black men fashion out of rods,
Clay, and brittle bits of stone,
In a likeness like their own,
My conversion came high-priced;
I belong to Jesus Christ,
Preacher of humility;
Heathen gods are naught to me.

Father, Son, and Holy Ghost,
So I make an idle boast;
Jesus of the twice-turned cheek,
Lamb of God, although I speak
With my mouth thus, in my heart
Do I play a double part.
Ever at Thy glowing altar
Must my heart grow sick and falter,
Wishing He I served were black,
Thinking then it would not lack
Precedent of pain to guide it,
Let who would or might deride it;
Surely then this flesh would know
Yours had borne a kindred woe.
Lord, I fashion dark gods, too,
Daring even to give You
Dark despairing features where,
Crowned with dark rebellious hair,
Patience wavers just so much as
Mortal grief compels, while touches
Quick and hot, of anger, rise
To smitten cheek and weary eyes.
Lord, forgive me if my need
Sometimes shapes a human creed.

All day long and all night through,
One thing only must I do:
Quench my pride and cool my blood,
Lest I perish in the flood.
Lest a hidden ember set
Timber that I thought was wet
Burning like the dryest flax,
Melting like the merest wax,
Lest the grave restore its dead.
Not yet has my heart or head
In the least way realized
They and I are civilized.

Codex Note

For Harold Jackman

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and dedication l. 1

    Cullen (1903-1946) was still a student when 'Heritage' appeared in the Harlem number of Survey Graphic, edited by Alain Locke, in a version slightly different from the one collected that autumn in his first book, Color. The book version is dedicated to Harold Jackman, a Harlem schoolteacher and Cullen's closest friend. The poem was also included in Locke's anthology The New Negro (1925), and it became a central text in debates about what Africa could mean to Black Americans three centuries after the slave trade.

    African American Poetry: A Digital Anthology (Lehigh University), 'Heritage' (for Harold Jackman); Alain Locke, ed., The New Negro: An Interpretation (Albert and Charles Boni, 1925); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  2. The refrain question l. 10

    The question frames the first section and returns in the middle of the poem (l. 63, closing a repeated stanza, ll. 60-63). Grammatically it can be dismissive ('What does Africa matter to me?') or searching ('What is Africa, for me?'). Critics have read the poem's oscillation between these senses as its subject: the speaker's attempts to dismiss Africa keep failing. The line may also recall Hamlet's 'What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba?' (Hamlet 2.2), another question about feeling for a distant cause.

    Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; William Shakespeare, Hamlet 2.2, the Hecuba soliloquy

  3. Trochaic tetrameter couplets ll. 2-3

    Most lines have four stresses beginning on a stressed syllable and lacking a final unstressed syllable ('COP-per SUN or SCAR-let SEA'). The measure is chant-like and drumming, appropriate to a poem about the 'throbbing' drums and rain that invade the speaker's body. Cullen, a deliberately traditional poet who admired Keats, uses an English form to explore an African theme, a tension central to the poem.

    Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); William Blake, 'The Tyger' (1794)

  4. Birds of Eden l. 6

    The line places the speaker's ancestors in a primal, paradisal past. Genesis locates Eden near rivers including Gihon, which 'compasseth the whole land of Ethiopia' (Genesis 2:13 KJV), so the identification has a biblical basis. The romantic image of Africa as a lost garden is part of the poem's self-consciously exotic vision, which the speaker later admits is 'A book one thumbs'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:8-14; Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  5. Three centuries removed ll. 7-8

    The first enslaved Africans recorded in English Virginia arrived in 1619, roughly three centuries before the poem. 'Removed' carries both the sense of distance in descent and of forcible transport. The impersonal 'One... his fathers' generalises the speaker into a representative African American. The phrase 'his fathers loved' assumes an ancestral attachment that the speaker cannot himself feel, which is the poem's problem from the start.

    National Park Service, Historic Jamestowne, 'The 1619 landing'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  6. Spicy grove, cinnamon tree l. 9

    Cinnamon is among the spices of the beloved's garden in the Song of Songs (4:14), and 'spicy breezes' blowing over Ceylon appear in Reginald Heber's missionary hymn 'From Greenland's Icy Mountains' (1819), widely sung in American churches. Whether or not Cullen intended the hymn echo, the line draws on a Western, partly missionary image of tropical lands; the irony is that the Christian speaker's idea of Africa is itself secondhand.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Song of Solomon 4:14; Reginald Heber, 'From Greenland's Icy Mountains' (1819), Hymnary.org; Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  7. 'So I lie' l. 11

    'Lie' can mean recline (he lies awake hearing the drums) or speak falsely. The repeated formula 'So I lie, who...' (ll. 11, 19, 23, 64, 71) introduces each admission that he cannot shut Africa out, so that the second sense exposes the first section's question as a pose. Most critics treat this pun as the key to the poem's irony.

    Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  8. Juggernauts l. 15

    The word comes from Hindi Jagannath, 'lord of the world', a form of Vishnu whose huge temple car at Puri was described by European writers as crushing devotees under its wheels. By the twentieth century it meant any overwhelming force. The jungle herds are imagined at an epic, unstoppable scale, part of the poem's lush, stylised Africa. The word's Indian origin also shows how freely the poem mixes exotic vocabularies.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'juggernaut, n.'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  9. Plighting troth l. 18

    'Plight' means pledge and 'troth' means truth or fidelity; the phrase is familiar from the Book of Common Prayer ('and thereto I plight thee my troth'). Applied to 'young forest lovers', it gives the imagined African scene the dignity of Christian marriage while also marking the speaker's distance, since he describes African love in English liturgical terms. The phrase belongs to the speaker's fantasy of Africa.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'troth, n.'; The Book of Common Prayer (1662), The Form of Solemnization of Matrimony

  10. The drums ll. 19-22

    The comic-desperate gesture of cramming thumbs into his ears shows that the 'sound' is internal. The drum was a central and much-romanticised symbol of Africa in Harlem Renaissance writing, and Cullen uses it to dramatise ancestral memory as pulse and blood. The next lines make the blood itself a 'dammed' tide pressing against the 'chafing net' of his veins. The image is comic as well.

    Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); George Hutchinson, The Harlem Renaissance in Black and White (Harvard University Press, 1995)

  11. Primitivism l. 13

    White and Black writers of the 1920s often imagined Africa as a realm of untamed instinct, opposed to 'civilized' restraint. Some critics fault 'Heritage' for repeating these stereotypes, since Cullen had never been to Africa; others argue that the poem presents them as fantasy ('A book one thumbs') and dramatises the American speaker's confusion. Langston Hughes's 1926 essay 'The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain' famously criticised a young poet, generally identified as Cullen, for wanting to be 'a poet - not a Negro poet'.

    Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Langston Hughes, 'The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain', The Nation, 23 June 1926; George Hutchinson, The Harlem Renaissance in Black and White (Harvard University Press, 1995)

  12. Africa as a book ll. 31-32

    The line attempts dismissal: Africa is a dull text, its beasts 'unremembered'. But the lush description that follows (bats, cats, the lion's 'bugle-throated roar', snakes, 'leprous flowers') contradicts the claim of boredom, so the denial undermines itself. 'Thumbs' links back to the thumbs in his ears (l. 21). The phrase also hints at the source of the speaker's Africa: travel writing, missionary literature and romance, rather than memory or experience.

    Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  13. Claws in scabbards ll. 38-40

    A scabbard is a sheath for a sword. The metaphor makes the big cat's retractable claws into blades, and the 'monarch' is the lion, king of beasts. The heraldic, epic register shows Cullen's literary training: Africa is rendered through the conventions of English poetry. Lions sheathing their claws like swords give the jungle a chivalric dignity, which again shows Africa seen through European literary lenses rather than at first hand.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scabbard, n.'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  14. leprous flowers; corollas ll. 46-47

    From Latin corolla, 'little crown or garland'. The adjective 'leprous' gives the tropical flowers a decadent, even sinister beauty, in the manner of fin-de-siecle poetry. The image belongs to the passage in which the speaker insists that 'Here', in America, no such things exist. 'Rear' (raise up) gives the flowers an aggressive, almost animal energy; even the vegetation, in this imagined Africa, is fierce and sensual.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'corolla, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'leprous, adj.'

  15. Last year's snow ll. 52-59

    Francois Villon's 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', known in English through Dante Gabriel Rossetti's 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies', asks where the snows of past years have gone. Cullen turns the lament into a question of indifference: what is the past to me? The tree that 'must forget / How its past arose or set' offers a natural analogy for forgetting origins, but the poem's next movement shows that forgetting is impossible.

    Dante Gabriel Rossetti, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies' (translation of Villon); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  16. The rain ll. 71-84

    The rain's 'weird refrain' and 'primal measures' are compared to a mad soul and a 'baited worm', strong images of compulsion and discomfort. The rain's voice urges him to shed the 'new exuberance', presumably his American, Christian, civilised self, for an 'old remembered way'. The passage most clearly dramatises the poem's idea of racial memory as bodily, an idea later critics have questioned as essentialist.

    Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction

  17. Conversion ll. 89-92

    The line is often read as an allusion to the slave trade: Christianity reached his ancestors through enslavement. Cullen, the adopted son of the Rev. Frederick A. Cullen, minister of Salem Methodist Episcopal Church in Harlem, writes from inside the church. The confident 'Heathen gods are naught to me' then echoes the refrain's pose of indifference and is undercut in the next section.

    Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Countee Cullen, Collected Poems, ed. Major Jackson (Library of America, 2013), notes

  18. The twice-turned cheek ll. 95-100

    'Twice-turned' compresses the command to turn the other cheek after the first has been struck. 'Lamb of God' (John 1:29) adds the image of the sacrificial victim. The speaker's 'idle boast' of faith is spoken 'with my mouth' while 'in my heart / Do I play a double part', echoing Isaiah 29:13 ('this people draw near me with their mouth... but have removed their heart far from me').

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 5:39; John 1:29; Isaiah 29:13; Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974)

  19. A Black Christ ll. 107-118

    The idea of a Black Christ who bears 'a kindred woe' anticipates Cullen's long poem The Black Christ (1929). Here the speaker 'fashions dark gods' just as the 'heathen' do (ll. 85-86), which collapses his earlier contrast between pagan and Christian. His Christ has patience that 'wavers' and anger that rises to the 'smitten cheek'. The section ends with a plea for forgiveness that 'my need / Sometimes shapes a human creed', an admission that theology is shaped by experience.

    Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Countee Cullen, The Black Christ and Other Poems (Harper and Brothers, 1929)

  20. Flood and fire ll. 119-125

    The ending draws together the poem's blood-tide and fire imagery. 'Burning like the dryest flax' recalls Judges 15:14 and Isaiah 1:31, and 'Melting like the merest wax' Psalm 22:14 and 68:2 ('as wax melteth before the fire'). The 'hidden ember' in timber 'that I thought was wet' suggests that suppressed feeling can still catch fire. 'Lest the grave restore its dead' echoes Revelation 20:13, where 'the sea gave up the dead': the past might rise again.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Judges 15:14; Isaiah 1:31; Psalm 22:14; Psalm 68:2; Revelation 20:13; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  21. 'They and I are civilized' ll. 126-128

    'They' are probably his heart and head, the faculties that have not accepted civilisation, though some readers take the word to mean his African ancestors. The flat closing word 'civilized' echoes contemporary arguments about 'civilized' and 'primitive' races and turns them back ironically. Critics differ on the tone: some find a confession of permanent division, others an ironic critique of the very idea of being civilised.

    Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)
