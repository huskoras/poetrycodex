Social Commentaries

Incident

by Countee Cullen 1903–1946

From Color (1925)

Social Commentaries

Once riding in old Baltimore,
Heart-filled, head-filled with glee,
I saw a Baltimorean
Keep looking straight at me.

Now I was eight and very small,
And he was no whit bigger,
And so I smiled, but he poked out
His tongue, and called me, “Nigger.”

I saw the whole of Baltimore
From May until December;
Of all the things that happened there
That’s all that I remember.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

For Eric Walrond

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and dedication l. 1

    Eric Walrond (1898-1966), born in British Guiana and raised in Barbados and Panama, was a Harlem journalist and author of Tropic Death (1926). The dedication links the poem to a fellow writer who had experienced American racism as a newcomer. Cullen gave varying accounts of his birthplace, including Baltimore, so whether the incident is autobiographical is uncertain, though many readers take it so. Baltimore was a segregated border city in the early twentieth century.

    Countee Cullen, Color (New York: Harper and Brothers, 1925); Countee Cullen, Collected Poems, ed. Major Jackson (Library of America, 2013), notes; Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction; Wikipedia, 'Incident (poem)' (publication and reception, with references)

  2. Riding in old Baltimore l. 1

    The poem does not say what the child was riding, but the setting suggests public transport, one of the main sites of racial confrontation in the Jim Crow era. Baltimore, in the border state of Maryland, passed residential segregation ordinances from 1910 and enforced segregation in many public facilities. 'Old Baltimore' has a storybook ring, like the opening of a ballad ('Once...'), which the slur will break. The University of Baltimore's Baltimore Literary Heritage Project has described the poem's picture of the city as ugly but accurate.

    Wikipedia, 'Incident (poem)', citing the Baltimore Literary Heritage Project; Garrett Power, 'Apartheid Baltimore Style: The Residential Segregation Laws of 1910-1913', Maryland Law Review 42 (1983); Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  3. Ballad stanza st. 1

    Cullen uses the simplest traditional form, with a lilting rhythm suited to a child's memory. Cary Nelson has argued that the slur's eruption inside this 'childlike' form makes it more shocking than a modernist fragment would. The compound adjectives 'Heart-filled, head-filled' convey a total, innocent happiness that the poem will destroy. Cullen often chose such traditional forms, which he defended against the free verse of his contemporaries.

    Cary Nelson, ed., Anthology of Modern American Poetry (Oxford University Press, 2000), headnote on Cullen; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974)

  4. A Baltimorean l. 3

    The demonym is formal and slightly comic, and it makes the other boy representative of his city's attitudes. The poem never states his colour, but the slur makes it clear. Readers have noted that 'Keep looking straight at me' suggests the speaker's openness: he takes the look as an invitation to friendship. The adult speaker recalls the scene, but the voice keeps the child's perspective.

    Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Countee Cullen, Collected Poems, ed. Major Jackson (Library of America, 2013), notes

  5. Age eight l. 5

    'Now' here is a storytelling marker ('it happened that...') rather than a reference to the present. By insisting that he was eight and the other boy 'no whit bigger', the speaker makes clear that the insult was not the product of strength or experience but of something the other child had absorbed from his surroundings. The plain, monosyllabic line mirrors a child's way of telling. Many teachers use the poem to show how early racial identity is imposed from outside, a theme also present in Du Bois's account, in The Souls of Black Folk, of the schoolgirl who refused his visiting-card.

    W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 1, 'Of Our Spiritual Strivings'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974)

  6. no whit l. 6

    'Whit' (from Old English wiht, a thing, creature) survives mainly in the negative phrases 'no whit' and 'not a whit'. The point is that the two boys are equals in size and age, so the insult has no basis but race. Some readers hear a glance at 'white' in the word, a reading that may be over-subtle but fits the poem's compressed irony.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'whit, n.'

  7. The slur ll. 7-8

    The speaker's friendly smile meets a double insult, childish (sticking out the tongue) and racial. Cullen places the word at the end of the stanza as a rhyme for 'bigger', so the form itself delivers the blow. Critics such as Trudier Harris have called the poem one of Cullen's most effective, and its plainness has made it a standard teaching text on how racism is learned and suffered in childhood.

    Trudier Harris, quoted in Wikipedia, 'Incident (poem)'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974)

  8. What memory keeps ll. 9-12

    The final stanza enlarges the scene in time: 'the whole of Baltimore' over seven months, an implied store of sights and pleasures, has been erased by a single moment. The understatement ('That's all that I remember') leaves the emotional meaning to the reader. 'May' to 'December' also suggests youth to age, as if the incident has shadowed the speaker's life.

    Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); Houston A. Baker Jr., A Many-Colored Coat of Dreams: The Poetry of Countee Cullen (Broadside Press, 1974); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Anchor/Doubleday, 1991), introduction
