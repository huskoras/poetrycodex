Religion

Yet Do I Marvel

by Countee Cullen 1903–1946

From Color (1925)

Religion

I doubt not God is good, well-meaning, kind,
And did He stoop to quibble could tell why
The little buried mole continues blind,
Why flesh that mirrors Him must some day die,
Make plain the reason tortured Tantalus
Is baited by the fickle fruit, declare
If merely brute caprice dooms Sisyphus
To struggle up a never-ending stair.
Inscrutable His ways are, and immune
To catechism by a mind too strewn
With petty cares to slightly understand
What awful brain compels His awful hand.
Yet do I marvel at this curious thing:
To make a poet black, and bid him sing!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion l. 1

    Countee Cullen (1903-1946), raised in Harlem by the Reverend Frederick Cullen of Salem Methodist Episcopal Church, published Color in 1925, the year he graduated from New York University. 'Yet Do I Marvel' became his best-known poem. Its opening is a formal profession of faith, 'I doubt not', whose emphatic negative already hints at the doubt it denies. The poem is a theodicy, a meditation on why a good God permits suffering, in the manner of Job, and Cullen's command of traditional English verse was central to his programme of proving Black poets masters of the canonical forms. Gerald Early's edition collects his work.

    Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Doubleday/Anchor, 1991), introduction; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'

  2. stoop to quibble l. 2

    The syntax is conditional: '[God] could tell why ... did He stoop to quibble', i.e. if He would lower Himself to argue details. 'Quibble' means to raise petty objections or evade with wordplay, so the speaker suggests ironically that asking God to explain is beneath Him, and also that the explanations might be mere quibbles. The tone is courteous but barbed, the first of many small ironies.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quibble, v.'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  3. the buried mole l. 3

    Moles have tiny eyes, and folk belief held them to be blind; they live 'buried' in earth, a living image of the grave. The mole is the first of four examples of apparently purposeless suffering, and the most humble; Isaiah 2:20 consigns idols 'to the moles and to the bats', creatures of darkness. Critics have suggested that the blind, buried creature also prefigures the Black poet kept in obscurity, though the poem does not say so until its last line.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 2:20; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'

  4. flesh that mirrors Him l. 4

    Genesis 1:27 says God created man 'in his own image', and so human flesh 'mirrors Him'. The paradox is that this likeness of the eternal God must 'some day die'. EBSCO's summary notes the irony that the godlike body is mortal. The line moves from the animal (the mole) to the human, preparing for the mythological examples that follow, and it gives the theodicy its sharpest form: if humanity is God's image, its death seems to diminish God.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 1:27; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'

  5. Tantalus ll. 5-6

    In the Odyssey (11.582-92) Odysseus sees Tantalus standing in a pool that drains away when he stoops to drink, beneath fruit trees whose boughs the wind tosses away whenever he reaches. His crimes vary in the tradition: he served his son Pelops to the gods, or stole their nectar, or betrayed their secrets. Cullen omits the crime, so that the punishment seems arbitrary; EBSCO's summary notes this omission. 'Baited by the fickle fruit' makes the fruit a lure, with alliteration on f. Tantalus is an apt figure for desire perpetually frustrated.

    Homer, Odyssey 11.582-600; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tantalize, v.'; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'

  6. Sisyphus ll. 7-8

    In the Odyssey (11.593-600) Sisyphus heaves a great stone up a hill, which always rolls back just before the top. His offence, in the tradition, was cunning against the gods, including cheating death. Cullen again omits the crime and alters the myth: the hill becomes 'a never-ending stair', an image of aspiration and endless labour, perhaps suggesting social climbing or the 'stairs' of advancement denied to Black Americans. 'Brute caprice' (blind, arbitrary whim) is the poem's strongest accusation, put as a question.

    Homer, Odyssey 11.582-600; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  7. Inscrutable His ways l. 9

    Here the poem turns from questioning to the orthodox answer: God's ways are beyond human understanding. The phrasing recalls Romans 11:33 and Isaiah 55:8-9 ('my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways'), and the familiar hymn by William Cowper, 'God moves in a mysterious way / His wonders to perform' (1773). The word 'Inscrutable' (Latin scrutari, to search) is placed first for emphasis. Whether the speaker accepts this answer or mocks it is the poem's central question of tone.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Romans 11:33; Isaiah 55:8-9; William Cowper, 'Light Shining out of Darkness' (Olney Hymns, 1779); Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  8. catechism l. 10

    A catechism is a manual of religious instruction in question-and-answer form, and 'to catechise' is to question closely. Cullen inverts the usual order: here a human would catechise God, questioning Him, and God is 'immune'. The mind that would question Him is 'too strewn / With petty cares', scattered and cluttered with daily concerns, 'to slightly understand'. The self-deprecation may be ironic: the questions just asked (mole, death, Tantalus, Sisyphus) are hardly petty.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'catechism, n.'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984)

  9. awful l. 12

    'Awful' meant 'inspiring awe or dread' before it came to mean merely 'bad'. Both senses hover over the line, which credits God with a brain and a hand (EBSCO notes the anthropomorphism). The line echoes Blake's 'The Tyger': 'What immortal hand or eye ... What the hand? ... What dread hand? ... In what furnace was thy brain?', a poem that also asks what creator could make both the lamb and the tiger. Cullen's repeated 'awful' is a compressed version of Blake's awed questioning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'awful, adj.'; William Blake, 'The Tyger', Songs of Experience (1794), in G. Keynes, ed., Blake: Complete Writings; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'

  10. curious l. 13

    'Curious' now usually means 'strange', but its older sense was 'made with care and skill, intricate' (Latin curiosus, careful). Psalm 139:14-15 thanks God, 'I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works ... I was made in secret, and curiously wrought'. The psalm's vocabulary ('marvellous', 'curiously') sits behind Cullen's 'marvel' and 'curious thing', which makes the Black poet both a wonderful work of God and a strange anomaly. 'Marvel' likewise means both admire and wonder at, perhaps with disbelief.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'curious, adj.'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 139:14-15

  11. the final couplet ll. 13-14

    The couplet suddenly turns the general theodicy into a personal one, the most baffling of God's ways being the creation of a Black poet. Readers differ sharply on the tone. Many read it as a lament: as with Tantalus and Sisyphus, the poet is set a task that society makes impossible, singing for an audience that denies his humanity. Others read it as an exclamation of wonder or even pride, the resolution of a paradox rather than a complaint. A biblical subtext may be Psalm 137:3-4, where captors 'required of us a song ... How shall we sing the LORD's song in a strange land?' The ambiguity has made the line one of the most quoted of the Harlem Renaissance.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 137:3-4; Darwin T. Turner, 'Countee Cullen: The Lost Ariel', in In a Minor Chord (Southern Illinois UP, 1971); Gerald Early, ed., My Soul's High Song: The Collected Writings of Countee Cullen (Doubleday/Anchor, 1991), introduction; Modern American Poetry site, essay on 'Yet Do I Marvel' (reading of the poem as the resolution of a paradox)

  12. The sonnet form ll. 1-14

    The poem rhymes abab cdcd (kind/why/blind/die; Tantalus/declare/Sisyphus/stair), then in couplets (immune/strewn; understand/hand; thing/sing). It combines the Shakespearean sonnet's quatrains and final couplet with a sestet made of couplets, and its argument is Petrarchan in shape: the octave asks questions, l. 9 ('Inscrutable') turns to the pious answer, and the last couplet springs a second, decisive turn. Cullen's mastery of traditional form, much influenced by Keats, was central to his poetics: he wished to be judged as a poet, not as a 'Negro poet'.

    EBSCO Research Starters, 'Yet Do I Marvel by Countee Cullen'; Alan R. Shucard, Countee Cullen (Twayne, 1984); David E. Goldweber, 'Cullen, Keats, and the Privileged Liar', Papers on Language and Literature 38 (2002)
