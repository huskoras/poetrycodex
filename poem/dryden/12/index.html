Mac Flecknoe

by John Dryden 1631–1700

Arts & Sciences

All human things are subject to decay,
And when fate summons, monarchs must obey.
This Flecknoe found, who, like Augustus, young
Was call'd to empire, and had govern'd long;
In prose and verse, was own'd, without dispute,
Through all the realms of Nonsense, absolute.
This aged prince, now flourishing in peace,
And blest with issue of a large increase;
Worn out with business, did at length debate
To settle the succession of the state:
And, pondering which of all his sons was fit
To reign, and wage immortal war with wit,
Cried, 'Tis resolved; for nature pleads, that he
Should only rule, who most resembles me.
Shadwell alone my perfect image bears,
Mature in dulness from his tender years:
Shadwell alone, of all my sons, is he
Who stands confirm'd in full stupidity.
The rest to some faint meaning make pretence,
But Shadwell never deviates into sense.
Some beams of wit on other souls may fall,
Strike through, and make a lucid interval;
But Shadwell's genuine night admits no ray,
His rising fogs prevail upon the day.
Besides, his goodly fabric fills the eye,
And seems design'd for thoughtless majesty:
Thoughtless as monarch oaks, that shade the plain,
And, spread in solemn state, supinely reign.
Heywood and Shirley were but types of thee,
Thou last great prophet of tautology.
Even I, a dunce of more renown than they,
Was sent before but to prepare thy way;
And, coarsely clad in Norwich drugget, came
To teach the nations in thy greater name.
My warbling lute, the lute I whilom strung,
When to king John of Portugal I sung,
Was but the prelude to that glorious day,
When thou on silver Thames didst cut thy way,
With well-timed oars before the royal barge,
Swell'd with the pride of thy celestial charge;
And big with hymn, commander of an host,
The like was ne'er in Epsom blankets toss'd.
Methinks I see the new Arion sail,
The lute still trembling underneath thy nail.
At thy well-sharpen'd thumb, from shore to shore
The trebles squeak for fear, the basses roar:
Echoes from Pissing-Alley, Shadwell call,
And Shadwell they resound from Aston-Hall.
About thy boat the little fishes throng,
As at the morning toast that floats along.
Sometimes, as prince of thy harmonious band,
Thou wield'st thy papers in thy threshing hand.
St Andre's feet ne'er kept more equal time,
Not even the feet of thy own Psyche's rhyme:
Though they in number as in sense excel;
So just, so like tautology, they fell,
That, pale with envy, Singleton forswore
The lute and sword, which he in triumph bore,
And vow'd he ne'er would act Villerius more.

Here stopp'd the good old sire, and wept for joy,
In silent raptures of the hopeful boy.
All arguments, but most his plays, persuade,
That for anointed dulness he was made.

Close to the walls which fair Augusta bind
(The fair Augusta much to fears inclined),
An ancient fabric raised to inform the sight,
There stood of yore, and Barbican it hight:
A watch-tower once; but now, so fate ordains,
Of all the pile an empty name remains:
From its old ruins brothel-houses rise,
Scenes of lewd loves, and of polluted joys,
Where their vast courts the mother-strumpets keep,
And, undisturb'd by watch, in silence sleep.
Near these a Nursery erects its head,
Where queens are form'd, and future heroes bred;
Where unfledged actors learn to laugh and cry,
Where infant punks their tender voices try,
And little Maximins the gods defy.
Great Fletcher never treads in buskins here,
Nor greater Jonson dares in socks appear;
But gentle Simkin just reception finds
Amidst this monument of vanish'd minds:
Pure clinches the suburban muse affords,
And Panton waging harmless war with words.
Here Flecknoe, as a place to fame well known,
Ambitiously design'd his Shadwell's throne.
For ancient Decker prophesied long since,
That in this pile should reign a mighty prince,
Born for a scourge of wit, and flail of sense:
To whom true dulness should some Psyches owe,
But worlds of Misers from his pen should flow;
Humourists and hypocrites it should produce,
Whole Raymond families, and tribes of Bruce.

Now Empress Fame had publish'd the renown
Of Shadwell's coronation through the town.
Roused by report of fame, the nations meet,
From near Bunhill, and distant Watling Street.
No Persian carpets spread the imperial way,
But scatter'd limbs of mangled poets lay:
From dusty shops neglected authors come,
Martyrs of pies, and reliques of the bum.
Much Heywood, Shirley, Ogleby there lay,
But loads of Shadwell almost choked the way.
Bilk'd stationers for yeomen stood prepared,
And Herringman was captain of the guard.
The hoary prince in majesty appear'd,
High on a throne of his own labours rear'd.
At his right hand our young Ascanius sate,
Rome's other hope, and pillar of the state.
His brows thick fogs, instead of glories, grace,
And lambent dulness play'd around his face.
As Hannibal did to the altars come,
Sworn by his fire, a mortal foe to Rome;
So Shadwell swore, nor should his vow be vain,
That he till death true dulness would maintain;
And, in his father's right, and realm's defence,
Ne'er to have peace with wit, nor truce with sense.
The king himself the sacred unction made,
As king by office, and as priest by trade.
In his sinister hand, instead of ball,
He placed a mighty mug of potent ale;
Love's Kingdom to his right he did convey,
At once his sceptre and his rule of sway;
Whose righteous lore the prince had practised young,
And from whose loins recorded Psyche sprung.
His temples, last, with poppies were o'erspread,
That nodding seem'd to consecrate his head.
Just at the point of time, if fame not lie,
On his left hand twelve reverend owls did fly.
So Romulus, 'tis sung, by Tiber's brook,
Presage of sway from twice six vultures took.
The admiring throng loud acclamations make,
And omens of his future empire take.
The sire then shook the honours of his head,
And from his brows damps of oblivion shed,
Full on the filial dulness: long he stood,
Repelling from his breast the raging god;
At length burst out in this prophetic mood:

Heavens bless my son, from Ireland let him reign
To far Barbadoes on the western main;
Of his dominion may no end be known,
And greater than his father's be his throne;
Beyond Love's kingdom let him stretch his pen!--
He paused, and all the people cried, Amen.
Then thus continued he: My son, advance
Still in new impudence, new ignorance.
Success let others teach, learn thou from me
Pangs without birth, and fruitless industry.
Let Virtuosos in five years be writ;
Yet not one thought accuse thy toil of wit.
Let gentle George in triumph tread the stage,
Make Dorimant betray, and Loveit rage;
Let Cully, Cockwood, Fopling, charm the pit,
And in their folly show the writer's wit.
Yet still thy fools shall stand in thy defence,
And justify their author's want of sense.
Let them be all by thy own model made
Of dulness, and desire no foreign aid;
That they to future ages may be known,
Not copies drawn, but issue of thy own.
Nay, let thy men of wit too be the same,
All full of thee, and differing but in name.
But let no alien Sedley interpose,
To lard with wit thy hungry Epsom prose.
And when false flowers of rhetoric thou wouldst cull,
Trust nature, do not labour to be dull;
But write thy best, and top; and, in each line,
Sir Formal's oratory will be thine:
Sir Formal, though unsought, attends thy quill,
And does thy northern dedications fill.
Nor let false friends seduce thy mind to fame,
By arrogating Jonson's hostile name.
Let Father Flecknoe fire thy mind with praise,
And uncle Ogleby thy envy raise.
Thou art my blood, where Jonson has no part:
What share have we in nature, or in art?
Where did his wit on learning fix a brand,
And rail at arts he did not understand?
Where made he love in prince Nicander's vein,
Or swept the dust in Psyche's humble strain?
Where sold he bargains, whip-stitch, kiss my a--e,
Promised a play, and dwindled to a farce?
When did his muse from Fletcher scenes purloin,
As thou whole Etheridge dost transfuse to thine?
But so transfused, as oil and waters flow,
His always floats above, thine sinks below.
This is thy province, this thy wondrous way,
New humours to invent for each new play:
This is that boasted bias of thy mind,
By which one way to dulness 'tis inclined:
Which makes thy writings lean on one side still,
And, in all changes, that way bends thy will.
Nor let thy mountain-belly make pretence
Of likeness; thine's a tympany of sense.
A tun of man in thy large bulk is writ,
But sure thou'rt but a kilderkin of wit.
Like mine, thy gentle numbers feebly creep;
Thy tragic muse gives smiles, thy comic sleep.
With whate'er gall thou sett'st thyself to write,
Thy inoffensive satires never bite.
In thy felonious heart though venom lies,
It does but touch thy Irish pen, and dies.
Thy genius calls thee not to purchase fame
In keen Iambics, but mild Anagram.
Leave writing plays, and choose for thy command,
Some peaceful province in Acrostic land.
There thou mayst wings display and altars raise,
And torture one poor word ten thousand ways.
Or, if thou wouldst thy different talents suit,
Set thy own songs, and sing them to thy lute.

He said; but his last words were scarcely heard:
For Bruce and Longville had a trap prepared,
And down they sent the yet declaiming bard.
Sinking he left his drugget robe behind,
Borne upwards by a subterranean wind.
The mantle fell to the young prophet's part,
With double portion of his father's art.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening; date and occasion l. 1

    The first couplet sounds like the start of a solemn state poem on mortality and royal succession, and the comedy lies in the gap between that register and its subject. Mac Flecknoe was probably written in 1676-77, when Shadwell was loudly claiming Ben Jonson as his model; it circulated in manuscript, was printed without authority in October 1682 (with a title page calling it a satire on the 'True-Blue-Protestant Poet, T.S.', a label from the Exclusion Crisis), and appeared in an authorised text in Miscellany Poems (1684). Older critics tied the poem to the political quarrel of 1682; manuscript evidence assembled by David Vieth and others points instead to a literary quarrel of the mid-1670s. It is the first great English mock-heroic and the model for Pope's Dunciad.

    Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; Folger Shakespeare Library, Union First Line Index of English Verse, entry for 'All human things are subject to decay'; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.; D. M. McKeithan, 'The Occasion of MacFlecknoe', PMLA 47 (1932)

  2. Flecknoe; like Augustus l. 3

    Flecknoe published verse, letters and plays (Love's Kingdom, 1664, later serves as Shadwell's sceptre) and had already been mocked by Marvell in 'Flecknoe, an English Priest at Rome'. Like Augustus, who entered Roman politics at nineteen and ruled until AD 14, he came to power young and 'govern'd long'; Restoration panegyric habitually compared Charles II to Augustus, so the simile also mocks a courtly habit. Why Dryden chose Flecknoe as Shadwell's 'father' has never been fully explained (Hammond calls it a puzzle); he was a byword for copious bad writing, and his priesthood allows the jokes on unction and prophecy. The title uses the Gaelic patronymic 'Mac', making the Protestant Whig Shadwell son of an Irish Catholic priest.

    Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; Paul Hammond, 'Flecknoe and Mac Flecknoe', in The Making of Restoration Poetry (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2006); The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.

  3. Shadwell l. 15

    Shadwell wrote humours comedies such as The Sullen Lovers (1668), The Humorists (1671), Epsom Wells (1672) and The Virtuoso (1676), and an opera, Psyche (1675). In his prefaces he defended Jonson against Dryden's criticisms and hinted that Dryden's comedy was thin. Dryden's answer makes Shadwell the 'perfect image' of the dull Flecknoe, a parody of the Son as image of the Father (Hebrews 1:3). Lines 15-20 build the portrait through anaphora ('Shadwell alone') and climax in the famous paradox that he 'never deviates into sense'. After 1688 Shadwell replaced the Catholic Dryden as Laureate, an irony later readers often note.

    Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; Ben Jonson's Literary Record (Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson Online): 'Dryden: Shadwell not the heir to Jonson, 1676'; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Bible (KJV), Hebrews 1:3

  4. lucid interval l. 22

    In early modern law a 'lucid interval' was a temporary recovery of reason, during which a lunatic could, for instance, make a valid will (OED). Dryden turns the phrase into an image of light: beams of wit 'strike through' other minds, but Shadwell's 'genuine night' (genuine in its Latin sense of inborn) admits no ray, and his 'rising fogs' overcome the day. The imagery of fog and darkness returns at the coronation and in the 'damps of oblivion' that Flecknoe sheds. The following simile of 'monarch oaks' praises only Shadwell's bulk; 'supinely' means lying on the back, inertly.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lucid interval'; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  5. types ... prepare thy way l. 29

    In Christian typology a 'type' is an earlier person or event that prefigures a later fulfilment. Dryden makes the prolific older dramatists Thomas Heywood and James Shirley 'types' of Shadwell, and casts Flecknoe as a John the Baptist 'sent before' to 'prepare thy way' (Matthew 3:3, quoting Isaiah 40:3). The Baptist wore camel's hair (Matthew 3:4); Flecknoe wears Norwich drugget, a coarse woollen cloth, which returns at the end as the mantle he leaves behind. 'Prophet of tautology' (needless repetition) mocks Shadwell's repetitive humours. The sacred parody is sustained to the final allusion to Elijah and Elisha.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:3; Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:4; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.

  6. the new Arion l. 43

    Herodotus (1.23-24) tells how Arion, thrown overboard by sailors, was carried ashore by a dolphin. Flecknoe's own playing before King John IV of Portugal (whose patronage he boasted of) was only a 'prelude' to this triumph. The mock-epic diminishes everything: the 'trebles squeak', the echoes come from Pissing-Alley (a real London lane), the 'little fishes' throng to him as to the 'morning toast' floating on the river (toast was dipped in ale), and he beats time with his papers in a 'threshing hand'. The echo-device, in which places resound the hero's name, parodies pastoral and heroic convention; the line about 'Epsom blankets' glances at a blanket-tossing farce in Shadwell's comedies (Lynch cites The Virtuoso; other editors point to Epsom Wells).

    Herodotus, Histories 1.23-24 (Perseus Digital Library); Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  7. St Andre; Psyche; Singleton and Villerius l. 53

    The pun on 'feet' joins dance steps with metrical feet: the verse of Psyche keeps time like a dancing master and its rhythm is as monotonous as tautology. Psyche, adapted from Moliere with music by Matthew Locke, was one of the costliest spectacles of the Restoration stage and was much ridiculed; Dryden returns to it repeatedly. So jealous is Singleton that he renounces 'the lute and sword' and vows never to play Villerius again, the Grand Master in Sir William Davenant's The Siege of Rhodes (1656), the first English opera. The point is that Jonson's would-be heir sought fame through music and spectacle rather than wit.

    Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.

  8. fair Augusta much to fears inclined l. 65

    Augusta was a Roman name for London. Many editors, Lynch among them, gloss the city's fears as the hysteria of the Popish Plot, the invented Jesuit conspiracy announced by Titus Oates in 1678. If the poem was written in 1676-77, as most scholars now think, the line either refers to earlier anxieties (plague, the Great Fire of 1666, recurrent fears of popery) or was added in revision before the 1682 printing. The parenthesis keeps the tone light: the 'fair' city is timorous, a hint at the credulity that Shadwell's Whig allies exploited.

    Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.; D. M. McKeithan, 'The Occasion of MacFlecknoe', PMLA 47 (1932)

  9. Barbican; the mother-strumpets l. 72

    A barbican is an outer fortification guarding a gate; the old tower near Aldersgate had given its name to a poor quarter of brothels. Dryden describes it in mock-epic topography ('There stood of yore') and Spenserian diction ('hight', was called). In Book I of Abraham Cowley's Davideis (1656) the deep is a court where the mother-waters keep and sleep in silence; Dryden keeps the rhythm and nearly the words, substituting bawds for waters and the night-watch for the moon. Johnson noted that the Davideis had been imitated once, by Dryden in Mac Flecknoe. The pun on 'watch' (tower and constable) marks the decline from defence to vice.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Cowley', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81), on the Davideis (Project Gutenberg); The Joyce Project, note on 'her courts' (Ulysses, Proteus), tracing Dryden's parody of Cowley's Davideis; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hight'; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.

  10. Nursery; little Maximins l. 74

    The Nursery was licensed in the early Restoration to train young actors for the patent companies; the RPO note dates it to 1662. Dryden's catalogue makes it a school for 'unfledged actors' and 'infant punks' (punk is slang for a prostitute), with the anaphora of 'Where' imitating epic descriptions of royal education. 'Little Maximins' refers to the blaspheming emperor Maximin in Dryden's own heroic play Tyrannic Love (1669), a sign that Dryden can laugh at his own bombast.

    Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  11. buskins ... socks; Simkin; clinches l. 79

    Greek and Roman tragic actors wore the high boot (cothurnus, English buskin), comic actors the light shoe (soccus); Milton uses the pair in L'Allegro ('Jonson's learned sock') and Il Penseroso ('the buskin'd stage'). Dryden ranks 'Great Fletcher' and 'greater Jonson', the masters of the pre-war stage, above the Nursery's fare, which welcomes 'gentle Simkin', a clownish cobbler in drolls, and Panton, a noted punster. 'Clinches' are puns or quibbles (OED), which Restoration critics treated as the lowest form of wit.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clinch'; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; John Milton, L'Allegro l. 132 and Il Penseroso l. 102 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  12. ancient Decker; Misers, Raymond, Bruce l. 87

    Dekker (c.1572-1632) sided against Jonson in the 'War of the Theatres', so it is apt that he foretells an anti-Jonsonian 'scourge of wit, and flail of sense'. The prophecy names Shadwell's works: Psyche (1675), The Miser (1672), The Humorists (1671) and The Hypocrite, with Raymond from The Humorists and Bruce from The Virtuoso. The genealogical catalogue parodies the prophecies of a hero's descendants in Virgil (Aeneid 6).

    Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  13. Martyrs of pies l. 101

    Instead of Persian carpets, the coronation route is strewn with 'scatter'd limbs of mangled poets', torn sheets of unsold books. Waste paper was used by pastry cooks and in the privy, and the vocabulary of martyrdom and relics jokes on the Catholic colouring of the ceremony. Heywood, Shirley and John Ogilby (translator of Virgil and Homer) supply much of the litter, but Shadwell's works almost block the way. The 'bilk'd stationers' (booksellers cheated by unsaleable stock) form the guard under Henry Herringman, publisher of both Shadwell and Dryden. Pope reused the image in The Dunciad.

    The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  14. our young Ascanius ... Rome's other hope; lambent dulness; Hannibal l. 108

    In the Aeneid Ascanius (Iulus) is the heir in whom Rome's future rests, called in Book 12 the 'second hope of great Rome'. The next couplet parodies Aeneid 2.682-84, where a harmless flame plays around the boy's head as an omen of greatness: here 'lambent dulness' plays round Shadwell's face and fogs replace the halo. 'Lambent' (licking, playing lightly) is a Latinism and an oxymoron with dulness. Shadwell then swears eternal war on wit as the boy Hannibal swore at an altar to be Rome's enemy (Livy 21.1).

    Virgil, Aeneid 12.168 (The Latin Library); Virgil, Aeneid 2.682-84 (The Latin Library); Livy, Ab urbe condita 21.1 (The Latin Library); Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto

  15. The coronation regalia; twelve owls l. 121

    At a coronation the monarch is anointed and holds the orb in the left hand, the sceptre in the right. Flecknoe, 'king by office, and as priest by trade', anoints his son himself; in Shadwell's 'sinister' (left, and ill-omened) hand he places a mug of ale instead of the orb, and in the right his own Love's Kingdom (1664). Poppies are the flowers of sleep. The omen of twelve owls, birds of night and solemn stupidity, parodies Livy 1.7, where Romulus wins the kingship by seeing twelve vultures to Remus' six. Readers would have remembered Charles II's coronation of 1661.

    Livy, Ab urbe condita 1.7 (The Latin Library); The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.

  16. from Ireland ... To far Barbadoes l. 139

    Jupiter promises Rome an empire without end (Aeneid 1.279), and Gabriel says of Christ that 'of his kingdom there shall be no end' (Luke 1:33). 'Of his dominion may no end be known' echoes both. The boundaries, Ireland and Barbados, were places of exile, plantation and transportation; Ireland also prepares the jibe at Shadwell's 'Irish pen'. 'Beyond Love's kingdom' puns on Flecknoe's play, and the people's 'Amen' turns the blessing into mock liturgy. Flecknoe then 'repels the raging god' like the Sibyl in Aeneid 6, an inspired prophet of dullness.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 1:33; Virgil, Aeneid 1.278-79 (The Latin Library); The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.

  17. gentle George; Dorimant, Loveit, Cully, Cockwood, Fopling l. 151

    Cully comes from Etherege's The Comical Revenge (1664), Lady Cockwood from She Would If She Could (1668), and Dorimant, Mrs Loveit and Sir Fopling Flutter from The Man of Mode (March 1676), a reference that helps date the poem. In Etherege even the fools display the author's wit; in Shadwell they only 'justify their author's want of sense'. Flecknoe also forbids any 'alien Sedley' to 'lard' (enrich, as a cook threads fat into lean meat) Shadwell's 'hungry Epsom prose': Sir Charles Sedley wrote a prologue for Epsom Wells (1672) and was rumoured to have improved it. The passage restates Dryden's side of the wit-versus-humours quarrel.

    The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lard'

  18. Sir Formal; northern dedications l. 168

    In The Virtuoso (1676) Sir Formal Trifle is a pompous rhetorician who cannot stop talking. Flecknoe tells Shadwell he need not 'labour to be dull': if he simply writes his best, Sir Formal's style will come unsought. The 'northern dedications' are Shadwell's dedications to William Cavendish, Duke of Newcastle, whose seat was in Nottinghamshire and who was himself a playwright and patron. The Virtuoso supplies the trap-door trick on which the poem ends.

    Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.

  19. Jonson's hostile name l. 172

    In prefaces to The Sullen Lovers, The Humorists and The Virtuoso, Shadwell praised Jonson's comedy of humours and defended it against Dryden. Here Flecknoe calls Jonson's name 'hostile' to their dynasty: 'Thou art my blood, where Jonson has no part.' The rhetorical questions that follow list Shadwell's faults (railing at learning, writing love in Prince Nicander's vein, promising a play and producing a farce, plagiarising Etherege) and ask where Jonson ever did any such thing. The proof by negation lets Dryden praise Jonson while degrading his self-appointed heir. Shadwell's lumbering wit is, like oil under water, always below Etherege's.

    Ben Jonson's Literary Record (Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson Online): 'Dryden: Shadwell not the heir to Jonson, 1676'; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.

  20. prince Nicander; whip-stitch l. 179

    Psyche (1675) gives Prince Nicander extravagant love-speeches, and 'swept the dust in Psyche's humble strain' mocks the opera's lowlier verse; Dryden asks whether Jonson ever wrote like that. To 'sell bargains' was to answer an innocent question with a coarse retort, a low form of repartee; 'whip-stitch' (in a moment) and 'kiss my a--e' are cant catch-phrases of Shadwell's comic characters, which editors trace to The Virtuoso. The dashes are the printer's own censorship, kept in this text.

    Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'whip-stitch'; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.

  21. bias l. 189

    Shadwell prided himself on inventing new 'humours', after Jonson's theory that a character is governed by a ruling inclination. Dryden turns the idea against him: his one humour is a bias, the weight set into one side of a wooden bowl so that it runs in a curve (OED). His writings therefore 'lean on one side still', and whatever he attempts bends the same way. The conceit is sustained across six lines, a fine example of argument carried by the heroic couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bias'; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  22. tympany; tun; kilderkin l. 196

    Flecknoe denies that Shadwell's 'mountain-belly' is a true likeness of his own: it is a 'tympany of sense', a morbid swelling, especially of the abdomen, caused by wind (OED). The cask images play on Shadwell's girth and drinking: a tun held about 252 gallons, a kilderkin, half a barrel, only 16 or 18. The antithesis 'tun of man' / 'kilderkin of wit' is a showpiece of Dryden's balanced couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tympany'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kilderkin'; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  23. Irish pen l. 202

    According to legend St Patrick drove the snakes from Ireland, and it was said that nothing venomous could survive there. The jibe also extends the fiction of Shadwell's descent from the Irish priest Flecknoe, an insult to a vehemently Protestant Whig. His satires are 'inoffensive' and 'never bite', unlike Dryden's own. Flecknoe concludes that his son's genius lies not in 'keen Iambics' (satire, after the Greek iambic lampoon) but in harmless verbal toys.

    The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.; The Works of John Dryden, vol. II: Poems 1681-1684, ed. H. T. Swedenberg Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on Mac Flecknoe, ad loc.; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University

  24. Acrostic land; wings and altars l. 206

    An acrostic spells a word with the initial letters of its lines; a pattern poem is laid out on the page in a shape, like George Herbert's 'Easter Wings' and 'The Altar' (1633), a fashion of the early seventeenth century. By the Restoration such verbal games were regarded as false wit, the 'torture' of one word 'ten thousand ways', a judgement Addison later developed in Spectator 58-63. Flecknoe's last advice, to set and sing his own songs to the lute, brings the poem back to Shadwell's music.

    Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Joseph Addison, The Spectator nos. 58-63 (1711), on false wit; The Poems of John Dryden, ed. James Kinsley, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), commentary ad loc.

  25. The trap-door; the mantle and double portion l. 216

    In The Virtuoso the wits Bruce and Longvil drop the orator Sir Formal through a trap-door in mid-speech; Shadwell's own stage trick now silences the 'yet declaiming' Flecknoe. In 2 Kings 2:9-13 Elisha asks for a double portion of Elijah's spirit, Elijah is taken up in a whirlwind, and his mantle falls to his successor. Dryden inverts the miracle: the prophet goes down, while his drugget robe rises on a flatulent 'subterranean wind', and the young prophet inherits twice his father's 'art'. The couplet completes the frame of succession and the biblical parody that began with the forerunner and the 'types'.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Kings 2:9; Bible (KJV), 2 Kings 2:13; Representative Poetry Online, Mac Flecknoe, notes after Douglas Grant's Reynard Library edition (1952), University of Toronto; Jack Lynch, ed., Mac Flecknoe (annotated online text from the 1684 Miscellany Poems), Rutgers University
