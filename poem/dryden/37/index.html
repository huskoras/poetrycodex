Death & Dying

To the Memory of Mr Oldham

by John Dryden 1631–1700

Death & Dying

Farewell, too little, and too lately known,
Whom I began to think, and call my own:
For sure our souls were near allied, and thine
Cast in the same poetic mould with mine!
One common note on either lyre did strike,
And knaves and fools we both abhorr'd alike.
To the same goal did both our studies drive;
The last set out, the soonest did arrive.
Thus Nisus fell upon the slippery place,
Whilst his young friend performed, and won the race.
O early ripe! to thy abundant store
What could advancing age have added more?
It might (what nature never gives the young)
Have taught the smoothness of thy native tongue.
But satire needs not those, and wit will shine
Through the harsh cadence of a rugged line.
A noble error, and but seldom made,
When poets are by too much force betray'd.
Thy generous fruits, though gather'd ere their prime,
Still show'd a quickness; and maturing time
But mellows what we write, to the dull sweets of rhyme.
Once more, hail! and farewell, farewell, thou young,
But, ah! too short, Marcellus of our tongue!
Thy brows with ivy, and with laurels bound;
But fate and gloomy night encompass thee around.

Study notes by the editors

  1. To the Memory of Mr Oldham title; l. 1

    John Oldham, son of a Nonconformist minister and an Oxford graduate, was an usher at Whitgift School, Croydon, and a tutor before settling in London, where he became one of Dryden's circle. He died of smallpox at Holme Pierrepont, the Nottinghamshire seat of his patron the Earl of Kingston, on 9 December 1683. Dryden's twenty-five lines first appeared among the commendatory poems in the Remains of Mr. John Oldham in Verse and Prose (1684). The opening line, with its pair of 'too' phrases, sets the poem's double grief: the friendship was brief and it began late, when Dryden was already in his fifties. The poem is one of the most admired short elegies in English, and much of its force comes from its compression of Roman models (Virgil, Catullus) into a personal tribute.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy; Wikipedia, 'John Oldham (poet)'; Anthony Wood, Athenae Oxonienses, vol. 2, entry on Oldham

  2. too lately l. 1

    The adverb is not the modern 'recently' but the older sense of 'late', 'at a late time' (OED, lately, adv.). Dryden and Oldham seem to have met only around 1681-82, so the friendship was short both because Oldham died young and because it started late. The paired phrasing ('too little, and too lately') has the balanced, almost epigrammatic shape of the heroic couplet itself, and it anticipates the closing apostrophe, where the farewell is repeated. The phrase also quietly carries an apology, a confession that the older poet recognised Oldham's merit belatedly.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'lately, adv.'; Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; James Kinsley (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), notes to the poem

  3. near allied l. 3

    'Allied' carries the sense of kinship by blood or marriage as well as partnership. Dryden had used the phrase memorably in Absalom and Achitophel (1681): 'Great wits are sure to madness near allied'. Here the kinship is poetic: the two souls were 'cast in the same poetic mould', an image from metal-founding, as if both were poured into one casting. The claim matters because the poem will go on to distinguish them (Oldham rugged, Dryden smooth): the likeness is of temper and purpose, not of style.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'allied, adj.'; John Dryden, Absalom and Achitophel (1681), l. 163; Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'

  4. Cast in the same poetic mould l. 4

    To 'cast' is to pour molten metal into a 'mould'. The metaphor makes poetic gifts something innate, fixed at the making of a person, and joins the two writers as products of a single casting. It fits the period's thinking about 'genius' as a native endowment rather than an acquired skill. Readers have noticed that the image of moulding returns in the poem's later concern with the 'smoothness' that only time and labour can give, the polishing of a cast object; Oldham's metal, on this reading, was right but unfinished.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'cast, v.', 'mould, n.'; Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'

  5. One common note on either lyre l. 5

    The lyre is the conventional instrument of poetry, here oddly applied to satire, which classical theory associated with harsher music. The couplet defines the friendship through a shared moral antipathy: both 'abhorr'd' knaves (the wicked) and fools (the stupid), the twin targets of Restoration satire. Oldham's Satyrs upon the Jesuits (1681), written in the climate of the Popish Plot, and Dryden's Absalom and Achitophel (1681) and MacFlecknoe (printed 1682) belong to the same years. There is an irony critics have noted: Dryden, a future Catholic convert (1685/6), praises the author of the fiercest anti-Jesuit satires of the age, though the poem keeps the common ground general.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy; Harold F. Brooks and Raman Selden (eds.), The Poems of John Oldham (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1987), introduction

  6. the same goal ... the soonest did arrive ll. 7-8

    'Studies' means literary pursuits. The couplet turns on a paradox: Oldham, the younger and later starter, 'arrived' soonest. The goal is double: poetic achievement and, darkly, the end of life. The race prepares the Virgilian simile that follows (Nisus and Euryalus), and it plays on the Latin 'meta', the turning post or finishing post in a Roman race, which was also a figure for the end of life. The line's smooth antithesis ('last ... soonest') shows the very polish the poem will go on to discuss.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; James Kinsley (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), notes to the poem

  7. Nisus ... his young friend ll. 9-10

    In the funeral games for Anchises (Aeneid 5.315-39), Nisus is ahead when he slips on ground wet with the blood of slaughtered bullocks; he then trips Salius so that his beloved young friend Euryalus takes the prize. Dryden makes himself the older Nisus and Oldham the young winner. The simile is tender and also ominous, since readers would remember that in Aeneid 9 the pair die together on a night raid. Commentators differ on the exact weight of the parallel: some stress Dryden's generosity in giving the prize to Oldham; others note that Nisus 'falls' only by accident, so the older poet's precedence is not really surrendered. Dryden later translated the episode in his Aeneis (1697).

    Virgil, Aeneid 5.315-39 (Latin text); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy

  8. O early ripe ... abundant store ll. 11-12

    The image of early ripeness begins a fruit metaphor that runs through the poem to 'generous fruits', 'gather'd ere their prime', and 'mellows'. Early-ripe was proverbially double-edged: things that ripen early were thought to rot early (compare the proverb 'soon ripe, soon rotten'). The rhetorical question is generous, but it is qualified at once: age might have added one thing, 'smoothness'. Critics have long debated how far the praise is double-edged here: the poem praises Oldham's force while implying that the smooth versification Dryden himself perfected is the mark of maturity.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; Paul Hammond, John Oldham and the Renewal of Classical Culture (Cambridge University Press, 1983)

  9. smoothness of thy native tongue ll. 13-14

    'Numbers' and 'smoothness' were the key terms of Restoration verse reform associated with Waller and Denham and perfected, by his own account, by Dryden. The parenthesis ('what nature never gives the young') makes smoothness a gift of age and practice, not genius. This is the poem's central crux: is it pure praise of a rough satirist, or an assertion of Dryden's own superior art? Many readers take the qualification as honest critical judgement within the elegy, in keeping with Dryden's critical prose; others hear a self-regarding note. Either way, the following lines turn the defect into a decorum of the genre.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy; Paul Hammond, John Oldham and the Renewal of Classical Culture (Cambridge University Press, 1983)

  10. satire ... harsh cadence of a rugged line ll. 15-16

    Renaissance and Restoration critics, misled partly by a supposed link between 'satire' and Greek 'satyr', held that satire should be harsh in metre as well as matter; Juvenal and Persius were the models of a rough Roman style, and Donne's satires were admired for their ruggedness. Dryden himself explained in his Discourse concerning the Original and Progress of Satire (1693) that Latin 'satura' meant a mixed dish, not satyrs, and argued that the best satire was polished. Here, in 1684, he grants the harsh cadence as a decorous fault in the genre. 'Wit' means imaginative intelligence, which, like light through a rough surface, 'will shine' through.

    John Dryden, A Discourse concerning the Original and Progress of Satire (1693); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; James Kinsley (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), notes to the poem

  11. A noble error ll. 17-18

    The couplet restates the earlier judgement in the language of critical paradox familiar from classical criticism: a fault of greatness is better than a faultless mediocrity (the idea is developed in the treatise On the Sublime attributed to Longinus, which Dryden knew in Boileau's French version of 1674). 'Seldom made' is a compliment, since most poets err the other way, through weakness. 'Betray'd' personifies force as a traitor within the poet, so that his strength itself led him astray.

    [Longinus], On the Sublime, ch. 33 (on the faults of great writers); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'

  12. quickness l. 20

    'Quick' is the old word for 'alive' (as in 'the quick and the dead'), and 'quickness' meant vitality and mental sharpness; it could also mean pungency or sharpness of taste (OED, quickness). Oldham's fruits, 'gather'd ere their prime', retain the sharp tang of fruit picked young, which is precisely the quality of satire. Time would have 'mellowed' them, but mellowing is double-edged: it leads to 'the dull sweets of rhyme', a striking admission that polish can blunt edge.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'quickness, n.'; Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy

  13. triplet and alexandrine ll. 19-21

    Lines 19-21 rhyme together (prime/time/rhyme), a triplet, a device Dryden used for emphasis, and the third line stretches to six stresses. The lengthened line enacts slow ripening and the cloying 'dull sweets' it describes. The poem's final line is also an alexandrine, so the elegy's two expansions bracket its close. Dryden defended triplets and alexandrines as English licences, though Pope later mocked the needless alexandrine in An Essay on Criticism (1711). Here the effect is plainly deliberate: the most polished metrical moment in the poem speaks of the dullness polish brings.

    Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism (1711), ll. 356-7

  14. hail! and farewell l. 22

    Catullus 101 ends 'atque in perpetuum, frater, ave atque vale' ('and for ever, brother, hail and farewell'). By borrowing the Roman funeral formula Dryden turns the friendship into a kind of brotherhood ('call my own'), and the poem's first word, 'Farewell', returns, now doubled, to close the circle. 'Once more' marks the repetition and the finality: this is the last farewell, as the Roman words were spoken at the last rites.

    Catullus, Carmen 101 (Latin text); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; James Kinsley (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, 4 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958), notes to the poem

  15. Marcellus l. 23

    Marcus Claudius Marcellus, son of Augustus' sister Octavia, married to Julia and marked out as heir, died in 23 BC. At the end of the pageant of future Romans in Aeneid 6 (6.860-86), Aeneas sees a beautiful youth under a cloud of gloom and Anchises laments him, 'tu Marcellus eris' ('you will be Marcellus'). Dryden makes Oldham the Marcellus of English poetry: the young heir who would have inherited the empire of letters. The comparison is loaded, since Dryden, the reigning poet, becomes by implication the Augustus who loses his successor. The parenthetical 'ah!' and the broken rhythm of l. 23 imitate the catch in the voice.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.860-86 (Latin text); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy

  16. ivy ... laurels l. 24

    Horace calls ivy the prize of learned brows (Odes 1.1.29, 'doctarum hederae praemia frontium'), and in Virgil's Eclogues ivy crowns the rising poet (Ecl. 7.25, 8.13). Laurel belongs to Apollo and to triumph. The double garland honours Oldham as poet, but the line is grammatically incomplete (no verb: 'Thy brows [are] bound'), a frozen funeral image, as if the crowned head were a monument. It sets up the final contrast with the 'gloomy night' that surrounds the crowned head.

    Horace, Odes 1.1.29 (Latin text); Virgil, Eclogues 7.25 and 8.13 (Latin text); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'

  17. fate and gloomy night l. 25

    Anchises' lament for Marcellus includes the line 'sed nox atra caput tristi circumvolat umbra' ('but black night flits round his head with its sad shadow', Aen. 6.866). Dryden's last line keeps the Virgilian picture of a head ringed by darkness but sets it against the ivy and laurel of the previous line: the poet's crown and the encircling night are two rings around the same brow. The line is an alexandrine; with its slow monosyllables ('But fate and gloomy night') it closes the poem, rhyming with 'bound' in the line before. Dryden would translate the line himself in 1697.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.866 (Latin text); Paul Hammond (ed.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 2: 1682-1685 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1995), commentary on 'To the Memory of Mr Oldham'; H. T. Swedenberg, Jr. and Vinton A. Dearing (eds.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 2: Poems 1681-1684 (University of California Press, 1972), commentary on the Oldham elegy
