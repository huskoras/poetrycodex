Alexander's Feast; or, The Power of Music

by John Dryden 1631–1700

Arts & Sciences

1 'Twas at the royal feast, for Persia won
By Philip's warlike son:
Aloft in awful state
The godlike hero sate
On his imperial throne:
His valiant peers were placed around;
Their brows with roses and with myrtles bound
(So should desert in arms be crown'd).
The lovely Thais, by his side,
Sate like a blooming Eastern bride
In flower of youth and beauty's pride.
Happy, happy, happy pair!
None but the brave,
None but the brave,
None but the brave deserves the fair.

CHORUS.

Happy, happy, happy pair!
None but the brave,
None but the brave,
None but the brave deserves the fair.

2 Timotheus, placed on high
Amid the tuneful quire,
With flying fingers touch'd the lyre:
The trembling notes ascend the sky,
And heavenly joys inspire.
The song began from Jove,
Who left his blissful seats above
(Such is the power of mighty love).
A dragon's fiery form belied the god:
Sublime on radiant spires he rode,
When he to fair Olympia press'd:
And while he sought her snowy breast:
Then, round her slender waist he curl'd,
And stamp'd an image of himself, a sovereign of the world.
The listening crowd admire the lofty sound,
A present deity, they shout around,
A present deity, the vaulted roofs rebound:
With ravish'd ears
The monarch hears,
Assumes the god,
Affects to nod,
And seems to shake the spheres.

CHORUS.

With ravish'd ears
The monarch hears,
Assumes the god,
Affects to nod,
And seems to shake the spheres.

3 The praise of Bacchus then, the sweet musician sung;
Of Bacchus ever fair and ever young:
The jolly god in triumph comes;
Sound the trumpets; beat the drums;
Flush'd with a purple grace
He shows his honest face:
Now give the hautboys breath; he comes, he comes.
Bacchus, ever fair and young,
Drinking joys did first ordain;
Bacchus' blessings are a treasure,
Drinking is the soldier's pleasure:
Rich the treasure,
Sweet the pleasure;
Sweet is pleasure after pain.

CHORUS.

Bacchus' blessings are a treasure,
Drinking is the soldier's pleasure:
Rich the treasure,
Sweet the pleasure;
Sweet is pleasure after pain.

4 Soothed with the sound the king grew vain;
Fought all his battles o'er again;
And thrice he routed all his foes; and thrice he slew the slain.
The master saw the madness rise;
His glowing cheeks, his ardent eyes;
And while he heaven and earth defied,
Changed his hand, and check'd his pride.
He chose a mournful muse
Soft pity to infuse:
He sung Darius great and good,
By too severe a fate,
Fallen, fallen, fallen, fallen,
Fallen from his high estate,
And weltering in his blood;
Deserted, at his utmost need,
By those his former bounty fed;
On the bare earth exposed he lies,
With not a friend to close his eyes.
With downcast looks the joyless victor sate,
Revolving in his alter'd soul
The various turns of chance below;
And now and then a sigh he stole;
And tears began to flow.

CHORUS.

Revolving in his alter'd soul
The various turns of chance below;
And now and then a sigh he stole;
And tears began to flow.

5 The mighty master smiled, to see
That love was in the next degree:
'Twas but a kindred sound to move,
For pity melts the mind to love.
Softly sweet, in Lydian measures,
Soon he soothed his soul to pleasures.
War, he sung, is toil and trouble;
Honour, but an empty bubble;
Never ending, still beginning,
Fighting still, and still destroying:
If the world be worth thy winning,
Think, O think it worth enjoying:
Lovely Thais sits beside thee,
Take the good the gods provide thee.
The many rend the skies with loud applause;
So Love was crown'd, but Music won the cause.
The prince, unable to conceal his pain,
Gazed on the fair
Who caused his care,
And sigh'd and look'd, sigh'd and look'd,
Sigh'd and look'd, and sigh'd again:
At length, with love and wine at once oppress'd,
The vanquish'd victor sunk upon her breast.

CHORUS.

The prince, unable to conceal his pain,
Gazed on the fair
Who caused his care,
And sigh'd and look'd, sigh'd and look'd,
Sigh'd and look'd, and sigh'd again:
At length, with love and wine at once oppress'd,
The vanquish'd victor sunk upon her breast.

6 Now strike the golden lyre again:
A louder yet, and yet a louder strain.
Break his bands of sleep asunder,
And rouse him, like a rattling peal of thunder.
Hark, hark, the horrid sound
Has raised up his head:
As awaked from the dead,
And amazed, he stares around.
Revenge, Revenge, Timotheus cries,
See the Furies arise:
See the snakes that they rear,
How they hiss in their hair,
And the sparkles that flash from their eyes!
Behold a ghastly band,
Each a torch in his hand!
Those are Grecian ghosts, that in battle were slain,
And unburied remain
Inglorious on the plain:
Give the vengeance due
To the valiant crew.
Behold how they toss their torches on high,
How they point to the Persian abodes,
And glittering temples of their hostile gods.
The princes applaud, with a furious joy;
And the king seized a flambeau with zeal to destroy;
Thais led the way,
To light him to his prey,
And, like another Helen, fired another Troy.

CHORUS.

And the king seized a flambeau with zeal to destroy;
Thais led the way,
To light him to his prey,
And, like another Helen, fired another Troy.

Thus, long ago,
Ere heaving bellows learn'd to blow,
While organs yet were mute;
Timotheus, to his breathing flute,
And sounding lyre,
Could swell the soul to rage, or kindle soft desire.
At last divine Cecilia came,
Inventress of the vocal frame;
The sweet enthusiast, from her sacred store,
Enlarged the former narrow bounds,
And added length to solemn sounds,
With nature's mother-wit, and arts unknown before.
Let old Timotheus yield the prize,
Or both divide the crown;
He raised a mortal to the skies;
She drew an angel down.

GRAND CHORUS.

At last, divine Cecilia came,
Inventress of the vocal frame;
The sweet enthusiast, from her sacred store,
Enlarged the former narrow bounds,
And added length to solemn sounds,
With nature's mother-wit, and arts unknown before.
Let old Timotheus yield the prize,
Or both divide the crown;
He raised a mortal to the skies;
She drew an angel down.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Alexander's Feast; or, The Power of Music title; st. 1

    From 1683 a society of London musicians held an annual feast on St Cecilia's Day with a commissioned ode. Dryden had written the 1687 'Song for St Cecilia's Day'; this second ode, subtitled 'An Ode in Honour of St. Cecilia's Day', was performed in 1697 with music by Jeremiah Clarke (now lost). Handel's oratorio-like setting (first performed at Covent Garden in 1736, words adapted by Newburgh Hamilton) made the poem famous to later generations. Instead of praising music abstractly, Dryden dramatises its power: at Persepolis in 330 BC, the musician Timotheus plays Alexander the Great through a sequence of passions, ending in the burning of the Persian palace. Only the final stanza turns to Cecilia.

    Wikipedia, 'Alexander's Feast (Dryden)'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; Vinton A. Dearing (ed.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 7: Poems 1697-1700 (University of California Press, 2000), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  2. Pindaric stanzas and chorus sts. 1-7

    The English 'Pindaric', popularised by Cowley, used stanzas of irregular line length and rhyme. Dryden makes the irregularity expressive: martial lines crowd with drums ('Sound the trumpets; beat the drums'), the lament for Darius slows, the love section turns trochaic and lilting, and revenge breaks out in galloping anapaests ('Those are Grecian ghosts, that in battle were slain'). Each stanza closes with a chorus repeating its final lines, as a choir would in performance. The sequence enacts the seventeenth-century doctrine of musical 'affections', the belief that musical modes and rhythms could raise and calm particular passions. Pope praised the effect in An Essay on Criticism, where 'Timotheus' varied lays' move the world's victor.

    Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism (1711), ll. 374-83

  3. for Persia won ... Philip's warlike son st. 1, ll. 1-2

    After defeating Darius III at Gaugamela (331 BC) Alexander took the Persian royal capitals, including Persepolis, where the palace was burned in 330 BC. The ancient accounts (Plutarch, Life of Alexander 38; Diodorus 17.72; Curtius 5.7) describe a drinking party at which the courtesan Thais urged the burning. Dryden turns the drunken episode into a demonstration of music's power. The patronymic periphrasis 'Philip's warlike son' is epic style, and it sets up the poem's play with Alexander's claim to a divine father (stanza 2).

    Plutarch, Life of Alexander (Loeb trans. B. Perrin, 1919), chs. 2-3, 38, 43; Q. Curtius Rufus, Historiae Alexandri 5.7 (Latin text); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  4. awful state st. 1, l. 3

    In seventeenth-century English 'awful' meant 'commanding awe or reverence' (OED, awful, adj., sense 1), not 'very bad'. 'State' could denote both pomp and a canopied chair of state. The diction is solemn and Miltonic ('godlike hero', 'imperial throne'), but some readers sense a faint irony from the start: the 'godlike' hero will soon be played on like an instrument, and 'godlike' anticipates his assumption of divinity in stanza 2.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'awful, adj.'; 'state, n.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  5. roses and myrtles st. 1, l. 7

    Classical symposiasts wore garlands of roses and myrtle (Horace, Odes 1.38, rejects elaborate wreaths for plain myrtle). Myrtle was Venus' plant, so the wreaths already hint at the love-section to come. The parenthesis 'So should desert in arms be crown'd' sounds like a moral, but its proverb-like neatness also invites irony: military merit is rewarded with the emblems of pleasure.

    Horace, Odes 1.38 (Latin text); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  6. Thais st. 1, l. 9

    Plutarch (Alexander 38) says that Thais, an Athenian, at a drinking party urged that the palace of Xerxes be burned in revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens, and that she wished to set the fire herself while Alexander looked on; in his telling the king, garlanded and torch in hand, led the revellers out. He adds that she later became mistress of Ptolemy. Dryden sets her by Alexander as a 'blooming Eastern bride', an exotic and erotic presence, and gives her the lead at the final catastrophe ('Thais led the way'), where she is compared to Helen. She is thus both the 'fair' rewarded by bravery and the cause of destruction.

    Plutarch, Life of Alexander (Loeb trans. B. Perrin, 1919), chs. 2-3, 38, 43; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 17.72; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  7. None but the brave deserves the fair st. 1, refrain

    The thought is a commonplace of romance and of proverbs such as 'faint heart never won fair lady', but Dryden's line became the standard English form. Repetition here imitates musical setting, where words are repeated by voices. Whether the line is wholly straight is debated: Alexander has 'won' Thais as he won Persia, as spoils, and the poem will show the 'brave' victor made the plaything of music and the fair. Readers who take the poem as gently ironic about conquerors (and, some suggest, about William III, the warrior king whom the Jacobite Dryden refused to celebrate) point to such moments.

    Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; Vinton A. Dearing (ed.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 7: Poems 1697-1700 (University of California Press, 2000), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; Oxford Dictionary of English Proverbs, 'none but the brave deserve the fair'

  8. Timotheus st. 2, l. 1

    Dio Chrysostom (Discourse 1, On Kingship, opening) tells how the piper Timotheus played the Orthian strain so that Alexander sprang up and reached for his weapons. The more famous Timotheus of Miletus, the innovating lyre-poet, died around 357 BC, before Alexander's reign, so Dryden's lyre-playing Timotheus blends two figures; the poem later gives him both 'breathing flute' and 'sounding lyre'. 'Quire' is the old spelling of choir. 'Placed on high' puts the musician above the throne, physically and, the poem argues, in power.

    Dio Chrysostom, Discourse 1, On Kingship 1-2 (Loeb trans.); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  9. Jove ... dragon ... Olympia st. 2, ll. 6-14

    Plutarch (Alexander 2-3) reports that a serpent was seen lying beside the sleeping Olympias ('Olympia' here), and that Alexander was said to be the son of Zeus Ammon; Alexander encouraged the belief after the oracle at Siwa. 'Belied' means disguised, gave a false appearance to; 'spires' are coils (Latin spira); 'sublime' means aloft (Latin sublimis). The phrase 'stamp'd an image of himself' is a minting metaphor: Jove impressed his likeness on a son, as a king's image is stamped on coin, so the son is both copy and 'sovereign of the world'. Timotheus begins by flattering Alexander's divine birth.

    Plutarch, Life of Alexander (Loeb trans. B. Perrin, 1919), chs. 2-3, 38, 43; Oxford English Dictionary, 'belie, v.'; 'spire, n.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  10. A present deity st. 2, ll. 16-17

    Horace writes that Augustus will be held 'a present god' ('praesens divus habebitur', Odes 3.5.2), and 'praesens' was the word for a deity manifest and active on earth. The repeated shout is caught up by the 'vaulted roofs', an echo effect that suggests flattery feeding on itself. Music has produced political enthusiasm, a theme that may carry weight for a poet who had lost his laureateship in 1689 and watched new rulers celebrated.

    Horace, Odes 3.5.1-4 (Latin text); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  11. Assumes the god, / Affects to nod st. 2, ll. 20-22

    In Iliad 1.528-30 Zeus nods and great Olympus quakes; the nod became the classical sign of supreme divine assent. Alexander 'assumes' and 'affects' the god, verbs of role-playing, and 'seems' to shake the spheres (the celestial spheres whose motion was thought to make music). The short clipped lines are comic in rhythm. Many critics read this as the first clear touch of irony in the ode, showing a conqueror manipulated by art; others take it as admiring hyperbole about music's power, with the irony directed at vanity generally.

    Homer, Iliad 1.528-30 (Greek and Murray trans.); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  12. Bacchus ever fair and ever young st. 3

    Bacchus (Dionysus) was represented as a beautiful, unaging youth, and his 'triumph' (his return from conquering India in a procession) made him a natural model for Alexander, whose eastern campaigns were compared to it. 'Honest' face here means fine, comely, and frank; 'purple grace' is the flush of wine. 'Hautboys' are oboes (French hautbois, 'high wood'), loud reed instruments of seventeenth-century military and festive music. The rhyming jingle 'Rich the treasure, / Sweet the pleasure' is deliberately simple, a toast.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'hautboy, n.'; 'honest, adj.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  13. thrice he slew the slain st. 4, l. 3

    'Thrice' is an epic number (Homeric heroes do things three times), but killing the already slain is absurd, and the line is often cited as Dryden's sharpest irony at the conqueror's expense. Stanza 4 makes the turning point explicit: 'The master saw the madness rise' and 'check'd his pride'. 'The master' is Timotheus: the musician, not the king, is master of the scene. Critics who read the ode politically see here a satiric view of military glory.

    Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; Vinton A. Dearing (ed.), The Works of John Dryden, vol. 7: Poems 1697-1700 (University of California Press, 2000), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  14. Darius st. 4, ll. 10-18

    After Gaugamela, Darius fled east and was seized and killed by the satrap Bessus and his fellow conspirators; Plutarch (Alexander 43) says Alexander, arriving too late, covered the body with his cloak. Dryden's lines 'Deserted, at his utmost need, / By those his former bounty fed' stress ingratitude. The fourfold 'Fallen' imitates musical repetition and descending phrases in a lament. 'Weltering' means rolling or lying soaked in blood. The 'joyless victor' learns pity by seeing in the fallen king the turns of fortune ('the various turns of chance below') that could befall himself, a lesson in the tragic manner.

    Plutarch, Life of Alexander (Loeb trans. B. Perrin, 1919), chs. 2-3, 38, 43; Oxford English Dictionary, 'welter, v.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  15. Fallen, fallen, fallen, fallen st. 4, l. 12

    Dryden writes words for music, and repetitions like this invite the composer to word-paint a descending figure. The phrase 'Fallen from his high estate' has a biblical and tragic ring (compare the laments over fallen princes in 2 Samuel 1, 'How are the mighty fallen'). The same device returns in the love-section ('sigh'd and look'd, sigh'd and look'd').

    2 Samuel 1:19, 25, 27 (KJV); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  16. Lydian measures st. 5, l. 5

    Greek music theory assigned ethical effects to modes. Plato (Republic 398e) calls certain Lydian harmonies slack and convivial, unfit for guardians, while the Dorian was manly. Milton's 'L'Allegro' asks to be lapped 'in soft Lydian airs' (l. 136). The lines also state a psychological doctrine: 'pity melts the mind to love', love being 'a kindred sound'. The song then argues an epicurean carpe diem ('War ... is toil and trouble; / Honour, but an empty bubble'), a philosophy hard to reconcile with conquest and plainly addressed to the king's appetite.

    Plato, Republic 3.398e-399a; John Milton, 'L'Allegro', l. 136 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  17. Music won the cause st. 5, l. 16

    'Cause' means a lawsuit or case argued; music is the successful advocate. The line sums up the ode's subtitle, 'The Power of Music'. It then climaxes in the oxymoron 'The vanquish'd victor sunk upon her breast': the conqueror of Asia conquered by love and wine, prepared by a musician. The stanza's sighing repetitions keep the comic distance; the great king is reduced to a lover's helpless gestures.

    Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  18. Revenge ... the Furies st. 6, ll. 9-13

    The Furies (Greek Erinyes, Latin Furiae) pursue blood-guilt and are pictured with snakes in their hair and torches in their hands, as in Aeschylus' Eumenides and Virgil (Aeneid 6.570-75, 7.324ff). 'Horrid' keeps its Latin sense of bristling, terrifying (horridus). The anapaestic rhythm ('See the snakes that they rear, / How they hiss in their hair') is the most violent metrical change in the poem, and the hissing sibilants are onomatopoeic. The 'rattling peal of thunder' that wakes the king recalls Jove again.

    Virgil, Aeneid 7.324-40 (Allecto) (Latin text); Oxford English Dictionary, 'horrid, adj.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  19. Grecian ghosts ... unburied st. 6, ll. 16-18

    Patroklos' ghost begs Akhilleus for burial so that he may pass the gates of Hades (Iliad 23.65-74), and in Aeneid 6.325-30 the unburied must wander a hundred years by the Styx. The ghosts here are Greeks killed by the Persians, and Timotheus' song demands 'the vengeance due', echoing the official justification for burning Persepolis as revenge for Xerxes' sack of Athens in 480 BC. 'Inglorious' means without honour of burial or memorial.

    Homer, Iliad 23.65-74 (Greek and Murray trans.); Virgil, Aeneid 6.325-30 (Latin text); Plutarch, Life of Alexander (Loeb trans. B. Perrin, 1919), chs. 2-3, 38, 43

  20. flambeau st. 6, l. 25

    From French flambeau, a torch made of thick wicks dipped in wax (OED, flambeau, n.). The word, a fashionable French borrowing, is set in a long, hurrying anapaestic line. 'Zeal to destroy' is pointed: in Dryden's time 'zeal' was a word associated with religious enthusiasm, and the destruction of 'hostile gods' and their 'glittering temples' turns the scene into something like iconoclasm.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'flambeau, n.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  21. another Helen ... another Troy st. 6, l. 28

    Helen's elopement with Paris led to the Greek siege and burning of Troy. Here a Greek woman sets fire to an Asian capital, completing a historical cycle of East-West revenge that classical writers traced from Troy to the Persian Wars (Herodotus begins his Histories with such abductions). The phrase also recalls Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, where Helen's face 'burnt the topless towers of Ilium'. The line, ending the narrative proper, is the ode's climax: music has led a king from godhead to wine to pity to love to arson.

    Herodotus, Histories 1.1-5; Christopher Marlowe, Doctor Faustus (A-text, 1604), scene 13 (the Helen speech); Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  22. Ere heaving bellows ... organs yet were mute st. 7, ll. 1-6

    The final stanza sets the pagan musician against the Christian saint. Before organs existed, Timotheus with 'breathing flute' (the aulos, the reed-pipe) and 'sounding lyre' could 'swell the soul to rage, or kindle soft desire': ancient music works on the passions. The word 'learn'd' playfully treats the bellows as a pupil, and 'heaving' makes the organ almost a breathing body.

    Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  23. Cecilia, Inventress of the vocal frame st. 7, ll. 7-12

    The legend of Cecilia, a Roman noblewoman martyred perhaps in the third century, says that at her wedding she sang in her heart to God; from the late Middle Ages she was pictured at the organ and became the patron of musicians. Dryden credits her with inventing the organ ('the vocal frame') and with enlarging music's 'narrow bounds' and giving 'length to solemn sounds', i.e. sustained notes possible on the organ, unlike plucked strings. 'Enthusiast' keeps its Greek sense of one possessed or inspired by a god (entheos). 'Mother-wit' is native intelligence, here paired with 'arts unknown before'.

    Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), 'St. Cecilia'; Oxford English Dictionary, 'enthusiast, n.'; 'mother-wit, n.'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'

  24. He raised a mortal to the skies; / She drew an angel down st. 7, ll. 15-16

    In the Cecilia legend an angel guards her and appears with crowns of roses and lilies for her and her husband Valerian. The antithesis balances pagan and Christian music: one exalts a human being by flattery (Alexander's deification), the other brings the divine to earth. Critics divide over the ending. Johnson (Life of Dryden) objected that Timotheus' power was only metaphorical while Cecilia's was real, so the crown could not reasonably be divided; defenders reply that the offer to share is a courteous wit that leaves the Christian art plainly superior. The 'Grand Chorus' repeats the whole stanza.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Dryden', Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779-81); Catholic Encyclopedia (1908), 'St. Cecilia'; Paul Hammond and David Hopkins (eds.), The Poems of John Dryden, vol. 5: 1697-1700 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2005), commentary on 'Alexander's Feast'; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961), chapter on Dryden's Cecilian odes
