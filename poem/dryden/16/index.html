A Song for St Cecilia's Day, 1687

by John Dryden 1631–1700

Arts & Sciences

1 FROM harmony, from heavenly harmony
This universal frame began:
When nature underneath a heap
Of jarring atoms lay,
And could not heave her head,
The tuneful voice was heard from high,
Arise, ye more than dead.
Then cold, and hot, and moist, and dry,
In order to their stations leap,
And Music's power obey.
From harmony, from heavenly harmony
This universal frame began:
From harmony to harmony
Through all the compass of the notes it rail,
The diapason closing full in Man.

2 What passion cannot Music raise and quell?
When Jubal struck the chorded shell,
His listening brethren stood around,
And, wondering, on their faces fell
To worship that celestial sound.
Less than a God they thought there could not dwell
Within the hollow of that shell,
That spoke so sweetly and so well.
What passion cannot Music raise and quell?

3 The trumpet's loud clangour
Excites us to arms,
With shrill notes of anger,
And mortal alarms.
The double double double beat
Of the thundering drum
Cries, hark! the foes come;
Charge, charge!'tis too late to retreat.

4 The soft complaining flute
In dying notes discovers
The woes of hopeless lovers,
Whose dirge is whisper'd by the warbling lute.

5 Sharp violins proclaim
Their jealous pangs, and desperation,
Fury, frantic indignation,
Depth of pains, and height of passion,
For the fair, disdainful dame.

6 But oh! what art can teach,
What human voice can reach,
The sacred organ's praise?
Notes inspiring holy love,
Notes that wing their heavenly ways
To mend the choirs above.

7 Orpheus could lead the savage race;
And trees uprooted left their place,
Sequacious of the lyre:
But bright Cecilia raised the wonder higher:
When to her organ vocal breath was given,
An angel heard, and straight appear'd,
Mistaking earth for heaven.

GRAND CHORUS.

As from the power of sacred lays
The spheres began to move,
And sung the great Creator's praise
To all the bless'd above;
So when the last and dreadful hour
This crumbling pageant shall devour,
The trumpet shall be heard on high,
The dead shall live, the living die,
And Music shall untune the sky.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion st. 1

    From the early 1680s a society of London musicians and amateurs held an annual feast on St Cecilia's Day (22 November), with a commissioned ode, a service and a dinner. Dryden's ode was set by Draghi, an Italian musician at the court of James II, and the text was later collected in Examen Poeticum (1693). Handel set it anew in 1739. The ode's design moves from the creation of the world by music, through the power of music over human passions (Jubal, the instruments), to St Cecilia's organ, and ends with music's role at the Last Judgement. Dryden had become a Catholic in 1685-86, and a saint's day ode under a Catholic king had a public dimension.

    Wikipedia, 'A Song for St. Cecilia's Day' (performance history, Draghi and Handel settings, Motteux's account); Representative Poetry Online, A Song for St. Cecilia's Day, 1687, ed. G. G. Falle, text from Examen Poeticum (1693), University of Toronto; Roger Bray, 'Dryden and Draghi in Harmony in the 1687 Song for St Cecilia's Day', Music & Letters 78.3 (1997), 319-336; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  2. heavenly harmony ... universal frame st. 1

    Pythagorean and Platonic tradition (Plato's Timaeus, Boethius' musica mundana) held that the universe is ordered by musical proportion; Christian writers identified the creating 'tuneful voice' with God's Word (Genesis 1, John 1:1). 'Frame' means structure or fabric (OED), as in Hamlet's 'this goodly frame, the earth'. John Hollander's study of music in English poetry takes its title, The Untuning of the Sky, from the ode's last line. The refrain-like return of the opening lines mirrors the musical structure.

    John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; Bible (KJV), John 1:1

  3. a heap / Of jarring atoms st. 1

    Lucretius' De rerum natura describes a world formed by the chance collisions of atoms; Christian writers of the seventeenth century, wary of its atheism, recast atoms as raw material ordered by God. Ovid's account of creation (Metamorphoses 1.5-20) describes a 'rudis indigestaque moles', a crude unordered heap, in which the elements fought one another. Dryden borrows the 'heap' and the strife; 'jarring' is a musical word for discord, so chaos is literally untuned. Nature 'could not heave her head' until the creating voice called.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.5-20 (The Latin Library); John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  4. Arise, ye more than dead st. 1

    The elements are 'more than dead' because they have never lived. The command 'Arise' echoes the language of resurrection (compare 1 Corinthians 15 and Isaiah 26:19), so the first stanza's creation by sound already looks forward to the final stanza, where 'The trumpet shall be heard on high' and the dead shall live. The ode is thus framed by two sounding commands, at the beginning and the end of time.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  5. cold, and hot, and moist st. 1

    In Aristotelian physics, fire is hot and dry, air hot and moist, water cold and moist, earth cold and dry. Ovid's chaos is a war of these qualities ('frigida pugnabant calidis, umentia siccis', Metamorphoses 1.19). Dryden's line imitates the clash with its run of monosyllables joined by 'and', then resolves it as the qualities 'leap' into order, as if in a dance. The cosmos becomes a musical performance obeying 'Music's power'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.19 (The Latin Library); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961)

  6. it rail (for 'ran') st. 1

    The authoritative texts, from the 1687 broadside and Examen Poeticum (1693) onward, read 'Through all the compass of the notes it ran'. 'Rail' makes no sense here and is evidently a transcription error that has crept into this copy; readers should take the line as 'ran'. 'Compass' is the full range of an instrument or voice (OED), so the line describes creation as a scale rising through every note from the lowest creatures to the highest.

    Representative Poetry Online, A Song for St. Cecilia's Day, 1687, ed. G. G. Falle, text from Examen Poeticum (1693), University of Toronto; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'compass'

  7. diapason closing full in Man st. 1

    Greek dia pason (chordon), 'through all the strings', named the octave, which completes the scale and returns to the keynote in perfect consonance. In Genesis 1 man is created last, and Renaissance thought made him a microcosm summing up all the lower orders of being. Dryden fuses the scale of music with the scale of nature (the chain of being): the 'closing' is both a musical cadence and the completion of creation. Johnson, in his Life of Dryden, praised the first stanza as vigorous and elegant but thought 'diapason' too technical a word.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'diapason'; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26; Samuel Johnson, 'Dryden', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81), vol. 1 (Project Gutenberg)

  8. What passion cannot Music raise st. 2

    The idea that music moves the 'affections' was a commonplace from Plato and Aristotle to seventeenth-century musical theory, and it underlies the series of instruments and emotions in stanzas 3 to 5. The repeated question frames stanza 2 like a musical ritornello. Dryden returned to the theme a decade later in Alexander's Feast (1697), where Timotheus sways Alexander from pride to pity to love to rage.

    The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); Wikipedia, 'A Song for St. Cecilia's Day' (performance history, Draghi and Handel settings, Motteux's account)

  9. Jubal ... chorded shell st. 2

    Genesis 4:21 names Jubal, of the line of Cain, as the first musician. Dryden equips him with a 'chorded shell', the Greek lyre, which in the Homeric Hymn to Hermes the infant god made by stringing a tortoise shell; Latin testudo means both tortoise and lyre. The biblical and classical myths of origin are thus fused. His brethren fall on their faces to worship the sound, believing a god must live in the shell: a hint that music's power can be mistaken for divinity, which the final stanza on Cecilia picks up with the angel 'mistaking earth for heaven'. (The RPO note gives Genesis 5:21, a misprint for 4:21.)

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 4:21; Homeric Hymn 4, To Hermes, ll. 24-51; Representative Poetry Online, A Song for St. Cecilia's Day, 1687, ed. G. G. Falle, text from Examen Poeticum (1693), University of Toronto; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  10. The trumpet's loud clangour; the drum st. 3

    Stanza 3 uses short, mostly anapaestic lines and harsh consonants for the trumpet, and the 'double double double beat' of the drum imitates the roll of the side drum, ending in the breathless command 'Charge, charge!'. 'Clangour' (from Latin clangor) is a loud ringing sound. The ode's irregular, Pindaric stanzas, of different lengths and metres, were designed to give the composer contrasting movements; Bray's study shows how closely Draghi followed these shifts. Such onomatopoeia was much admired by eighteenth-century readers.

    Roger Bray, 'Dryden and Draghi in Harmony in the 1687 Song for St Cecilia's Day', Music & Letters 78.3 (1997), 319-336; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clangour'

  11. complaining flute ... warbling lute st. 4

    In Restoration England 'flute' normally meant the recorder, an instrument associated with love and mourning. 'Complaining' has the sense of the literary 'complaint', a lament; 'discovers' means reveals. 'Dying notes' plays on the fading sound and on the lover's 'death'. The stanza is a single sentence of four lines whose soft liquids and long vowels contrast with the drumming consonants of the stanza before.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'discover'; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961)

  12. Sharp violins st. 5

    Charles II had formed a band of twenty-four violins on the French model, and the violin family was displacing the softer viols. 'Sharp' describes its bright, cutting tone and perhaps its high pitch. The stanza's rhymes on '-ation' and '-ion' (desperation, indignation, passion) and its list structure create a frantic effect. 'The fair, disdainful dame' is the cruel mistress of love poetry.

    The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sharp'

  13. the sacred organ ... mend the choirs above st. 6

    After instruments of war and earthly love, stanza 6 rises to sacred music. 'Mend' here means to improve or augment (OED), so the organ's notes travel upward to add to the angelic choirs: a bold compliment, since heavenly music should need no mending. The progression from secular to sacred structures the ode and prepares for Cecilia, traditionally (and anachronistically) credited with inventing the organ.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mend'; Representative Poetry Online, A Song for St. Cecilia's Day, 1687, ed. G. G. Falle, text from Examen Poeticum (1693), University of Toronto; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  14. Orpheus ... Sequacious of the lyre st. 7

    In Ovid (Metamorphoses 10.86-105 and 11.1-2) trees and beasts gather to hear Orpheus. 'Sequacious' (from Latin sequax, following) is a learned Latinism meaning inclined to follow (OED); the rare word gives the uprooted trees a faintly comic docility, as if they were disciples. The comparison between pagan and Christian musicians is a set piece of Cecilian odes: Orpheus moved nature, Cecilia moves heaven, and the stanza's rising lines enact that ascent from earth to the angelic.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.86-105 (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sequacious'; The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.

  15. An angel heard ... Mistaking earth for heaven st. 7

    The legend of St Cecilia (an early Roman martyr, in versions such as the Golden Legend and Chaucer's Second Nun's Tale) tells that an angel guarded her chastity and appeared to her and her husband Valerian. Dryden transforms the story into a compliment to music: when breath was given to her organ (the air in its pipes), an angel 'straight appear'd', deceived into thinking he heard heaven. The witty conceit recalls Jubal's brethren mistaking a shell for a god, and it raises 'the wonder higher' than Orpheus.

    Geoffrey Chaucer, The Second Nun's Tale (Canterbury Tales); The Works of John Dryden, vol. III: Poems 1685-1692, ed. Earl Miner (University of California Press, 1969), commentary on the Song for St Cecilia's Day, ad loc.; Representative Poetry Online, A Song for St. Cecilia's Day, 1687, ed. G. G. Falle, text from Examen Poeticum (1693), University of Toronto

  16. Grand Chorus: untune the sky Grand Chorus

    The spheres were thought to move to divine music, singing the Creator's praise. At the Last Judgement 'the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised' (1 Corinthians 15:52); the earthly 'pageant' (a show, a stage-spectacle, with an echo of The Tempest's 'insubstantial pageant') will crumble. The final paradox, that music shall 'untune' the sky, has divided readers: Johnson, in his Life of Dryden, called the conclusion striking but wished the antithesis of music 'untuning' had found some other place; Hollander took the line for the title of his book. The line 'The dead shall live, the living die' is a chiasmus imitating that reversal.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; John Hollander, The Untuning of the Sky: Ideas of Music in English Poetry, 1500-1700 (Princeton University Press, 1961); Wikipedia, 'A Song for St. Cecilia's Day' (performance history, Draghi and Handel settings, Motteux's account); William Shakespeare, The Tempest 4.1.148-58 (Folger Shakespeare); Samuel Johnson, 'Dryden', Lives of the English Poets (1779-81), vol. 1 (Project Gutenberg)
