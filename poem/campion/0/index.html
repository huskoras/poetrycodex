Cherry-Ripe

by Thomas Campion 1567–1620

Love

There is a garden in her face
Where roses and white lilies blow;
A heavenly paradise is that place,
Wherein all pleasant fruits do flow:
There cherries grow which none may buy
Till ‘Cherry-ripe’ themselves do cry.

Those cherries fairly do enclose
Of orient pearl a double row,
Which when her lovely laughter shows,
They look like rose-buds fill’d with snow;
Yet them nor peer nor prince can buy
Till ‘Cherry-ripe’ themselves do cry.

Her eyes like angels watch them still;
Her brows like bended bows do stand,
Threat’ning with piercing frowns to kill
All that attempt with eye or hand
Those sacred cherries to come nigh,
Till ‘Cherry-ripe’ themselves do cry.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song and its printing title; l. 1

    Campion (1567-1620), physician, poet and composer, wrote words and music together, and the poem should be thought of as a song: short stanzas, a refrain, and verbal music suited to the lute. Versions of the text appeared earlier in Robert Jones's Ultimum Vale (1605) and Richard Allison's An Howres Recreation in Musicke (1606), set by those composers; Campion printed it with his own setting in The Third and Fourth Booke of Ayres (c. 1617). The archive's title 'Cherry-Ripe' is an editorial one taken from the refrain; it should not be confused with Herrick's later poem of the same name. Each stanza is six lines: a quatrain rhyming abab and a couplet whose second line is the refrain.

    "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Wikipedia (printed as a song in The Third and Fourth Booke of Ayres, 1617); Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  2. A garden in her face l. 1

    The Song of Songs calls the beloved 'a garden inclosed' and an orchard 'with pleasant fruits' (4:12-13), and the stanza echoes that language ('pleasant fruits', 'heavenly paradise'). The 'garden enclosed' was also read as a figure of chastity, which suits the poem's theme of cherries that cannot be bought. Calling the garden a paradise adds Eden, so the face becomes a place of innocent abundance guarded against intruders. The red and white of roses and lilies are the stock colours of a fair complexion in Elizabethan verse.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 4:12-13; Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7

  3. blow l. 2

    The verb 'blow' in the sense 'to flower' (Old English blowan) was common in poetry from Chaucer to the nineteenth century; compare Oberon's 'bank where the wild thyme blows' in A Midsummer Night's Dream. Roses and lilies stand for the red and white of the woman's cheeks: the lily's whiteness traditionally signifies purity, the rose's red beauty and love. The verb therefore pictures the cheeks in full flower.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "blow, v.2"; Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1.249

  4. flow l. 4

    The use of 'flow' for abundance rather than liquid movement is biblical in flavour (Exodus 3:8 and many later verses describe Canaan as flowing with milk and honey). Together with 'heavenly paradise', the line idealises the face as a land of plenty before the refrain reveals that its best fruit is not for sale. The plenty of the garden makes the restriction that follows all the more pointed.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "flow, v."

  5. "Cherry-ripe" (refrain) ll. 6, 12, 18

    'Cherry ripe!' was the call of street vendors selling cherries in London. The refrain turns the market cry into a joke about desire and consent: ordinarily anyone can buy cherries when the seller cries them, but these cherries (her lips) will be 'bought' by no one until they themselves cry 'Cherry-ripe', that is, until she offers her own kiss or, more broadly, until she is ready for love. The musical setting repeats and ornaments the words, mimicking the vendor's call. The refrain recurs unchanged at the end of each stanza, giving the song its structure.

    "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Wikipedia (printed as a song in The Third and Fourth Booke of Ayres, 1617); Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  6. Love that cannot be bought l. 5

    Each stanza's couplet develops the commercial metaphor: 'none may buy' (st. 1), 'nor peer nor prince can buy' (st. 2), and finally no one may 'come nigh' (st. 3). The progression from purchase to approach turns the wit about street selling into a statement about the woman's sovereignty over her own body. Campion keeps the tone light, but the poem belongs with Elizabethan lyrics that praise chastity by making it attractive and unattainable.

    Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Wikipedia (printed as a song in The Third and Fourth Booke of Ayres, 1617)

  7. orient pearl l. 8

    The cherries (lips) 'enclose' two rows of pearls, the teeth. 'Orient' as an adjective for pearls and gems meant brilliant, lustrous, of the highest quality, because the best pearls came from the East; by Campion's time it had become a general term of praise for shining things. The comparison of teeth to pearls is a Petrarchan commonplace, and Campion uses it with a light touch.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "orient, adj."; Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7

  8. rose-buds filled with snow l. 10

    The image repeats the red-white colour scheme of the first stanza in miniature: red lips (rosebuds) part to show white teeth (snow). The paradox of snow inside a flower adds a hint of coolness, perhaps of chastity, to the warmth of laughter. This is the kind of pictorial conceit that Elizabethan readers enjoyed in miniature painting and in the blazon (the poetic inventory of a woman's features).

    Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  9. peer nor prince l. 11

    'Peer' is a member of the nobility (originally an equal; then one of the lords of the realm). The alliterating pair is a formula for the highest social ranks. Campion's songs were written for an educated, courtly audience, and the line flatters the lady by placing her favour above the reach of power. Campion himself, a physician, moved in the circles of courtiers and patrons for whom such lines were written.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "peer, n.1"; Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7

  10. Eyes like angels l. 13

    The eyes keep watch over the 'sacred' cherries as angels guard a holy place, recalling the cherubim who guard Eden after the Fall (Genesis 3:24), apt for a face already called paradise. 'Still' means continually. Because the poem plays throughout with buying and selling, some readers detect a secondary pun on 'angel', the English gold coin stamped with St Michael; the joke would be that the only angels involved are the guardians, not the purchase price. The pun is possible rather than certain.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:24; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "angel, n. (coin)"

  11. Brows like bended bows l. 14

    The arched brow compared to a bow is a Petrarchan commonplace, and the bow is Cupid's weapon; here, however, the bows defend instead of wound with love. 'Piercing frowns' that kill turn the conventional killing glance of the beloved into a defensive weapon. The military imagery of the last stanza completes a movement from garden, to market, to guarded fortress.

    Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; "There Is a Garden in Her Face", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  12. sacred cherries l. 17

    'Sacred' gathers up the poem's religious language (paradise, angels) and makes the cherries something like the forbidden fruit of Eden or the sanctuary of a temple. 'With eye or hand' covers looking and touching, so the guard is complete until she herself consents. The final refrain therefore arrives with new force: it is no longer only a market joke but the condition on which the sacred may be approached.

    Percival Vivian, ed., Campion's Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), The Fourth Booke of Ayres, no. 7; Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:16-17
