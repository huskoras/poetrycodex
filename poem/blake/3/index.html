Religion

The Lamb

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Innocence

Religion

Little Lamb, who made thee
Dost thou know who made thee,
Gave thee life, and bid thee feed
By the stream and o'er the mead;
Gave thee clothing of delight,
Softest clothing, woolly, bright;
Gave thee such a tender voice,
Making all the vales rejoice?
Little Lamb, who made thee?
Dost thou know who made thee?

Little Lamb, I'll tell thee;
Little Lamb, I'll tell thee:
He is called by thy name,
For He calls Himself a Lamb
He is meek, and He is mild,
He became a little child.
I a child, and thou a lamb,
We are called by His name.
Little Lamb, God bless thee!
Little Lamb, God bless thee!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The innocent counterpart to ‘The Tyger.’ Here a child answers his own question with serene certainty: Christ, lamb and child collapse into one gentle identity — a faith not yet troubled by the darker creation Blake would set beside it.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Question and answer: a child's catechism ll. 1-10

    Blake etched The Lamb for Songs of Innocence (1789) and later placed it in the combined Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794), whose title page speaks of 'the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul'. The speaker is a child addressing a lamb, and the structure imitates the question-and-answer form of the catechism by which children learned doctrine ('Who made you?' 'God made me'). The tone also recalls the hymns for children of Isaac Watts and Charles Wesley. Each stanza is framed by a repeated couplet, giving a song-like refrain, and the lines are short, mostly seven-syllable trochaic couplets. The simplicity is deliberate: the poem enacts an untroubled faith whose answers come easily, which is why The Tyger's unanswered questions sound so different.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 8-9, text of 'The Lamb' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions); Bible (KJV), Psalm 100:3 ('it is he that hath made us')

  2. Illuminated printing and the design ll. 3-8

    Blake's 'illuminated printing' etched words and images together in relief on copper, printed them on his own press, and finished the impressions with watercolour, often with his wife Catherine's help. The Lamb therefore exists as a picture-poem: its text sits within a design of a naked child reaching toward a lamb before a thatched cottage, with sheep and two slender trees whose branches arch over the words. Copies differ in colouring and even in the order of plates, which is why scholars cite copies by letter in the William Blake Archive. Reading the poem in a facsimile or on the Archive shows how the design reinforces the text's tenderness. The list in these lines (life, food, clothing, voice) is the child's inventory of gifts.

    The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 8-9, text of 'The Lamb' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  3. stream and mead ll. 3-4

    'Mead' is a poetic word for meadow. The lines picture providence as a shepherd who 'bid thee feed' beside water, close to Psalm 23 ('He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters'). The Innocence plate surrounds the text with a pastoral scene of a child, a flock, a cottage and slender trees, so word and design together present nature as a gift. Blake's 'innocence' is not ignorance, however; in the combined Songs it is one state of the soul, set against 'experience'. Readers have noticed that the child speaks of a world where needs are simply met, with none of the labour, ownership or danger that the Experience poems make visible.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 23:1-2; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 8, text of 'The Lamb' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  4. clothing of delight ll. 5-6

    The child reads wool as a garment made for the lamb's delight, not for human use; 'woolly, bright' turns a farm commodity into radiance. The thought recalls Matthew 6:28-30, where God clothes the lilies of the field and the grass, so that natural beauty becomes evidence of providential care. Blake often uses clothing and garments as images of the body and the material world, but here the image is wholly benign. The 'tender voice' that makes 'all the vales rejoice' extends the idea: creation answers its maker with joy, as in Psalm 65:13, where the valleys 'shout for joy, they also sing'. The lamb's bleat becomes a hymn.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:28-30; Bible (KJV), Psalm 65:13

  5. He is called by thy name: the Lamb of God ll. 13-14

    The answer turns on a pun of names: the creator is 'called by thy name' because Christ 'calls Himself a Lamb'. John the Baptist hails Jesus as 'the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world' (John 1:29, 36), and Revelation repeatedly calls the risen Christ the Lamb. Strictly, Christ is called the Lamb by others rather than calling himself so, a liberty the child's logic takes. Behind the gentle image lies the sacrificial lamb of Isaiah 53:7, 'brought as a lamb to the slaughter', so the poem's innocence carries a shadow of suffering that it does not mention. The child resolves creation into kinship: maker, lamb and child share one identity.

    Bible (KJV), John 1:29; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 53:7; Bible (KJV), Revelation 5:6

  6. meek and mild; a little child ll. 15-16

    'Meek and mild' was a well-known pairing from Charles Wesley's hymn for children, 'Gentle Jesus, meek and mild', first printed in Hymns and Sacred Poems (1742) together with a companion piece beginning 'Lamb of God, I look to thee'. Behind both lies Matthew 11:29 ('I am meek and lowly in heart'). 'He became a little child' states the Incarnation in a child's terms (Isaiah 9:6, 'unto us a child is born') and recalls Christ's command to become as little children (Matthew 18:3). Blake's later writings are sharply critical of a merely 'meek' Christ; in The Everlasting Gospel he mocks that image. Some readers therefore hear in the Innocence lyric a view of Christ that Experience will complicate.

    Charles Wesley, 'Gentle Jesus, meek and mild', Hymns and Sacred Poems (1742), Hymnary.org entry; Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:29; Bible (KJV), Matthew 18:3

  7. I a child, and thou a lamb ll. 17-18

    The Christian is one who is 'called by His name' (compare Acts 11:26, 'the disciples were called Christians'), and the lamb bears the name Christ took for himself. The line completes a triangle of identities: Christ became a child and is called a Lamb; the speaker is a child; the addressee is a lamb. Blake's idea that the divine is present in the human and the natural, later stated as 'All deities reside in the human breast' in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, is here expressed with complete trust. The benediction 'God bless thee', repeated, closes the poem with a priestly gesture performed by a child, reversing the usual hierarchy of teacher and taught.

    Bible (KJV), Acts 11:26; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 9, text of 'The Lamb' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  8. The Lamb and The Tyger ll. 19-20

    Blake designed the two collections as contraries, and The Lamb is most often read beside The Tyger. Where the child here answers its own question at once, the Experience speaker asks question after question and receives no answer, and the creator imagined is not a gentle shepherd but a smith at a furnace. Blake does not present innocence as simply wrong or experience as simply wiser: in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell he insists that 'Without Contraries is no progression'. A good way to read the poem is therefore to ask what its serenity leaves out, and what The Tyger's terror cannot see.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 8-9, 24-25, text of 'The Lamb' and 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, plate 3 (Erdman p. 34)
