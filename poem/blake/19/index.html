Introduction (Experience)

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Arts & Sciences

Hear the voice of the Bard,
Who present, past, and future, sees;
Whose ears have heard
The Holy Word
That walked among the ancient tree;

Calling the lapsed soul,
And weeping in the evening dew;
That might control
The starry pole,
And fallen, fallen light renew!

"O Earth, O Earth, return!
Arise from out the dewy grass!
Night is worn,
And the morn
Rises from the slumbrous mass.

"Turn away no more;
Why wilt thou turn away?
The starry floor,
The watery shore,
Are given thee till the break of day."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hear the voice of the Bard l. 1

    In the 1794 Songs the Innocence 'Introduction' presents a piper inspired by a child on a cloud; this poem's speaker is a bard who sees 'present, past, and future', like the Hebrew prophets and like Gray's Welsh bard, a figure of prophetic resistance. The Bard calls on Earth to return, but Earth, in the next poem, replies bitterly that the 'Starry Jealousy', a selfish father-god, keeps her chained. Gleckner and others argue that the Bard is himself partly bound to the fallen view, so that his call is to be weighed against Earth's answer rather than accepted.

    Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E18 (Blake Digital Text Project); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  2. Who present, past, and future, sees l. 2

    The power to see all times is a mark of the prophet. The formula recalls Revelation 1:8 and 4:8, and the classical seer Calchas in Iliad I.70, who knew 'the things that were, and that were to be, and that had been before'. Blake regarded the poet as a prophet in the Old Testament sense, one who speaks truth to the present rather than merely foretells. The ordering 'present, past, and future' puts the present first.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Revelation 1:8; 4:8; Homer, Iliad I.70 (Calchas); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  3. The Holy Word / That walked among the ancient tree ll. 4-5

    Genesis 3:8-9: Adam and Eve 'heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day', and God called to Adam, 'Where art thou?' John 1:1 identifies the Word with God. The Bard has heard this Word, which calls the 'lapsed soul' and weeps in 'the evening dew', so that God is both the punitive creator of Genesis and a mourning, loving presence. Critics read the ambiguity in Blake's terms: the God of the Fall may be the jealous Urizen of the prophecies, or the Christ who seeks to redeem.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Genesis 3:8-9; John 1:1; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957)

  4. the ancient tree: the text l. 5

    Erdman's text, from Blake's etched plates, reads 'That walk'd among the ancient trees'. The singular 'tree' found in some nineteenth-century printings narrows the reference to the Tree of Knowledge, but the authorial text has the plural, referring to the trees of the garden in Genesis 3:8. Students quoting the poem should check Erdman's edition or the Blake Archive. The plural also matters for the rhyme scheme, since 'trees' answers 'sees' in l. 2, and the singular breaks the rhyme Blake designed.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E18 (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), text and Notebook variants

  5. Calling the lapsed soul l. 6

    In Paradise Lost III.175-176 God says of fallen man: 'Once more I will renew / His lapsed powers'. Blake's stanza echoes both words, 'lapsed' and 'renew', so the Bard presents a Miltonic drama of fall and restoration. 'Lapsed' (Latin lapsus, a slip, fall) is also a theological term for the fallen state. The soul is both humanity and the Earth, who will be addressed as the fallen female in the next stanzas.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (2nd ed., 1674), III.175-176; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  6. That might control / The starry pole ll. 6-10

    'Calling' and 'weeping' could modify the Bard, the Holy Word, or both; 'That might control' could refer to the Word (which has power over the heavens) or to the soul (which, if renewed, might govern the stars, as fallen humanity once ruled creation). Frye and others resolve the ambiguity in favour of the soul's lost power; Gleckner argued that the confusion is deliberate, since the Bard speaks from within Experience and blurs the jealous god and the human soul. The 'starry pole' is the axis of the heavens, and in Blake's myth the stars often stand for the rigid order of reason and law.

    Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  7. And fallen, fallen light renew l. 10

    The 'fallen light' is Lucifer, the morning star, and also fallen humanity and the fallen world. The repetition recalls Revelation 14:8 and 18:2 ('Babylon is fallen, is fallen'), and Dryden's 'Fallen, fallen, fallen, fallen' in 'Alexander's Feast'. Blake's hope is that the fallen light might be renewed, in keeping with his belief that the Fall could be reversed by imagination.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Isaiah 14:12; Revelation 14:8; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  8. O Earth, O Earth, return! l. 11

    The Bard (or the Word he reports) calls on Earth to rise from the 'dewy grass', since night is 'worn' (worn out) and morning rises from the 'slumbrous mass', the sleeping bulk of the world. In the following poem, 'Earth's Answer', Earth replies that she is chained by 'Starry Jealousy', the father of the ancient men, and asks for the bondage of love to be broken. The two poems together make a dialogue, and the Bard's optimism is checked by Earth's account of her imprisonment.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Jeremiah 22:29; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E18 (Blake Digital Text Project); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957)

  9. Rises from the slumbrous mass l. 15

    'Mass' suggests the dark, undifferentiated matter of the night world, and in Blake's later myth the material world as a sleep from which humanity must wake. The phrasing in Blake's plate is 'slumberous mass'; the archive's 'slumbrous' is a later regularisation, with no change of sense. Morning here is the dawn of a renewed creation, though Earth's Answer will question whether it is truly coming.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E18 (Blake Digital Text Project); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'slumberous'

  10. The starry floor, / The watery shore ll. 18-20

    The 'starry floor' (the sky seen as a floor of heaven) and the 'watery shore' (the boundary of the sea) are given to Earth until daybreak. Readers disagree whether these limits are merciful, preserving the world from chaos (compare Job 38:8-11, where God sets bounds to the sea), or the bars of a prison, as Earth says in her answer. Frye reads the stars and the sea in Blake as symbols of the fallen world of Urizenic order and of the 'Sea of Time and Space'. The design shows a reclining figure, perhaps Earth herself, beneath the text.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Job 38:8-11; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)
