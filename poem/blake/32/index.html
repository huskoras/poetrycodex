Time & Brevity

Ah Sunflower

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Time & Brevity

Ah Sunflower, weary of time,
Who countest the steps of the sun;
Seeking after that sweet golden clime
Where the traveller's journey is done;

Where the Youth pined away with desire,
And the pale virgin shrouded in snow,
Arise from their graves, and aspire
Where my Sunflower wishes to go!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ah! Sun-flower: date and plate l. 1

    The poem's three companions on a single plate are all flower poems about desire, jealousy and restraint. Blake's own title and spelling are 'Ah! Sun-flower'; the archive's text normalises them. The poem has two quatrains of mainly anapaestic trimeter, rhymed abab. The opening 'Ah' can be read as sympathy, or weariness, or longing, and the sentence is incomplete: the whole poem is a single sentence, an address to the flower whose main verb, 'Arise', is held back until the seventh line, which suits its theme of desire that never arrives. The flower is 'weary of time' because it is bound to the sun's daily course.

    The William Blake Archive (ed. M. Eaves, R. N. Essick, J. Viscomi): illuminated copies; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (Oxford UP, 1970), introduction and commentary

  2. Counting the sun's steps: Clytie l. 2

    In Metamorphoses 4, the nymph Clytie, abandoned by the Sun, sits on the ground nine days watching his course until she becomes a flower (the heliotrope) that still turns towards him. Later tradition identified the flower with the sunflower. Blake's flower 'countest the steps of the sun', measuring time by its motion: devotion becomes slavery to time. The image combines natural observation (heliotropism) with the classical emblem of unrequited love.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.256-270 (Clytie); S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965); 'Ah! Sun-flower', Wikipedia

  3. The golden clime l. 3

    'Clime' means a region or zone. The sunset west has long been the place of the Hesperides and the Isles of the Blessed, and in Christian imagery of heaven. The traveller whose 'journey is done' suggests life's pilgrimage ending in death. Blake's irony, as many readers see it, is that this heaven is only another form of the sunflower's endless longing: it is defined by being always elsewhere.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963)

  4. The traveller's journey l. 4

    Hebrews 11:13-16 calls the faithful 'strangers and pilgrims on the earth' who seek 'a better country, that is, an heavenly'. Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress (1678), which Blake later illustrated, makes the journey to the Celestial City the plot of a Christian life. Blake's line keeps the comfort of that idea but places it in the sunflower's longing gaze: rest is imagined at the sunset, the point where the day's journey, and life itself, ends. The quiet monosyllables of 'is done' give the line a dying fall.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Hebrews 11:13-16; Revelation 14:4; John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), the Land of Beulah and the Celestial City; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  5. The pale virgin shrouded in snow l. 6

    White and snow are traditional emblems of virginity (compare the virgins who follow the Lamb in Revelation 14:4). Blake turns the emblem cold: the virgin is 'pale' and 'shrouded', so her purity is a kind of death in life. In the companion poems on the same plate, 'My Pretty Rose Tree' and 'The Lilly', Blake also explores refusal and jealousy in love; Some commentators read the three poems on the plate as a group about the costs of restraint. 'Shrouded' also means hidden: her desire is concealed even from herself.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 14:4; S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  6. The Youth and the pale Virgin ll. 5-6

    The Youth's wasting recalls the lovelorn figures of pastoral and Ovidian tradition, and the virgin 'shrouded in snow' suggests a chaste death, her shroud the whiteness of virginity and frigidity. In Blake's Songs of Experience, sexual restraint imposed by religion and morality is a recurrent target ('Thou shalt not' writ over the door in 'The Garden of Love'). These figures 'Arise from their graves' and aspire to the same place as the sunflower: Blake suggests that their heaven is the sublimated form of their denied desire.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963); William Blake, 'The Garden of Love', Songs of Experience (1794)

  7. Arise ... and aspire l. 7

    Grammatically 'Arise' could be indicative (they arise), subjunctive (where they may arise), or imperative. 'Aspire' combines rising (Latin aspirare, to strive upward) with ambition. Readers divide: some take the poem as a sympathetic picture of longing for transcendence; many others take it as an ironic critique, since the dead 'arise' only into another longing. The final line, 'Where my Sunflower wishes to go', ends with wishing, not arriving, and the speaker's 'my' implies that he shares the desire he describes.

    Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  8. Desire deferred l. 8

    The repeated 'Where' (ll. 4, 5, 8) ties the poem into a loop. It is often read alongside Blake's proverb in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, 'He who desires but acts not, breeds pestilence'. In 1948 the poet Allen Ginsberg reported a visionary experience in Harlem of hearing Blake's voice reciting this poem, which he later described often; it is one example of the poem's influence on the Beat poets.

    William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), 'Proverbs of Hell'; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; 'Ah! Sun-flower', Wikipedia (reception, including Ginsberg)
