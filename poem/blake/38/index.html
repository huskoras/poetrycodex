Infant Sorrow

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Living

My mother groaned, my father wept:
Into the dangerous world I leapt,
Helpless, naked, piping loud,
Like a fiend hid in a cloud.

Struggling in my father's hands,
Striving against my swaddling-bands,
Bound and weary, I thought best
To sulk upon my mother's breast.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Infant Sorrow and Infant Joy title; l. 1

    In 'Infant Joy' a two-day-old baby says 'I happy am, / Joy is my name'. 'Infant Sorrow', published in Songs of Experience (1794), gives the newborn's own voice too, but describes a hostile entry into life: the mother groans in labour, the father weeps. A longer Notebook draft continues the infant's story into youth and conflict with a priest; Blake printed only the first two stanzas. The poem's brevity and violence make it one of the most compressed in Experience.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (plate images and transcriptions); The William Blake Archive, Blake's Notebook (the 'Rossetti Manuscript'); Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  2. Into the dangerous world I leapt l. 2

    The verb 'leapt' gives the infant a will. Blake values energy ('Energy is Eternal Delight', The Marriage of Heaven and Hell), and the infant's leap is an expression of it. 'Dangerous' marks the world of Experience. Job 3:3, 'Let the day perish wherein I was born', is an earlier expression of birth as a calamity, and critics have noted the poem's affinities with Job, a book Blake later illustrated.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Bible, King James Version, Job 3:3

  3. Helpless, naked l. 3

    Job 1:21 states the condition of human birth. Critics have also compared the 'naked new-born babe' of Macbeth (I.vii.21), an image of pity. 'Piping' means crying shrilly, but the word recalls the Piper of the Introduction to Songs of Innocence, so that the music of Innocence becomes the infant's wail. The three-part list moves from weakness (helpless, naked) to power (piping loud).

    Bible, King James Version, Job 1:21; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  4. a fiend hid in a cloud l. 4

    In the Introduction to Songs of Innocence the Piper sees 'On a cloud ... a child' who inspires his songs. Here the child is himself a 'fiend' in a cloud, hostile and mysterious. Critics differ on the tone: the image may express the parents' view of the screaming baby, the infant's own rebellious energy (Blake's devils in The Marriage are energetic rebels), or the fallen nature of humanity. The cloud may also suggest the mystery of the soul's coming into the body.

    Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  5. Struggling l. 5

    The participles 'Struggling', 'Striving' and 'Bound' build the stanza's narrative of resistance and defeat. The father's hands, which might hold the child tenderly, become constraints, anticipating the restraining father figures in Blake's prophetic books (Urizen, the oppressive father). The parallel 'father's hands ... mother's breast' at the ends of ll. 5 and 8 shapes the stanza. Critics who read the poem through Blake's later myth see in this father the first figure of patriarchal law that the child will spend his life resisting, though the poem itself keeps to a domestic scene.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  6. swaddling-bands l. 6

    Swaddling was the normal practice of the time, though it was being criticised by reformers influenced by Rousseau's Emile (1762). The KJV uses 'swaddlingband' in Job 38:9, where God makes 'thick darkness a swaddlingband' for the sea, and 'swaddling clothes' of the infant Jesus in Luke 2:7. Blake's infant fights the bands, as he later fights moral and religious constraints.

    Bible, King James Version, Luke 2:7; Job 38:9; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swaddling-band'

  7. Metre and structure ll. 7-8

    The poem's eight lines are mostly iambic or trochaic tetrameter in couplets (wept/leapt, loud/cloud, hands/bands, best/breast). The first stanza is energetic, the second shows exhaustion. 'Bound and weary' turns the physical swaddling into a state of mind; the line's 'I thought best' introduces calculation, the first act of the infant's reasoning in the world of Experience. The final rhyme 'best / breast' joins calculation and nourishment, a pointed ending.

    W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  8. To sulk l. 8

    'Sulk' is the poem's crux. Some critics read the line as defeat, the infant tamed into dependence. Others stress that sulking is a hidden resistance: the infant 'thought best' to appear submissive, like the speaker of 'A Poison Tree' who conceals his wrath. The breast, the source of nourishment, becomes a place of resentment. The longer Notebook draft, in which the infant turns to tricks and smiles and later strikes down the priest, supports a reading of suppressed anger.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1983); W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes
