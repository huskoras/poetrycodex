The Mind

The Human Abstract

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

The Mind

Pity would be no more
If we did not make somebody poor,
And Mercy no more could be
If all were as happy as we.

And mutual fear brings Peace,
Till the selfish loves increase;
Then Cruelty knits a snare,
And spreads his baits with care.

He sits down with his holy fears,
And waters the ground with tears;
Then Humility takes its root
Underneath his foot.

Soon spreads the dismal shade
Of Mystery over his head,
And the caterpillar and fly
Feed on the Mystery.

And it bears the fruit of Deceit,
Ruddy and sweet to eat,
And the raven his nest has made
In its thickest shade.

The gods of the earth and sea
Sought through nature to find this tree,
But their search was all in vain:
There grows one in the human Brain.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Human Abstract: title and pairing title; l. 1

    Blake's Notebook draft of the poem bears the title 'The Human Image', which makes the pairing with Innocence's 'The Divine Image' clear. The final title adds the idea of abstraction, which for Blake meant reasoning separated from imagination and the living particular, the faculty personified as Urizen. The opening reverses 'The Divine Image': if pity and mercy require poverty and unhappiness, then the virtues praised in Innocence depend on, and may help preserve, the inequality that produces the need for them.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), text and Notebook variants; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E27 (Blake Digital Text Project); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957)

  2. If we did not make somebody poor ll. 1-4

    The verb 'make' is the poem's key: poverty is caused by social arrangements, and charity can be a way of preserving them. 'If all were as happy as we' reveals a speaker who is comfortable, and some critics hear him as the voice of selfish rationalisation, others as Blake's own irony at such reasoning. The stanza anticipates the social critique of 'Holy Thursday' and 'London' in Experience, and reverses the earlier song's identification of pity with the divine.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  3. And mutual fear brings Peace l. 5

    Hobbes argued that the natural condition of mankind is a war of all against all and that men accept government from fear of violent death (Leviathan, 1651, ch. 13-17). Blake's line describes the same armed peace, internationally a balance of terror between states, and personally a wary co-existence. 'Till the selfish loves increase' shows its instability: when self-interest grows, Cruelty begins to lay traps. The stanza transforms Peace, the fourth of the Innocence virtues, into its experiential counterpart.

    Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan (1651), chs. 13-17; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  4. Cruelty knits a snare, / And spreads his baits with care ll. 7-8

    The biblical image of the fowler's snare (Psalm 91:3, 'the snare of the fowler') describes the plots of the wicked. Blake makes Cruelty a patient, careful figure who 'sits down with his holy fears' and 'waters the ground with tears', the language of piety masking aggression. Erdman read the figure as the priest and the moral law combined. The garden imagery, watering and roots, begins here, and it grows into the tree of the next stanzas.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Psalm 91:3; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  5. He sits down with his holy fears ll. 9-10

    'Holy fear' is a devotional phrase for reverent awe of God, as in the 'fear of the Lord' that is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10). Blake turns it into a tool of control: Cruelty's pious fears and tears nourish the growth of submission. The same pattern of watering appears in 'A Poison Tree', where the speaker waters his wrath 'in fears' and 'with my tears'. Glen and others stress how the Songs of Experience show religious emotion converted into domination.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Proverbs 9:10; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E27-28 (Blake Digital Text Project)

  6. Then Humility takes its root / Underneath his foot ll. 11-12

    In conventional Christian ethics humility is a cardinal virtue; Blake shows it as the result of oppression, rooted literally under the oppressor's heel. The image corresponds to The Book of Ahania (1795), where the Tree of Mystery first appears in Blake's prophetic books as Urizen's creation, and where Fuzon's body is nailed to it. The grammar of growth, root, shade, fruit, nest, follows organically from the first act of cruelty.

    William Blake, The Book of Ahania (1795); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E27 (Blake Digital Text Project)

  7. the dismal shade / Of Mystery ll. 13-16

    Revelation 17:5 describes the woman on the beast with the name 'MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS'. Blake associated 'Mystery' with the state religion that hides power behind incomprehensible doctrine; in his later prophecies the Tree of Mystery is a central symbol of the moral law. The caterpillar and fly that 'Feed on the Mystery' suggest priests and parasites who live off the system. Erdman connected this imagery with Blake's radical politics of the 1790s and its attacks on church and state.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Revelation 17:5; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  8. the caterpillar and fly ll. 15-16

    The caterpillar appears in Blake's Proverbs of Hell: 'As the caterpiller chooses the fairest leaves to lay her eggs on, so the priest lays his curse on the fairest joys'. The fly is also an insect of decay. Editors therefore read the creatures as priests and as the moral and social parasites who live on religion. The image is consistent with the tree's growth as a natural process that produces its own pests.

    William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), 'Proverbs of Hell', plates 7-10; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977)

  9. the fruit of Deceit, / Ruddy and sweet to eat ll. 17-20

    Genesis 3:6 describes the forbidden fruit as 'good for food' and 'pleasant to the eyes'. Blake's 'Ruddy and sweet' fruit is a temptation that conceals its nature, as the poison apple in 'A Poison Tree' does. The raven is an ill-omened carrion bird; Noah's raven (Genesis 8:7) did not return to the ark, and in folklore the raven feeds on the dead. Some critics read it as a figure of death or of priestly power nesting in the darkest shade of Mystery.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Genesis 3:6; 8:7; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  10. There grows one in the human Brain ll. 21-24

    The closing stanza turns the allegory inward. The 'gods of the earth and sea' may be pagan nature deities or the natural philosophers and priests who sought a natural basis for moral law; nature contains no such tree. The tree of Mystery, with its cruelty, humility and deceit, is a creation of the mind, specifically of the abstracting reason that Blake later called Urizen. The point is hopeful as well as severe: what the mind has made, the mind can unmake. The Book of Ahania gives the tree's mythic origin.

    Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E27 (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, The Book of Ahania (1795)
