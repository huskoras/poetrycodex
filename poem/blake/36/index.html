Social Commentaries

London

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Social Commentaries

I wandered through each chartered street,
Near where the chartered Thames does flow,
A mark in every face I meet,
Marks of weakness, marks of woe.

In every cry of every man,
In every infant's cry of fear,
In every voice, in every ban,
The mind-forged manacles I hear:

How the chimney-sweeper's cry
Every blackening church appalls,
And the hapless soldier's sigh
Runs in blood down palace-walls.

But most, through midnight streets I hear
How the youthful harlot's curse
Blasts the new-born infant's tear,
And blights with plagues the marriage-hearse.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Blake walks a city where even the streets and the river are ‘charter’d’ — owned, mapped, sold. His ‘mind-forg’d manacles’ locate oppression not only in church, palace and law but in the minds that consent to them: public anguish, heard street by street.

Study notes by the editors

  1. I wandered through: the text l. 1

    Blake's etched plate in Songs of Experience (1794) reads 'I wander thro' each charter'd street'. The present tense matters: the speaker is walking now, and what he hears is ongoing, not remembered. The past tense 'wandered', the spellings 'chartered' and 'mind-forged', and 'A mark' in line 3 come from later printed versions. Sampson's 1905 edition, which collated Blake's originals with nineteenth-century editions, shows how editors such as W. M. Rossetti introduced changes. Students quoting the poem should check Blake's own text in Erdman's edition or the Blake Archive.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 26-27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, 'London' and textual notes; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  2. chartered street l. 1

    A charter is a legal grant of privileges, like Magna Carta or the royal charters of trading companies and boroughs. The patriotic sense is freedom guaranteed; Blake's sense is the opposite, every street owned, regulated and exploited. In Rights of Man (Part I, 1791) Thomas Paine wrote that 'Every chartered town is an aristocratical monopoly in itself', and in Part II (1792) that charters work by taking rights away. Sampson records that Blake's Notebook draft read 'dirty' for 'charter'd' in both lines one and two; the revision turns physical filth into a political diagnosis. RPO's note adds that the word may glance ironically at 'Rule, Britannia', with its boast of Britain's divine 'charter' and of Britons who never will be slaves.

    Thomas Paine, Rights of Man, Part I (1791) and Part II (1792), ch. V (Project Gutenberg); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on ll. 1-2; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto; ed. Northrop Frye), 'London', editorial notes; E. P. Thompson, Witness Against the Beast: William Blake and the Moral Law (Cambridge, 1993), chapter on 'London'

  3. the chartered Thames l. 2

    The repetition extends commercial ownership from the man-made street to nature itself. The Thames was the commercial artery of London, its traffic and riverside rights governed by the City Corporation and chartered companies. To call the river 'chartered' is to suggest that nothing, not even flowing water, escapes being owned. The word also turns the patriotic language of liberty back on itself: the 'charter' that was supposed to make Britons free has become the instrument of their confinement. The doubled word, like the many repetitions that follow, gives the walk a numbing sameness: everywhere the speaker turns, he meets the same condition.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on ll. 1-2; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto; ed. Northrop Frye), 'London', editorial notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 26, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  4. And mark / A mark: a textual crux l. 3

    In Blake's plate the speaker actively marks, that is, notices or registers, the 'marks' on every face, so the verb and noun play against each other: the observer's attention is part of what the poem examines. Sampson records that the Notebook draft first read 'And see', and that 'A mark' is a misreading of W. M. Rossetti (corrected in some later editions), followed by Ellis and Yeats. The archive text here prints that variant, which loses the speaker's agency and the pun. With Blake's reading the line implies that the observer may be imposing as well as discovering the signs he reads, a point some critics, such as Heather Glen, develop.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on l. 3; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 26, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  5. Marks of weakness, marks of woe l. 4

    In Ezekiel 9:4 God commands a man with an inkhorn to 'set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations' in Jerusalem, before the city's destruction. In Revelation 13:16-17 the beast causes all to receive a mark without which no one can buy or sell, an image of a commercial tyranny that fits a 'chartered' city. The mark of Cain (Genesis 4:15) is a third possibility. Blake's speaker reads faces like a prophet, and the biblical echoes cast London as a city under judgment. The line's balanced repetition, with 'woe' sounding through the poem's later cries, sets the tone of lamentation.

    Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 9:4; Bible (KJV), Revelation 13:16-17; Bible (KJV), Genesis 4:15

  6. every ... every: anaphora and the poem's form ll. 5-7

    The second stanza piles up 'every' five times in three lines, a rhetorical device called anaphora when it begins successive clauses. The effect is totalising: there is no exception, no private space outside the condition described. The poem's form is simple, four quatrains of mostly eight-syllable lines rhymed alternately, the measure of hymn and ballad, and some lines drop an initial syllable to begin with a stress ('How the chimney-sweeper's cry'), quickening the rhythm. Sense moves from sight (stanza 1) to hearing (stanzas 2-4): the speaker 'hears' the manacles, the cries and the curse, so the city is above all a soundscape of distress.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 26-27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  7. ban l. 7

    'Ban' had several senses current in Blake's day: a formal curse or anathema; an official prohibition or proclamation, such as the royal proclamations against seditious writings and meetings in the early 1790s; and the 'banns' of marriage read in church, which anticipates the 'marriage-hearse' of the final line. Every voice, then, carries prohibition, curse or legal binding. The sequence 'cry', 'cry', 'voice', 'ban' moves from involuntary sound to articulate, institutional speech, preparing the claim that the manacles are heard in all of it. Historical critics such as Erdman have read the stanza against the government's repression of radical societies in 1792-4.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ban, n.1' and 'banns, n.'; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977), on 'London'; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  8. mind-forged manacles l. 8

    The phrase is the poem's most quoted. It locates oppression not only in institutions but in consciousness: the citizens are bound by beliefs and habits that minds have forged, whether the minds of rulers or their own. Sampson records the Notebook's first reading, 'The german forged links I hear', deleted. RPO's note suggests this may reflect popular resentment at German mercenaries in Britain; the Hanoverian royal family was also German. Blake's revision universalises a topical political jibe into a psychological and philosophical claim. Critics differ on emphasis: some stress self-imposed submission, others the way power manufactures consent. Blake etched 'mind-forg'd'.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on l. 8; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto; ed. Northrop Frye), 'London', editorial notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  9. the chimney-sweeper's cry l. 9

    Climbing boys, some as young as four or five, were apprenticed to master sweeps and forced up narrow flues, suffering burns, deformities and cancers of the scrotum. Their street cry, ''weep, 'weep', is punned on in both Songs titled 'The Chimney Sweeper', in Innocence and in Experience, where the child says his parents 'are both gone up to the church to pray'. An Act of 1788 tried, largely ineffectively, to regulate the trade. Here the sweep's cry 'appalls' the church: it should horrify the institution that ignores, or profits from, such children.

    William Blake, 'The Chimney Sweeper' (Innocence, 1789; Experience, 1794), Erdman pp. 10, 22-23; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  10. blackening church appalls l. 10

    'Appal' comes from Old French apalir, to grow or make pale, and Blake's readers would also hear 'pall', the black cloth spread over a coffin. The sweep's cry both shocks the church and drapes it in mourning or guilt. 'Blackening' points to the soot of London, which literally blackened church walls, and to the church's growing moral darkness through complicity with child labour. Sampson records a Notebook reading, 'Blackens o'er the churches walls', which made the cry itself the agent that stains the church. The final text keeps the ambiguity of who is appalled and who is blackened.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'appal, v.' (etymology: Old French apalir); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on l. 10; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  11. the hapless soldier's sigh l. 11

    'Hapless' means unfortunate. Soldiers in the 1790s were drawn largely from the poor, recruited by bounty, poverty or coercion, and the war against France that began in 1793 made the army's demands visible in London. The soldier's sigh, a near-silent sound, becomes in the next line a stream of blood. The church (stanza 3, line 2) and the palace (line 4) are paired as the two powers responsible: one presides over the exploitation of children, the other over the sacrifice of soldiers.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977), on 'London' and the war of 1793

  12. Runs in blood down palace-walls l. 12

    A sigh cannot literally run in blood, and the synaesthesia (sound becoming visible liquid) makes the image prophetic. Blood on palace walls recalls Daniel 5, where a hand writes on the wall of Belshazzar's palace that his kingdom is numbered and finished. Many readers also think of the attack on the Tuileries in Paris on 10 August 1792, when the royal palace was stormed and its guards killed, so the image can imply both the guilt of the state that sheds soldiers' blood and the warning of revolution. Blake keeps both senses: the blood accuses and threatens.

    Bible (KJV), Daniel 5:5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  13. The hidden acrostic HEAR ll. 9-12

    Modern readers have noticed that the third stanza opens its lines with H, E, A and R. Whether intended or not, the acrostic matches the poem's movement from seeing to hearing ('I hear' ends stanza 2, and stanza 4 returns to 'I hear'), and the prophetic command 'Hear!' that opens many Old Testament oracles (for instance Isaiah 1:2, 'Hear, O heavens'). In Blake's etched text the stanza reads 'How the Chimney-sweepers cry / Every blackning Church appalls, / And the hapless Soldiers sigh / Runs in blood down Palace walls', so the acrostic is present in his own words, not only in later printings. The Introduction to Songs of Experience also begins 'Hear the voice of the Bard!'.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); Bible (KJV), Isaiah 1:2; William Blake, 'Introduction', Songs of Experience (Erdman p. 18)

  14. the youthful harlot's curse l. 14

    Prostitution was widespread in late eighteenth-century London, and many prostitutes were very young. 'Curse' works on several levels: the oaths shouted in the midnight streets; a malediction on the society that has made her what she is; and, many critics argue, venereal disease, passed to clients, to their wives, and to children at birth. Sampson records that the Notebook draft first read 'the midnight harlot's curse' and that the stanza first began 'But most the midnight harlot's curse'; 'youthful' in the final text makes her a child as well as a victim, linking her to the sweep and the infant.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on ll. 13-16; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  15. Blasts the new-born infant's tear l. 15

    To blast is to wither by a destructive wind or blight, a farming and biblical word (compare the 'blasted' ears of corn in Pharaoh's dream, Genesis 41:6). The newborn's tear is withered, not soothed: the child is damaged from the first moment of life. A common reading takes this as a reference to congenital infection, which could blind newborns, transmitted from parents infected through prostitution; more broadly, the line shows the harlot's curse passing to the next generation. The rhyme 'curse'/'hearse' and the sound pattern 'blasts'/'blights' bind the stanza together with the force of an incantation.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 41:6; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on ll. 13-16

  16. marriage-hearse l. 16

    The final compound joins the beginning and end of the social life-cycle in a single vehicle. Readings include the literal spread of disease from the brothel into marriage, the hypocrisy of a moral system in which respectable marriage and prostitution sustain each other, and the deadening of love by law (Blake attacks marriage as a legal bond elsewhere, as in Visions of the Daughters of Albion). Sampson records that the Notebook draft first read 'Weaves around the marriage hearse'. Blake's 'blights with plagues' recalls the plagues of Egypt and of Revelation, completing the poem's prophetic frame.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 131, textual note on ll. 13-16; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 27, text of 'London' (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, Visions of the Daughters of Albion (1793), Blake Digital Text Project
