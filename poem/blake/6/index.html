The Chimney-Sweeper

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Innocence

Social Commentaries

When my mother died I was very young,
And my father sold me while yet my tongue
Could scarcely cry "Weep! weep! weep! weep!"
So your chimneys I sweep, and in soot I sleep.

There's little Tom Dacre, who cried when his head,
That curled like a lamb's back, was shaved; so I said,
"Hush, Tom! never mind it, for, when your head's bare,
You know that the soot cannot spoil your white hair."

And so he was quiet, and that very night,
As Tom was a-sleeping, he had such a sight!--
That thousands of sweepers, Dick, Joe, Ned, and Jack,
Were all of them locked up in coffins of black.

And by came an angel, who had a bright key,
And he opened the coffins, and let them all free;
Then down a green plain, leaping, laughing, they run,
And wash in a river, and shine in the sun.

Then naked and white, all their bags left behind,
They rise upon clouds, and sport in the wind;
And the Angel told Tom, if he'd be a good boy,
He'd have God for his father, and never want joy.

And so Tom awoke, and we rose in the dark,
And got with our bags and our brushes to work.
Though the morning was cold, Tom was happy and warm:
So, if all do their duty, they need not fear harm.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence): context l. 1

    The poem appeared in Songs of Innocence (1789) and was later bound with its counterpart in the combined Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794). Its subject was a public scandal. Small boys, often only four to six years old, were apprenticed to master sweeps to climb inside narrow flues, where they were burned, stunted and choked with soot. Jonas Hanway's Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers (1785) helped bring about the Act of 1788, which set a minimum age of eight but was not enforced. The poem's first appearance in type came in 1824, when Charles Lamb sent it to James Montgomery's campaigning anthology The Chimney-Sweeper's Friend. The speaker is himself a sweep, and nothing in his voice condemns the system. The indictment lies in what he reports, not in what he concludes.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785); UK Parliament, Living Heritage: 'Children and chimneys' (the Acts of 1788, 1834, 1840 and 1875); James Montgomery, ed., The Chimney-Sweeper's Friend, and Climbing-Boy's Album (London: Longman, 1824) [prints Blake's Innocence poem, 'communicated' by Charles Lamb]; 'The Chimney Sweeper', Wikipedia (dates, plate order and historical context)

  2. 'sold me' l. 2

    Climbing-boys were bound by indenture to a master sweep, often until their teens, and poor parents or parish officers received a small payment. The child's flat verb 'sold' names the transaction without legal euphemism. The two-line history, mother dead and father selling, leaves him with no natural parent. The loss is answered at the end, where the angel promises Tom that he will have 'God for his father'. Hanway's book and the parliamentary debates of the 1780s document this trade in children, and Erdman's Prophet Against Empire reads the Songs against such evidence.

    Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785); UK Parliament, Living Heritage: 'Children and chimneys' (the Acts of 1788, 1834, 1840 and 1875); David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977)

  3. 'Weep! weep!' l. 3

    Sweeps advertised for work by crying 'Sweep!' through the streets. A child 'very young', whose tongue 'Could scarcely cry', manages only 'weep', and so his trade cry turns into a lament without his knowing it. The pun is the poem's central irony in small: the speaker never complains, but his words do the weeping for him. The same cry returns in the Experience counterpart, where the observer hears it openly as 'notes of woe'.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; 'The Chimney Sweeper', Wikipedia (dates, plate order and historical context); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  4. 'your chimneys' l. 4

    Until this point the stanza is autobiography. With 'your' the child addresses whoever is reading, presumably someone with chimneys of their own. The line is simple, almost cheerful, but it leaves the responsibility at the reader's door. Heather Glen and Zachary Leader both stress that the Innocence speaker does not judge; the judgment is passed to the audience by moments of direct address like this one.

    Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  5. 'in soot I sleep' l. 4

    Witnesses before parliamentary committees and writers such as Hanway described boys sleeping on the soot they had gathered, and going unwashed for long periods. The internal rhyme 'sweep'/'sleep' binds labour and rest together, so that even sleep belongs to the trade. Tom's dream in the following stanzas happens in this same sooty sleep. The detail is easy to pass over in a light, rhyming line, and that is part of Blake's method: the worst facts are stated as plain matters of course.

    Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785); UK Parliament, Living Heritage: 'Children and chimneys' (the Acts of 1788, 1834, 1840 and 1875)

  6. Tom Dacre l. 5

    No real boy has been identified behind the name. Readers have heard a play on 'dark' in 'Dacre', or noted that Dacre was a noble surname, but these are conjectures that the standard editions do not endorse. What matters is that Tom is singled out from the anonymous 'thousands of sweepers' (l. 11) and given a dream, an inner life the trade ignores. The speaker, a little older in experience, plays the comforter, and the friendship of the two boys carries much of the poem's tenderness.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., William Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience (facsimile with introduction and commentary; Oxford, 1970)

  7. 'like a lamb's back' l. 6

    The comparison ties Tom to 'The Lamb' in the same collection and to the Lamb of God of John 1:29. Shearing is the lot of sheep, and Isaiah's image of the suffering servant ('as a sheep before her shearers is dumb', Isaiah 53:7) shadows the scene: Tom cries, then is 'quiet'. Sweeps' heads were shaved to keep out soot and vermin and to stop hair catching fire in a hot flue.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785)

  8. 'was shaved' and the comfort ll. 6-8

    The comfort in ll. 7-8 is a child's logic: if the hair is gone, the soot cannot ruin it. It turns a loss into a gain, as the whole poem turns suffering into acceptance. Readers differ on whether Blake wants us to smile or to wince. Glen reads such moments as an honest picture of how the oppressed accommodate themselves to their condition, so that we see both the kindness and its cost.

    Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  9. 'white hair' l. 8

    White and black organise the poem: soot, 'coffins of black' and 'the dark' on one side, white hair and the sweepers 'naked and white' on the other. The colours are moral as well as literal, and Blake's readers would know the robes of the redeemed, 'made ... white in the blood of the Lamb' (Revelation 7:14). The whiteness that cannot be spoiled anticipates the washed bodies of the dream, but here it survives only by being removed, a bleak hint about how innocence is preserved.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  10. Tom's dream ll. 10-20

    Stanzas 3 to 5 report what Tom saw asleep: sweepers locked in coffins, an angel with a key, release into a green land, washing and ascent. The dream can be read as genuine visionary consolation, for Blake valued dreams and visions, or as the internalised promise of a heaven that makes earthly suffering bearable. Gleckner and later critics stress that both readings remain open because Blake gives us only the child's point of view. The illuminated plate illustrates the poem, and students should look at it in the William Blake Archive alongside the text.

    The William Blake Archive, ed. Morris Eaves, Robert N. Essick and Joseph Viscomi (images and transcriptions of the illuminated plates); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  11. 'coffins of black' l. 12

    Flues could be very narrow, and climbing-boys died from suffocation, burns and falls, or later from soot-caused disease; Percivall Pott described the sweeps' cancer of the scrotum in 1775. Tom's dream fuses workplace and grave, so that to be a sweep is already to be buried alive. 'Locked up' prepares for the angel's key, and the names 'Dick, Joe, Ned, and Jack' make the 'thousands' individual and ordinary.

    Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785); UK Parliament, Living Heritage: 'Children and chimneys' (the Acts of 1788, 1834, 1840 and 1875); 'The Chimney Sweeper', Wikipedia (dates, plate order and historical context)

  12. The angel with the key l. 13

    In Revelation 1:18 the risen Christ has 'the keys of hell and of death', and in Revelation 20:1 an angel comes down 'having the key of the bottomless pit'. Tom's angel uses his key to set the boys free. The promise is resurrection, but the dream also voices an earthly wish, to be let out of the chimneys. Critics differ over whether the angel speaks for Blake's God of vision or for the Church's counsel of patience, since in l. 19 the same angel attaches a condition: 'if he'd be a good boy'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  13. 'a green plain ... a river' ll. 15-16

    The children run, leap, laugh, wash and shine: everything the trade denies them, from freedom of movement to cleanliness, warmth and light. The 'green plain' recalls the 'green pastures' and 'still waters' of Psalm 23:2, and the river suggests both baptism and the 'pure river of water of life' of Revelation 22:1. The tense shifts to the present ('they run'), so the vision feels as if it is happening now. This green world is the usual setting of Innocence, as in 'The Ecchoing Green'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  14. 'naked and white' l. 17

    The bags are the sacks in which sweeps carried soot, so leaving them behind means leaving labour itself. Nakedness here is not shame but the freedom of Eden and of resurrected bodies, and their whiteness answers the black of the coffins. 'They rise upon clouds, and sport in the wind' gives them an ascension. In the last stanza the bags come back, 'with our bags and our brushes to work', a reversal that returns the dream to daily fact.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  15. 'if he'd be a good boy' l. 19

    Up to here the dream offers release without conditions. Now the angel speaks the language of the nursery and the Sunday school. Readers divide. Some take the line straightforwardly: a loving God rewards the faithful child, and Tom's faith leaves him 'happy and warm'. Others, notably Glen and Leader, see the poem exposing how promises of future reward encourage present submission. The child's comfort is real, but so is the use to which it can be put. The Experience counterpart makes the criticism explicit with 'God and his priest and king'.

    Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966)

  16. 'God for his father' l. 20

    The promise closes the circle opened by 'my father sold me'. It echoes biblical adoption language: 'I ... will be a Father unto you' (2 Corinthians 6:18), and 'When my father and my mother forsake me, then the Lord will take me up' (Psalm 27:10). 'Never want joy' uses 'want' in the sense of 'lack', so the children who lack everything now are promised plenty hereafter. Whether that is consolation or compensation is the question the poem leaves with its reader.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  17. 'we rose in the dark' l. 21

    The last stanza returns from vision to routine. Rising 'in the dark' was the climbing-boy's ordinary day, and the 'cold' morning is literal, since the boys were poorly clothed and often barefoot. The contrast with the sunlit dream is sharp, and yet Tom is 'happy and warm'. The warmth can be taken as the real inner effect of faith or as an illusion that keeps him at work.

    Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  18. 'bags and ... brushes' l. 22

    The repeated word links the two worlds: in the dream the boys leave their bags behind (l. 17), and awake they pick them up again. Bags and brushes are the plain inventory of a sweep's working life, and their flatness after the vision gives the stanza its quiet irony. The alliteration of 'bags' and 'brushes' gives the line a brisk, workaday sound, and the plural 'our' quietly includes the speaker among the workers, as he has been all along.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  19. The closing moral l. 24

    The line has the shape of the improving maxims of children's verse such as Isaac Watts's Divine Songs (1715), which Blake's Songs often answer. In context it is bitterly ironic: the sweeps' 'duty' is to climb the chimneys that maim and kill them, and harm is all around them. Critics differ on where the irony lies. Gleckner sets the child's innocent view against the reader's knowledge; Glen argues that the poem shows how an ideology of duty is absorbed by its victims, without mocking the child's faith. Either way, the moral is the speaker's, not Blake's.

    Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  20. Metre and rhyme whole poem

    Each stanza rhymes aabb, with four strong beats a line and often two light syllables between them ('There's little Tom Dacre, who cried when his head'). The skipping rhythm suits a child's voice and the popular songs Blake imitates, and it sits uneasily with the subject; that friction is part of the effect. Internal rhyme adds to the sing-song quality: 'sweep'/'sleep' in l. 4, the long vowel of 'white' echoed in 'behind' in l. 17.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  21. The two Chimney Sweepers whole poem

    The two poems are meant to be read together as 'contrary states'; the combined title page calls the Songs 'Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul'. Innocence gives a child who accepts and is consoled. Experience gives a child who knows exactly who has made him miserable: parents gone 'to praise God and his priest and king'. Reading each poem through the other changes both. The Innocence moral seems darker, and the Experience anger seems to grow from the same situation.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)
