Religion

From 'Milton'

by William Blake 1757–1827

From The Oxford Book of English Mystical Verse, ed. D. H. S. Nicholson and A. H. E. Lee (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1917)

Religion

And did those feet in ancient time
Walk upon England's mountains green?
And was the holy Lamb of God
On England's pleasant pastures seen?

And did the Countenance Divine
Shine forth upon our clouded hills?
And was Jerusalem builded here
Among these dark Satanic Mills?

Bring me my bow of burning gold!
Bring me my arrows of desire!
Bring me my spear! O clouds, unfold!
Bring me my chariot of fire!

I will not cease from mental fight,
Nor shall my sword sleep in my hand,
Till we have built Jerusalem
In England's green and pleasant land.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Extract from Milton (the lyric 'And did those feet in ancient time').

Study notes by the editors

  1. The preface to Milton l. 1

    Blake's Milton: A Poem in Two Books has 1804 on its title page, but the plates were etched and printed later. The Preface attacks the 'Stolen and Perverted Writings of Homer and Ovid' and calls on 'Young Men of the New Age' to oppose 'the hirelings in the Camp, the Court, & the University'. The lyric follows, and the plate ends with Numbers 11:29: 'Would to God that all the Lords people were Prophets'. Of the four surviving copies, only A and B include the Preface. The lyric had no title; it became 'Jerusalem' through Parry's 1916 setting.

    The William Blake Archive (ed. M. Eaves, R. N. Essick, J. Viscomi): illuminated copies; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Numbers 11:29

  2. Christ in England: the legend ll. 1-4

    Medieval Glastonbury legend made Joseph of Arimathea, who buried Christ (Matthew 27:57-60), the founder of English Christianity; a later tradition had him bring the boy Jesus to Britain. Blake also held a mythic belief, shared with some antiquaries of his day, that Britain was the original home of the patriarchs and Druidism of primeval religion ('All things Begin & End in Albions Ancient Druid Rocky Shore', Jerusalem plate 27). The questions are grammatically open: most readers think the implied answer is 'no', not literally, but the questions ask whether the divine could be present here now.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 27:57-60; 'And did those feet in ancient time', Wikipedia (the Glastonbury legend); S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965)

  3. The Lamb of God l. 3

    The Lamb is Christ as sacrificial victim (John 1:29; Revelation 5-7, where the Lamb reigns in the New Jerusalem). In Blake it is also the emblem of Innocence, as in the Songs of Innocence poem 'The Lamb'. The image of a Lamb on 'pleasant pastures' links England's pastoral landscape with divine presence, a pastoral ideal that the 'dark Satanic Mills' of stanza 2 threaten.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 1:29; Revelation 21:22-23; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  4. Four questions, then four commands st. 1-2

    The structure moves from doubt to resolve. The 'Countenance Divine' shining forth echoes the Aaronic blessing ('The LORD make his face shine upon thee', Numbers 6:25). 'Clouded hills' may be literal English weather or a figure of spiritual darkness. Stanza 3 answers with 'Bring me...', and stanza 4 ends with an oath, so that the question of whether Jerusalem was ever here becomes a vow to build it. Quatrains of iambic tetrameter rhymed abcb give the hymn-like movement Parry later exploited.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Numbers 6:24-26; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  5. Jerusalem l. 7

    'Builded' is the archaic past participle found in the King James Bible ('Jerusalem is builded as a city that is compact together', Psalm 122:3). In Revelation 21 the New Jerusalem descends from heaven. In Blake's own myth, Jerusalem is the emanation (feminine counterpart) of Albion, the spiritual form of England, and signifies freedom and the forgiveness of sins; his later epic Jerusalem (1804-20) develops the figure. Here the question is whether this holy city was ever 'builded' in industrial England.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 122:3; Revelation 21:2; S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  6. Dark Satanic Mills l. 8

    The Albion Flour Mills near Blackfriars Bridge, a steam-powered mill near Blake's home in Lambeth, burned down in 1791; Erdman and others suggest it as a source. In Blake's prophecies, however, the 'Mills of Satan' grind with the 'Starry Wheels' of rationalist science ('Bacon, Newton & Locke'), and the 'Satanic' world is one of abstraction and law. Some have also proposed the churches, or the universities of the Preface's 'hirelings'. The phrase has passed into common usage for industrial blight, but Blake's sense is wider: the mills are any system that grinds the human imagination.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  7. Bow, arrows, spear ll. 9-11

    The weapons are visionary. Gold is the colour of divine light; 'arrows of desire' express Blake's view that desire is holy energy (compare 'Ah! Sun-flower' and The Marriage of Heaven and Hell). The bow may also recall the archer-poet of Psalms and the bow in the cloud of Genesis 9 as a sign of covenant. The commands rise to a cry to the clouds to 'unfold', perhaps to reveal heaven.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963); S. Foster Damon, A Blake Dictionary (1965)

  8. Chariot of fire l. 12

    In 2 Kings 2:11, Elijah is taken up in a whirlwind with a chariot of fire, and his mantle passes to Elisha. Blake's Milton is a poem about prophetic succession, Milton descending to Earth to enter Blake: the request for the chariot claims the prophet's inheritance. The epigraph from Numbers ('all the Lords people were Prophets') makes this inheritance universal. The phrase gave its title to the 1981 film Chariots of Fire.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Kings 2:11-13; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  9. Mental fight l. 13

    The Preface to Milton accuses the 'Hirelings' of seeking to 'depress Mental & prolong Corporeal War', so 'Mental Fight' is the war of ideas and imagination by which Jerusalem is to be built. The sword that will not 'sleep' in the hand recalls the 'sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God' (Ephesians 6:17). The image was later used for patriotic purposes, especially in the First World War, an irony noted by many critics, since Blake had been tried for sedition in 1804 after an argument with a soldier.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Ephesians 6:13-17; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  10. "Till we have built Jerusalem" ll. 15-16

    The tense shifts from the questions about 'ancient time' to the future. The pronoun moves from 'I' and 'my' to 'we', making the poem communal. The final line, 'In England's green and pleasant land', returns to the pastoral imagery of the opening but now as the site of a work still to be done. Some readers note that Blake's own idea of Jerusalem was universal, not national, so the patriotic reading of the hymn narrows Blake's sense.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963)

  11. Parry's 'Jerusalem' (1916) l. 16

    Robert Bridges included the lyric in his anthology The Spirit of Man (1916) and asked Parry to set it to 'simple music'. The tune was first performed at a Fight for Right meeting in the Queen's Hall, London, in March 1916. Parry, uneasy with the movement's jingoism, later gave the song to the women's suffrage campaign, and Elgar orchestrated it in 1922. It has since been sung at the Women's Institute, at political conferences of left and right, and at sporting events, so its meaning is contested: patriotic hymn, socialist anthem or visionary protest.

    'And did those feet in ancient time', Wikipedia (Parry's setting and reception); Robert Bridges, ed., The Spirit of Man (1916)
