The Garden Of Love

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Religion

I laid me down upon a bank,
Where Love lay sleeping;
I heard among the rushes dank
Weeping, weeping.

Then I went to the heath and the wild,
To the thistles and thorns of the waste;
And they told me how they were beguiled,
Driven out, and compelled to the chaste.

I went to the Garden of Love,
And saw what I never had seen;
A Chapel was built in the midst,
Where I used to play on the green.

And the gates of this Chapel were shut
And "Thou shalt not," writ over the door;
So I turned to the Garden of Love
That so many sweet flowers bore.

And I saw it was filled with graves,
And tombstones where flowers should be;
And priests in black gowns were walking their rounds,
And binding with briars my joys and desires.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The text of the poem sts. 1-2

    In Blake's illuminated Songs of Experience (1794), 'The Garden of Love' has three stanzas only, beginning at l. 9 here. The eight lines beginning 'I laid me down upon a bank' are a separate poem in Blake's Notebook (the Rossetti Manuscript); modern editors such as Erdman print it separately among the Notebook poems. Some older printed texts, like the one in the archive, joined the two. The Notebook lines are thematically close: Love asleep, weeping, and the waste places of the fallen world. Students should cite the engraved three-stanza text as the published poem.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (plate images and transcriptions); The William Blake Archive, Blake's Notebook (the 'Rossetti Manuscript'); Poems of William Blake, Project Gutenberg eBook 574 (a text that prints the Notebook lines with 'The Garden of Love')

  2. Love asleep; rushes dank ll. 1-4

    'Dank' means unpleasantly damp. The sleeping figure of Love may be Cupid or Eros, a familiar pastoral motif, but Blake's sleep is ominous, a suspension of vital energy. The weeping in the rushes is not explained, which is typical of Blake's lyrics: it may be the voices of the 'beguiled' in the next stanza. Read with the engraved poem, the lines suggest a world in which love has been put to sleep by prohibition.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dank'

  3. thistles and thorns l. 6

    The 'heath', 'wild' and 'waste' are uncultivated land, the fallen world outside Eden. In Genesis 3:18 God curses the ground after Adam's sin. Blake's figures are 'driven out', like Adam and Eve expelled from the garden (Genesis 3:24). The heath could also suggest the commons being enclosed in Blake's time, though this reading is a critical suggestion rather than a certainty.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:17-24; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  4. beguiled ... compelled to the chaste ll. 7-8

    Blake reverses the biblical story: the deception is not temptation to sin but persuasion into self-denial. 'Compelled to the chaste' uses the adjective as a noun, 'compelled to join the chaste' or 'to chastity'. Blake's hostility to enforced chastity is a constant theme, as in the Proverbs of Hell in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell ('Prisons are built with stones of Law, Brothels with bricks of Religion') and in Visions of the Daughters of Albion (1793).

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:13; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  5. The Garden of Love l. 9

    The Song of Solomon 4:12 calls the beloved 'A garden inclosed', and medieval allegory made the garden of love a place of courtship (as in the Roman de la Rose). For Blake the garden is also Eden, and the speaker's childhood green, the free play of Innocence. 'Saw what I never had seen' marks the speaker's arrival at Experience. Critics have read the poem as an attack on the Church of England's sexual morality and on institutional religion generally; others stress personal repression, the internalised law.

    Bible, King James Version, Song of Solomon 4:12; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1983)

  6. in the midst l. 11

    In Genesis 2:9 the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil are 'in the midst of the garden', and 3:3 repeats the prohibition of that tree. Blake replaces the tree with a chapel, so that religion itself becomes the place of prohibition. A 'chapel' may suggest a Dissenting chapel or a chapel of ease of the established Church; critics differ on whether Blake has a specific denomination in mind or means organised religion generally.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 2:9; 3:3; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  7. the green l. 12

    In Songs of Innocence 'The Ecchoing Green' celebrates children playing on a common green watched by the old. The Experience poem builds a chapel on that space. The contrast of the two volumes, 'shewing the two contrary states of the human soul' (as Blake's title page puts it), is enacted within a single line. The plate design shows mourners kneeling at a tomb, which supports the reading of play turned into mourning.

    The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (plate images and transcriptions); Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  8. Thou shalt not l. 14

    Eight of the Ten Commandments in Exodus 20:3-17 begin 'Thou shalt not'. Blake consistently opposed what he saw as a religion of prohibition to the gospel of forgiveness and energy; in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell he has Jesus break the commandments, acting 'from impulse, not from rules'. The shut gates contrast with the open, playing green. Some critics hear in the shut gates a reference to the custom of keeping churches locked except at services.

    Bible, King James Version, Exodus 20:3-17; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977)

  9. flowers and tombstones ll. 15-18

    Flowers in Blake often stand for natural desires and their fulfilment (compare 'Ah! Sun-flower' and 'My Pretty Rose Tree' in the same volume). The substitution of tombstones means that desire, denied, has died. The garden becomes a churchyard, the burial ground beside a church, so that the space of love becomes the space of death. The end-rhyme that 'be' leads us to expect is never supplied; the last two lines rhyme inwardly instead (gowns/rounds, briars/desires), a formal sign of the frustration the image describes.

    Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  10. graves l. 17

    Critics read the graves as the buried desires of those 'compelled to the chaste', and also as the literal deaths that religion, in Blake's view, makes its central concern (the priest's office for burial). The plate's design of kneeling mourners at a tomb makes the connection visual. The line's simple syntax ('And I saw') keeps the visionary tone of the narrative.

    The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (plate images and transcriptions); Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  11. priests in black gowns l. 19

    The black gown was the dress of Anglican clergy when preaching, and also of Dissenting ministers, so the image covers clergy generally. 'Rounds' are the regular patrols of a watchman or warder, implying surveillance. Blake's anticlericalism appears throughout the Songs of Experience, for example in 'The Little Vagabond' and 'London'. Erdman reads the poem within the radical politics of the 1790s; Glen stresses the critique of a morality internalised as well as imposed.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1983); W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  12. The lengthened last lines ll. 19-20

    The poem's anapaestic lines are fairly regular until the final couplet, which grows longer and carries internal rhymes in place of the expected end-rhymes with 'be'. Critics have heard in the dragging, rhyming line the effect of binding: the sound mimics the action. Briars (thorny shrubs) recall the curse on the earth (Genesis 3:18) and perhaps the crown of thorns. 'Joys and desires' ends the poem with what has been bound, not with those who bind.

    W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary
