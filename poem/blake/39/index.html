The Mind

A Poison Tree

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

The Mind

I was angry with my friend:
I told my wrath, my wrath did end.
I was angry with my foe:
I told it not, my wrath did grow.

And I watered it in fears
Night and morning with my tears,
And I sunned it with smiles
And with soft deceitful wiles.

And it grew both day and night,
Till it bore an apple bright,
And my foe beheld it shine,
and he knew that it was mine,--

And into my garden stole
When the night had veiled the pole;
In the morning, glad, I see
My foe outstretched beneath the tree.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Wrath spoken dies; wrath hidden grows into a tree bearing poisoned fruit. Blake stages repression as cultivation — watered with tears, sunned with false smiles — until the enemy lies dead beneath it, and Eden’s tree is reborn as vengeance.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and composition title; st. 1

    The Notebook draft's title makes the satire clear: what passes for Christian forbearance, refraining from expressing anger, is shown to breed hidden malice. The change to 'A Poison Tree' shifts the emphasis from the moral pretension to its fruit. The poem appeared in Songs of Experience (1794). Its brevity and nursery-rhyme manner disguise a psychological insight that later critics have compared to modern ideas of repression, though the language is that of Blake's own moral vision.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; 'A Poison Tree', Wikipedia (Notebook title 'Christian Forbearance'); W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; The William Blake Archive, Blake's Notebook (the 'Rossetti Manuscript')

  2. Telling wrath ll. 1-4

    The first stanza is a perfect antithesis: friend and foe, told and told not, end and grow. Ephesians 4:26 advises 'Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath'. Blake's paradox is that the conventional Christian virtue of suppressing anger towards an enemy (Matthew 5:44, 'Love your enemies') can produce the opposite of love. The Proverbs of Hell in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell make the same point: 'He who desires but acts not, breeds pestilence'.

    Bible, King James Version, Ephesians 4:26; Bible, King James Version, Matthew 5:44; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  3. Metre and growth st. 2

    Most lines have seven syllables and four stresses, beginning and ending on a stress (catalectic trochaic tetrameter), with an initial unstressed 'And' added in several lines. The rhyming couplets and simple vocabulary resemble children's verse and Isaac Watts's Divine Songs, which Blake often parodied. The metaphor of the wrath as a plant is introduced without comment: 'it' is watered by fearful tears, so that the image grows as the anger does.

    W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (William Blake Trust / Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  4. Smiles and wiles ll. 7-8

    Tears water the plant and smiles 'sun' it, so that both grief and pretended friendliness feed the growth. 'Wiles' are tricks or stratagems. The speaker's hypocrisy is described with the gardener's care, a chilling domestic image. Blake's 'The Human Abstract', in the same volume, also shows a tree of cruelty, Mystery, rooted in false virtues and watered with 'holy fears'.

    W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wile'

  5. The apple l. 10

    Genesis does not name the fruit, but the apple was its traditional representation. In Blake's poem the tree is not God's but the speaker's, and the fruit is a lure: the foe is tempted to steal it, as Eve was tempted. The speaker thus plays the role of the tempter, or of the jealous God who forbids. 'Bright' stresses its allure. The tree also anticipates the Tree of Mystery in The Book of Ahania and The Four Zoas.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:1-7; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  6. he knew that it was mine l. 12

    The line is ambiguous: the foe may want the apple because he knows it is the speaker's, out of envy or rivalry, or he may recognise it as the product of the speaker's hatred. Critics have noted that the poem implicates both men: the speaker cultivates a trap, and the foe is a thief. The dash at the end of the line in this text (as in many printings) creates a pause before the night theft.

    W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1983); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  7. stole into my garden l. 13

    The verb 'stole' means crept stealthily, and the context of the apple adds the sense of theft. The garden is the speaker's private Eden, and the trespass recalls Satan's entry into Paradise in Paradise Lost (Book IV), where Satan leaps the wall like a thief. The speaker's garden of hatred contrasts with 'The Garden of Love' in the same volume.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost, iv.183-93; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary

  8. the pole l. 14

    In poetic usage 'the pole' could mean the sky, the heavens, or the pole star (Latin polus, the sky; Greek polos, the axis). 'When the night had veiled the pole' means when darkness had covered the sky. The elevated phrase contrasts with the poem's simple diction, giving a touch of Miltonic gloom to the murder. The night also conceals the speaker's own guilt: he cultivates the tree in daylight with smiles, but the deed is done in darkness. 'Veiled' suggests concealment and mystery, a word Blake elsewhere associates with the veil of the Temple and of priestly secrecy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pole'; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes

  9. The present tense and gladness ll. 15-16

    The narrative has been in the past tense; the final couplet moves into the present, as if the scene were before us. The word 'glad', isolated between commas, is the poem's most disturbing word: it shows the speaker's satisfaction. 'Outstretched' could mean dead or poisoned, or merely lying under the tree; most readers take him to be dead. The poem ends without moral comment, leaving the reader to judge the 'Christian forbearance' that has produced this result.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Experience and Notebook poems, with Bloom's commentary; W. H. Stevenson, ed., Blake: The Complete Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 3rd edn, 2007), notes; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1983)
