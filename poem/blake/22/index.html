Holy Thursday (Experience)

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Religion

Is this a holy thing to see
In a rich and fruitful land,--
Babes reduced to misery,
Fed with cold and usurous hand?

Is that trembling cry a song?
Can it be a song of joy?
And so many children poor?
It is a land of poverty!

And their son does never shine,
And their fields are bleak and bare,
And their ways are filled with thorns:
It is eternal winter there.

For where'er the sun does shine,
And where'er the rain does fall,
Babes should never hunger there,
Nor poverty the mind appall.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Holy Thursday (Experience): context l. 1

    From 1704 the children of the London charity schools met each year for a service of thanksgiving, held at St Paul's by the later eighteenth century. Thousands of children in their school uniforms sang before their patrons, a sight Haydn and Berlioz were among many to remark on. Blake's Innocence poem describes it with wonder. The Experience poem, published in 1794, looks past the spectacle to the poverty that makes charity necessary. 'Holy Thursday' could mean Ascension Day, and Blake's title links the service with it, though surviving records show the meeting held on a Thursday in late May or June, not always on Ascension Day itself.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; 'Charity Children Choirs', Wikipedia (the annual meeting of the London charity schools, 1704-1877); Wellcome Collection: sermon preached at St Paul's on 14 June 1792, at the yearly meeting of the charity-school children; Museum of London: print, 'The Anniversary Meeting of the Charity Children in the Cathedral of St. Paul'; 'Holy Thursday (Songs of Experience)', Wikipedia

  2. 'a holy thing to see' l. 1

    The opening question challenges both the occasion and the Innocence poem. The service was meant to edify by displaying Christian charity, and the speaker asks whether it is holy to watch children reduced to needing it. 'See' matters: the event was a spectacle, and the poem asks what the spectators are really looking at. The word 'thing' is pointed as well: the children become an object on display, much as the sweep is 'a little black thing' in the Experience 'Chimney Sweeper'.

    Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  3. 'a rich and fruitful land' l. 2

    The phrase recalls the biblical promised land, 'a good land ... a land of wheat, and barley, and vines' (Deuteronomy 8:7-9). Britain's growing commercial wealth in the 1790s made the poverty of its children more damning. The point anticipates the last stanza: where nature is bountiful, hunger is man-made. The dash after 'land' in the archive text marks the pause before the shocking third line, where the land's riches meet its children's misery.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977)

  4. 'Babes' l. 3

    The word recalls 'Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise' (Matthew 21:16, quoting Psalm 8:2), a verse that suited a children's service of praise. 'Reduced' implies a fall from a proper condition, as if poverty were a demotion imposed by others. The word belongs to the language of scripture and hymn which the children had been taught, and it contrasts with the 'aged men' who preside over them in the Innocence poem. The very young are made the measure of the nation's virtue.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  5. 'usurous hand' l. 4

    Usury, condemned in scripture (Exodus 22:25; Psalm 15:5), meant exploitative money-lending. Blake suggests that charity-school patronage was a transaction: donors gained credit and a pious reputation, and the schools trained the poor for service. The 'cold' hand looks ahead to the 'eternal winter' of l. 12. 'Usurous' is an older form of 'usurious'. Blake makes the same link between pity and poverty in 'The Human Abstract': 'Pity would be no more, / If we did not make somebody Poor'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977)

  6. 'that trembling cry' l. 5

    In the Innocence poem the children's voices rise 'like a mighty wind' and 'like harmonious thunderings'. Here the same sound is a 'trembling cry', of cold, fear or hunger, and the speaker refuses to call it 'a song of joy'. The question also touches the Songs themselves: Blake calls his poems songs, and this one asks what kind of song can come out of misery.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966)

  7. 'a land of poverty' l. 8

    The paradox sums up the poem's double vision: the nation is rich, and the children's world within it is poor. The exclamation answers the questions of the opening. Stanza 2 is also where the rhyme fails (song, joy, poor, poverty hardly chime), as though the verse itself were losing its music. The line can also be read as the direct answer to l. 7: with so many poor children, England must be called poor, whatever its wealth.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966)

  8. 'their son': the text l. 9

    In the illuminated copies the line reads 'And their sun does never shine', matching 'where'er the sun does shine' in l. 13 (Erdman prints 'sun'). The 'son' in the archive text is not Blake's reading and is almost certainly a misprint. A reader might be tempted to find a pun on the Son, Christ, who does not shine on these children, but the reading has no textual authority. The sunless world belongs to the poem's picture of perpetual winter.

    The William Blake Archive, ed. Morris Eaves, Robert N. Essick and Joseph Viscomi (images and transcriptions of the illuminated plates); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; 'Holy Thursday (Songs of Experience)', Wikipedia

  9. 'filled with thorns' l. 11

    After the Fall God tells Adam that the ground will bring forth 'thorns also and thistles' (Genesis 3:18), and Proverbs 22:5 warns that 'thorns and snares are in the way of the froward'. The children are made to live under the curse without having sinned. In a poem about a church festival the crown of thorns of the Passion is a further association.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  10. 'eternal winter' l. 12

    The stanza builds a landscape of deprivation: no sun, 'bleak and bare' fields, thorny paths. 'Eternal' makes the condition unchanging, unlike the natural turn of seasons, and Frye links such frozen worlds to Blake's tyrannical Urizen. The point is that the children's state is not natural. The last stanza shows that wherever nature works freely there is plenty. The phrase answers the 'rich and fruitful land' of l. 2.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  11. The final stanza ll. 13-16

    The last stanza argues from nature. Sun and rain are given to all alike, as in Matthew 5:45, where God 'maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust'. They should produce plenty for all, so if babes go hungry in such a land, human greed and injustice starve them. The argument is close to the case for public provision for the poor that Paine made in Rights of Man (1792).

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Thomas Paine, Rights of Man, Part II (1792), ch. 5; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977)

  12. 'appall' l. 16

    The word comes from Old French apalir, 'to grow pale', and by Blake's time meant to fill with horror or to daunt. The line makes poverty a damage to the mind, not only a material lack, a theme Blake pursues in 'London' with its 'mind-forg'd manacles'. The poem ends on mental, not physical, suffering. The full rhyme 'fall'/'appall' makes the last word land heavily.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  13. Metre and rhyme whole poem

    Most lines start on the stress ('Babes reduced to misery'), with an occasional eight-syllable line ('Is this a holy thing to see'). The insistent beat suits a run of questions and exclamations. Stanza 1 rhymes fully abab (see/misery, land/hand); stanzas 3 and 4 rhyme only their second and fourth lines (bare/there, fall/appall); stanza 2 hardly rhymes at all. The Innocence 'Holy Thursday' uses long, flowing seven-stress lines; the contrast in metre matches the contrast in vision.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  14. The two 'Holy Thursday' poems whole poem

    The pair shows Blake's two contrary states. In Innocence ('Twas on a Holy Thursday') the children with 'innocent faces clean' file into St Paul's, the 'aged men, wise guardians of the poor' sit beneath them, and the poem ends 'Then cherish pity, lest you drive an angel from your door'. Readers have long debated whether that poem is already ironic; the beadles with 'wands as white as snow' can look like jailers. The Experience poem does not describe the scene at all; it questions it.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)
