Religion

The Divine Image

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Innocence

Religion

To Mercy, Pity, Peace, and Love,
All pray in their distress,
And to these virtues of delight
Return their thankfulness.

For Mercy, Pity, Peace, and Love,
Is God our Father dear;
And Mercy, Pity, Peace, and Love,
Is man, his child and care.

For Mercy has a human heart
Pity, a human face;
And Love, the human form divine;
And Peace, the human dress.

Then every man, of every clime,
That prays in his distress,
Prays to the human form divine:
Love, Mercy, Pity, Peace.

And all must love the human form,
In heathen, Turk, or Jew.
Where Mercy, Love, and Pity dwell,
There God is dwelling too.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Mercy, Pity, Peace, and Love ll. 1-4

    The poem is a short hymn in which a sequence of identifications builds a theology: we pray to these virtues; these virtues are God; they are also man; so in praying to them we pray to the human form. Blake's argument has been connected with his reading of Swedenborg, whose Divine Love and Divine Wisdom (English translation 1788) Blake annotated around 1789, and which identifies the divine with the human. Blake's annotations accept Swedenborg's claim that God is a Man. The four virtues are later 'Love, Mercy, Pity, Peace' (l. 16), the order varying in a hymn-like litany.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E12-13 (Blake Digital Text Project); Blake, annotations to Swedenborg, Divine Love and Divine Wisdom (c. 1789), in Erdman, Complete Poetry and Prose; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  2. Is God our Father dear ... Is man, his child and care ll. 5-8

    The grammar, plural subject and singular verb, presents Mercy, Pity, Peace and Love as one being. The hymn identifies God as Father and man as his child, the language of Christian devotion and of the Lord's Prayer, but the poem's logic goes further: if the virtues are both God and man, then God is present wherever humans show these virtues. Frye and Erdman read this as Blake's humanised Christianity, in which, as he later wrote, 'God only Acts & Is, in existing beings or Men' (The Marriage of Heaven and Hell).

    Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), plate 16

  3. the human form divine l. 11

    Milton, lamenting his blindness, lists what he cannot see, ending 'or flocks, or herds, or human face divine' (PL III.44). Blake turns the phrase into 'the human form divine', and makes it not just an object of sight but the place where divinity resides. Each virtue is given a human attribute: Mercy a heart, Pity a face, Love the whole form, Peace the 'dress', perhaps the outward demeanour or the body's clothing. The phrase recurs in l. 15, and becomes a key term in Blake's later prophecies.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (2nd ed., 1674), III.44; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E13 (Blake Digital Text Project); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  4. Peace, the human dress l. 12

    The rejected Experience plate 'A Divine Image' (etched c. 1794 but included in very few copies) answers this stanza line by line: 'Cruelty has a Human Heart / And Jealousy a Human Face / Terror, the Human Form Divine / And Secrecy, the Human Dress'; that dress is 'forged Iron'. Read together, the poems show that the human form can embody either set of qualities, so that the Innocence vision is not false but partial. Erdman prints 'A Divine Image' among the Songs.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E32 (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  5. every man, of every clime ll. 13-16

    'Clime' means region or climate, hence country. The stanza universalises the argument: prayer offered in distress anywhere is addressed to the same divinity. This prepares the explicit inclusion of 'heathen, Turk, or Jew' in the final stanza, and sets the poem against narrower Christian hymns for children. The stanza also repeats 'prays in his distress' from the first stanza, closing a circle: the opening's general 'All' is now spelled out as every individual, everywhere, whatever his faith.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clime'; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947)

  6. In heathen, Turk, or Jew l. 18

    The Good Friday collect in the Book of Common Prayer prays for 'all Jews, Turks, Infidels, and Hereticks' to be brought into the fold, treating them as outsiders. Isaac Watts's popular Divine Songs for children (1715) taught children to thank God that they were born 'of Christian race, / And not a heathen or a Jew'. Blake insists that the human form must be loved in all of them, since mercy, love and pity are the divine wherever they are. The poem was written when anti-Catholic and anti-Jewish prejudice was common in English public life.

    The Book of Common Prayer (1662), third collect for Good Friday; Isaac Watts, Divine Songs Attempted in Easy Language for the Use of Children (1715), Song VI, 'Praise for the Gospel'; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977)

  7. Where Mercy, Love, and Pity dwell, / There God is dwelling too ll. 19-20

    The final statement makes God immanent in human virtue: wherever mercy, love and pity live, God lives. The thought rests on 1 John 4:12-16 ('If we love one another, God dwelleth in us'), and the medieval hymn 'Ubi caritas et amor, Deus ibi est' ('Where charity and love are, God is there') expresses the same idea. Peace is omitted from the last list, perhaps simply for metre, or because the active virtues are what makes God present.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), 1 John 4:12-16; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  8. Contraries: 'The Human Abstract' and 'A Divine Image' whole poem

    The full title of the combined Songs (1794) speaks of 'the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul'. 'The Human Abstract' argues that pity and mercy depend on poverty and unhappiness, and so exposes the social conditions behind the Innocence virtues; its Notebook draft was titled 'The Human Image', signalling the pairing. 'A Divine Image' inverts the third stanza directly. Gleckner argued that the Songs must be read in such pairs and in the context of their speaker's state, so that neither statement is Blake's final word.

    Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), text and Notebook variants; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E27, E32 (Blake Digital Text Project)

  9. Form whole poem

    Common metre (8.6.8.6 syllables) was the standard measure of English congregational hymns and of Watts's Divine Songs, which Blake's Songs of Innocence imitate and answer. Using it for a hymn that includes 'heathen, Turk, or Jew' is pointed: Blake takes over the form of devotional verse for children to teach a broader religion. The repetitions of the four virtues give the poem a liturgical sound.

    Isaac Watts, Divine Songs Attempted in Easy Language for the Use of Children (1715), Song VI, 'Praise for the Gospel'; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates
