The Tyger

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Religion

Tyger, tyger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
What immortal hand or eye
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies
Burnt the fire of thine eyes?
On what wings dare he aspire?
What the hand dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder and what art
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And, when thy heart began to beat,
What dread hand and what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain?
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp
Dare its deadly terrors clasp?

When the stars threw down their spears,
And watered heaven with their tears,
Did he smile his work to see?
Did he who made the lamb make thee?

Tyger, tyger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
What immortal hand or eye
Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Blake’s most famous lyric asks whether the God who made the gentle Lamb could also forge the Tyger. Its hammering, forge-loud questions never receive an answer — the poem holds creation’s terror and its beauty in the same awed breath.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Tyger, tyger, burning bright l. 1

    The Tyger was etched for Songs of Experience (1794) as the contrary of The Lamb. Its lines are mostly seven-syllable trochaic tetrameters (stressed, unstressed, ending on a stress), the hammering rhythm of charms and children's verse, in rhymed couplets grouped in quatrains. The spelling 'Tyger' was old-fashioned, giving the beast an emblematic, almost heraldic quality. 'Burning bright' identifies the tiger with fire from the first line: its stripes and eyes blaze in the dark. Sampson notes that Blake etched the poem on a single plate from drafts on two facing pages of his Notebook, which reveal extensive revision, and that it was first printed in ordinary type in B. H. Malkin's A Father's Memoirs of His Child (1806).

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 110, 'The Tyger' and headnote; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  2. the forests of the night l. 2

    The genitive turns night into a place, dense as jungle, against which the tiger's fire stands out. Sampson records that Charles Lamb, quoting the poem from memory in 1824, wrote 'Thro' the desarts of the night', a sign of how the poem circulated before it was widely printed, and that Malkin and others printed the singular 'forest'. Readers have interpreted the 'forests' as the dark world of experience, the fallen material world, or the mind's own obscurity; Blake's later myth often uses forests and night for error. The image is also simply vivid: a tiger glimpsed by firelight or in the imagination of someone who has only seen tigers in prints and menageries.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 110, textual note on l. 2; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  3. fearful symmetry l. 4

    Symmetry comes from Greek summetria, 'due proportion', the quality of a well-made body or building. Applied to the tiger, it suggests an object designed with an artist's precision, which raises the question of what kind of designer could intend such terrible beauty. 'Fearful' is ambiguous: it describes the tiger as causing fear, and perhaps the creator or speaker as feeling it. The rhyme 'eye'/'symmetry' was probably imperfect even in Blake's speech, and the slight jolt adds to the unease. Northrop Frye used the phrase as the title of his landmark study of Blake (Fearful Symmetry, 1947), and it has become shorthand for the union of beauty and terror in creation.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'symmetry, n.'

  4. distant deeps or skies l. 5

    The alternatives are vertical: deep below (the sea, the abyss, hell) or high above (the heavens). In the Notebook Blake tried several versions of this stanza; Sampson transcribes a revision on the facing page, 'Burnt in distant deeps or skies / The cruel fire of thine eyes? / Could heart descend, or wings aspire?', which Swinburne explained as asking whether divine power could descend so far, or a lower power rise so high. The engraved text keeps the polarity but removes 'cruel', leaving the moral nature of the fire undecided. The question of origin is the poem's governing uncertainty: the tiger could be the work of God, of a rebel, or of some intermediate maker.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 112-14, note on the Notebook drafts; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  5. On what wings dare he aspire? l. 7

    'Aspire' keeps its Latin sense of rising or mounting up (ad + spirare), as well as ambition. The image of a winged figure daring to fly toward fire evokes Icarus, whose wings melted near the sun, and Milton's Satan, who ventures upward on 'sail-broad vans' through Chaos in Paradise Lost II. The word 'dare' runs through the poem and changes its emphasis from capacity ('could') to audacity and transgression. If the creator must 'dare', creation itself is a risky, perhaps forbidden, act. The pronoun 'he' is the poem's only name for the maker, and it is never identified.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); John Milton, Paradise Lost II.927-38 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII.183-235 (Daedalus and Icarus)

  6. seize the fire: Prometheus l. 8

    Prometheus, in Greek myth, stole fire from Zeus and gave it to mortals; in Aeschylus' Prometheus Bound he is chained to a rock for his defiance. RPO's editorial note identifies the allusion. The figure fits the poem's sense of creation as theft or rebellion against an established order, and links the tiger's maker with the artist and the revolutionary, both of whom Blake associated with fire. Blake's own word 'sieze' in the engraved text is a common eighteenth-century spelling. The stanza's questions, none of them answered, build a portrait of the creator only through his body: hand, eye, wings, shoulder, feet.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto; ed. Northrop Frye), 'The Tyger', editorial notes; Hesiod, Theogony 561-70 (Prometheus' theft of fire), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Loeb, 1914; Project Gutenberg); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  7. shoulder, art, sinews ll. 9-10

    The stanza moves from cosmic space to the workshop. 'Shoulder' implies the labourer's force, 'art' the artisan's skill; Blake, a professional engraver who worked with acids, metal plates and presses, often imagined creation as manual craft. To 'twist the sinews' is to make the tendons and muscle fibres of the heart, as a smith twists metal or a rope-maker twists fibres. Blake's mythic smith Los, who labours at his forge in the later prophecies, offers a parallel for this creator. The heart image prepares the moment when the made thing comes alive and 'began to beat', a startling transition from artefact to animal.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 24, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, The Book of Urizen, chs. IV-V (Los forging Urizen's body), Erdman pp. 70-83

  8. what dread feet l. 12

    Readers have long asked whose hand and feet are meant (the maker's or the tiger's) and what they do. Sampson's account of the Notebook explains the problem: Blake first wrote the line as the start of a sentence continuing 'Could fetch it from the furnace deep / And in thy horrid ribs dare steep', then cancelled the continuation, leaving the line suspended. In the engraved text Blake's pointing ('What dread hand? & what dread feet?') turns it into two exclamatory questions, parallel to 'What the hammer? what the chain?'. Malkin's 1806 printing, probably from a copy Blake supplied, reads 'What dread hand forged thy dread feet?', and Sampson thought it a later authorial revision. Swinburne's emendation 'framed' has, Sampson argues, no manuscript or printed authority; most modern editors keep the engraved reading and its broken syntax.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 111-12, textual note on l. 12 and 'Note on The Tyger'; Martin K. Nurmi, 'Blake's Revisions of The Tyger', PMLA 71 (1956), 669-85; B. H. Malkin, A Father's Memoirs of His Child (London, 1806), p. xxxix (Wikisource)

  9. hammer, chain, furnace, anvil ll. 13-16

    The forge supplies the poem's most insistent imagery, with the rhythm of the questions mimicking hammer-blows. The 'chain' may be part of the smith's equipment (for lifting or holding) or a hint of bondage. Blake associated the forge with Los, the blacksmith-prophet of his later myth, who hammers out forms on his anvil, and many critics also hear the new industrial furnaces of 1790s London, an age of iron. The tiger's 'brain' made in a furnace suggests that its ferocity is the product of fire and pressure. The Notebook shows Blake revising these lines before he settled on the engraved wording. The 'deadly terrors' the maker 'dare' grasp are the tiger's own powers, which the creator must handle at his peril.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 113-14, Notebook transcript, st. 5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 25, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, The Four Zoas, Night V (Los at his forge), Blake Digital Text Project

  10. dread grasp ... deadly terrors clasp ll. 15-16

    Sampson's transcript of the Notebook shows Blake revising the couplet several times: the second foot of 'what the arm' became 'what dread grasp', and the verb shifted between 'grasp' and 'clasp', with 'Could' altered to 'Dare'. The final version keeps both words, the grasping hand and the clasping of terrors, so that the maker seems to embrace what would kill. The change from 'Could' to 'Dare' repeats the poem's larger movement, completed in the last stanza, from questions of power to questions of will. 'Deadly terrors' are the tiger's capacity for killing, but also the terrors the creator must have felt or chosen to make.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 114, Notebook transcript, st. 5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 25, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  11. stars threw down their spears l. 17

    Stars were traditionally angels (Job 38:7: 'the morning stars sang together'; Revelation 12:4, the dragon's tail draws a third of the stars from heaven). RPO's note reads them as angels fighting in the war in heaven, so the throwing down of spears marks defeat or surrender, after which creation of the fallen world, including the tiger, proceeds. In The Four Zoas (Night V, p. 64) Urizen, Blake's figure of reason and law, recalls calling the stars to his feet and how 'The stars threw down their spears & fled naked away. / We fell.' Kathleen Raine, in 'Who Made the Tyger?' (1954), used that parallel to read the poem's creator as a fallen demiurge. Others see the stars' act as awe or as reconciliation, the end of war in heaven. The stanza is the poem's most disputed.

    William Blake, The Four Zoas, Night V, p. 64, l. 27 (Erdman p. 344), Blake Digital Text Project; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto; ed. Northrop Frye), 'The Tyger', editorial notes; Bible (KJV), Job 38:7; Kathleen Raine, 'Who Made the Tyger?', Encounter 2.6 (June 1954)

  12. watered heaven with their tears l. 18

    To 'water' is to sprinkle or irrigate, so the stars' tears become a cosmic rain, perhaps the dew that Blake elsewhere associates with the material world, perhaps the glitter of starlight itself. The tears may be grief at their own fall, sorrow at the making of a world containing the tiger, or the tears of repentance. Sampson notes that Allan Cunningham's early, unauthorised version (1830) altered the line to 'And sprinkled heaven with shining tears' and 'spears' to 'spheres', showing how readers tried to tame the image. In the Notebook Blake numbered the two star lines to stand first in the stanza.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 114-15, Notebook transcript and note on Cunningham's text; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 25, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  13. smile his work to see l. 19

    The question echoes the refrain of Genesis 1, where God sees each day's work and finds it good, culminating in 1:31: 'God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good.' If the tiger is part of that creation, the smile would be approval of terror as well as of beauty. Sampson's transcript shows Blake drafting 'And did he laugh his work to see?', then trying 'dare he smile' and 'laugh' before choosing 'smile'. A laugh would have made the creator grotesque or cruel; the smile is subtler, perhaps the satisfaction of an artist. In The Book of Job God's answer from the whirlwind delights in Behemoth and Leviathan, creatures of terrible power (Job 40-41), a frequently cited parallel.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:31; John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 114, Notebook transcript, st. 5; Bible (KJV), Job 41:19-21

  14. made the lamb make thee l. 20

    The line explicitly links the two Songs. In The Lamb a child answers 'who made thee?' at once: God, who is himself called the Lamb. Here the speaker cannot answer whether the same God made the tiger. The question is the old problem of evil and of the sublime: if God is good, why does creation contain predatory violence, and is that violence also a form of divine energy? Sampson quotes a letter from the Notebook's owner, W. A. White, explaining how Blake renumbered and rearranged the draft stanzas. Critics differ on whether the speaker's awe is Blake's own: some (for instance in historical readings by David Erdman) connect the tiger to the revolutionary violence of the 1790s, while others stress that the speaker is a limited voice of Experience projecting his fear onto the beast.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 8-9, 25, text of 'The Lamb' and 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project); John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 112, note on the Notebook drafts; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977), on The Tyger

  15. Could frame / Dare frame l. 24

    The poem is circular, returning to its opening stanza, but the single substitution of 'Dare' for 'Could' changes its meaning. At the start the speaker wonders what power was able to make the tiger; by the end, after the forge and the falling stars, the question is what being would dare to do so, implying that making the tiger was a moral risk or a transgression. Sampson notes that the Notebook fair copy already read 'dare frame' in the first stanza and 'hand and eye' in the first and last stanzas, so Blake deliberately changed the first stanza to 'Could frame' and 'hand or eye' when engraving. The repetition also gives the poem the shape of a chant, ending where it began, without an answer.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 110-13, note on the Notebook fair copy; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 25, text of 'The Tyger' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  16. Who made the Tyger? Main critical readings ll. 3, 23

    Because the poem consists almost entirely of questions, criticism has divided over its implied answer. Northrop Frye (Fearful Symmetry, 1947) read the tiger within Blake's myth as an image of the wrath and energy that a fallen world cannot comprehend. David Erdman read it historically, alongside the terror and promise of the French Revolution. Kathleen Raine argued from esoteric traditions and from the Four Zoas parallel that its maker is not the true God but a fallen creator. Others emphasise the speaker: the questions reveal his terror more than the tiger's nature. Blake's own illumination complicates every reading, since in many copies the painted tiger looks mild, even comical, beneath the fierce words. Students should notice how each reading must decide whether the speaker's view is Blake's.

    Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Kathleen Raine, 'Who Made the Tyger?', Encounter 2.6 (June 1954); Martin K. Nurmi, 'Blake's Revisions of The Tyger', PMLA 71 (1956), 669-85; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)
