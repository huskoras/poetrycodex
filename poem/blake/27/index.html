Love

The Sick Rose

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Love

O rose, thou art sick!
The invisible worm,
That flies in the night,
In the howling storm,

Has found out thy bed
Of crimson joy,
And his dark secret love
Does thy life destroy.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Eight lines that never name what they mean. The worm’s ‘dark secret love’ turns desire into disease — Blake makes concealment itself the corruption, the rose ruined less by the worm than by the secrecy that surrounds it.

Study notes by the editors

  1. O rose, thou art sick! l. 1

    The poem opens with a diagnosis delivered directly to the flower. The rose is a traditional emblem of love, beauty, the beloved woman and, in Christian symbolism, of the Virgin and of divine love; Blake leaves its meaning open. Sampson records that the engraved text was taken from the copy in Blake's Notebook (the 'MS. Book'), and that a sketch in the Notebook differs from the engraved design: there a youth lies prostrate as a female figure, the spirit of the rose, flies away. On the etched plate a drooping rose bends to the ground and a small figure emerges from the bloom while other figures lie on the thorny stem. Eight short lines, two quatrains of mostly two-beat, anapaestic lines, carry a story without naming any human actor.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 123, 'The Sick Rose' and note; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 23, text of 'The Sick Rose' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  2. worm l. 2

    'Worm' has both a garden sense (the grub or caterpillar, the 'canker', that destroys buds) and an older sense, from Old English wyrm, of serpent or dragon, which brings in the tempter of Eden and phallic suggestion. Shakespeare had already linked secrecy, love and the worm in the bud: Viola's sister 'never told her love, / But let concealment, like a worm i' th' bud, / Feed on her damask cheek' (Twelfth Night II.iv), and Sonnet 35 notes that 'loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud'. 'Invisible' adds paradox and menace: the worm is unseen because it works inside, at night, and perhaps because it is not a creature at all but a state of mind.

    Shakespeare, Twelfth Night II.iv (Folger Shakespeare); Shakespeare, Sonnet 35 (Folger Shakespeare); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'worm, n.' (senses 'serpent, dragon' and 'grub')

  3. flies in the night l. 3

    Worms do not fly, so the line moves the poem out of the garden into nightmare. The night suggests the dark world of Experience and the secret hours of desire. The phrasing recalls the Psalmist's terrors that strike unseen, 'the pestilence that walketh in darkness' (Psalm 91:6), so the worm becomes an infection as well as an animal. In Blake's design the rose bends under the weight of its bloom while caterpillar-like forms appear on the leaves. The poem's refusal to say whether this is a moth, a disease, a lover or a spirit is the source of its power, and of the range of critical readings it has generated.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 91:5-6; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  4. the howling storm l. 4

    Night, darkness and winter recur in Songs of Experience as the climate of a fallen world: compare 'Earth's Answer', which opens in 'darkness dread & drear', and 'Holy Thursday', where for the poor 'it is eternal winter'. In The Sick Rose the storm gives the worm its element: it travels through chaos to reach the secluded, protected flower. 'Howling' personifies the wind as an animal or a mourner and adds sound to an otherwise silent poem. Some readers take the storm as the turbulence of passion itself, others as the hostile social world that invades private love. The four short lines of the first stanza move from diagnosis to agent to setting, so that the rose's sickness is explained only by atmosphere.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 23, text of 'The Sick Rose' (Blake Digital Text Project); William Blake, 'Earth's Answer' and 'Holy Thursday', Songs of Experience (Erdman pp. 18-19)

  5. Has found out l. 5

    The phrasal verb suggests deliberate pursuit: the worm has sought the rose through night and storm and finally discovered her hiding place. It also carries the sense of detection and exposure, as when wrongdoing is 'found out', so that the secret is not only the worm's but the rose's. Sampson notes that Blake's Notebook copy reads 'Hath found out', the older, more biblical verb form, which Blake changed to the plainer 'Has' when etching. The present perfect tense ('Has found') implies that the discovery is complete and irreversible: the poem begins after the event, at the moment of diagnosis.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 123, textual note on l. 5; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'find, v.', phrasal verb 'to find out'

  6. bed of crimson joy ll. 5-6

    The pun on 'bed' carries the poem from horticulture to sexuality: a flower-bed, the heart of the bloom, and a marriage or love bed. The worm 'has found out' the bed, as if seeking a secret hiding place. 'Crimson joy' makes the colour of the rose the colour of passion and of blood, and the line-break, which isolates 'Of crimson joy', holds the moment of fulfilment before the destruction of the final couplet. In Blake's Notebook draft the verb is 'Hath found out', which Blake modernised when engraving. Compare 'My Pretty Rose Tree', also in Songs of Experience, where a rose's jealousy turns love into thorns.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 123, textual note on l. 5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 23, text of 'The Sick Rose' (Blake Digital Text Project)

  7. dark secret love l. 7

    The phrase is the poem's crux. Is the 'love' the worm's predatory lust, or the rose's own hidden passion, or the secrecy that a moral code imposes on sexual feeling? Many readers connect the poem to Blake's attacks, in Visions of the Daughters of Albion and the Proverbs of Hell, on the repression of desire, so that concealment rather than desire is the disease. Others give it a literal medical sense or read it as a parable of jealous, possessive love. The Notebook draft supports a less personal reading: Sampson records that Blake first wrote 'A dark secret love / Doth life destroy', and then added a pronoun, 'her', above an erased word, before etching 'his'. The final choice makes the worm male and the destruction an act of possession.

    John Sampson, ed., The Poetical Works of William Blake: A New and Verbatim Text (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1905), p. 123, textual notes on ll. 7-8; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 23, text of 'The Sick Rose' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  8. joy / destroy l. 8

    The poem has only two rhymes per stanza, and the last pairs 'joy' with 'destroy', binding pleasure to annihilation. The syntax is a single sentence running from line 2 to line 8, so the worm remains the grammatical subject throughout while the rose is only addressed and acted upon. The inverted word order ('thy life destroy') is the idiom of hymns and ballads, which makes the ending sound like proverbial truth. Experience poems often end on such a reversal; compare 'London', where the marriage bed becomes a hearse. Blake's compression invites, but never settles, allegory.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), p. 23, text of 'The Sick Rose' (Blake Digital Text Project); The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)
