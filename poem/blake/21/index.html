Love

The Clod And The Pebble

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Love

"Love seeketh not itself to please,
Nor for itself hath any care,
But for another gives it ease,
And builds a heaven in hell's despair."

So sang a little clod of clay,
Trodden with the cattle's feet,
But a pebble of the brook
Warbled out these metres meet:

"Love seeketh only Self to please,
To bind another to its delight,
Joys in another's loss of ease,
And builds a hell in heaven's despite."

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Clod and the Pebble: context and allusion st. 1

    The clod's first line echoes 1 Corinthians 13:5, where charity (love) 'seeketh not her own'. The clod speaks for self-sacrificing love; the pebble for possessive, selfish love. The poem is one of the Songs of Experience, Blake's companion volume to the Songs of Innocence (1789), which show 'the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul'. Its three quatrains, mostly of four-stress lines, use a frame (the middle stanza) that introduces each speaker in turn.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 13:4-7; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (Oxford UP, 1970), introduction and commentary

  2. Heaven in hell, hell in heaven l. 4; l. 12

    In Paradise Lost 1.254-255 Satan says that the mind 'in itself / Can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven'. Blake divides the line between two speakers: the clod's love builds heaven even in hell's despair; the pebble's builds hell 'in heaven's despite'. Blake admired Milton but believed that Milton's Satan expresses energy and desire, as he argued in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell. The allusion suggests that both kinds of love are acts of the mind that make the world they live in.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost 1.254-255 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963)

  3. The clod of clay ll. 5-6

    Clay also suggests the human body, made from dust (Genesis 2:7), and passive, malleable humility. The clod's selflessness is admirable, yet it is trodden down. Some readers think Blake shows that selfless love is purchased by being trampled, and is perhaps a kind of martyrdom or masochism. In Blake's earlier Book of Thel (1789), the 'Clod of Clay' appears as a figure of humble, maternal love.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:7; William Blake, The Book of Thel (1789), plates 4-5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  4. The pebble of the brook l. 7

    A stone in a brook is polished by the water but never yields. The pebble 'Warbled', a word for birdsong, so its selfish creed has its own sweetness and confidence. A biblical echo may be present: David chose 'five smooth stones out of the brook' to kill Goliath (1 Samuel 17:40). Clod and pebble are both earth, differing only in softness, which supports readings in which their two creeds are aspects of one nature.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Samuel 17:40; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (Oxford UP, 1970), introduction and commentary

  5. metres meet l. 8

    'Meet' is an archaic adjective meaning proper or apt, familiar from the Prayer Book ('It is meet and right so to do'). The phrase draws attention to the poem's own metres: the two songs are metrically parallel, and the pebble's echoes the clod's line by line, inverting its sense. The shift from the clod's 'sang' to the pebble's 'Warbled' may be a slight judgment, or simply variety.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; The Book of Common Prayer (1662), Holy Communion ('It is meet and right so to do')

  6. Binding love l. 10

    Binding is one of Blake's key images for repression and control: priests 'binding with briars my joys and desires' in 'The Garden of Love', and the 'mind-forg'd manacles' of 'London'. The pebble's love takes pleasure in another's 'loss of ease'. Yet the pebble is honest about desire, while the clod may be self-deceived; this is why critics debate whether Blake prefers either.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963); William Blake, 'London' and 'The Garden of Love', Songs of Experience (1794)

  7. in heaven's despite l. 12

    'Despite' as a noun means scorn, contempt, or malice. The rhyme with 'delight' (l. 10) binds the pebble's pleasure to spite. The final line mirrors the first stanza's 'heaven in hell's despair' with a chiastic reversal: despair becomes despite, heaven and hell exchange places. The symmetry makes the poem seem a balanced debate without a verdict. Blake often uses such exact verbal mirroring in the Songs, as in the paired 'Holy Thursday' and 'Nurse's Song' poems of Innocence and Experience.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom

  8. Contraries: which is right? whole poem

    In The Marriage of Heaven and Hell Blake wrote 'Without Contraries is no progression'. Some readers take the clod as right and the pebble as the voice of Experience corrupting love. Others note that the clod's self-sacrifice is the morality Blake elsewhere criticises as self-annihilating and passive, and that the pebble at least knows what it wants. A third reading is that each love is partial: true love would integrate self and other. The placement in Songs of Experience suggests both voices belong to a fallen state.

    William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), plate 3; Harold Bloom, Blake's Apocalypse (1963); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (rev. ed., 1982), text and commentary by Harold Bloom
