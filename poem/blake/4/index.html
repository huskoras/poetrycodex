The Little Black Boy

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Innocence

Religion

My mother bore me in the southern wild,
And I am black, but oh my soul is white!
White as an angel is the English child,
But I am black, as if bereaved of light.

My mother taught me underneath a tree,
And, sitting down before the heat of day,
She took me on her lap and kissed me,
And, pointed to the east, began to say:

"Look on the rising sun: there God does live,
And gives His light, and gives His heat away,
And flowers and trees and beasts and men receive
Comfort in morning, joy in the noonday.

"And we are put on earth a little space,
That we may learn to bear the beams of love
And these black bodies and this sunburnt face
Is but a cloud, and like a shady grove.

"For when our souls have learn'd the heat to bear,
The cloud will vanish, we shall hear His voice,
Saying, 'Come out from the grove, my love and care
And round my golden tent like lambs rejoice',"

Thus did my mother say, and kissed me;
And thus I say to little English boy.
When I from black and he from white cloud free,
And round the tent of God like lambs we joy

I'll shade him from the heat till he can bear
To lean in joy upon our Father's knee;
And then I'll stand and stroke his silver hair,
And be like him, and he will then love me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Little Black Boy: context l. 1

    The Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade was founded in London in 1787, and Wilberforce introduced his first parliamentary motion in 1789, the date on the Innocence title-page. Blake later engraved plates for J. G. Stedman's Narrative of ... the Revolted Negroes of Surinam (1796), with its images of slave torture. 'The southern wild' places the boy's birth in Africa, outside civilisation in English eyes. Erdman read the poem as a protest against slavery and racial hierarchy; later critics, including Glen, stress the poem's double vision, in which the child's consoling theology also shows the effects of a culture that has taught him to see his blackness as a lack.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E9 (Blake Digital Text Project)

  2. I am black, but oh my soul is white! l. 2

    In the KJV the bride says 'I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem' (Song of Solomon 1:5). The boy's 'but' concedes a hierarchy even as it claims spiritual equality: his soul is 'white', the colour of purity in Christian symbolism. Erdman's text reads 'And I am black, but O! my soul is white'. The next line inverts expectations, since the English child is 'White as an angel', but the black child is 'as if bereaved of light', as though his colour were a loss. Critics differ over whether Blake endorses, ironises or simply records this language.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Song of Solomon 1:5; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E9 (Blake Digital Text Project); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  3. as if bereaved of light l. 4

    The phrase 'as if' is important: he only seems bereaved. The mother's teaching in stanzas 3-5 reverses the image, since dark skin is a 'cloud' or 'shady grove' given to bear the intensity of God's love, so blackness becomes protection, not deprivation. The poem thus moves from a received racial image to a theology in which all bodies, black or white, are temporary clouds. Heather Glen notes how the child's language is shaped by what he has been taught.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bereave'; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  4. My mother taught me underneath a tree ll. 5-8

    The scene is one of natural instruction, like that of the Innocence 'Introduction' and of 'The Echoing Green', and it is set 'before the heat of day', in the cool of morning. In Blake's design for the first plate the mother sits under a tree with the boy and points towards the sunrise; on the second plate a Christ-like shepherd figure receives the two children. Keynes and Lincoln describe the plates. Facing the east, the direction of sunrise and of Eden and paradise in traditional symbolism, she offers a natural religion of the sun as God's dwelling.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience, Blake's Illuminated Books vol. 2 (William Blake Trust/Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary; The William Blake Archive, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (copy images and descriptions)

  5. Look on the rising sun: there God does live ll. 9-12

    Psalm 19:4-6 describes God setting 'a tabernacle for the sun', which goes forth 'as a bridegroom' and from whose heat nothing is hid. The mother's teaching makes the sun God's dwelling, and his gift of light and heat a universal blessing on 'flowers and trees and beasts and men'. Some critics hear an echo of African sun-worship as reported in travel writing, others of Swedenborg, whom Blake read in 1788-9, and for whom the spiritual sun is the divine love. Either way, the theology is generous: all receive comfort and joy.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Psalm 19:4-6; Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  6. learn to bear the beams of love ll. 13-14

    The idea that divine love can be painful, a fire that unprepared souls cannot endure, has roots in mystical writing and in Swedenborg; Blake develops it in his prophetic books too. 'A little space' is a short time. The bodies, black or white, become instruments of this education, shade against the heat; the lesson is to grow strong enough to bear God's presence. The phrase also suggests that the white child, who has less 'shade', may find the sun harder to bear, which the last stanza develops.

    Northrop Frye, Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake (Princeton, 1947); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience, Blake's Illuminated Books vol. 2 (William Blake Trust/Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  7. these black bodies ... Is but a cloud ll. 15-18

    The singular verb ('these black bodies ... Is but a cloud') gives the line the feel of a child's grammar. The body as a cloud that vanishes recalls Pauline ideas of the earthly body as a veil to be put off (2 Corinthians 5:1-4). The 'grove' is shade from the sun, and when the cloud vanishes God will call 'Come out from the grove'. The image democratises colour, since the English boy is also in a 'white cloud' (l. 23), and both must be freed.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), 2 Corinthians 5:1-4; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates

  8. round my golden tent like lambs rejoice l. 20

    The tent recalls the tabernacle of the Israelites in the wilderness and the heavenly dwelling in Isaiah 40:22 and Revelation 21:3 ('the tabernacle of God is with men'). The lambs connect the scene with 'The Lamb' and the pastoral Christ of Innocence, the Good Shepherd. In the sixth stanza, 'round the tent of God like lambs we joy', the boy repeats his mother's words, and the plate's second design shows the shepherd with the two children.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Isaiah 40:22; Revelation 21:3; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience (London: Rupert Hart-Davis/Trianon, 1967; Oxford pbk 1970), commentary on the plates; Andrew Lincoln, ed., Songs of Innocence and of Experience, Blake's Illuminated Books vol. 2 (William Blake Trust/Tate Gallery, 1991), commentary

  9. And be like him, and he will then love me ll. 25-28

    The boy imagines a heaven where he protects the white child from God's heat, an inversion of earthly roles in which he is the stronger, more prepared soul. Yet the last line suggests he can be loved only when he is 'like him', which can be read as a hope for equality, as the residue of internalised racism, or as Blake's ironic exposure of the limits of a consoling theology. Erdman stressed the poem's anti-slavery thrust; Leader and Glen emphasise its double perspective, in which the speaker's innocence reveals more than he knows. Many teachers present the debate itself as the poem's point.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd ed. 1977); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981); Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake (newly rev. ed., 1982), E9 (Blake Digital Text Project)
