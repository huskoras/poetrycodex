The Chimney Sweeper

by William Blake 1757–1827

From Songs Of Experience

Social Commentaries

A little black thing in the snow,
Crying "weep! weep!" in notes of woe!
"Where are thy father and mother? Say!"--
"They are both gone up to the church to pray.

"Because I was happy upon the heath,
And smiled among the winter's snow,
They clothed me in the clothes of death,
And taught me to sing the notes of woe.

"And because I am happy and dance and sing,
They think they have done me no injury,
And are gone to praise God and his priest and king,
Who make up a heaven of our misery."

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Chimney Sweeper (Experience): context l. 1

    Blake drafted the poem in his Notebook, now in the British Library, before etching it for Songs of Experience (1794), where it answers the Innocence 'Chimney Sweeper'. The first two lines are the observer's, the third his question, and the rest is the child's reply. Where the Innocence sweeper accepts his lot and finds comfort in a dream of heaven, this child has seen through the comfort and blames his parents and the institutions they serve. Erdman's Prophet Against Empire places the Songs of Experience in the climate of the early 1790s: the French Revolution, Paine, and government repression at home.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Blake's Notebook (British Library Add. MS 49460), first draft of 'The Chimney Sweeper' (Experience), transcribed on Wikisource; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977); 'The Chimney Sweeper', Wikipedia (dates, plate order and historical context)

  2. 'A little black thing' l. 1

    The phrase reduces the boy to an object and a colour before he speaks. The black-and-white contrast which in Innocence separated soot from the white of the dream here sets the child against the snow. Blake's 'The Little Black Boy' in Innocence shows how readily he connected blackness with questions of human worth. The child becomes a person only when he answers, and the rest of the poem is his.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)

  3. 'in the snow': the text l. 1

    In Blake's own copies, and already in the Notebook draft, the line reads 'A little black thing among the snow'. 'Among' is slightly odd, as if the child were one more object amid the flakes, and it chimes with 'smil'd among the winter's snow' in l. 6. The text kept in this archive normalises it to 'in the snow'. Students quoting the poem should follow a standard edition such as Erdman's, or the plates themselves in the William Blake Archive.

    The William Blake Archive, ed. Morris Eaves, Robert N. Essick and Joseph Viscomi (images and transcriptions of the illuminated plates); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Blake's Notebook (British Library Add. MS 49460), first draft of 'The Chimney Sweeper' (Experience), transcribed on Wikisource

  4. 'weep! weep!' l. 2

    In the Innocence poem 'weep' is the child's mispronounced 'sweep', and its double meaning is the reader's discovery. Here the observer names the lament at once. The phrase 'notes of woe' returns at the end of stanza 2, where the child says he was 'taught' to sing them. The sad cry is not natural to the child; it is a lesson imposed on him, part of the trade.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966)

  5. The question l. 3

    The observer's question, in quotation marks, sets up the dialogue, and the child's answer is the first shock: his parents are alive, respectable, and at church. Unlike the orphan of Innocence, this child has been put to the trade by living parents. In Erdman's text, following the plates, the line reads 'Where are thy father & mother? say?'; the archive's punctuation differs slightly.

    The William Blake Archive, ed. Morris Eaves, Robert N. Essick and Joseph Viscomi (images and transcriptions of the illuminated plates); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  6. 'gone up to the church' l. 4

    The rhyme 'Say'/'pray' is pointed, and 'gone up' suggests both the walk to church and a self-satisfied ascent. The parents can be at prayer while their child cries in the snow; churchgoing becomes a way of not seeing. The charge is developed in the last stanza and links this poem with 'Holy Thursday' and 'The Garden of Love' in Experience, where the Church is likewise a place of exclusion and restraint.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977); E. P. Thompson, Witness Against the Beast: William Blake and the Moral Law (Cambridge, 1993)

  7. 'upon the heath' l. 5

    A heath is uncultivated open land, a place for play like the green of 'The Ecchoing Green'. The child's logic in stanza 2 is that his parents punished his happiness: because he was happy, they dressed him for death. Whether this is a real cause or a child's sense of injustice, it presents adult authority as hostile to joy. Blake's 'Proverbs of Hell' make the opposite case for joy, as in 'Joys impregnate. Sorrows bring forth.'

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), 'Proverbs of Hell', in Erdman, Complete Poetry and Prose

  8. 'the clothes of death' l. 7

    Black was the colour of mourning, and the climbing-boy's clothes were black with soot. The phrase also recalls a shroud, as the 'coffins of black' did in Innocence: the parents have dressed the child for an early death. The rhyme 'heath'/'death' ties his lost freedom to his fate. The verb 'clothed' is pointed too: clothing children is a parent's first duty, and these parents have performed it in the deadliest way. The Innocence sweepers were 'naked and white' in their dream; this child is dressed for the grave.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Jonas Hanway, A Sentimental History of Chimney Sweepers, in London & Westminster (London, 1785)

  9. 'taught me to sing' l. 8

    Repeating 'notes of woe' from l. 2 turns the observer's description into the child's own complaint, and 'taught' turns the trade cry into a lesson. His natural song was joy; his parents' teaching replaced it with woe. Glen and others see here Blake's interest in how social roles are learned and passed on. The line may also glance at the hymns that poor children were drilled to sing, as in the charity-school service of 'Holy Thursday'.

    Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983); Robert F. Gleckner, 'Point of View and Context in Blake's Songs', Bulletin of the New York Public Library 61 (1957); repr. in Blake: A Collection of Critical Essays, ed. Northrop Frye (1966)

  10. 'dance and sing' l. 9

    The child's resilience is used against him: because he can still be happy, his parents think 'they have done me no injury'. London sweeps were known for their May Day festivities, when they paraded with brushes and shovels, danced for money and accompanied the Jack-in-the-Green, as Joseph Strutt described in 1801. Spectators could take such dancing as evidence that the boys were content. Whether or not Blake has May Day in mind, the line exposes the comfortable reasoning of those who live off a child's labour.

    Joseph Strutt, The Sports and Pastimes of the People of England (London, 1801), on the sweepers' May Day; Alistair Robinson, 'Spring, Streets, and Chimney Sweeps: May Day in Regency London and Benjamin Robert Haydon's Punch (1829)', Project MUSE; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  11. 'no injury' l. 10

    From Latin injuria, a wrong (in-, 'not', and jus, 'right'), 'injury' in Blake's day still carried the legal sense of a violation of right, besides harm to the body. The child sees both. The weak rhyme 'injury'/'misery', resting on light final syllables, gives the stanza a falling cadence. The parents' complacency is the point. They have wronged the child in every sense, and their churchgoing assures them they have done nothing of the kind; the child's exact word convicts them.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  12. 'God and his priest and king' l. 11

    The line yokes divine, clerical and royal authority, the alliance of Church and State that radical writers of the 1790s attacked. 'His priest and king' makes the God of conventional piety the patron of both. Blake is attacking not God as such but 'God' as the established Church presents him, an idea he develops in the 'Nobodaddy' verses of the Notebook and in Urizen. Erdman and E. P. Thompson relate such lines to Blake's radical and dissenting sympathies.

    David V. Erdman, Blake: Prophet Against Empire (Princeton, 1954; 3rd edn 1977); E. P. Thompson, Witness Against the Beast: William Blake and the Moral Law (Cambridge, 1993); David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience

  13. 'make up a heaven of our misery' l. 12

    'Make up' can mean compose, build or invent. The line implies that the heaven promised by Church and State is a fiction, and that the comfort of the powerful, their heaven here and now, is made of the poor's suffering. In the Notebook Blake first wrote 'Who wrap themselves up in our misery', then struck it out for the present line, moving from smug self-comfort to active construction. The line answers the Innocence dream: there the child was consoled by heaven; here he sees how it is made.

    Blake's Notebook (British Library Add. MS 49460), first draft of 'The Chimney Sweeper' (Experience), transcribed on Wikisource; David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Heather Glen, Vision and Disenchantment: Blake's Songs and Wordsworth's Lyrical Ballads (Cambridge, 1983)

  14. Form whole poem

    Stanza 1 rhymes aabb ('snow'/'woe', 'Say'/'pray'), like its Innocence counterpart; stanzas 2 and 3 switch to abab. The line is a loose four-beat measure. The change from couplet to cross-rhyme coincides with the child's taking over the poem, and his answer then runs for nine lines without interruption. The poem's twelve lines, against the Innocence poem's twenty-four, suit the compressed indictment typical of Experience.

    David V. Erdman, ed., The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, rev. edn (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), Songs of Innocence and of Experience; Zachary Leader, Reading Blake's Songs (London, 1981)
