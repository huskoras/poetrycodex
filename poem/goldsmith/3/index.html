Love

When lovely woman stoops to folly

by Oliver Goldsmith 1728–1774

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Love

When lovely woman stoops to folly
And finds too late that men betray,--
What charm can soothe her melancholy,
What art can wash her guilt away?

The only art her guilt to cover,
To hide her shame from every eye,
To give repentance to her lover
And wring his bosom, is--to die.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song in The Vicar of Wakefield l. 1

    The lines are a song in the novel, not an independent lyric. In chapter 24 ('Fresh calamities') the family breakfast on the honeysuckle bank where Olivia first met her seducer, and her mother asks for 'that little melancholy air your pappa was so fond of'. Olivia sings it so movingly that her voice breaks, and as she finishes Thornhill's carriage appears. The archive text comes from Palgrave's Golden Treasury (1861), which printed it as a lyric. In the novel the song looks back on her betrayal: Thornhill had deceived her with a wedding he intended to be a sham.

    Oliver Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), ch. 24 (Project Gutenberg); Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lovely Woman Stoops to Folly' (text from the 1766 Salisbury edition, II, 78)

  2. stoops to folly l. 1

    'Folly' had a strong sense of wickedness and especially unchastity, inherited from biblical usage: a woman who has 'wrought folly in Israel, to play the whore' (Deuteronomy 22:21). The OED records the sense of lewdness or wantonness. 'Stoops' implies descent from a height of virtue, and the phrase is a polite periphrasis for a woman's seduction. 'Lovely woman' generalises Olivia's case into a type, as the song's sententious manner requires.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'folly, n.1' (lewdness, wantonness) and 'stoop, v.1'; Deuteronomy 22:21 (King James Version)

  3. men betray l. 2

    In the novel Thornhill has arranged a sham wedding with a false priest and licence; Olivia learns too late that she was 'betrayed'. The generalisation 'men betray' turns her experience into a rule, and the song's grief is directed both at herself ('her guilt') and at men. The phrase 'too late' becomes ironic in the novel, which finally reveals that Thornhill's accomplice had procured a true priest and licence, so the marriage was valid after all (ch. 31).

    Oliver Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), chs. 24 and 31 (Project Gutenberg); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'betray, v.' (to seduce and desert)

  4. charm l. 3

    'Charm' comes through French charme from Latin carmen, song or incantation. The question 'What charm can soothe' has an ironic edge, since the song itself is such a charm and Olivia sings it to soothe her family. 'Art' in the next line means skill or contrivance. The two rhetorical questions of the first quatrain are answered by the second, which picks up 'art' and gives the 'only' remedy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'charm, n.1'; Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'charm'

  5. wash her guilt away l. 4

    The image of washing guilt is biblical: 'Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin' (Psalm 51:2), and Pilate washes his hands of guilt (Matthew 27:24). Shakespeare's Macbeth asks whether 'all great Neptune's ocean' will wash the blood from his hand (II.ii). The song denies any such cleansing short of death; the moral severity is part of the novel's sentimental scheme, in which Olivia's suffering earns sympathy.

    Psalm 51:2 (King James Version); Shakespeare, Macbeth II.ii (Folger Shakespeare)

  6. form: two quatrains and the turn ll. 5-8

    The song is a pair of quatrains in iambic tetrameter, with alternating feminine (two-syllable) and masculine rhymes: 'folly / melancholy', 'cover / lover'. The second stanza takes up 'art' and 'guilt' from the first and answers its questions with a list of four purposes (to cover guilt, hide shame, bring the lover to repentance, and 'wring his bosom'), all held in suspense until the dash and the last two words. The arrangement makes death the solution to a problem in social arithmetic, which gives the song its epigrammatic force and, for modern readers, a chilling neatness.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lovely Woman Stoops to Folly'; Oliver Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), ch. 24 (Project Gutenberg)

  7. wring his bosom l. 8

    'Wring' means to twist or squeeze, and figuratively to torment, distress (the OED records 'wring the heart' from the sixteenth century). The 'bosom' is the seat of feeling. The idea that a betrayed woman's death would bring her seducer to 'repentance' is common in sentimental fiction and ballad; the song states it as the only revenge open to her. In the novel Thornhill shows no such remorse, which adds irony.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wring, v.'; Oliver Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), chs. 24-31 (Project Gutenberg)

  8. is -- to die l. 8

    The dash holds back the shocking answer. Read in its novel, the song's prescription is not carried out. In chapter 28 the imprisoned Vicar is told that Olivia has died, but the report is false, and in chapter 31 Jenkinson reveals that the marriage Thornhill thought a sham was valid. Critics therefore differ: some take the song at face value as an expression of the period's sexual double standard; others stress that Goldsmith's comic plot quietly refuses its logic. T. S. Eliot parodied it in The Waste Land (1922), III, where 'When lovely woman stoops to folly and / Paces about her room again, alone' describes the typist after a loveless seduction.

    Oliver Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), chs. 28 and 31 (Project Gutenberg); T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land (1922), III, 'The Fire Sermon', ll. 253-6 (Project Gutenberg)
