The Soldier

by Rupert Brooke 1887–1915

From 1914 and Other Poems (1915)

History & PoliticsDeath & Dying

If I should die, think only this of me:
That there's some corner of a foreign field
That is for ever England. There shall be
In that rich earth a richer dust concealed;
A dust whom England bore, shaped, made aware,
Gave, once, her flowers to love, her ways to roam,
A body of England's, breathing English air,
Washed by the rivers, blest by suns of home.

And think, this heart, all evil shed away,
A pulse in the eternal mind, no less
Gives somewhere back the thoughts by England given;
Her sights and sounds; dreams happy as her day;
And laughter, learnt of friends; and gentleness,
In hearts at peace, under an English heaven.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Perhaps the most widely quoted English poem of the First World War: if the speaker dies abroad, 'some corner of a foreign field' will be 'for ever England'. Written in 1914 and read all the more after Brooke's death at sea in April 1915.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening, date and reception l. 1

    Brooke served with the Royal Naval Division at Antwerp in October 1914 and trained at Blandford Camp, Dorset, where he began this sonnet; a pencil draft at King's College, Cambridge, is titled 'The Recruit'. The five sonnets appeared in the quarterly New Numbers (no. 4, dated December 1914) and in 1914 and Other Poems (1915). On Easter Sunday, 4 April 1915, Dean W. R. Inge read it from the pulpit of St Paul's. Brooke died of sepsis on a French hospital ship off Skyros on 23 April 1915, and the poem became the classic statement of idealistic sacrifice. The opening conditional calmly imagines the speaker's death and directs how he is to be remembered.

    King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook); Dorset Life, 'For Ever Dorset: Rupert Brooke's Dorset connections' (Blandford Camp, 'The Recruit'); The Spectator, 1 May 1915, 'Rupert Brooke' (on the sonnets 'published in New Numbers last December'); Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets

  2. Sonnet form ll. 1-14

    The octave rhymes ababcdcd and the sestet efgefg (away/less/given, day/gentleness/heaven), combining the English and Italian sonnet patterns. The octave concerns the body, the 'dust' that will enrich foreign soil; the sestet, after the volta at l. 9, concerns the heart and spirit, purified and returned to the 'eternal mind'. Both parts repeat the imperative 'think', so the whole sonnet is an instruction to the reader on how to mourn. The words 'England' and 'English' occur six times, an insistence that later readers have found either moving or chauvinistic.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  3. some corner of a foreign field ll. 2-3

    The conceit is that the soldier's buried body claims the ground it lies in for England, a patriotic version of the idea that the dead become part of the earth. The phrase was soon realised literally: Brooke was buried on the Greek island of Skyros on 23 April 1915, and the poem is inscribed on his grave. The lines were widely used on headstones and memorials, and were read at funerals of soldiers throughout the war. 'For ever England' converts a moment of death into permanence and possession.

    King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook); 'There's some corner', Epitaphs of the Great War

  4. In that rich earth a richer dust l. 4

    The line builds on the biblical sentence on Adam, 'for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19), and the burial service's 'earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust'. Brooke plays on 'rich': the foreign earth is already fertile, but the English dust is 'richer', enriched by everything England gave it. Readers have found the comparative either a fine conceit or an unconscious national arrogance. 'Concealed' turns the grave into a hiding place for treasure.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 12:7; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets

  5. A dust whom England bore, shaped, made aware ll. 5-6

    England is personified as a mother (feminine 'her' throughout) who 'bore, shaped, made aware' the speaker: birth, upbringing and education in a single rising sequence of verbs. The personal pronoun 'whom' for dust is unusual and makes the remains a person still. 'Gave, once, her flowers to love, her ways to roam' recalls the pastoral England of the Georgian poets, Brooke's own circle, and of his 'Grantchester'. The pause around 'once' hints at the brevity of a life cut short.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  6. A body of England's, breathing English air ll. 7-8

    The speaker's body belongs to England ('A body of England's'), formed by its air, 'washed' by its rivers and 'blest' by its suns. The verbs have religious overtones (washing as baptism, blessing), so that nature performs the rites of a national church. Critics have noted that this is the idealised landscape of southern England, gentle and rural, quite unlike the trench landscapes of later war poetry. 'Suns of home' (plural) suggests the many summers of a life.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  7. And think, this heart . . . l. 9

    The sestet repeats 'think' from l. 1 but moves from body to soul. The 'heart', with 'all evil shed away', recalls Christian ideas of purification at death and the Platonic idea of the soul freed from the body. The syntax is complex: 'this heart . . . no less / Gives somewhere back the thoughts by England given'. Brooke's letters around this time speak of the war as a cleansing, a mood also found in the first sonnet of the sequence, 'Peace', with its 'swimmers into cleanness leaping'.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  8. A pulse in the eternal mind l. 10

    The idea that the dead are absorbed into a cosmic intelligence recalls Neoplatonism and especially Shelley's elegy for Keats, Adonais (1821), where the dead poet 'is made one with Nature' and becomes 'a portion of the loveliness / Which once he made more lovely' (stanzas 42-43). Brooke, a Cambridge Apostle and admirer of the Romantics, fuses this with Christian heaven in the last line. 'Pulse' makes the eternal mind a living body, keeping the sonnet's physical imagery.

    P. B. Shelley, Adonais (1821), stanzas 42-43, Project Gutenberg text of Shelley's Complete Poetical Works; Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets

  9. Gives somewhere back the thoughts by England given l. 11

    The line completes a pattern of exchange: England 'bore', 'gave' and 'washed' the speaker; now the heart 'gives somewhere back' the thoughts it received. 'Somewhere' keeps the afterlife indefinite. The list that follows ('Her sights and sounds; dreams happy as her day; / And laughter, learnt of friends; and gentleness') makes Englishness a matter of domestic and social happiness rather than military glory; the poem nowhere mentions fighting, the enemy, or killing.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  10. under an English heaven l. 14

    The closing phrase is deliberately double: 'heaven' is the English sky under which friends laughed, and the Christian heaven to which the soul goes. The ending thus completes the sonnet's identification of England with the sacred. For many readers in 1915 this was consoling; later readers, after the Somme and Owen and Sassoon, often found it complacent. Either way, the sonnet's calm, untroubled cadence is part of its meaning: death is imagined without pain or horror.

    Geoffrey Keynes, ed., The Poetical Works of Rupert Brooke (London: Faber, 1946), '1914' sonnets; King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook)

  11. Reception and critique l. 1

    The poem's fame was almost immediate. Dean Inge praised its pure and elevated patriotism in his sermon at St Paul's on 4 April 1915, and Brooke's death three weeks later, followed by Winston Churchill's tribute in The Times, made him a national symbol. Not all soldier-poets agreed: Charles Sorley, killed at Loos in October 1915, wrote that Brooke had clothed his attitudes in fine words but had 'taken the sentimental attitude'. Owen's 'Anthem for Doomed Youth' and Sassoon's satires are often read as replies to this kind of idealism. The imperative 'think only this' asks the reader to exclude every other thought, including grief.

    King's College, Cambridge, Archive Centre, 'Rupert Brooke: Section 9, The Soldier' (drafts RCB/V/1; Dean Inge's sermon notebook); Jean Moorcroft Wilson, ed., The Collected Letters of Charles Hamilton Sorley (London: Cecil Woolf, 1990), as quoted in D. J. Enright's review, London Review of Books 13.4 (1991); The Spectator, 1 May 1915, 'Rupert Brooke' (on the sonnets 'published in New Numbers last December')
