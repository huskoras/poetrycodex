Love

Amoretti: Sonnet LXVII

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

From Amoretti

Love

Lyke as a huntsman, after weary chace,
Seeing the game from him escapt away,
Sits downe to rest him in some shady place,
With panting hounds, beguiled of their pray,
So, after long pursuit and vaine assay,
When I all weary had the chace forsooke,
The gentle deer returnd the selfe-same way,
Thinking to quench her thirst at the next brooke.
There she, beholding me with mylder looke,
Sought not to fly, but fearlesse still did bide,
Till I in hand her yet halfe trembling tooke,
And with her own goodwill her fyrmely tyde.
Strange thing, me seemd, to see a beast so wyld
So goodly wonne, with her owne will beguyld.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Amoretti 67: the hunt that ends in surrender l. 1

    Amoretti, Spenser's sequence of 89 sonnets, was published in 1595 with Epithalamion and records his courtship of Elizabeth Boyle, whom he married in June 1594. Sonnet 67 reworks a famous Petrarchan figure: in Petrarch's Rime 190 and in Wyatt's 'Whoso list to hunt', the lover pursues a deer that cannot be caught. Spenser reverses the outcome. The hunter gives up, and only then does the deer come back and let herself be taken. Critics read the poem as the turning point of the sequence, where pursuit gives way to mutual consent, and as a statement that love cannot be won by force.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar; Petrarch, Rime 190 ('Una candida cerva'); Wyatt, 'Whoso list to hunt'

  2. Lyke as ... So ll. 1-5

    The octave is built as a formal simile in the epic manner, 'Like as ... So ...', a structure Spenser uses often in The Faerie Queene and in several Amoretti sonnets (34, 'Lyke as a ship'; 89, 'Lyke as the culver'). The first quatrain gives the huntsman resting in shade with his panting hounds, 'beguiled of their pray', cheated of their prey; line 5 applies the picture to the lover. The slow syntax enacts the weariness of the chase.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti

  3. vaine assay l. 5

    'Assay' means a trial, attempt or endeavour, often with a sense of testing. 'Vaine assay' is an attempt that comes to nothing. The word is common in Spenser and recurs in the opening of Amoretti 63, 'tempests sad assay', where it means an assault or ordeal. Here the 'long pursuit' of the courtship, recorded across the earlier sonnets, has failed, and the speaker gives up the chase; the turn of the sonnet depends on that giving up.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assay'; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  4. The gentle deer l. 7

    The beloved is a deer and also 'dear', a pun that Wyatt's poem and the whole Petrarchan hunting tradition exploit (hart and heart are another). 'Gentle' carries its Elizabethan double sense of mild, tractable, and of gentle birth. The deer returns 'the selfe-same way', the path by which she fled, so that her coming back reads as a free reversal, not a capture.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dear'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gentle'; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti

  5. quench her thirst at the next brooke l. 8

    The deer coming to drink recalls Psalm 42, in which the thirsting hart stands for the soul longing for God. The psalm was traditionally sung at the Easter Vigil in the Latin rite, on the procession to the baptismal font, which fits the reading of this sonnet as the eve of Amoretti 68, the Easter sonnet. The allusion gives the earthly courtship a spiritual frame without making it allegory: the beloved's thirst is real, and it is also the soul's desire for grace. 'Next' means nearest.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 42:1; Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  6. mylder looke l. 9

    Through much of Amoretti the lady is proud and cruel, with eyes that kill or wound (sonnets 7, 49, 56). Here, at the volta of the sonnet, the look is 'mylder', and she 'Sought not to fly, but fearlesse still did bide': she stays without fear. The change of the eyes marks the change in the relationship. 'Still' means motionless as well as continually.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti

  7. yet halfe trembling l. 11

    The deer allows herself to be taken but is still 'halfe trembling'. Readers have taken the detail as a touch of realism about an animal, as a sign of the bride's modesty, or, in more sceptical recent criticism, as a reminder that the courtship is narrated entirely by the man and that the language of the hunt implies power. The sonnet insists that she is tied 'with her own goodwill', but the tension between capture and consent is part of what keeps it debated.

    Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  8. with her own goodwill ... tyde l. 12

    The tying is the bond of betrothal and marriage, and the stress falls on 'her own goodwill'. Spenser's sequence repeatedly tries to reconcile the Petrarchan language of conquest with the Protestant ideal of companionate marriage, which required the free consent of both parties (compare Amoretti 65 on the 'bands' that bring liberty). Whether the poem fully escapes the hunter's perspective is debated; most editors take it as a deliberate correction of Petrarch and Wyatt, in which the beloved is the agent of the ending.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar

  9. with her owne will beguyld l. 14

    The couplet calls the outcome 'strange': a wild beast 'So goodly wonne', won so well or so easily, 'with her owne will beguyld'. 'Beguile' can mean to deceive or cheat (as with the hounds 'beguiled of their pray' in line 4) but also to charm or divert. The repetition ties the two halves of the poem together: the hounds were cheated of prey, and the deer is beguiled into being caught, though only by herself. The word lets the poem keep a hint of the old hunting game while making the decision hers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'beguile'; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti

  10. Amoretti and the calendar of 1594 ll. 1-14

    Kenneth Larsen has argued that Amoretti matches the daily Scripture readings of the Book of Common Prayer for early 1594, so that sonnet 22 marks Ash Wednesday and sonnet 68 Easter Day (31 March 1594). On this scheme sonnet 67 falls on Easter Eve, and its deer, thirsting for water, recalls Psalm 42. Not every critic accepts the full calendar, and some sonnets fit it poorly, but the Easter placement of 67-68 is widely accepted. The reading makes the deer's surrender a kind of grace that comes when striving stops.

    Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar; Bible (KJV), Psalms 42:1

  11. Form: the Spenserian sonnet ll. 13-14

    The Spenserian sonnet carries the last rhyme of each quatrain into the first rhyme of the next (here: chace/away/place/pray, assay/forsooke/way/brooke, looke/bide/tooke/tyde, then wyld/beguyld). The chain of rhymes gives the poem the continuous, linked movement of a single long sentence, suitable for a narrative of pursuit and return. The couplet comments on the action with a note of wonder: 'me seemd', it seemed to me.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti
