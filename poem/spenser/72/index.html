Love

Amoretti: Sonnet LXVIII

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

From Amoretti

Love

Most glorious Lord of lyfe! that on this day
Didst make thy triumph over death and sin,
And, having harrowd hell, didst bring away
Captivity thence captive, us to win,
This ioyous day, dear Lord, with ioy begin;
And grant that we, for whom thou diddest dy,
Being with thy deare blood clene washt from sin,
May live for ever in felicity;
And that thy love we weighing worthily,
May likewise love thee for the same againe,
And for thy sake, that all lyke deare didst buy,
With love may one another entertayne!
So let us love, deare Love, lyke as we ought:
Love is the lesson which the Lord us taught.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Amoretti 68: the Easter sonnet l. 1

    'This day' is Easter Sunday, the feast of the Resurrection, when Christ, the 'Lord of lyfe', triumphed over death and sin. Amoretti 68 is a prayer, and its address to Christ is unique in the sequence; only the final couplet turns to the woman ('deare Love'). On Kenneth Larsen's reading of Amoretti as a calendar of 1594, it falls on Easter Day, 31 March 1594, a little over two months before Spenser's wedding to Elizabeth Boyle on 11 June. The sonnet reconciles sacred and human love, which the whole sequence aims to do.

    Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti

  2. Lord of lyfe l. 1

    Christ is called 'the Prince of life' in Acts 3:15 and says 'I am the resurrection, and the life' in John 11:25. The title is set against death in the next line, where the Resurrection is described as a 'triumph', a Roman victory procession: Christ the conqueror leads death and sin as defeated enemies. The image anticipates 1 Corinthians 15:55-57, 'O death, where is thy sting? ... thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory'.

    Bible (KJV), Acts 3:15; Bible (KJV), John 11:25; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:55-57

  3. harrowd hell l. 3

    In medieval and Reformation teaching, between the Crucifixion and the Resurrection Christ descended into hell and released the souls of the patriarchs and the just who had died before him. The story is told in the apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus and was a favourite of the mystery plays; its scriptural basis was found in 1 Peter 3:19 ('he went and preached unto the spirits in prison') and the Apostles' Creed ('he descended into hell'). To 'harrow' is to despoil or plunder.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 3:19; Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate), Descent into Hell; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'harrow'

  4. Captivity thence captive l. 4

    Spenser quotes the Pauline phrase 'When he ascended up on high, he led captivity captive, and gave gifts unto men' (Ephesians 4:8), itself quoting Psalm 68:18. The figure means that Christ has made captive the power that held humanity captive, and in context refers to the freeing of souls from hell. 'Us to win' adds the language of courtship: Christ wins us as a lover wins, which prepares the turn to human love in the couplet. Some readers have noted that the psalm's number matches the sonnet's.

    Bible (KJV), Ephesians 4:8; Bible (KJV), Psalms 68:18; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  5. clene washt from sin l. 7

    The line draws on Revelation 1:5 and on the language of the Communion service. 'Deare' means precious and costly as well as beloved; the word recurs in lines 5, 7 and 11 and binds the poem's two loves together. 'Felicity' in line 8 is everlasting bliss, the joy of heaven. The image of washing also recalls baptism, through which, in Prayer Book teaching, the Christian is cleansed from sin and joined to Christ's death and resurrection; Easter was the ancient season for baptism.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 1:5; Book of Common Prayer (1559), Order for the Holy Communion

  6. weighing worthily l. 9

    To 'weigh' something 'worthily' is to estimate it at its proper value. The sense is that if we understand how much Christ's love cost, we will love him in return 'for the same', in exchange. Love is thus treated as a debt and a reciprocity, a theme that the couplet extends from God to the beloved. The verb 'weigh' fits the economic language of the poem, in which Christ's blood is a price paid and love is the return owed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weigh'; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  7. all lyke deare didst buy l. 11

    The line means: for the sake of you, who bought all of us equally dearly. Paul says 'ye are bought with a price' (1 Corinthians 6:20), and the doctrine of redemption, literally a buying back, is everywhere in Protestant devotion. The equality of the price ('all lyke deare') grounds the command to love one another, since every person has the same value in Christ's sight. To 'entertayne' one another with love is to receive and treat one another with it.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 6:20; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'entertain'; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  8. deare Love l. 13

    Most editors take 'deare Love' as an address to Elizabeth Boyle: the lovers should love each other as they ought, as Christ taught. The capital letter and the preceding address to the Lord allow a second sense, Love as God, so that the line holds both loves at once. Critics see here the sequence's reconciliation of eros with Christian charity, and of courtship with marriage. The pun in 'Love ... love ... Love ... Love' makes the word itself the poem's subject.

    The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti; Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar

  9. Love is the lesson l. 14

    The closing line summarises the 'new commandment' of John 13:34, and 1 John 4:11, 'Beloved, if God so loved us, we ought also to love one another', from which the 'as we ought' of line 13 seems to come. The heavy alliteration of l and the plain monosyllables give the line the shape of a proverb or catechism answer. The Easter collect of the Prayer Book, praying that God will 'put into our minds good desires', is often cited as a further background.

    Bible (KJV), John 13:34; Bible (KJV), 1 John 4:11; The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser, ed. W. A. Oram et al. (Yale UP, 1989), notes to Amoretti

  10. A sonnet as collect ll. 5-12

    Critics have compared the sonnet to the collects of the Book of Common Prayer, short prayers that address God, recall a saving act, and then ask for a grace in consequence. Here the address is lines 1-4, the remembered act the Resurrection and Harrowing, and the petitions follow: 'grant that we ... May live' and 'that thy love we ... May likewise love thee'. Its rhyme scheme is the interlocking Spenserian abab bcbc cdcd ee. 'Ioyous' and 'ioy' are joyous and joy, with i for j.

    Book of Common Prayer (1559), Collect for Easter Day; Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition, ed. Kenneth J. Larsen (Tempe: Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), notes and calendar; Edmund Spenser's Poetry, ed. Hugh Maclean and Anne Lake Prescott, Norton Critical Edition, 3rd ed. (1993), notes to Amoretti
