Love

Epithalamion

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

Love

Ye learnèd sisters, which have oftentimes
Beene to me ayding, others to adorne,
Whom ye thought worthy of your gracefull rymes,
That even the greatest did not greatly scorne
To heare theyr names sung in your simple layes,
But joyèd in theyr praise;
And when ye list your owne mishaps to mourne,
Which death, or love, or fortunes wreck did rayse,
Your string could soone to sadder tenor turne,
And teach the woods and waters to lament
Your dolefull dreriment:
Now lay those sorrowfull complaints aside;
And, having all your heads with girlands crownd,
Helpe me mine owne loves prayses to resound;
Ne let the same of any be envide:
So Orpheus did for his owne bride!
So I unto my selfe alone will sing;
The woods shall to me answer, and my Eccho ring.

Early, before the worlds light-giving lampe
His golden beame upon the hils doth spred,
Having disperst the nights unchearefull dampe,
Doe ye awake; and, with fresh lusty-hed,
Go to the bowre of my belovèd love,
My truest turtle dove;
Bid her awake; for Hymen is awake,
And long since ready forth his maske to move,
With his bright Tead that flames with many a flake,
And many a bachelor to waite on him,
In theyr fresh garments trim.
Bid her awake therefore, and soone her dight,
For lo! the wishèd day is come at last,
That shall, for all the paynes and sorrowes past,
Pay to her usury of long delight:
And, whylest she doth her dight,
Doe ye to her of joy and solace sing,
That all the woods may answer, and your eccho ring.

Bring with you all the Nymphes that you can heare
Both of the rivers and the forrests greene,

And of the sea that neighbours to her neare:
Al with gay girlands goodly wel beseene.
And let them also with them bring in hand
Another gay girland
For my fayre love, of lillyes and of roses,
Bound truelove wize, with a blew silke riband.
And let them make great store of bridale poses,
And let them eeke bring store of other flowers,
To deck the bridale bowers.
And let the ground whereas her foot shall tread,
For feare the stones her tender foot should wrong,
Be strewed with fragrant flowers all along,
And diapred lyke the discolored mead.
Which done, doe at her chamber dore awayt,
For she will waken strayt;
The whiles doe ye this song unto her sing,
The woods shall to you answer, and your Eccho ring.

Ye Nymphes of Mulla, which with carefull heed
The silver scaly trouts doe tend full well,
And greedy pikes which use therein to feed;
(Those trouts and pikes all others doo excell;)
And ye likewise, which keepe the rushy lake,
Where none doo fishes take;
Bynd up the locks the which hang scatterd light,
And in his waters, which your mirror make,
Behold your faces as the christall bright,
That when you come whereas my love doth lie,
No blemish she may spie.
And eke, ye lightfoot mayds, which keepe the deere,
That on the hoary mountayne used to towre;
And the wylde wolves, which seeke them to devoure,
With your steele darts doo chace from comming neer;
Be also present heere,

To helpe to decke her, and to help to sing,
That all the woods may answer, and your eccho ring.

Wake now, my love, awake! for it is time;
The Rosy Morne long since left Tithones bed,
All ready to her silver coche to clyme;
And Phœbus gins to shew his glorious hed.
Hark! how the cheerefull birds do chaunt theyr laies
And carroll of Loves praise.
The merry Larke hir mattins sings aloft;
The Thrush replyes; the Mavis descant playes;
The Ouzell shrills; the Ruddock warbles soft;
So goodly all agree, with sweet consent,
To this dayes merriment.
Ah! my deere love, why doe ye sleepe thus long?
When meeter were that ye should now awake,
T’ awayt the comming of your joyous make,
And hearken to the birds love-learnèd song,
The deawy leaves among!
Nor they of joy and pleasance to you sing,
That all the woods them answer, and theyr eccho ring.
My love is now awake out of her dreames,
And her fayre eyes, like stars that dimmèd were
With darksome cloud, now shew theyr goodly beams
More bright then Hesperus his head doth rere.
Come now, ye damzels, daughters of delight,
Helpe quickly her to dight:
But first come ye fayre houres, which were begot
In Joves sweet paradice of Day and Night;
Which doe the seasons of the yeare allot,
And al, that ever in this world is fayre,
Doe make and still repayre:

And ye three handmayds of the Cyprian Queene,
The which doe still adorne her beauties pride,
Helpe to addorne my beautifullest bride:
And, as ye her array, still throw betweene
Some graces to be seene;
And, as ye use to Venus, to her sing,
The whiles the woods shal answer, and your eccho ring.

Now is my love all ready forth to come:
Let all the virgins therefore well awayt:
And ye fresh boyes, that tend upon her groome,
Prepare your selves; for he is comming strayt.
Set all your things in seemely good aray,
Fit for so joyfull day:
The joyfulst day that ever sunne did see.
Faire Sun! shew forth thy favourable ray,
And let thy lifull heat not fervent be,
For feare of burning her sunshyny face,
Her beauty to disgrace.
O fayrest Phœbus! father of the Muse!
If ever I did honour thee aright,
Or sing the thing that mote thy mind delight,
Doe not thy servants simple boone refuse;
But let this day, let this one day, be myne;
Let all the rest be thine.
Then I thy soverayne prayses loud wil sing,
That all the woods shal answer, and theyr eccho ring.

Harke! how the Minstrils gin to shrill aloud
Their merry Musick that resounds from far,
The pipe, the tabor, and the trembling Croud,
That well agree withouten breach or jar.

But, most of all, the Damzels doe delite
When they their tymbrels smyte,
And thereunto doe daunce and carrol sweet,
That all the sences they doe ravish quite;
The whyles the boyes run up and downe the street,
Crying aloud with strong confusèd noyce,
As if it were one voyce,
Hymen, iö Hymen, Hymen, they do shout;
That even to the heavens theyr shouting shrill
Doth reach, and all the firmament doth fill;
To which the people standing all about,
As in approvance, doe thereto applaud,
And loud advaunce her laud;
And evermore they Hymen, Hymen sing,
That al the woods them answer, and theyr eccho ring.

Loe! where she comes along with portly pace,
Lyke Phœbe, from her chamber of the East,
Arysing forth to run her mighty race,
Clad all in white, that seemes a virgin best.
So well it her beseemes, that ye would weene
Some angell she had beene.
Her long loose yellow locks lyke golden wyre,
Sprinckled with perle, and perling flowres atweene,
Doe lyke a golden mantle her attyre;
And, being crownèd with a girland greene,
Seeme lyke some mayden Queene.
Her modest eyes, abashèd to behold
So many gazers as on her do stare,
Upon the lowly ground affixèd are;
Ne dare lift up her countenance too bold,
But blush to heare her prayses sung so loud,
So farre from being proud.
Nathlesse doe ye still loud her prayses sing,
That all the woods may answer, and your eccho ring.

Tell me, ye merchants daughters, did ye see
So fayre a creature in your towne before;
So sweet, so lovely, and so mild as she,
Adornd with beautyes grace and vertues store?
Her goodly eyes lyke Saphyres shining bright,
Her forehead yvory white,
Her cheekes lyke apples which the sun hath rudded,
Her lips lyke cherryes charming men to byte,
Her brest like to a bowle of creame uncrudded,
Her paps lyke lyllies budded,
Her snowie necke lyke to a marble towre;
And all her body like a pallace fayre,
Ascending up, with many a stately stayre,
To honors seat and chastities sweet bowre.
Why stand ye still ye virgins in amaze,
Upon her so to gaze,
Whiles ye forget your former lay to sing,
To which the woods did answer, and your eccho ring?

But if ye saw that which no eyes can see,
The inward beauty of her lively spright,
Garnisht with heavenly guifts of high degree,
Much more then would ye wonder at that sight,
And stand astonisht lyke to those which red
Medusaes mazeful hed.
There dwels sweet love, and constant chastity,
Unspotted fayth, and comely womanhood,
Regard of honour, and mild modesty;
There vertue raynes as Queene in royal throne,
And giveth lawes alone,
The which the base affections doe obay,
And yeeld theyr services unto her will;
Ne thought of thing uncomely ever may
Thereto approch to tempt her mind to ill.
Had ye once seene these her celestial threasures,
And unrevealèd pleasures,
Then would ye wonder, and her prayses sing,
That al the woods should answer, and your echo ring.

Open the temple gates unto my love,
Open them wide that she may enter in,
And all the postes adorne as doth behove,
And all the pillours deck with girlands trim,
For to receyve this Saynt with honour dew,
That commeth in to you.
With trembling steps, and humble reverence,
She commeth in, before th’ Almighties view;
Of her ye virgins learne obedience,
When so ye come into those holy places,
To humble your proud faces:
Bring her up to th’ high altar, that she may
The sacred ceremonies there partake,
The which do endlesse matrimony make;
And let the roring Organs loudly play
The praises of the Lord in lively notes;
The whiles, with hollow throates,
The Choristers the joyous Antheme sing,
That al the woods may answere, and their eccho ring.

Behold, whiles she before the altar stands,
Hearing the holy priest that to her speakes,
And blesseth her with his two happy hands,
How the red roses flush up in her cheekes,
And the pure snow, with goodly vermill stayne
Like crimsin dyde in grayne:
That even th’ Angels, which continually
About the sacred Altare doe remaine,
Forget their service and about her fly,
Ofte peeping in her face, that seems more fayre,
The more they on it stare.
But her sad eyes, still fastened on the ground,
Are governèd with goodly modesty,
That suffers not one looke to glaunce awry,
Which may let in a little thought unsownd.
Why blush ye, love, to give to me your hand,
The pledge of all our band!
Sing, ye sweet Angels, Alleluya sing,
That all the woods may answere, and your eccho ring.

Now al is done: bring home the bride againe;
Bring home the triumph of our victory:
Bring home with you the glory of her gaine;
With joyance bring her and with jollity.
Never had man more joyfull day then this,
Whom heaven would heape with blis,
Make feast therefore now all this live-long day;
This day for ever to me holy is.
Poure out the wine without restraint or stay,
Poure not by cups, but by the belly full,
Poure out to all that wull,
And sprinkle all the postes and wals with wine,
That they may sweat, and drunken be withall.
Crowne ye God Bacchus with a coronall,
And Hymen also crowne with wreathes of vine;
And let the Graces daunce unto the rest,
For they can doo it best:
The whiles the maydens doe theyr carroll sing,
To which the woods shall answer, and theyr eccho ring.

Ring ye the bels, ye yong men of the towne,
And leave your wonted labors for this day:
This day is holy; doe ye write it downe,
That ye for ever it remember may.
This day the sunne is in his chiefest hight,
With Barnaby the bright,
From whence declining daily by degrees,
He somewhat loseth of his heat and light,
When once the Crab behind his back he sees.
But for this time it ill ordainèd was,
To chose the longest day in all the yeare,
And shortest night, when longest fitter weare:
Yet never day so long, but late would passe.
Ring ye the bels, to make it weare away,
And bonefiers make all day;
And daunce about them, and about them sing,
That all the woods may answer, and your eccho ring.

Ah! when will this long weary day have end,
And lende me leave to come unto my love?
How slowly do the houres theyr numbers spend?
How slowly does sad Time his feathers move?
Hast thee, O fayrest Planet, to thy home,
Within the Westerne fome:
Thy tyrèd steedes long since have need of rest.
Long though it be, at last I see it gloome,
And the bright evening-star with golden creast
Appeare out of the East.
Fayre childe of beauty! glorious lampe of love!
That all the host of heaven in rankes doost lead,
And guydest lovers through the nights sad dread,
How chearefully thou lookest from above,
And seemst to laugh atweene thy twinkling light,
As joying in the sight
Of these glad many, which for joy doe sing,
That all the woods them answer, and their echo ring!

Now ceasse, ye damsels, your delights fore-past;
Enough it is that all the day was youres:
Now day is doen, and night is nighing fast,
Now bring the Bryde into the brydall boures.
The night is come, now soon her disaray,
And in her bed her lay;
Lay her in lillies and in violets,
And silken courteins over her display,
And odourd sheetes, and Arras coverlets.
Behold how goodly my faire love does ly,
In proud humility!
Like unto Maia, when as Jove her took
In Tempe, lying on the flowry gras,
Twixt sleepe and wake, after she weary was,
With bathing in the Acidalian brooke.
Now it is night, ye damsels may be gon,
And leave my love alone,
And leave likewise your former lay to sing:
The woods no more shall answere, nor your echo ring.

Now welcome, night! thou night so long expected,
That long daies labour doest at last defray,
And all my cares, which cruell Love collected,
Hast sumd in one, and cancellèd for aye:
Spread thy broad wing over my love and me,
That no man may us see;
And in thy sable mantle us enwrap,
From feare of perrill and foule horror free.
Let no false treason seeke us to entrap,
Nor any dread disquiet once annoy
The safety of our joy;
But let the night be calme, and quietsome,
Without tempestuous storms or sad afray:
Lyke as when Jove with fayre Alcmena lay,
When he begot the great Tirynthian groome:
Or lyke as when he with thy selfe did lie
And begot Majesty.
And let the mayds and yong men cease to sing;
Ne let the woods them answer nor theyr eccho ring.

Let no lamenting cryes, nor dolefull teares,
Be heard all night within, nor yet without:
Ne let false whispers, breeding hidden feares,
Breake gentle sleepe with misconceivèd dout.
Let no deluding dreames, nor dreadfull sights,
Make sudden sad affrights;
Ne let house-fyres, nor lightnings helpelesse harmes,
Ne let the Pouke, nor other evill sprights,
Ne let mischivous witches with theyr charmes,
Ne let hob Goblins, names whose sence we see not,
Fray us with things that be not:
Let not the shriech Oule nor the Storke be heard,
Nor the night Raven, that still deadly yels;
Nor damnèd ghosts, cald up with mighty spels,
Nor griesly vultures, make us once affeard:
Ne let th’ unpleasant Quyre of Frogs still croking
Make us to wish theyr choking.
Let none of these theyr drery accents sing;
Ne let the woods them answer, nor theyr eccho ring.

But let stil Silence trew night-watches keepe,
That sacred Peace may in assurance rayne,
And tymely Sleep, when it is tyme to sleepe,
May poure his limbs forth on your pleasant playne;
The whiles an hundred little wingèd loves,
Like divers-fethered doves,
Shall fly and flutter round about your bed,
And in the secret darke, that none reproves,
Their prety stealthes shal worke, and snares shal spread
To filch away sweet snatches of delight,
Conceald through covert night.
Ye sonnes of Venus, play your sports at will!
For greedy pleasure, carelesse of your toyes,
Thinks more upon her paradise of joyes,
Then what ye do, albe it good or ill.
All night therefore attend your merry play,
For it will soone be day:
Now none doth hinder you, that say or sing;
Ne will the woods now answer, nor your Eccho ring.

Who is the same, which at my window peepes?
Or whose is that faire face that shines so bright?
Is it not Cinthia, she that never sleepes,
But walkes about high heaven al the night?
O! fayrest goddesse, do thou not envy
My love with me to spy:
For thou likewise didst love, though now unthought,
And for a fleece of wooll, which privily
The Latmian shepherd once unto thee brought,
His pleasures with thee wrought.
Therefore to us be favorable now;
And sith of wemens labours thou hast charge,
And generation goodly dost enlarge,
Encline thy will t’effect our wishfull vow,
And the chast wombe informe with timely seed
That may our comfort breed:
Till which we cease our hopefull hap to sing;
Ne let the woods us answere, nor our Eccho ring.

And thou, great Juno! which with awful might
The lawes of wedlock still dost patronize;
And the religion of the faith first plight
With sacred rites hast taught to solemnize;
And eeke for comfort often callèd art
Of women in their smart;
Eternally bind thou this lovely band,
And all thy blessings unto us impart.
And thou, glad Genius! in whose gentle hand
The bridale bowre and geniall bed remaine,
Without blemish or staine;
And the sweet pleasures of theyr loves delight
With secret ayde doest succour and supply,
Till they bring forth the fruitfull progeny;
Send us the timely fruit of this same night.
And thou, fayre Hebe! and thou, Hymen free!
Grant that it may so be.
Til which we cease your further prayse to sing;
Ne any woods shall answer, nor your Eccho ring.

And ye high heavens, the temple of the gods,
In which a thousand torches flaming bright
Doe burne, that to us wretched earthly clods
In dreadful darknesse lend desirèd light
And all ye powers which in the same remayne,
More then we men can fayne!
Poure out your blessing on us plentiously,
And happy influence upon us raine,
That we may raise a large posterity,
Which from the earth, which they may long possesse
With lasting happinesse,
Up to your haughty pallaces may mount;
And, for the guerdon of theyr glorious merit,
May heavenly tabernacles there inherit,
Of blessèd Saints for to increase the count.
So let us rest, sweet love, in hope of this,
And cease till then our tymely joyes to sing:
The woods no more us answer, nor our eccho ring!

Song! made in lieu of many ornaments.
With which my love should duly have been dect,
Which cutting off through hasty accidents,
Ye would not stay your dew time to expect
But promist both to recompens;
Be unto her a goodly ornament,
And for short time an endlesse moniment.

Study notes by the editors

  1. learnèd sisters l. 1

    The 'learned sisters' are the Muses. Spenser reminds them that they have helped him praise others (the great patrons of his earlier poems) and have also mourned their 'owne mishaps', which editors take as a glance at his Teares of the Muses (1591), where each Muse laments the neglect of learning, and at his elegies and 'complaints'. The opening stanza therefore surveys Spenser's own career, moving from public praise and lament to a private subject: 'mine owne loves prayses'. The invocation follows the epithalamic convention of calling the singer's helpers to the wedding, but it is unusually personal, because the poet will be his own bridegroom.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Hesiod, Theogony, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Perseus Digital Library)

  2. Orpheus and his bride l. 16

    Orpheus, son of the Muse Calliope, is the model of the poet whose song moves woods, rocks and beasts, which prepares the refrain's answering woods. The precedent is double-edged. In Ovid's account the wedding of Orpheus and Eurydice was ill-omened: Hymen came, but his torch only sputtered and smoked, and the bride soon died of a snakebite (Metamorphoses 10.1-10). Critics have observed that Spenser's choice of Orpheus introduces, from the first stanza, the shadow of loss and the fragility of time that the poem's numerical and seasonal design tries to master. The allusion also anticipates the poem's own torch-bearing Hymen in the next stanza.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  3. The occasion: the poet's own wedding l. 17

    An epithalamium was normally written by a poet for someone else's wedding, as Catullus did for Manlius Torquatus (Carmen 61). Spenser's innovation is to be both singer and groom. He married Elizabeth Boyle, a kinswoman of Richard Boyle (later Earl of Cork), on 11 June 1594 (the usual date, inferred largely from the poem's own reference to St Barnabas's Day), probably in or near Cork; a local tradition places the ceremony in Cork, but this is not documented. The poem was published by William Ponsonby in 1595 in a single volume, Amoretti and Epithalamion, so that the sonnet sequence of courtship leads into the marriage song. 'Alone' stresses the self-sufficiency of the poet's voice, answered only by woods and echo.

    Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; 'Epithalamion (poem)', Wikipedia (structure and publication summary); Catullus, Carmen 61 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text)

  4. The refrain l. 18

    The long final line of every stanza, an alexandrine, is a refrain modelled on the refrains of classical wedding songs (Catullus's repeated cry to Hymen) and on the Italian canzone, whose stanza form Spenser adapts. The refrain is never quite identical: pronouns shift ('me', 'you', 'theyr'), the verb moves from future to present, and after the bride is put to bed it becomes negative ('The woods no more shall answere, nor your echo ring', l. 314). Readers have treated these modulations as a clock: the public noise of the day gives way to the silence and privacy of night. Echo, the nymph who can only repeat, makes the natural world a chorus to the poet's single voice.

    Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; Catullus, Carmen 61 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text)

  5. Hymen ... his bright Tead l. 27

    Hymen (Hymenaeus) is the classical god of marriage, whose name was shouted in the wedding procession. His 'maske' is a masque or ceremonial procession, the pageant that escorted the bride. 'Tead' is a Spenserian borrowing of Latin taeda, the pine torch carried before the bride in Roman weddings and one of Hymen's standard attributes; 'flake' here means a spark or flash of flame. Catullus calls on Hymen to shake the pine torch (Carmen 61), which is the likely model. The 'bachelors' in fresh garments are the groom's young male attendants, matched later by the 'damzels' of the bride.

    Catullus, Carmen 61 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  6. usury of long delight l. 33

    Usury is interest on a loan. The image treats the pains of the long courtship recorded in the Amoretti as a debt or investment that the wedding will repay with interest in the form of lasting delight. The conceit is daring, since usury was morally suspect in Spenser's England (the 1571 statute allowed it only within limits), but here it is harmless and joyful. The same accounting language returns at nightfall, when night 'defrays' the day's labour and 'sums' and 'cancels' the lover's cares (ll. 316-18), so the poem frames love as a ledger finally settled.

    Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  7. turtle dove l. 24

    The turtle-dove was proverbial for constancy, since it was believed to take only one mate. Spenser's call to wake the bride recalls the Song of Songs 2:10-14: 'Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away ... the voice of the turtle is heard in our land ... O my dove'. Editors have long noted that the poem blends this biblical aubade with classical epithalamic convention, so that Christian and pagan sources of marriage poetry run together throughout. The Canticles' imagery recurs more strongly in the blazon of stanza 10.

    The Bible (King James Version), Song of Solomon 2:10-14; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  8. Mulla l. 56

    Spenser lived at Kilcolman Castle, near Doneraile in north Cork, on land granted to him in the Munster plantation. In Colin Clouts Come Home Againe and The Faerie Queene (IV.xi.41) he calls the local river Awbeg 'Mulla' and mythologises it. Here the classical nymphs of river, lake and mountain are given Irish homes: Mulla's trout and pike, the 'rushy lake' (usually taken as the marshy lough by Kilcolman), and the hills where deer are hunted. Critics working on Spenser in Ireland note that the stanza turns contested colonial land into a pastoral landscape that serves the English poet's wedding.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'

  9. The Rosy Morne ... Tithones bed l. 75

    Aurora, the Dawn, was married to the Trojan prince Tithonus, for whom she won immortality but not eternal youth. The epic formula of Dawn leaving Tithonus's bed opens Odyssey 5 and is imitated by Virgil ('Tithoni croceum linquens Aurora cubile', Aeneid 4.585). 'Rosy Morne' renders Homer's 'rosy-fingered' Dawn. Spenser borrows epic grandeur for the hour of the bride's waking; the myth's undertone of a love that endures while the mortal partner ages suits a poem preoccupied with time. Phoebus (the sun) follows in the next line.

    Virgil, Aeneid 4.584-5 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  10. Larke ... Mavis ... Ouzell ... Ruddock l. 81

    The birds form a choir singing the 'mattins' (morning office) of the church of love. 'Mavis' is a dialect name for the song-thrush and 'Ouzell' for the blackbird; 'Ruddock' is the robin. Since thrush and mavis may be the same bird, editors suggest Spenser means two kinds of thrush (the mistle-thrush and song-thrush) or simply enjoys the list. The vocabulary is musical: a 'descant' is a melody sung above a plainsong, and 'consent' means harmony (Latin concentus) as well as agreement. The bird chorus was a commonplace of the medieval reverdie and of wedding poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  11. ye fayre houres l. 98

    In Hesiod the Horae are daughters of Zeus and Themis (Theogony 901-3) and guardians of the seasons. Spenser instead makes them children of Jove's paradise 'of Day and Night', a genealogy that suits a poem about one day and one night. The Hours were attendants of Venus as well as keepers of time, so here they join the Graces in adorning the bride. A. Kent Hieatt argued that the hours are the hidden structural key: the poem's 24 stanzas correspond to the hours of the wedding day and night. Not all critics accept his full scheme, but the hours' presence at this point is generally read as self-referential.

    Hesiod, Theogony, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Perseus Digital Library); A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line

  12. three handmayds of the Cyprian Queene l. 103

    Venus was born from the sea near Cyprus and was worshipped at Paphos; hence 'Cyprian Queene'. The three Graces (Euphrosyne, Aglaia, Thalia in Hesiod's naming) were her handmaids. Spenser gave them a major visionary role in The Faerie Queene VI.x, where Colin Clout pipes to them on Mount Acidale and a fourth grace, usually identified with Colin's own beloved, stands in their midst; many critics connect that scene with Elizabeth Boyle. The pun in 'Some graces to be seene' (graceful touches, and the goddesses themselves) is typical of Spenser's wordplay.

    Hesiod, Theogony, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Perseus Digital Library); Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  13. pipe ... tabor ... Croud ... tymbrels l. 131

    The minstrels play popular instruments: the pipe and tabor (a small drum played by the same performer) and the 'croud' or crowd, a bowed stringed instrument, from Welsh crwth, often used loosely for a fiddle. 'Trembling' describes its vibrating tone. The 'tymbrels' are tambourines; the word recalls Miriam and the women of Israel dancing with timbrels in Exodus 15:20. 'Withouten breach or jar' carries a moral as well as musical sense: harmony among instruments mirrors social and marital concord.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; The Bible (King James Version), Exodus 15:20; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  14. Hymen, iö Hymen l. 140

    Roman wedding processions escorted the bride to her husband's house with the cry 'io Hymen Hymenaee', which serves as the refrain of Catullus 61 and 62. The diaeresis in 'iö' marks two syllables. Spenser transfers the cry to the boys of an Irish town running through the street, and the crowd 'applauds' as if at a theatre. The scene emphasises the public, communal phase of the day, which later gives way to the hushed private night; James Lambert and others have described this as the poem's movement from communal ritual to private affect.

    Catullus, Carmen 61 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; 'Epithalamion (poem)', Wikipedia (structure and publication summary)

  15. Arysing forth to run her mighty race l. 150

    Psalm 19:4-5 describes the sun, 'which is as a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, and rejoiceth as a strong man to run a race'. Spenser reverses the genders and the planet: the bride, compared to the moon goddess, comes from 'her chamber of the East' to run her race. The allusion gives the procession a cosmic and scriptural dignity and links the bride to the heavenly bodies that measure time, one of the poem's controlling concerns. It is one of several places where the poem joins classical deities with the language of the Psalms.

    The Bible (King James Version), Psalm 19:4-5; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  16. ye merchants daughters l. 167

    After invoking nymphs and goddesses, the poet appeals to the merchants' daughters of the 'towne', usually taken to be Cork or Youghal, the ports near the Boyle family. The shift of audience is striking: the bride's beauty is displayed before ordinary townswomen as well as before the deities of the earlier stanzas. Critics have read the line as a mark of the poem's social realism and of the Anglo-Irish urban setting of the marriage. The question 'did ye see / So fayre a creature' introduces the formal catalogue of the bride's beauty, the blazon.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'

  17. The blazon l. 175

    Stanza 10 is a blazon, the Petrarchan inventory of a woman's features, but its homely comparisons (apples, cherries, a bowl of cream, budding lilies) recall the Song of Songs, whose bride has a neck 'like the tower of David' (4:4) and 'as a tower of ivory' (7:4); compare 'snowie necke lyke to a marble towre'. 'Uncrudded' means not curdled, smooth and fresh; 'rudded' means reddened. Readers disagree over the effect: some find the domestic images charmingly rustic, others see a deliberately sensuous catalogue that the next stanza corrects by praising the 'inward beauty' no eye can see.

    The Bible (King James Version), Song of Solomon 4:1-5; 7:4; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  18. like a palace fair l. 178

    The image of the body as a building governed from its upper storeys is ancient (Plato's tripartite soul, with reason in the head) and is developed at length in Spenser's House of Alma in The Faerie Queene II.ix. Here the ascent ends in 'honors seat and chastities sweet bowre', the head and the mind where virtue rules. The figure turns the blazon's physical catalogue toward moral meaning and prepares for the next stanza, in which virtue reigns 'as Queene in royal throne' over the 'base affections'.

    Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  19. Medusaes mazeful hed l. 190

    Medusa, the Gorgon slain by Perseus, turned to stone whoever looked at her (Ovid, Metamorphoses 4-5). 'Red' is a past tense of 'read' in the old sense of perceive or look upon; 'mazeful' means bewildering, amazing. The comparison is bold, since Medusa is monstrous, but its point is the stunning, petrifying power of a sight beyond ordinary sight. The stanza's 'inward beauty' and 'heavenly guifts' reflect the Neoplatonic doctrine, developed in Spenser's Fowre Hymnes, that true beauty is of the soul, while the 'base affections' obeying virtue echo the moral psychology of the House of Alma.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line

  20. Open the temple gates l. 204

    The command to open the gates and deck the posts recalls both Psalm 24:7-9 ('Lift up your heads, O ye gates ... and the King of glory shall come in') and the Roman custom of adorning the doorposts of the bridegroom's house. The 'temple' is the parish church; the bride is called a 'Saynt', and the ceremony that makes 'endlesse matrimony' is the Protestant marriage service of the Book of Common Prayer, with organ and anthem. Critics have treated stanzas 12-13 as the sacred centre of the poem, where classical festivity gives way to Christian sacrament without either being rejected.

    The Bible (King James Version), Psalm 24:7-9; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  21. dyde in grayne l. 228

    'Grain' was the name for kermes, the dried insects that yielded scarlet dye, and to dye 'in grain' was to dye fast and thoroughly. The blush spreading over the 'pure snow' of the bride's face is thus permanent in colour though momentary in occurrence, a characteristic Spenserian paradox. The red-and-white complexion is a Petrarchan commonplace, but the setting before the altar makes it a sign of modesty under the priest's blessing. The angels who forget their service to peep at her face are a daring, half-playful touch that critics have often admired.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  22. sad eyes l. 234

    In Elizabethan English 'sad' often meant serious, sober or steadfast (from Old English sæd, sated, then firm); the sense of sorrowful was only one of its meanings. The bride's eyes are fixed on the ground in modesty and governed so that no glance strays 'awry' to admit 'a little thought unsownd' (unsound, improper). Her hand given to the groom is 'the pledge of all our band', the band (bond) of marriage, joined in the service's handfasting. The stanza ends with the angels singing Alleluia, the climax of the sacred action.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line

  23. Bacchus and the feast l. 253

    After the church service the poem resumes classical festivity. Pouring wine 'by the belly full' and sprinkling the posts and walls recall ancient wedding and festival customs; the walls that 'sweat' and become 'drunken' are a comic hyperbole. Bacchus, god of wine, is crowned with a 'coronall' (garland), and Hymen with vine leaves. Critics differ on how far this licence is controlled: some see a happy coexistence of pagan joy and Christian sacrament, others a deliberate contrast that the following stanzas, with their anxiety about time, temper.

    Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; Catullus, Carmen 61 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text)

  24. Barnaby the bright l. 266

    Spenser's wedding took place on St Barnabas's Day, 11 June 1594. In the Julian calendar, still used in England, the date coincided with the summer solstice, hence the proverb 'Barnaby bright, the longest day and the shortest night'. After the solstice the sun begins to decline once it 'sees the Crab behind his back', that is, once it has entered the zodiacal sign of Cancer. The poet complains comically that he has chosen the longest day, when a long night would have suited him better. Hieatt's numerical reading depends on this date: the poem's proportion of day to night stanzas matches the hours of daylight at the solstice in southern Ireland.

    A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line

  25. Stanza 15: the short stanza l. 261

    Most stanzas of the poem have eighteen or nineteen lines, with short trimeter lines placed at regular intervals. The bell-ringing stanza has only seventeen, because it omits the expected short line after its ninth line, though its rhyme scheme is complete. Explanations range from authorial slip to number symbolism. John Hale and Stefan Lane weigh the possibilities without settling the matter: a slip or printing error seems unlikely given the complete rhyme scheme, numerology explains little, and they find the idea that the missing short line enacts the complaint about the long day and short night rather obvious. They also consider that the irregular rhythm might imitate the jangling of the bells the stanza addresses.

    John Hale and Stefan Lane, 'The Mystery of the Missing Line: Spenser's Epithalamion stanza 15', Deep South 2.2 (1996); A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; 'Epithalamion (poem)', Wikipedia (structure and publication summary)

  26. the bright evening-star ... out of the East l. 287

    The evening star is Hesperus, the planet Venus seen after sunset, and it always appears in the western sky, near where the sun has set. Spenser's 'out of the East' is therefore astronomically impossible. Some editors take it as a slip or a loose use of a conventional phrase; others propose symbolic readings, for instance that the star rises like the bride (who earlier came 'from her chamber of the East') or that Venus as both morning and evening star joins dawn and dusk. The Variorum commentary records the problem. Spenser earlier compared the bride's eyes to Hesperus (l. 95), and the star is addressed here as Venus's 'lampe of love'.

    The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  27. Maia ... Tempe ... the Acidalian brooke l. 307

    Maia, eldest of the Pleiades, bore Mercury (Hermes) to Jove. Tempe is the beautiful valley of the river Peneus in Thessaly. The Acidalian spring, associated in ancient commentary with Venus and the Graces, gives its name to Mount Acidale in The Faerie Queene VI.x, where Colin Clout sees the Graces dance. No classical source tells this particular story of Maia, and editors regard the scene as Spenser's own combination of mythic places. The comparison eroticises the bride while keeping her in a landscape of innocence and grace, 'Twixt sleepe and wake'.

    Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  28. Now welcome, night! l. 315

    Stanza 17 puts the bride to bed ('Now it is night'), and stanza 18 welcomes night itself, so the poem passes from public day to private night. Night is welcomed in the financial language seen earlier: it 'defrays' the long day's labour and has 'sumd' and 'cancellèd' the lover's cares. The bride's attendants have been dismissed, and the refrain now commands the woods and echo to be silent. Hieatt noted that night falls at the point corresponding to the number of daylight hours on the wedding day; whether or not one accepts his calculation, the shift in the refrain makes the change audible.

    A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  29. Alcmena ... the great Tirynthian groome ... Majesty l. 329

    Jove, disguised as Amphitryon, lay with Alcmena and prolonged the night to enjoy her, begetting Hercules, called 'Tirynthian' from Tiryns, the city of his foster-father (the story is told in Plautus's Amphitruo and elsewhere). 'Groome' here means young man or hero. The second myth, that Jove lay with Night and 'begot Majesty', has no clear classical source: in Ovid's Fasti (5.11-52) Majesty is the child of Honour and Reverence. Editors therefore treat it as Spenser's invention, apt for a poem that hopes the marriage night will produce noble offspring.

    The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  30. the Pouke ... hob Goblins l. 341

    The 'Pouke' is the puck or pooka, a mischievous or malign spirit of English and Irish folklore (Irish púca), later domesticated by Shakespeare's Robin Goodfellow in A Midsummer Night's Dream. 'Hob Goblins' are household goblins; Spenser adds the sceptical aside 'names whose sence we see not'. The stanza is an apotropaic catalogue, a formal warding-off of evil, in which owls, the night raven and croaking frogs are omens of death. Such charms belong to the epithalamic tradition, which often prays that no ill omen will disturb the couple; the Irish setting gives the folklore a local colour.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line

  31. Cinthia ... the Latmian shepherd l. 374

    Cynthia (Diana, from Mount Cynthus on Delos) is the moon. Her love for Endymion, the shepherd of Mount Latmos, was a favourite Elizabethan story. Spenser's detail of 'a fleece of wooll' comes from Virgil's Georgics 3.391-3, where the god Pan wins the Moon with a gift of snowy wool; Spenser transfers the gift to Endymion. As Lucina, the moon goddess presided over childbirth ('wemens labours'), so she is asked to 'informe' (give form to) the womb with seed. The prayers for children in the closing stanzas reflect the Prayer Book's first purpose of marriage, the procreation of children.

    Virgil, Georgics 3.391-3 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  32. Juno ... Genius ... Hebe l. 398

    Juno was the Roman goddess of marriage (Juno Pronuba) and of childbirth (Juno Lucina), hence called on 'of women in their smart'. Genius was the guardian spirit of procreation; the marriage bed was the lectus genialis, so 'geniall bed' plays on the Latin. Spenser's Genius also appears at the gate of the Garden of Adonis (Faerie Queene III.vi.31-3), the mythic seedbed of all generation. Hebe, daughter of Juno, is goddess of youth. The poem thus moves through a ladder of powers, from the moon to Juno to the gods of heaven, each asked for fruitful offspring.

    Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  33. heavenly tabernacles l. 422

    The last stanza addresses the heavens as a temple lit by the stars' 'thousand torches' and prays for a 'large posterity' who will possess the earth and then 'inherit' heavenly dwellings. 'Tabernacles' is biblical (compare 'everlasting habitations', Luke 16:9, and the earthly 'tabernacle' of 2 Corinthians 5:1); 'influence' is the astrological flowing of stellar power. The prayer ties the one day of the wedding to the end of time, completing the poem's movement from hours to years to eternity, and to the Protestant ideal of marriage as producing godly heirs.

    The Bible (King James Version), 2 Corinthians 5:1; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  34. The envoy l. 427

    The envoy, in the manner of the Italian canzone's closing stanza, addresses the poem itself. It is to replace 'many ornaments' with which the bride should have been adorned but which, 'through hasty accidents', were cut off. What these accidents were is unknown. Suggestions include ornaments or gifts that failed to arrive in time, a hurried wedding, or a poem (or another work) that Spenser had meant to finish for the occasion; 'Ye would not stay your dew time to expect' is taken by some to address the occasion itself, by others the ornaments. The syntax is notoriously knotted, and editors print different punctuation.

    The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe: MRTS, 1997), note to the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  35. for short time an endlesse moniment l. 433

    The poem has 24 stanzas (23 with the refrain plus the closing envoy) and 365 long lines, which A. Kent Hieatt (1960) read as the hours of the day and the days of the year, so that one day of marriage stands for the year's cycle and, through it, for time made eternal. Hieatt's further claims, such as the pairing of stanzas across the two halves and the arrival of night at the seventeenth stanza (matching the daylight hours of a southern Irish midsummer), have been more contested, but the basic counts are accepted. The final phrase also echoes the classical boast that poetry outlasts bronze (Horace, Odes 3.30, 'exegi monumentum'): the short time of one wedding becomes endless in verse.

    A. Kent Hieatt, Short Time's Endless Monument: The Symbolism of the Numbers in Edmund Spenser's 'Epithalamion' (New York: Columbia UP, 1960); summarised in contemporary reviews; 'Epithalamion (poem)', Wikipedia (structure and publication summary); John Hale and Stefan Lane, 'The Mystery of the Missing Line: Spenser's Epithalamion stanza 15', Deep South 2.2 (1996)
