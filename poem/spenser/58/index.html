Love

Amoretti: Sonnet LIV

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

From Amoretti

Love

Of this worlds theatre in which we stay,
My Love, like the spectator, ydly sits,
Beholding me, that all the pageants play,
Disguysing diversly my troubled wits.
Sometimes I ioy when glad occasion fits,
And mask in myrth lyke to a comedy:
Soone after, when my ioy to sorrow flits,
I waile, and make my woes a tragedy.
Yet she, beholding me with constant eye,
Delights not in my merth, nor rues my smart:
But when I laugh, she mocks; and when I cry,
She laughs, and hardens evermore her hart.
What then can move her? If nor merth, nor mone,
She is no woman, but a sencelesse stone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Amoretti 54 l. 1

    The Amoretti was printed in 1595 together with the Epithalamion, Spenser's marriage poem. Unlike most Petrarchan sequences, it ends in marriage, and several critics have argued that the sonnets follow the church calendar of 1594 (Lent to Easter). Sonnet 54 is one of several that complain of the lady's hardness before she accepts him. The archive keeps the 1595 spelling ('worlds', 'ydly', 'ioy', 'hart', 'sencelesse').

    Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54; Amoretti 54, text (monadnock.net Spenser pages)

  2. The world's theatre l. 1

    The idea goes back to antiquity: the Stoic Epictetus compares life to a play in which each person acts an assigned part (Enchiridion 17). It runs through the Renaissance, in Erasmus's Praise of Folly and in Jaques's 'All the world's a stage' (As You Like It 2.7). Spenser makes it private: the world is a theatre and his love is its only audience. The usual spectator of the world's play is God, so putting the lady in that seat suggests a god-like indifference.

    Epictetus, Enchiridion 17; William Shakespeare, As You Like It 2.7; Ernst Robert Curtius, European Literature and the Latin Middle Ages (trans. 1953), on the theatrum mundi topos; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54

  3. The idle spectator l. 2

    'Ydly' (idly) means without action or care. Her leisure is set against his effort, and the picture suggests a lady of rank at a play or court entertainment. In 'we stay' (l. 1), 'stay' means dwell for a time: life on earth is a short stay. The word comes from Latin spectare, to watch, and the lady does nothing but watch. The lover plays for an audience of one, and everything in the poem depends on her response, which never comes.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'idly, adv.'; Amoretti 54, text (monadnock.net Spenser pages)

  4. pageants l. 3

    A pageant was first the wagon-stage of a mystery play, then the scene acted on it, and then any show or spectacle. To 'play one's pageant' meant to act a part. 'Disguysing diversly' (l. 4) means presenting his troubled feelings in different costumes, and 'disguise' carries a sense of masquing. The plural suggests a whole festival programme. Each mood the lover shows is a new scene for the same unmoved audience.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pageant, n.'; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54

  5. mask l. 6

    The court masque, with its disguised dancers, was a popular entertainment. 'Mask in myrth' suggests that his joy is a performance put on for her, 'lyke to a comedy'. 'When glad occasion fits' (l. 5) means when a happy chance arises. The verb also suggests that his mirth hides his real feelings, as a mask hides a face. The lover is both actor and victim of his own moods, which change 'when glad occasion fits', so that the stage image fits a life ruled by chance and by her moods.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mask, v.2'; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54

  6. Comedy and tragedy ll. 6-8

    The octave pairs the two genres, comedy as the play of joy and tragedy as the play of woe. Renaissance readers knew this distinction from the essays on drama by Evanthius and Donatus printed with editions of Terence. The Spenserian rhyme scheme (abab bcbc cdcd ee) links each quatrain to the next, so the move from 'comedy' to 'tragedy' happens on interlocking rhymes.

    Evanthius and Aelius Donatus, essays on comedy (4th century), printed in Renaissance editions of Terence; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54

  7. The turn l. 9

    'Constant' is an ironic compliment. Constancy is the lover's virtue, but here it is the steadiness of indifference. In line 10, 'rues my smart' means pities my pain: to 'rue' is to feel sorrow for, and 'smart' is sharp pain. The 'eye' that watches without changing returns to the image of the spectator in l. 2. Where Petrarchan sonnets often turn at line 9 from complaint to resolution, here the turn shows only that the lover's whole performance has failed to touch her.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rue, v.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'smart, n.'; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54

  8. The perverse spectator ll. 11-12

    Classical theory expected an audience to feel pity and fear at tragedy (Aristotle, Poetics 13-14), and Horace says that if you want me to weep, you must first grieve yourself (Ars Poetica 102-3). A spectator who laughs at tragedy and mocks mirth breaks those expectations. Her heart that 'hardens evermore' recalls the hardening of Pharaoh's heart (Exodus 7-11).

    Horace, Ars Poetica 101-103; Bible (KJV), Exodus 7:13; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54

  9. mone l. 13

    'Move' is the key verb of Renaissance rhetoric and poetics, which held that poetry should teach, delight and move (as in Sidney's Defence of Poesy). Judged by every art of persuasion, the lover's double performance has failed. 'Merth' and 'mone' sum up the comedy and tragedy of the octave in two alliterating words. The couplet's question then receives its own answer in the last line, a sharp ending typical of the Spenserian couplet.

    Philip Sidney, The Defence of Poesy (1595), on poetry's power to move; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'moan, n.'

  10. The senseless stone l. 14

    The stony mistress is a convention that goes back to Petrarch and to Dante's rime petrose ('stony rhymes'). 'Sencelesse' means without feeling and also without understanding. Some readers see a sly hint of Pygmalion, whose statue came to life through love (Ovid, Metamorphoses 10), looking ahead to the lady's later softening in the sequence. The insult of the last line turns the theatre conceit into an image of sculpture.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.243-97 (Pygmalion); Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Tempe, 1997), notes to sonnet 54; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (Penguin, 1999), notes to Amoretti 54
