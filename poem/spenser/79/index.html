Amoretti: Sonnet LXXV

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

From Amoretti

Love

One day I wrote her name upon the strand,
But came the waves and washed it away:
Agayne I wrote it with a second hand;
But came the tyde, and made my paynes his pray.
“Vayne man,” sayd she, “that doest in vaine assay
A mortall thing so to immortalize;
For I my selve shall lyke to this decay,
And eke my name bee wyped out lykewize.”
“Not so,” quod I; “let baser things devize
To dy in dust, but you shall live by fame:
My verse your vertues rare shall eternize,
And in the hevens wryte your glorious name.
Where, when as death shall all the world subdew,
Our love shall live, and later life renew.”

Study notes by the editors

  1. Amoretti 75: occasion and place in the sequence l. 1

    Amoretti ('little loves', the Italian word for the Cupids of love poetry) is a sequence of 89 sonnets published by William Ponsonby in 1595 in one volume with the Anacreontics and Epithalamion, the marriage ode. Unlike most Elizabethan sequences, it moves towards a successful, Christian marriage, generally identified with Spenser's marriage to Elizabeth Boyle in Ireland in 1594. Sonnet 75 follows directly on Sonnet 74, which praises the 'happy letters' of the name Elizabeth, shared by his mother, his queen and his beloved; 'her name' in line 1 therefore carries that name without stating it. Larsen's edition reads the sequence against the church calendar of 1594, placing the late sonnets in the season after Easter, which gives the closing promise of renewed life an extra resonance.

    Wikipedia, 'Amoretti'; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), note on Sonnet 75; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  2. strand l. 1

    'Strand' is the native word for the sea-shore (still alive in names such as the Strand in London, once the river bank). Writing in sand was a proverbial figure for futile or short-lived effort, and Spenser turns the proverb into a small narrative: the poet writes, the waves undo it, he writes again. The beach setting prepares the contrast, central to the sestet, between the dissolving medium of sand and the lasting medium of verse, and it anticipates the poem's argument about the 'tyde' of time.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strand, n.1'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), Amoretti LXXV, text and notes

  3. with a second hand l. 3

    'Hand' here means an attempt or turn (as in 'try one's hand'), and also, punningly, handwriting: he writes the name again in a fresh hand. The repetition (lines 1-2 and 3-4 share a pattern: 'I wrote ... But came the waves'; 'Agayne I wrote ... But came the tyde') enacts the futility the lady will point out. The parallel syntax is a rhetorical device, a kind of anaphora with variation, and the second failure is worse than the first: the waves merely wash the name away, the tide makes his labour its prey.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hand, n.1'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  4. made my paynes his pray l. 4

    'Paynes' means pains in the sense of efforts or labours (as in 'take pains'), not suffering; 'pray' is prey or booty. The tide is gendered 'his', a personification that links it to the devouring Time of classical and Renaissance poetry (tempus edax rerum, 'time, devourer of things', Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.234). Spenser's archaic spelling (paynes, pray, tyde) is typical of his deliberately old-fashioned orthography, which recalls Chaucer; modernised editions print 'pains' and 'prey'. The tides were also a commonplace emblem of mutability, a theme Spenser treats at length in the Mutabilitie Cantos of The Faerie Queene.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.234 ('tempus edax rerum'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pain, n.1'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  5. Vayne man ... in vaine assay l. 5

    The sonnet is unusual in giving the beloved a voice, and she speaks with gentle irony. 'Vayne' first means foolish or presumptuous (he would immortalise a mortal), then, in 'in vaine', futile; Spenser uses two spellings, vayne and vaine, for the same sound. 'Assay', from Old French essai, means to try or attempt. Her objection is theological as well as practical: a mortal creature cannot be made immortal by human effort. Her humility also answers the Petrarchan convention of the proud, disdainful mistress, which Spenser's sequence repeatedly revises.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assay, v.'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), Amoretti LXXV, text and notes

  6. A mortall thing so to immortalize l. 6

    The line sets mortall against immortalize, a play of sound that contains the whole debate. The claim that verse outlasts monuments goes back to Horace (Odes 3.30, a monument 'more lasting than bronze') and to Ovid's epilogue to the Metamorphoses (15.871-879), where the poet says his name will live wherever Rome rules. Renaissance sonneteers made the topos their own; Shakespeare's Sonnets 18, 55 and 65 are the best-known English examples. Spenser's sonnet stands out because it first lets the beloved deny the boast, and because its final answer moves from earthly fame to heaven.

    Horace, Odes 3.30 ('Exegi monumentum aere perennius'); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.871-879 (the epilogue 'Iamque opus exegi'); William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  7. eke l. 8

    'Eke' (Old English eac, related to German auch) was common in Middle English and in Chaucer, Spenser's acknowledged master. By Spenser's time it was antique, and Ben Jonson complained that Spenser, 'in affecting the ancients, writ no language'. Here the old word suits the lady's sober prediction: not only she but also her name will be wiped out. Note the rhyme lykewize / immortalize / devize / eternize, which carries one sound through lines 6, 8, 9 and 11 and binds the second and third quatrains together, as Spenser's interlaced scheme requires.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eke, adv.'; Ben Jonson, Timber, or Discoveries (1641), on Spenser's language

  8. quod I l. 9

    'Quod' is a Middle English form of 'quoth' (Old English cwaeth), common in Chaucer ('quod he'). It marks the turn from her speech to his, and the reply opens with a flat contradiction, 'Not so'. The argument is a dialogue: two quatrains of narrative and objection, then the lover's rebuttal running from line 9 to the end. Placing the turn at line 9 recalls the Italian volta even within Spenser's English form.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quoth, v.'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  9. let baser things devize / To dy in dust ll. 9-10

    'Devize' means to plan or contrive; the idea that base things 'devise' their own death is a slightly ironic personification. 'To dy in dust' recalls Genesis 3:19, 'for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return', the sentence on fallen humanity. The lover concedes mortality for 'baser things' while claiming an exemption for his lady through 'fame' (l. 10), the classical reward of virtue. Many readers find a tension here between pagan fame and Christian hope, which the closing lines resolve by moving the name from earth to heaven.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 3:19; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'devise, v.'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  10. eternize l. 11

    'Eternize' entered English in the later sixteenth century and was much used by sonneteers for the immortalising power of poetry. Spenser specifies what will be preserved: not her beauty, the usual subject, but her 'vertues rare', in keeping with the moral and Neoplatonic emphasis of the sequence, in which the lady's inner virtue is what the lover finally serves. The word answers the lady's 'immortalize' in line 6 with a near synonym, turning her objection into his promise.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eternize, v.'; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), Amoretti LXXV, text and notes

  11. in the hevens wryte your glorious name l. 12

    The line rises from sand to sky. It suggests stellification, the classical honour of being set among the stars, and also Christ's words in Luke 10:20, 'rejoice, because your names are written in heaven', and the 'book of life' of Revelation (20:12). The name written on the strand and the name written in heaven are the two halves of the poem's argument: human writing perishes, writing in heaven endures. Readers differ on how far the poet still claims the credit (his verse does the writing) and how far the close hands the work over to divine grace.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 10:20; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 20:12; Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), note on Sonnet 75

  12. Where, when as l. 13

    'Where' refers back to 'the hevens' of line 12, and 'when as' simply means 'when'. 'Subdew' (subdue) gives Death the role of a conqueror, a figure from biblical and medieval tradition (compare 1 Corinthians 15:26, 'The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death'). The couplet is indented in the archive text, as in many editions, marking it off as the conclusion, though in Spenser's interlaced scheme it is less of a detachable epigram than the Shakespearean couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'whenas, adv.'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:26; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75

  13. Our love shall live, and later life renew l. 14

    The final line allows two readings. In the first, 'later life' means later generations, whom the love recorded in his verse will inspire and refresh, a secular fame like that of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18. In the second, the line points beyond the end of 'all the world' to the life of the resurrection, a Christian renewal; this fits Larsen's liturgical reading of the sequence and its movement towards marriage in Epithalamion. Note too that the subject has shifted from 'your name' to 'Our love': the poem ends not with the lady's fame but with mutual love, which many readers see as Spenser's quiet revision of one-sided Petrarchan devotion.

    Kenneth J. Larsen, ed., Edmund Spenser's Amoretti and Epithalamion: A Critical Edition (Medieval & Renaissance Texts & Studies, 1997), note on Sonnet 75; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75; Wikipedia, 'Amoretti'

  14. Spenserian sonnet whole poem

    The Spenserian sonnet links each quatrain to the next by a shared rhyme (here strand / hand; away / pray / assay / decay; immortalize / lykewize / devize / eternize; fame / name; subdew / renew). It uses fewer rhyme sounds than the Shakespearean form and moves more continuously, like the chained rhymes of the Faerie Queene stanza. In this poem the form matches the argument: narrative and the lady's speech fill the first eight lines, the lover's 'Not so' turns the poem at line 9, and the couplet gives the final promise. Line 2 shows Spenser's metrical freedom: 'washed' is probably to be read as two syllables to fill the pentameter.

    William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (Yale University Press, 1989), notes on Amoretti 75; Paul Fussell, Poetic Meter and Poetic Form (rev. ed., 1979), on the Spenserian sonnet; Wikipedia, 'Amoretti'
