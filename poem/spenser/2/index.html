Love

Prothalamion

by Edmund Spenser 1552–1599

Love

Calme was the day, and through the trembling ayre
Sweete-breathing Zephyrus did softly play
A gentle spirit, that lightly did delay
Hot Titans beames, which then did glyster fayre;
When I, (whom sullein care,
Through discontent of my long fruitlesse stay
In Princes Court, and expectation vayne
Of idle hopes, which still doe fly away,
Like empty shaddowes, did afflict my brayne,)
Walkt forth to ease my payne
Along the shoare of silver streaming Themmes;
Whose rutty Bancke, the which his River hemmes,
Was paynted all with variable flowers,
And all the meades adornd with daintie gemmes
Fit to decke maydens bowres,
And crowne their Paramours
Against the Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

There, in a Meadow, by the Rivers side,
A Flocke of Nymphes I chauncèd to espy,
All lovely Daughters of the Flood thereby,
With goodly greenish locks, all loose untyde,
As each had bene a Bryde;
And each one had a little wicker basket,
Made of fine twigs, entraylèd curiously,
In which they gathered flowers to fill their flasket,
And with fine Fingers cropt full feateously
The tender stalkes on hye.
Of every sort, which in that Meadow grew,
They gathered some; the Violet, pallid blew,

The little Dazie, that at evening closes,
The virgin Lillie, and the Primrose trew,
With store of vermeil Roses,
To decke their Bridegromes posies
Against the Brydale day, which was not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

With that I saw two Swannes of goodly hewe
Come softly swimming downe along the Lee;
Two fairer Birds I yet did never see;
The snow, which doth the top of Pindus strew,
Did never whiter shew;
Nor Jove himselfe, when he a Swan would be,
For love of Leda, whiter did appeare;
Yet Leda was (they say) as white as he,
Yet not so white as these, nor nothing neare;
So purely white they were,
That even the gentle streame, the which them bare,
Seem’d foule to them, and bad his billowes spare
To wet their silken feathers, least they might
Soyle their fayre plumes with water not so fayre,
And marre their beauties bright,
That shone as heavens light,
Against their Brydale day, which was not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.
Eftsoones the Nymphes, which now had Flowers their fill,
Ran all in haste to see that silver brood,
As they came floating on the Christal Flood;
Whom when they sawe, they stood amazèd still,
Their wondring eyes to fill;
Them seem’d they never saw a sight so fayre,
Of Fowles, so lovely, that they sure did deeme
Them heavenly borne, or to be that same payre
Which through the Skie draw Venus silver Teeme;
For sure they did not seeme
To be begot of any earthly Seede,
But rather Angels, or of Angels breede;
Yet were they bred of Somers-heat, they say,
In sweetest Season, when each Flower and weede
The earth did fresh aray;
So fresh they seem’d as day,
Even as their Brydale day, which was not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

Then forth they all out of their baskets drew
Great store of Flowers, the honour of the field,
That to the sense did fragrant odours yield,
All which upon those goodly Birds they threw
And all the Waves did strew,
That like old Peneus Waters they did seeme,
When downe along by pleasant Tempes shore,
Scattred with Flowres, through Thessaly they streeme,
That they appeare, through Lillies plenteous store,
Like a Brydes Chamber flore.
Two of those Nymphes, meane while, two Garlands bound
Of freshest Flowres which in that Mead they found,
The which presenting all in trim Array,
Their snowie Foreheads therewithall they crownd,
Whil’st one did sing this Lay,
Prepared against that Day,
Against their Brydale day, which was not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

‘Ye gentle Birdes! the worlds faire ornament,
And heavens glorie, whom this happie hower
Doth leade unto your lovers blisfull bower,
Joy may you have, and gentle hearts content
Of your loves couplement;
And let faire Venus, that is Queene of love,
With her heart-quelling Sonne upon you smile,
Whose smile, they say, hath vertue to remove
All Loves dislike, and friendships faultie guile
For ever to assoile.
Let endlesse Peace your steadfast hearts accord,
And blessèd Plentie wait upon your bord;
And let your bed with pleasures chast abound,
That fruitfull issue may to you afford,
Which may your foes confound,
And make your joyes redound
Upon your Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softlie, till I end my Song.’

So ended she; and all the rest around
To her redoubled that her undersong,
Which said their brydale daye should not be long:
And gentle Eccho from the neighbour ground
Their accents did resound.
So forth those joyous Birdes did passe along,
Adowne the Lee, that to them murmurde low,
As he would speake, but that he lackt a tong,
Yet did by signes his glad affection show,
Making his streame run slow.
And all the foule which in his flood did dwell
Gan flock about these twaine, that did excell
The rest, so far as Cynthia doth shend
The lesser starres. So they, enrangèd well,
Did on those two attend,
And their best service lend
Against their wedding day, which was not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

At length they all to mery London came,
To mery London, my most kyndly Nurse,
That to me gave this Lifes first native sourse,
Though from another place I take my name,
An house of auncient fame:
There when they came, whereas those bricky towres
The which on Themmes brode agèd backe doe ryde,
Where now the studious Lawyers have their bowers,
There whylome wont the Templer Knights to byde,
Till they decayd through pride:
Next whereunto there standes a stately place,
Where oft I gaynèd giftes and goodly grace
Of that great Lord, which therein wont to dwell,
Whose want too well now feeles my freendles case;
But ah! here fits not well
Olde woes, but joyes, to tell
Against the Brydale daye, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

Yet therein now doth lodge a noble Peer,
Great Englands glory, and the Worlds wide wonder,
Whose dreadfull name late through all Spaine did thunder,
And Hercules two pillors standing neere
Did make to quake and feare:
Faire branch of Honor, flower of Chevalrie!
That fillest England with thy triumphes fame,
Joy have thou of thy noble victorie,
And endlesse happinesse of thine owne name
That promiseth the same;
That through thy prowesse, and victorious armes,
Thy country may be freed from forraine harmes;
And great Elisaes glorious name may ring
Through al the world, fil’d with thy wide Alarmes,
Which some brave muse may sing
To ages following,
Upon the Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly till I end my Song.

From those high Towers this noble Lord issuing,
Like Radiant Hesper, when his golden hayre
In th’ Ocean billowes he hath bathèd fayre,
Descended to the Rivers open vewing,
With a great traine ensuing.
Above the rest were goodly to bee seene
Two gentle Knights of lovely face and feature,
Beseeming well the bower of anie Queene,
With gifts of wit, and ornaments of nature,
Fit for so goodly stature,
That like the twins of Jove they seem’d in sight,
Which decke the Bauldricke of the Heavens bright;
They two, forth pacing to the Rivers side,
Received those two faire Brides, their Loves delight;
Which, at th’ appointed tyde,
Each one did make his Bryde
Against their Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes! runne softly, till I end my Song.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Prothalamion: title and occasion l. 1

    'Prothalamion' appears to be Spenser's coinage, formed on the model of 'epithalamion' (song at the bridal chamber) with Greek pro, 'before'; it is a song preceding the wedding. The full title calls it a 'Spousall Verse' in honour of the double marriage of Lady Elizabeth and Lady Katherine Somerset, daughters of Edward Somerset, 4th Earl of Worcester, to Henry Guildford and William Petre. The weddings took place at Essex House on 8 November 1596; the poem, printed by William Ponsonby in 1596, imagines the brides' journey down the Thames to the house of the Earl of Essex, its probable patron.

    'Prothalamion', Wikipedia (occasion, couples, Eliot); Luminarium Encyclopedia, 'Edward Somerset, 4th Earl of Worcester'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Prothalamion', notes; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  2. Zephyrus ... Titans beames l. 4

    Zephyrus is the west wind, bringer of spring in classical poetry. 'Titan' is an epithet of the sun (Sol or Helios, son of the Titan Hyperion) used by Ovid and Virgil. The wind 'delays' (tempers, allays) the heat. The opening thus sets an idealised summer day, although the actual wedding was in November; critics note that the poem's season is symbolic, matching the later pun on 'Somers-heat'. 'Glyster' means glitter or shine.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  3. sullein care ... long fruitlesse stay in Princes Court l. 5

    Spenser came to England from Ireland in 1595-6, partly to see the second instalment of The Faerie Queene through the press. These lines express bitterness at his 'long fruitlesse stay' at court and 'expectation vayne' of reward, a complaint he also makes in Colin Clouts Come Home Againe and Mother Hubberds Tale. Critics have often noted that this personal grievance, unusual in a marriage poem, gives Prothalamion its elegiac undertone. 'Sullein' means gloomy, sullen; the image of hopes flying 'like empty shaddowes' recalls classical descriptions of the shades of the dead.

    Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  4. Sweete Themmes! runne softly l. 18

    Each of the ten eighteen-line stanzas ends with a refrain addressing the Thames, preceded by a line about the bridal day 'which is not long' (that is, not far off). The tense and wording shift slightly ('was not long', 'should not be long'), and editors have noted the ambiguity of 'not long', which can also suggest the brevity of joy. The river is personified as a gentle male deity whose flow accompanies and measures the song. T. S. Eliot quotes the line in 'The Fire Sermon' of The Waste Land (1922, l. 176), setting Spenser's idealised river against the litter of the modern Thames.

    T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land (1922), l. 176 (Project Gutenberg); 'Prothalamion', Wikipedia (occasion, couples, Eliot); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  5. rutty l. 12

    Editors usually gloss 'rutty' as 'rooty', covered with tree and plant roots, which suits a river bank; the OED records a separate sense of rutted. The bank 'hemmes' (borders) the river and is 'paynted' with 'variable' (varied, many-coloured) flowers. The 'daintie gemmes' of the meadows are flowers, compared to jewels fit to adorn maidens' bowers and to crown their 'Paramours' (lovers, a word without the later suggestion of illicit love).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  6. A Flocke of Nymphes ... Daughters of the Flood l. 21

    The nymphs are naiads, daughters of the river god, a classical motif Spenser had used at length in the marriage of Thames and Medway (The Faerie Queene IV.xi). Their 'greenish locks' are the colour of river weed; loose, untied hair was the sign of a bride in English custom, hence 'As each had bene a Bryde'. The scene of maidens gathering flowers in a meadow has classical antecedents in Proserpina's flower-gathering (Ovid, Metamorphoses 5) and in the pastoral tradition.

    Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited; Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  7. entraylèd curiously ... flasket ... feateously l. 25

    'Entrailed' means interlaced or entwined, from Old French entreillier (to trellis), and 'curiously' means with careful, intricate art. A 'flasket' is a long shallow basket. 'Feateously' means deftly, neatly (from 'feat', skilful). Spenser's archaic diction, much criticised by Ben Jonson for 'writing no language', is deliberate here: it gives the scene the texture of old English pastoral, the manner of The Shepheardes Calender.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  8. Violet ... Dazie ... Lillie ... vermeil Roses l. 33

    'Vermeil' is vermilion, bright red. The flowers carry conventional associations: the lily of virginity, the primrose 'trew' (perhaps of fidelity), the rose of love, and the daisy, 'day's eye', which closes at evening. Spenser had produced a famous flower catalogue in the April eclogue of The Shepheardes Calender. The flowers are gathered for the 'Bridegromes posies', bunches or nosegays, and later strewn on the swans' path like a bride's chamber floor.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  9. two Swannes l. 37

    The brides are figured as two swans. The Thames swan was a familiar sight and protected by royal right; swans were emblems of purity, of Venus (whose chariot they draw) and of poets, since they were thought to sing before death. The allegory allows Spenser to stage a river pageant like the water processions of Elizabethan London without describing the actual brides, who probably made no such journey. The stanza's hyperboles of whiteness, outdoing the snows of Pindus and Jove's swan-disguise, prepare for the pun on their family name in stanza 4.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Prothalamion', notes

  10. Pindus l. 40

    Pindus is the great mountain range of northern Greece, separating Thessaly from Epirus, and was associated with the Muses and Apollo. Its snow-capped summits provide a classical standard of whiteness which the swans surpass. The comparison typifies the poem's hyperbolic structure, in which each image is introduced only to be outdone ('Yet not so white as these, nor nothing neare').

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  11. Jove ... Leda l. 43

    Jupiter took the form of a swan to approach Leda, wife of Tyndareus; from their union came Helen and the twins Castor and Pollux (Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.109). The swans of the poem are whiter even than Jove in disguise. The myth is erotic and even violent, and some critics see a sly hint of sexuality beneath the poem's purity; others stress that the allusion prepares for the closing comparison of the bridegrooms to 'the twins of Jove', Leda's sons.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  12. Venus silver Teeme l. 63

    In Ovid's account Venus drives a chariot drawn by swans (Metamorphoses 10.708 and 717-18, where she yokes her swans to fly to Cyprus). The nymphs think the birds are 'heavenly borne', or the very pair that draws the goddess of love, and even 'Angels'. The rapid climb from classical to Christian superlative is typical of Spenser's syncretism. The stanza then corrects itself: they were in fact 'bred of Somers-heat'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  13. Somers-heat l. 67

    The compound 'Somers-heat' puns on Somerset, the surname of the Earls of Worcester. The pun explains the otherwise strange summer setting of a November wedding: the swans are born of summer's heat because they are daughters of the house of Somerset. Such name-play was a standard compliment in Elizabethan occasional verse, and Spenser repeats it with Essex's name later in the poem.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Prothalamion', notes

  14. Peneus ... Tempe ... Thessaly l. 78

    The Peneus flows through the vale of Tempe in Thessaly, celebrated in ancient literature for its beauty; in Ovid the river god Peneus is the father of Daphne. The Thames, strewn with flowers, is compared to this classical landscape, and then, more domestically, to 'a Brydes Chamber flore', alluding to the English custom of strewing the bridal chamber with flowers and rushes. Spenser also places Tempe in the Epithalamion (Maia lying in Tempe).

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  15. couplement ... heart-quelling Sonne ... assoile l. 95

    The nymph's song is a miniature epithalamium. 'Couplement' (union in pairs) is a rare word; Cupid is 'heart-quelling' because he subdues hearts; his smile has 'vertue' (power) to remove dislike and 'faultie guile', and to 'assoile' them, a word meaning to absolve or to dispel. The blessings asked, peace, plenty and fruitful issue that will 'confound' the families' foes, are the standard aims of the marriage hymn and echo the prayers of the Epithalamion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  16. undersong l. 110

    An 'undersong' is the burden or refrain of a song, sung beneath or after the main voice. The other nymphs 'redoubled' the singer's refrain, and Echo resounds it from the 'neighbour ground', recalling the echoing refrain of the Epithalamion. The river Lee, unable to speak, shows its 'glad affection' by slowing its stream, a fine instance of the poem's courteous personification of landscape.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  17. the Lee ll. 38, 114-16

    The Lea joins the Thames at Bow, east of the City, so a journey 'downe along the Lee' to the Temple would be geographically awkward. Some editors therefore take 'Lee' as a general word for a river or stream (or a meadow, 'lea') and refer it to the Thames; others accept a loose allusion to the Lea, which Spenser includes among the rivers of the Thames-Medway marriage canto (The Faerie Queene IV.xi). The personified Lee that 'murmurde low' and 'lackt a tong' (ll. 114-16, where the swans pass 'Adowne the Lee') is in either case the water on which the swans travel.

    The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; Spenser, The Faerie Queene, ed. A. C. Hamilton (2nd edn, Harlow: Longman, 2001), note to the passage cited

  18. as Cynthia doth shend / The lesser starres l. 121

    'Shend' means to put to shame, disgrace, or (as here) to outshine. Cynthia is the moon and, in Elizabethan poetry, often a name for Queen Elizabeth herself (Ralegh's 'Cynthia', Spenser's own Colin Clouts Come Home Againe). The simile of the moon among the stars is classical (Horace, Odes 1.12.46-8, of the Julian star 'velut inter ignis luna minores'). 'Enrangèd' means set in ranks, arranged in order.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; Horace, Odes 1.12 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text)

  19. mery London, my most kyndly Nurse l. 128

    Spenser was born in London about 1552 and educated at Merchant Taylors' School. 'Kyndly' means natural, by birth (from 'kind', nature) as well as affectionate. 'Though from another place I take my name, / An house of auncient fame' is his claim of kinship with the Spencers of Althorp in Northamptonshire, whose daughters he praised in Colin Clouts Come Home Againe and to whom he dedicated several poems. The genealogical link is not documented, but the Spencers apparently accepted the compliment.

    Spenser Online (University of Cambridge), 'Biography'; Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line

  20. bricky towres ... the Templer Knights l. 135

    The Temple, between Fleet Street and the Thames, was the London headquarters of the Knights Templar until the order was suppressed in 1312, partly on charges of corruption and pride ('Till they decayd through pride'). It later passed to the lawyers of the Inner and Middle Temple, two of the Inns of Court, the 'studious Lawyers' and their 'bowers' (chambers). 'Whylome' means formerly. The moral aside on the Templars' fall is one of several shadows the poem casts across its celebration.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Prothalamion', notes

  21. a stately place ... that great Lord l. 137

    Next to the Temple stood Leicester House, the London residence of Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, Queen Elizabeth's favourite, in whose household Spenser served around 1579-80 and from whom he 'gaynèd giftes and goodly grace'. After Leicester's death in 1588 the house passed to his stepson Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex, and was renamed Essex House; the Somerset weddings took place there. Spenser's lament for his 'freendles case' after Leicester's death is cut short: 'here fits not well / Olde woes, but joyes, to tell'.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Luminarium Encyclopedia, 'Edward Somerset, 4th Earl of Worcester'

  22. a noble Peer ... Hercules two pillors l. 145

    The 'noble Peer' is the Earl of Essex, who in June 1596 led, with Lord Howard of Effingham, the expedition that sacked Cadiz. 'Hercules two pillors' are the promontories at the Straits of Gibraltar (Calpe and Abyla), which Hercules was said to have set up at the edge of the known world; Cadiz lies just beyond them. Spenser praises Essex as 'flower of Chevalrie' and prays for his further victories, a political compliment that later readers find poignant, since Essex rebelled and was executed in 1601. Some critics see the passage as Spenser's bid for a new patron.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Prothalamion', notes

  23. happinesse of thine owne name l. 153

    The phrase 'endlesse happinesse of thine owne name / That promiseth the same' is generally taken to pun on Devereux, the Earl's surname, as if from French 'heureux', happy (or 'devenir heureux'). As with 'Somers-heat', the compliment works by finding a prophecy in a name. The prayer that 'great Elisaes glorious name may ring / Through al the world' places Essex's glory under the Queen's, a necessary courtesy given Elizabeth's suspicion of her favourite's popularity.

    Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A. Oram et al., eds., The Yale Edition of the Shorter Poems of Edmund Spenser (New Haven: Yale UP, 1989), note to the line; The Works of Edmund Spenser: A Variorum Edition, The Minor Poems, vol. 2, ed. C. G. Osgood et al. (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1947), commentary on the line

  24. Radiant Hesper l. 164

    Hesper (Hesperus) is the evening star, the planet Venus. Classical poets imagined the stars bathing in Ocean before rising (Virgil, Aeneid 8.589-91, of Lucifer the morning star wet with Ocean's wave). The image presents Essex as glorious and as leading a train, as the evening star leads the other stars. Since Hesperus was traditionally invoked at weddings (as in Catullus 62, 'Vesper adest'), the comparison is also apt to the marriage theme.

    Virgil, Aeneid 8.589-91 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); Catullus, Carmen 62 (Perseus Digital Library, Latin text); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line

  25. the twins of Jove ... the Bauldricke of the Heavens l. 173

    Castor and Pollux, the Dioscuri, were sons of Leda (Pollux by Jove), and after death became the constellation Gemini. A baldric is a belt worn diagonally across the body, and the 'Bauldricke of the Heavens' is the zodiac, the band of constellations crossing the sky, in which Gemini is one sign. The comparison neatly closes the swan imagery: the brides were whiter than Jove's swan, and the grooms are the swan's twin sons. The poem ends with the grooms, Henry Guildford and William Petre, receiving their brides 'at th' appointed tyde'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); Richard A. McCabe, ed., Edmund Spenser: The Shorter Poems (London: Penguin, 1999), note to the line; William A.
