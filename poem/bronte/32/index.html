Religion

Last Lines

by Emily Brontë 1818–1848

Religion

No coward soul is mine,
No trembler in the world's storm-troubled sphere:
I see Heaven's glories shine,
And faith shines equal, arming me from fear.

O God, within my breast,
Almighty, ever-present Deity!
Life--that in me has rest,
As I--undying Life--have power in Thee.

Vain are the thousand creeds
That move men's hearts: unutterably vain;
Worthless as withered weeds,
Or idlest froth amid the boundless main,

To waken doubt in one
Holding so fast to Thine infinity;
So surely anchored on
The steadfast rock of immortality,

With wide-embracing love
Thy spirit animates eternal years,
Pervades and broods above,
Changes, sustains, dissolves, creates, and rears.

Though earth and man were gone,
And suns and universes ceased to be,
And Thou were left alone,
Every existence would exist in Thee.

There is not room for Death,
Nor atom that his might could render void:
Thou--Thou art Being and Breath,
And what Thou art may never be destroyed.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and date: 'Last Lines' l. 1

    The poem has no title in Emily Brontë's notebook, where it is dated 2 January 1846, almost three years before her death in December 1848. Charlotte Brontë first printed it in the 1850 edition (December 1850) of Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey, among selections from 'Ellis Bell', with a note saying these were the last lines her sister ever wrote; hence the archive's title 'Last Lines'. Editors note that other poems carry later dates, so Charlotte's claim is better read as a piece of family myth-making: it frames the poem as a deathbed testament of fearless faith. Charlotte also made small changes of her own (the notebook carries her pencil revisions), so her printed text differs in detail from Emily's manuscript; the Hatfield and Gezari editions are the ones to consult for Emily's own readings. Read the poem as a statement of belief written in mid-career, the same year the sisters' joint Poems appeared, rather than as a literal final utterance.

    Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'; C. W. Hatfield, ed., The Complete Poems of Emily Jane Brontë (Columbia UP, 1941), text and notes; Morgan Library & Museum, Charlotte Brontë's autograph transcript of the opening stanza, with her note

  2. Stanza and metre ll. 1-4

    Each of the seven stanzas sets a short iambic trimeter line ('No coward soul is mine', indented in the archive) against a full pentameter, rhyming abab. The pattern suits the poem's argument: the brief lines make compact declarations or address ('O God, within my breast'), and the long lines open out into the cosmic scale of 'the world's storm-troubled sphere' or 'the boundless main'. The opening double negative, 'No coward ... No trembler', defines the speaker by what she refuses to be before saying what she sees. The form is close to the hymn stanzas Brontë knew from church and chapel, which helps explain the poem's later use at funerals, yet its theology, as later stanzas show, departs from any creed.

    Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'; C. W. Hatfield, ed., The Complete Poems of Emily Jane Brontë (Columbia UP, 1941), text and notes; Representative Poetry Online, University of Toronto, editorial apparatus on Brontë

  3. storm-troubled sphere l. 2

    'Sphere' here means the earth or the world of human experience, with an echo of the older cosmology in which each planet moved in its own sphere. The compound 'storm-troubled' makes the whole globe a place of weather and danger; storms were Brontë's habitual figure for both the moorland climate and spiritual struggle, as in Wuthering Heights. The phrase sets up the poem's basic contrast between a turbulent, changing world and the steady inward vision of stanza 1, line 3. The later sea images ('froth amid the boundless main', 'anchored') continue the storm motif.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sphere'; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  4. faith ... arming me from fear l. 4

    The metaphor of faith as armour recalls Ephesians 6:13-17, where the Christian is told to put on 'the whole armour of God' and to take 'the shield of faith'. 'Arming me from fear' uses 'from' in the older sense of protection against, so faith defends the speaker from fear as armour defends from blows. The phrase 'shines equal' is striking: faith is said to shine as brightly as the 'glories' of Heaven themselves, so that inward conviction and outward vision are of equal strength. The military language answers the opening denial of cowardice.

    Bible (KJV), Ephesians 6:16; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  5. God within the breast l. 5

    The apostrophe locates the 'Almighty, ever-present Deity' within the speaker's breast. This inward, immediate relation to God is the poem's centre, and critics have connected it with Brontë's visionary poems such as 'The Prisoner' (Julian M. and A. G. Rochelle), where liberation comes through private ecstasy. Some readers call the poem mystical, others pantheist or panentheist, since God here both indwells the self and pervades the universe (stanzas 5-6). Scholars disagree about how far it remains Christian: it echoes biblical language throughout, yet it dismisses all creeds and never mentions Christ, church or sin. Gezari reads Brontë's last poems as a sustained thinking-through of death and the self rather than conventional piety.

    Janet Gezari, Last Things: Emily Brontë's Poems (Oxford UP, 2007); Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'; Stevie Davies, Emily Brontë: Heretic (Women's Press, 1994)

  6. Life ... undying Life ll. 7-8

    Lines 7-8 are the poem's knottiest syntax. One reading takes 'Life' as an address to God (God is Life itself, which rests in the speaker), while the speaker in turn, 'undying Life', draws power from God: a mutual indwelling. The crossed structure (Life in me / I in Thee) recalls Christ's words 'Abide in me, and I in you' (John 15:4) and 'I am the way, the truth, and the life' (John 14:6). Because the speaker calls herself 'undying Life', the lines can also be read as claiming that the soul shares God's own immortality, a bolder claim than orthodox doctrine allows. The punctuation of the line, with its dashes in the archive text, affects how the apposition is heard and may differ from the manuscript's; consult a manuscript-based edition such as Hatfield or Gezari.

    Bible (KJV), John 15:4; Bible (KJV), John 14:6; C. W. Hatfield, ed., The Complete Poems of Emily Jane Brontë (Columbia UP, 1941), text and notes; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  7. the thousand creeds ll. 9-10

    A 'creed' (Latin credo, 'I believe') is a formal statement of doctrine. The Brontës grew up amid fierce religious controversy: their father was an evangelical Anglican clergyman, their aunt Branwell a Methodist, and the Calvinist doctrine of predestination troubled the household, especially Anne. Brontë's 'thousand creeds' rejects the whole competition of sects as 'unutterably vain' (empty, futile, as in Ecclesiastes 1:2, 'vanity of vanities'). Note that the creeds are vain specifically 'To waken doubt' in the speaker (l. 13): the claim is less that every creed is false than that none can touch a faith grounded in direct experience. This is why the poem has been read both as a hymn of faith and as heterodox.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:2; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'; Juliet Barker, The Brontës (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1994)

  8. idlest froth ... the boundless main l. 12

    'The main' is a poetic word for the open sea (from 'main sea', the great or principal sea). The creeds are compared first to 'withered weeds', dead and useless, then to foam, the most transient thing on the vast ocean. 'Idlest' means most useless or ineffectual. The image anticipates the anchor and rock of stanza 4: the sea of opinion is boundless and shifting, while the soul is fixed. The two similes move from land to sea, from decay to evanescence, enlarging the scale as the long line does.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'main'; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  9. anchored on / The steadfast rock ll. 15-16

    The words 'surely', 'anchored' and 'steadfast' gather together Hebrews 6:19: 'Which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast'. The 'rock' recalls the Psalms ('The LORD is my rock', Psalm 18:2) and Christ's parable of the wise man who built his house upon a rock (Matthew 7:24-25). Brontë relocates the image: the rock is not Christ or the Church but 'immortality' itself. The nautical figure completes the sea imagery of the previous stanza: amid the 'boundless main' of creeds, the soul rides safely at anchor.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 6:19; Bible (KJV), Psalms 18:2; Bible (KJV), Matthew 7:24

  10. broods above l. 19

    Genesis 1:2 says 'the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters'; the Hebrew verb suggests a bird hovering over its nest, and Milton made the image famous when he invoked the Spirit that 'Dove-like satst brooding on the vast Abyss' (Paradise Lost 1.21). Brontë's Spirit both 'pervades' (spreads through everything, from within) and 'broods above' (hovers over it, from outside): the line holds together immanence and transcendence. 'Animates' in line 18 (Latin anima, soul, breath) prepares for 'Breath' in the final stanza.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:2; John Milton, Paradise Lost 1.19-22 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  11. The five verbs l. 20

    The line strings five verbs together, nearly without conjunctions, to describe the Spirit's work. Its order matters: 'dissolves' comes before 'creates', so dissolution belongs inside the divine life rather than opposing it. 'Rears' means raises up or nurtures. The vision of a single spirit that animates, changes and renews all things has been compared to Romantic nature-philosophy, such as Wordsworth's 'motion and a spirit' that 'rolls through all things' in 'Tintern Abbey', and to Shelley's 'Spirit' of change, though Brontë makes the force personal and addresses it as 'Thou'. The packed monosyllabic and disyllabic stresses slow the pentameter down, enacting the weight of the process.

    Janet Gezari, Last Things: Emily Brontë's Poems (Oxford UP, 2007); William Wordsworth, 'Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey', ll. 100-102 (Wikisource)

  12. Thou were left alone l. 23

    Stanza 6 imagines the end of everything: earth, humanity, 'suns and universes'. 'Were gone', 'ceased' and 'were left' are subjunctives of supposition: 'Though earth and man should vanish ... and only Thou remained'. With 'Thou', older English would normally use 'wert', the form Charlotte's 1850 text prints; the archive's text reads 'were', so check it against a manuscript-based edition such as Hatfield or Gezari. The scenario recalls apocalyptic scripture ('the heavens shall pass away', 2 Peter 3:10), but Brontë draws a metaphysical rather than a judicial conclusion: nothing would really be lost, because all being already exists in God.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:10; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'were'; C. W. Hatfield, ed., The Complete Poems of Emily Jane Brontë (Columbia UP, 1941), text and notes

  13. Every existence would exist in Thee l. 24

    The line, with its deliberate repetition 'existence ... exist', claims that every created thing has its true being in God. It echoes St Paul at Athens: 'in him we live, and move, and have our being' (Acts 17:28). The thought is often called panentheist (all things in God) rather than pantheist (all things are God), since God remains distinct as 'Thou'. It provides the logical ground for the final stanza: if all existence is in God, death can destroy nothing. Critics differ on whether individual persons survive in this scheme or are absorbed into the divine; the poem does not say.

    Bible (KJV), Acts 17:28; Janet Gezari, Last Things: Emily Brontë's Poems (Oxford UP, 2007); Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  14. There is not room for Death l. 25

    The final stanza draws the conclusion. Since God is everything that is, there is no empty space for Death, nor a single 'atom' that Death's power could 'render void' (annihilate). Paul's triumph over death in 1 Corinthians 15 ('The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death', 15:26; 'O death, where is thy sting?', 15:55) is the obvious model, but Brontë replaces resurrection with an argument from plenitude. The word 'atom' gives the claim a physical edge, perhaps recalling the language of contemporary science and of ancient atomism, in which matter was indestructible. Compare Donne's 'Death, be not proud', which ends 'Death, thou shalt die'.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:26; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:55; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  15. Being and Breath l. 27

    The alliterating pair names God by two of the deepest biblical images. 'Being' recalls the name given at the burning bush, 'I AM THAT I AM' (Exodus 3:14), read by theologians as God's identity with existence itself. 'Breath' recalls Genesis 2:7, where God 'breathed into his nostrils the breath of life'; in Hebrew, Greek and Latin the words for breath and spirit are the same (ruach, pneuma, spiritus). The repeated 'Thou--Thou' gives the line a rare emphatic pause, and the closing line returns to the poem's opening courage: what God is cannot be destroyed, and so the speaker need not fear.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:14; Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:7; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'

  16. Afterlife: Dickinson's funeral l. 28

    The poem had a notable afterlife in America. Emily Dickinson admired the Brontës and quoted Emily Brontë in her letters, and at Dickinson's funeral in Amherst in May 1886 Thomas Wentworth Higginson read 'No coward soul is mine' (under Charlotte's title, 'Last Lines'), reportedly at her request. The choice suits both poets: each wrote an intensely private, unorthodox religious poetry that refuses the comforts of conventional creeds while keeping the language of scripture. The poem has since been read at many funerals, a use that depends on Charlotte's framing of it as a deathbed testament.

    Emily Dickinson Museum, 'The Brontës: No Coward Souls'; Emily Dickinson Museum, 'T. W. Higginson, Preceptor'; Janet Gezari, ed., Emily Jane Brontë: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1992), notes to 'No coward soul is mine'
