Concord Hymn

by Ralph Waldo Emerson 1803–1882

History & Politics

SUNG AT THE COMPLETION OF THE BATTLE
MONUMENT, JULY 4, 1837
By the rude bridge that arched the flood,
Their flag to April's breeze unfurled,
Here once the embattled farmers stood
And fired the shot heard round the world.
The foe long since in silence slept;
Alike the conqueror silent sleeps;
And Time the ruined bridge has swept
Down the dark stream which seaward creeps.
On this green bank, by this soft stream,
We set to-day a votive stone;
That memory may their deed redeem,
When, like our sires, our sons are gone.
Spirit, that made those heroes dare
To die, and leave their children free,
Bid Time and Nature gently spare
The shaft we raise to them and thee.
* * * * *
II
MAY-DAY AND OTHER PIECES
* * * * *

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: the Battle Monument, 1837 title

    The monument, a granite obelisk on the site of the fight of 19 April 1775, stands on land given by Emerson's step-grandfather, the Rev. Ezra Ripley of the Old Manse. At the town's Fourth of July celebration in 1837 the hymn was distributed in print and sung by a local choir to the tune 'Old Hundredth'. The poem was collected in Poems (1847); early printings carry differing titles and dates, and the date given here follows the archive's source. The year before, Emerson had published Nature (1836), and the closing appeal to 'Time and Nature' carries that essay's vocabulary.

    The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'; The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org)

  2. Hymn measure l. 2

    Long metre (four lines of eight syllables) is a standard metre of Protestant hymnody, and 'Old Hundredth' ('All people that on earth do dwell') is its best-known tune. Writing in that measure made the poem singable by an assembly and gave the civic occasion a religious frame. Emerson relies on plain monosyllables and end-stopped lines, and each stanza is a single sentence. The four stanzas move from the past (the battle), to the work of time, to the present ceremony, to a closing prayer.

    Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'; The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org); Norton Anthology of American Literature, vol. B, note to 'Concord Hymn'

  3. rude l. 1

    In the older sense recorded by the OED, 'rude' means roughly made or unpolished. The bridge was a simple timber structure over the Concord River. The 1775 bridge did not survive: it was taken down in the late eighteenth century (sources differ between about 1788 and 1793) after the road was rerouted, and later bridges came and went. This is why stanza 2 says Time 'has swept' it away. The bridge now standing at the site is a modern reconstruction.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rude'; Minute Man National Historical Park (US National Park Service), North Bridge and the Battle Monument; The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org)

  4. flood l. 1

    The OED records 'flood' in the sense of a river or flowing water from Old English, a sense that survives chiefly in poetry and hymns ('Jordan's flood'). The word lifts a small New England river into an epic register. The verb 'arched' makes the bridge a little triumphal arch over the water, an image the monument itself would complete. Emerson's diction thus mixes the local and the heroic from the first line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flood'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'

  5. 19 April 1775 l. 2

    British regulars marched from Boston to seize colonial military stores at Concord. After the clash on Lexington Green, militia and minutemen gathered on the hill above the North Bridge and advanced on the British guard at the bridge; the regulars fired first, and the militia returned fire on command. Two soldiers on each side were killed. It was the first time colonial forces fired in an organised body, on command, against the King's troops. Emerson's grandfather William Emerson, the minister of Concord, was at the Old Manse near the bridge.

    Minute Man National Historical Park (US National Park Service), North Bridge and the Battle Monument; The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'; The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org)

  6. embattled l. 3

    The OED gives 'embattled' in the military sense of arrayed for battle, as well as the architectural sense of fitted with battlements. Emerson's phrase joins a heroic epithet to the plainest noun, 'farmers': the militia were citizen-soldiers who left their fields. The modern sense 'beset with difficulties' came later and should not be read back into the line. The adjective also sets up the contrast of stanza 3, where the same ground is a peaceful 'green bank'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'embattled'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'

  7. 'the shot heard round the world' l. 4

    The hyperbole asserts that the Revolution began a political change felt everywhere, a view many nineteenth-century Americans held of 1775 as the opening of a democratic age. Strictly, the first shots of the day were fired at Lexington, but Concord's was the first ordered colonial volley. The first stanza is carved on the base of Daniel Chester French's Minute Man statue (1875) at the bridge, and the phrase has since been borrowed for many other 'shots', in politics and in sport.

    Minute Man National Historical Park (US National Park Service), North Bridge and the Battle Monument; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'; The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org)

  8. Reconciliation in death l. 5

    The second stanza levels the two sides: the foe 'slept', and 'Alike the conqueror silent sleeps'. The British soldiers killed at the bridge were buried near it, and by 1837 the generation that fought was nearly gone. The chiasmus (slept... silence / silent sleeps) mirrors the equality it describes. The note of reconciliation is typical of Emerson's generation, sixty years after the war.

    Minute Man National Historical Park (US National Park Service), North Bridge and the Battle Monument; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'

  9. Time sweeps the bridge away l. 7

    Time is personified, as again in the last stanza, and the bridge is literally gone. The inverted syntax ('Time the ruined bridge has swept') imitates a hymn's formal diction. The image prepares the poem's question: what memorial can survive Time, and how can a stone 'redeem' a deed already passing out of memory? The word 'ruined' is anticipatory: in the poem's logic every human structure, the monument included, is already on its way to ruin.

    The Concord Hymn, text and historical note (constitution.org); The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'

  10. The dark stream l. 8

    The Concord River is notably sluggish (Thoreau, in A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers, 1849, dwells on the gentleness of its current), so 'creeps' is accurate as well as symbolic. 'Dark stream' suggests the classical rivers of forgetting and death; the seaward movement suggests eternity. In stanza 3 the same water becomes a 'soft stream' beside a 'green bank': the dark river of time and the gentle river of the present day.

    Henry David Thoreau, A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers (1849), 'Concord River'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'

  11. votive stone l. 10

    Romans set up votive altars and tablets in thanks to a god for a vow granted. Calling the obelisk 'votive' makes the townspeople worshippers and prepares for the closing address to 'Spirit'. The word places the monument's meaning in the intention of those who raise it rather than in the stone itself. Emerson's choice of a Latin religious term for a civic memorial is typical of the hymn's blending of patriotism and piety, and it frames the act of commemoration as a pledge made by the living to the dead.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'votive'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'

  12. redeem l. 11

    The stone exists so that memory may 'redeem' the deed, buying it back from Time. The theological colour of the word (redemption from sin and death) fits the hymn form. The next line widens the scope: when 'our sons' are gone, like 'our sires', only the monument will remain to speak. The chain of generations (sires, we, sons, children) runs through the whole poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'redeem'; The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'

  13. The invocation to Spirit l. 13

    The 'Spirit' is left undefined: the spirit of liberty, the Holy Spirit of the hymn tradition, or the universal soul of Emerson's Nature (1836) and his later essay 'The Over-Soul'. The ambiguity is characteristic. The final line, 'The shaft we raise to them and thee', dedicates the obelisk both to the dead and to the Spirit, so the monument honours the principle as much as the men.

    Ralph Waldo Emerson, Collected Poems and Translations, ed. Harold Bloom and Paul Kane (Library of America, 1994), notes to 'Concord Hymn'; Ralph Waldo Emerson, Nature (1836); The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'

  14. 'leave their children free' l. 14

    The phrase defines the Revolution as an inheritance: freedom passed from fathers to children. Modern readers note that in 1837 that freedom did not extend to enslaved Americans, a contradiction Emerson himself would confront in his antislavery addresses of the 1840s and 1850s; the hymn does not raise it. The line nonetheless became one of the poem's most quoted phrases, because it compresses the idea of sacrifice for posterity that the whole monument was meant to express. 'Dare / To die' runs over the line-break, one of the hymn's few enjambments, so the daring is enacted in the syntax.

    The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 9: Poems, ed. Albert J. von Frank and Thomas Wortham (Harvard UP, 2011), 'Concord Hymn'; Len Gougeon, Virtue's Hero: Emerson, Antislavery, and Reform (U of Georgia P, 1990)

  15. shaft l. 16

    The OED records 'shaft' as the body of a column or a monumental pillar. The Concord monument is a plain granite obelisk. The closing request that 'Time and Nature gently spare' it admits that the stone, too, may one day go the way of the bridge; the poem, sung and printed, becomes the more lasting memorial, a version of Horace's boast of a monument more lasting than bronze (Odes 3.30).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shaft'; Minute Man National Historical Park (US National Park Service), North Bridge and the Battle Monument; Horace, Odes 3.30
