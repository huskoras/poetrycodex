Nature

Elhan-ı Şita

by Cenap Şahabettin 1870–1934

From Servet-i Fünûn, Kasım 1897

Nature

Bir beyaz lerze, bir dumanlı uçuş;
Eşini gâib eyleyen bir kuş
gibi kar
Geçen eyyâm-ı nevbaharı arar...
Ey kulûbun sürûd-ı şeydâsı
Ey kebûterlerin neşideleri,
O baharın bu işte ferdâsı:
Kapladı bir derin sükûta yeri
karlar
Ki hamûşâne dem-be-dem ağlar
Ey uçarken düşüp ölen kelebek
Bir beyaz rîşe-i cenâh-ı melek
gibi kar
Seni solgun hadîkalarda arar;
Sen açarken çiçekler üstünde
Ufacık bir çiçekli yelpaze,
Na'şın üstünde şimdi ey mürde
Başladı parça parça pervâze
karlar
Ki semâdan düşer, düşer ağlar!
Uçtunuz, gittiniz siz ey kuşlar;
Küçücük, ser-sefîd baykuşlar
gibi kar
Sizi dallarda lânelerde arar.
Gittiniz, gittiniz siz ey mürgân,
Şimdi boş kaldı ser-te-ser yuvalar;
Yuvalarda yetim-i bî-efgan!
Son kalan mai tüyleri kovalar
karlar
Ki havada uçar uçar ağlar!
Destinde ey semâ-yı şitâ tûde tûdedir
Berg-i semen cenâh-ı kebûter, sehâb-ı ter...
Dök ey semâ-revân-ı tabiat gunûdedir:-
Hâk-i siyâhın üstüne sâfî şükûfeler!
Her şâhsâr şimdi ne yaprak, ne bir çiçek!
Bir tûde-i zılâl ü siyeh-reng ü nâ-ümid...
Ey dest-i âsmân-ı şitâ, durma, durma, çek
Her şâhsârın üstüne bir sütre-i sefîd!
Göklerden emeller gibi rizân oluyor kar,
Her sûda hayalim gibi pûyân oluyor kar
Bir bâd-ı hamûşun per-i sâfında uyuklar
Tarzında durur bir aralık, sonra uçarlar.
Soldan sağa, sağdan sola lerzân ü girizân
Gâh uçmada tüyler gibi, gâh olmada rizân,
Karlar... bütün elhânı mezâmir-i sükûtun
Karlar... bütün ezhârı riyâz-ı melekûtun
Dök ey hâk-i siyâhın üstüne, ey dest-i semâ dök,
Ey dest-i semâ, dest-i kerem. dest-i şitâ dök:
Ezhâr-ı baharın yerine berf-i sefidi
Elhân-ı tuyûrun yerine samt-ı ümîdi!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Text from the Vikikaynak transliteration (after Cenab Şahabeddin, Bütün Şiirleri, Dergâh Yayınları, 2018), not from the 1897 periodical scan; a few evident transcription slips have been corrected.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Elhân-ı Şitâ: 'Melodies of Winter' (1897) title; l. 1

    'Elhân' is the Arabic plural of lahn, melody; 'şitâ' (Arabic shitāʾ) is winter. The poem appeared in a special issue of Servet-i Fünûn in 1313 Rumi (1897), the magazine of the Edebiyât-ı Cedîde (New Literature) group of 1896–1901. Kure Ansiklopedi notes that Istanbul newspapers used to print it on the front page when the first snow fell. Tarakçı lists it with 'Yakazât-ı Leyliyye' and 'Temâşâ-yı Leyâl' among Cenab's poems read across generations. Kaplan devotes a chapter to it in Şiir Tahlilleri 1. It is the classic example of the Servet-i Fünûn wish to make poetry a picture and a music rather than a statement.

    'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81); Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  2. lerze: a trembling l. 1

    'Lerze' (Persian larza) is a tremor, a shiver. The opening refuses to name its subject: we get a colour (beyaz), a movement (lerze) and a blur (dumanlı, smoky, misty) before the word 'kar' arrives at the end of the third line. This delay of the noun is the impressionist method critics see in the poem: Kure describes it as moving from description to image in a flow of impressions. The noun-phrases in apposition, without a main verb, are themselves a novelty in Turkish verse.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'lerze'; 'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81)

  3. The short line 'gibi kar' ll. 3, 13, 23

    'Like a bird that has lost its mate, the snow seeks the bygone days of spring.' The simile is split so that 'gibi kar' falls alone on its own short line, indented in the printed text. The device recurs in the first, third and fifth sections, and its partner 'karlar' (snows) in the second, fourth and sixth. The short lines are formally the shortened feet of the metre (feilün, . . −), but visually they look like flakes falling between the longer lines, and aurally they act as a refrain. This free mixing of long and short lines recalls the müstezat, a classical form with short added lines, which the Servet-i Fünûn poets loosened.

    'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81); Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  4. Three sections, three metres ll. 1, 31, 39

    From the text: ll. 1–30 scan in hafîf (Bir be-yaz ler / ze bir du-man / lı u-çuş: fâilâtün mefâilün feilün), with the short 'gibi kar' and 'karlar' lines as clipped feet. Line 31 begins a new metre, Des-tin-de / ey se-mâ-yı / şi-tâ tû-de / tû-de-dir, mef'ûlü fâilâtü mefâîlü fâilün, the muzâri used, as Uzun notes, for many elegies. Line 39 shifts again, Gök-ler-de / ne-mel-ler gi / bi rî-zâ-no / lu-yor kar, to the hezec-i ahreb (mef'ûlü mefâîlü mefâîlü feûlün), the bouncing metre of the şarkı. Mixing metres in one poem was one of the Servet-i Fünûn experiments; the scansions here are offered from the text and should be checked in a prosody handbook.

    Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Terciibend', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 40 (2011), pp. 487–489; Tevfik Rüştü Topuzoğlu, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 17 (1998), pp. 302–304; Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349; Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  5. kebûter, neşîde: doves and songs ll. 5–6

    'Kebûter' (Persian kabūtar) is the dove or pigeon; 'neşîde' (Arabic nashīda) is a song or hymn sung in company; 'sürûd-ı şeydâ' (Persian surūd, song; shaydā, love-mad) is the song of a love-maddened heart. The snow is spring's tomorrow, 'ferdâ' (Persian fardā): what the music of spring has come to. The paradox, a song turned into deep silence (derin sükût), is the poem's central idea: these winter 'melodies' are melodies of silence.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'neşide'; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  6. The weeping snows: 'karlar ki ... ağlar' ll. 9–10, 19–20, 29–30

    The plural 'karlar' (snows) stands alone on its short line and is completed by a relative clause in the Persian manner ('ki', who/which): the snows 'that weep silently moment by moment' (hamûşâne dem-be-dem), 'that fall and fall from the sky and weep', 'that fly and fly in the air and weep'. The verbs repeated in pairs (düşer düşer, uçar uçar) imitate the steady fall. Snow as tears is the poem's pathetic fallacy: nature mourns the lost spring, its butterflies and birds. The rhyme ağlar answers 'arar' (seeks) in the 'gibi kar' sections, so searching and weeping alternate.

    'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81); Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  7. The dead butterfly and the angel's feather ll. 11–20

    'Rîşe-i cenâh-ı melek' is 'the plume of an angel's wing': 'rîşe' (Persian rīsha, fibre, quill), 'cenâh' (Arabic janāḥ, wing), 'melek' (angel). The butterfly that once opened like a 'tiny flowered fan' over the flowers is now 'mürde' (Persian murda, dead), and the snow begins to flutter 'piece by piece' (parça parça pervâze) above its corpse (na'ş, Arabic naʿsh, bier). Snow as an angel's feather lends the burial a heavenly quality.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'cenah'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'naaş'; Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349

  8. 'Tiny white-headed owls' l. 22

    'Ser-sefîd' (Persian sar-safīd) means white-headed. The owl (baykuş) in Ottoman tradition is a bird of ruins and ill omen, haunting abandoned places; tiny white owls are thus an apt image for flakes that settle on empty nests (lâne, Persian lāna). Readers may find the comparison either brilliantly exact, since flakes do look like small soft-feathered bodies, or overstrained. Either way it shows the Servet-i Fünûn search for the unexpected, visually precise image, which Tarakçı links to Parnassian pictorialism.

    Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  9. mürgân, yetîm-i bî-efgan: the vanished birds ll. 25–28

    'Mürgân' is the Persian plural of murgh, bird. 'Ser-te-ser' (Persian sar-ta-sar) means from end to end; 'yetîm-i bî-efgan' is an orphan without a voice to lament (Persian afghān, wailing), perhaps an orphaned fledgling too weak to cry. 'Mai' is the old Turkish word for blue (from Arabic māʾī, water-coloured). The pathos of the empty nest closes the first half of the poem, which has moved from bird to butterfly to birds, all lost summer creatures sought by the snow.

    Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  10. tûde: heaps; berg-i semen, sehâb-ı ter ll. 31–32

    'Tûde' (Persian tūda) is a heap or mound; 'dest' (Persian dast) the hand. The sky is imagined as a giver with full hands, holding piles of 'berg-i semen' (jasmine leaves; Persian barg, leaf, and saman, jasmine), 'cenâh-ı kebûter' (dove-wing) and 'sehâb-ı ter' (fresh, moist cloud; Arabic saḥāb). It is asked to pour them as 'pure flowers' (sâfî şükûfeler, Persian shukūfa, blossom) on the black earth (hâk-i siyâh). Semen and its colour were a Servet-i Fünûn favourite: Ahmed Midhat mocked Cenab's phrase 'sâât-i semen-fâm' (jasmine-coloured hours).

    İsmail Parlatır, 'Servet-i Fünûn', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 36 (2009), pp. 573–575; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'semen'

  11. gunûde: asleep l. 33

    'Gunûde' (Persian ghunūda) means sleeping, drowsy. 'Semâ-revân-ı tabiat' is roughly 'you who travel the heaven of nature'. The archive prints the line with ':-', apparently reproducing old punctuation. The idea of winter as sleep rather than death prepares the end of the poem, where snow brings 'the silence of hope': the earth is resting, not dead, and the snow becomes the coverlet of a sleeper rather than the shroud of a corpse.

    Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing)

  12. sütre-i sefîd: a white veil ll. 35–38

    'Şâhsâr' (Persian shākhsār) is a mass of branches, a bough; 'zılâl' (Arabic ẓilāl) shadows; 'siyeh-reng' (Persian siyah-rang) black-coloured; 'nâ-ümîd' hopeless. 'Sütre' (Arabic sutra) is a curtain or covering, and 'sefîd' (Persian safīd) white. The white veil over the black branch suggests at once a bride's veil and a shroud, covering the bareness of winter with whiteness. 'Durma, durma, çek' (do not stop, do not stop, draw) gives the plea an urgent, repeated rhythm.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'sütre'

  13. 'Like hopes, the snow is falling' ll. 39–44

    'Rîzân' (Persian rīzān, shedding, pouring) and 'pûyân' (Persian pūyān, running, seeking) are present participles; 'lerzân ü girîzân' (trembling and fleeing) gives two more. The Persian participles in -ân, all on the rhyme, create the dancing sound of this part, and the hezec-i ahreb metre speeds the movement. For the first time the snow is compared to inner states (emeller, hopes; hayalim, my fancy): the poem moves from landscape to the poet's soul, a symbolist turn.

    Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing); Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349; Tevfik Rüştü Topuzoğlu, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 17 (1998), pp. 302–304

  14. mezâmir: psalms of silence; riyâz-ı melekût ll. 45–46

    'Mezâmir' (Arabic mazāmīr) means pipes or reed flutes, and also the Psalms of David (Zebur) chanted in a musical mode; 'melekût' (Arabic malakūt) is the invisible world of spirits and angels. The two lines unite the poem's two series, music (elhân) and flowers (ezhâr): the snow is a silent music and a heavenly flower. This oxymoron, melodies of silence, gives the title its meaning: the 'melodies of winter' are heard only as stillness. It is also the poem's closest approach to the symbolist ideal of correspondence between senses.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mezamir'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'melekût'; Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349

  15. samt-ı ümîd: the silence of hope ll. 47–50

    'Berf' (Persian barf) is snow; 'tuyûr' (Arabic ṭuyūr) the plural of ṭayr, bird; 'samt' (Arabic ṣamt) silence. The sky is now 'dest-i kerem', the hand of generosity, and the final line trades 'the melodies of the birds' for 'the silence of hope'. The ending is not elegiac but expectant: silence and whiteness promise a coming spring. The 'hope' can be read as both seasonal and inward, the poet's own wish for peace; Kaplan's analysis is a good place to compare readings.

    Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Elhân-ı Şitâ / Cenab Şahabeddin' (from p. 101 in the current Dergâh printing); 'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81)

  16. Music and the Servet-i Fünûn ideal l. 50

    Tarakçı summarises Cenab's poetics: poetry has no aim but beauty; he valued harmony and music in verse, preferred aruz to syllabic metre for that reason, and borrowed from the French symbolists the idea of musical poetry, though he seldom achieved it fully. 'Elhân-ı Şitâ' is usually taken as one of the successes: Kure stresses its sound repetitions and rhythmic structure that imitate the continuity of snowfall. The same features, Persian compounds and new images, were what conservative critics attacked, so the poem is a good text for testing the claims of both sides.

    Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349; 'Elhân-ı Şitâ (Şiir)', Kure Ansiklopedi (giving the first printing as Servet-i Fünûn, special issue, 1313/1897, pp. 79–81); İsmail Parlatır, 'Servet-i Fünûn', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 36 (2009), pp. 573–575

  17. The 'decadent' controversy of 1897 l. 49

    Around the time 'Elhân-ı Şitâ' appeared, Ahmed Midhat Efendi published his article 'Dekadanlar' in the newspaper Sabah, using the French word décadent for the young poets' obscure, French-influenced language and singling out expressions such as Cenab's 'sâât-i semen-fâm'. Cenab replied in Servet-i Fünûn with 'Sembolizm Nedir?' (11 Eylül 1313) and 'Dekadizm Nedir?' (2 Teşrînievvel 1313), arguing that artistic values change and old words can be replaced to renew literature. Compounds like 'ezhâr-ı bahar' or 'samt-ı ümîd' are the kind of diction at issue: classical in material, new in combination.

    İsmail Parlatır, 'Servet-i Fünûn', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 36 (2009), pp. 573–575; Celal Tarakçı, 'Cenab Şahabeddin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 7 (1993), pp. 346–349
