Time & Brevity

A Tavern Which Each Moment Takes a Life

by Ziyâ Beg (Ziya Pasha) d. c. 1879

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

Time & Brevity

A tavern which each moment takes a life as pleasure's pay is earth;
A glass which for a thousand souls doth sell each drop of spray is earth.

The world's a Magian that adores the flame of power and fortune high;
If thou should brightly shine, a moth about thy taper's ray is earth.

Anon one is, anon is not — thus ever runs the course of time;
From end to end a warning-fraught, a strange, romantic lay is earth.

'Twixt sense and frenzy 'tis indeed right hard to draw the sund'ring line.
Ah me! if understanding's wise, demented sooth alway is earth.

The desolation of the world beside its weal is truth itself;
Just as prosperity it seems, so ruin and decay is earth.

How many Khusrevs and Jemshîds have come, and from its bower have past!
A theatre that vieweth many and many an act and play is earth.

Ziyâ, a thousand caravans of wise men through its realms have past;
But yet not one can tell its tale, and all unknown this day is earth.

Codex Note

Gazel.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, the poet and the translation l. 1 and whole poem

    Ziya Paşa was, with Namık Kemal, a leader of the Young Ottomans; he fled to Europe in 1867 and co-edited Hürriyet in London from 1868. Gibb, who printed this poem in Ottoman Poems (1882), later judged that Ziya's best lyrics, written between about 1854 and 1867, were the earliest Turkish poems produced under the direct influence of Western culture, while their imagery and forms stayed wholly Eastern. This gazel shows that mix: an old theme, the vanity of the world, with a new note of sceptical realism. Gibb translated 'in the original forms', so the English keeps the monorhyme and the refrain.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Abdullah Uçman, 'Ziyâ Paşa', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 44 (2013), pp. 475–479

  2. The refrain 'is earth' (redif dünyâ) every second line

    A gazel is a short lyric of couplets on one rhyme, often followed by a repeated word or phrase, the redif. Gibb's transcription of the first line, printed above the translation, shows that the original ends in 'dünyâ' (the world), and his English imitates the effect with 'is earth' at the end of the first couplet's two lines and of every later couplet, preceded by a rhyme in -ay (pay, spray, ray, lay...). Each couplet is thus a new definition: the world is a tavern, a moth, a romance, a theatre. Andrews describes how such fixed definitions let the gazel work as a set of variations on a single theme.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  3. The world as tavern (meyhâne) l. 1

    The tavern (meyhâne) is one of the great spaces of Ottoman lyric, where wine is drunk outside the law and, mystically, where the soul is freed from worldly convention. Ziya reverses its usual promise: this tavern takes a life as payment for each moment's pleasure. The image would have reminded readers of Ziya's own anthology, Harâbât (The Tavern, 1874–75), and of the taverns where, as Gibb recounts, Ziya and his friends spent much of their youth. The 'glass' and its 'spray' (the drop or cür'a that a drinker spills) carry the economic metaphor on: life is the dearest price for the smallest pleasure.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Abdullah Uçman, 'Ziyâ Paşa', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 44 (2013), pp. 475–479

  4. The Magian, fire-worshipper l. 3

    The Magians (Mecûsî, mugân) are the Zoroastrians, who in Islamic eyes worship fire. Gibb explains that because wine in Persia was chiefly sold by Magians, the word in poetry means a vintner or tavern-keeper, and mystically a holy teacher (the pîr-i mugân, Elder of the Magians). Ziya uses neither positive sense: the world is a fire-worshipper because it adores the 'flame' of power (ikbâl) and fortune, a satire of a society that courts whoever is in office. Gibb's 'adores the flame' keeps the pun on fire and fortune.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  5. The moth and the candle l. 4

    The moth (pervâne) that circles a candle (şem') and burns in its flame is the classic image of the lover who dies for the beloved, and in Sufi poetry of the soul annihilated in God. Ziya himself, in the preface to his Harâbât (printed as 'Ziya Pasha's Story of his Childhood'), recalled admiring as a boy the stock images of the old poets: the cypress, the rose and nightingale, and the moth as the taper's lover. Here the image is reversed and made social: if you succeed, the world will swarm about you, not out of love but because you shine. The couplet completes the previous line's satire of a world that worships fortune.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994)

  6. Being and non-being ll. 5–6

    'Anon' means 'at one moment ... at another'. The line states the fleetingness of existence in the plainest terms, and the second line calls the world 'a warning-fraught, a strange, romantic lay', that is, a tale (hikâye, efsâne) full of ibret, admonitory examples. Ziya's famous Tercî-i Bend (dated 1859 by Gibb, about 1861 by Uçman) turns the same problem of existence into bewildered questioning; Gibb thought its inspiration wholly Western, a nineteenth-century agnosticism in Eastern dress. Kaplan's analysis of that poem is a good companion to this gazel.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Terci'-i Bend / Ziya Paşa' (from p. 48 in the current Dergâh printing); Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Terciibend', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 40 (2011), pp. 487–489

  7. Reason and madness ll. 7–8

    The couplet plays with the old opposition of 'akl' (reason) and 'cünûn' (madness), which in mystical poetry favours madness: Mecnun, the lover driven mad by love, knows more than the sober. Ziya gives the pair a sceptical twist: judged by reason, the world itself is demented. Gibb noticed that Ziya, a man of the world and no mystic, wanted poetry to state 'practically the truth', at the cost, sometimes, of verses that were 'little else than rather ordinary prose thrown into metrical form'. 'Sooth' means truth; 'alway', always.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  8. Khusrev and Jemshîd l. 11

    Gibb's notes explain both names. Jemshîd was a king of the Pishdadian dynasty in the Shāhnāma, famous for learning and splendour, who in his pride was overthrown by the usurper Zahhak; 'his splendour and subsequent fall are favourite themes with the Eastern poets'. Khusrev is the title of the Sasanian kings, sometimes applied especially to Khusrev Perviz, as Caesar is to the Roman emperors. The plural ('how many Khusrevs and Jemshîds') makes them types: countless great kings have passed through the world's garden. The same pair appears in the first stanza of Ziya's tesdis in this archive ('What was the splendour of Jemshîd?').

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes

  9. The world as theatre (tiyatro-hâne) l. 12

    The world-as-stage is ancient in Europe (theatrum mundi) and has Persian cousins in the shadow-play image, but the word itself is new: Gibb's note 290 records that Ziya wrote 'tiyatro-hâne', a European loan with a Persian suffix, which Gibb thought 'perhaps the first mention of a "theatre" in Oriental poetry'. Gibb dates Ahmed Vefik Pasha's Molière translations to 1869–70, says they were afterwards staged in the first true dramatic performance ever given in Turkish, and describes the permanent theatre later founded at Gedik Pasha. Here a modern institution enters the gazel's classical vocabulary, a small instance of what Gibb meant by Western spirit in Oriental imagery.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907)

  10. The maqta: the poet's pen name ll. 13–14

    The closing couplet of a gazel, the makta, conventionally contains the poet's pen name (mahlas), here 'Ziyâ' (light). The image of caravans (kervân) crossing a desert is a standard figure for generations of humanity passing through the world; the caravanserai or inn is the world itself. The ending is agnostic: all the wisdom of the past leaves the world 'all unknown this day'. Gibb read Ziya's poetry of this period as marked by a Western-influenced scepticism, though Uçman's article stresses rather a fatalist, Cebriyye-like submission in his Tercî-i Bend; both readings can be tested on this couplet.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Abdullah Uçman, 'Ziyâ Paşa', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 44 (2013), pp. 475–479; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary
