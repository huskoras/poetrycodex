Death & Dying

CVI

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Death & Dying

Ring out wild bells to the wild sky,
The flying cloud, the frosty light:
The year is dying in the night;
Ring out, wild bells, and let him die.

Ring out the old, ring in the new,
Ring, happy bells, across the snow:
The year is going, let him go;
Ring out the false, ring in the true.

Ring out the grief that saps the mind,
For those that here we see no more;
Ring out the feud of rich and poor,
Ring in redress to all mankind.

Ring out a slowly dying cause,
And ancient forms of party strife;
Ring in the nobler modes of life,
With sweeter manners, purer laws.

Ring out the want, the care, the sin,
The faithless coldness of the times;
Ring out, ring out my mournful rhymes,
But ring the fuller minstrel in.

Ring out false pride in place and blood,
The civic slander and the spite;
Ring in the love of truth and right,
Ring in the common love of good.

Ring out old shapes of foul disease,
Ring out the narrowing lust of gold;
Ring out the thousand wars of old,
Ring in the thousand years of peace.

Ring in the valiant man and free,
The larger heart, the kindlier hand;
Ring out the darkness of the land,
Ring in the Christ that is to be.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam CVI: 'Ring out, wild bells' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section CVI in the standard numbering, set on New Year's Eve after the move from Somersby. Bradley notes that the mood of CV continues and rises: the bells sound wild and jubilant, and the poet turns from grief to the future of man, favouring the powers that unite people. A local tradition connects the bells with Waltham Abbey near High Beech, where the Tennysons lived from 1837, but this is unproved. The section became a popular New Year text and hymn.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI; Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, High Beech chapter; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI

  2. Ring out ... ring in l. 5

    The section's structure is a sequence of antitheses: out the old, in the new; out the false, in the true. The repeated imperatives imitate the bells' insistent rhythm and give the section the form of a public hymn or litany rather than the private brooding of most of In Memoriam. The In Memoriam stanza, with its enclosed abba rhyme, suits the call-and-response of 'out' and 'in'.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI

  3. The year is dying in the night l. 3

    Personifying the year as 'him' who dies in the night echoes the poem's real death, and 'let him die' / 'let him go' (l. 7) suggests release from mourning. Bradley notes that the poet turns from 'the grief that saps the mind' (l. 9), here rung out, to hope for others. The wild sky and frosty light set the scene in winter.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, ll. 3-7

  4. the grief that saps the mind l. 9

    To sap is to undermine, as by digging beneath a wall, or to drain of vital juices. Bradley comments that the feeling that his grief isolates him and is useless to others stirs the poet to overcome it. The personal is quickly joined to the social: the next line rings out 'the feud of rich and poor'. The phrase thus marks a turning point in the elegy as a whole, from private lament toward public hope.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sap'

  5. the feud of rich and poor l. 11

    The years in which much of In Memoriam was written were marked by economic distress and the Chartist movement for political reform (1838-48). Disraeli's novel Sybil (1845) described rich and poor as two nations. 'Redress to all mankind' hopes for remedy. Bradley cross-refers to the Prologue, l. 12, and observes that the forces the poet invokes are those that unite people.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI, l. 12; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, ll. 11-12

  6. a slowly dying cause, / And ancient forms of party strife ll. 13-14

    The section avoids naming particular causes, and editors do not identify 'a slowly dying cause'; the vagueness lets the hope apply generally. The contrast 'nobler modes of life, / With sweeter manners, purer laws' sets moral and social reform against party politics. 'Manners' has its older sense of customs and morals. The hope is reformist rather than revolutionary: it asks for gentler manners and better laws, not the overthrow of institutions.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, ll. 13-16; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI

  7. Ring out, ring out my mournful rhymes, / But ring the fuller minstrel in ll. 19-20

    The poem refers to itself: the 'mournful rhymes' are the sections of In Memoriam. The 'fuller minstrel' may be a future poet of fuller powers, or the poet's own voice in a fuller, more hopeful mode; the phrase does not decide. Bradley cross-refers these lines to the Prologue and to the Epilogue, the marriage song that ends the poem. 'The faithless coldness of the times' names a spiritual malaise of the age.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI, ll. 19-20; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, ll. 17-20

  8. false pride in place and blood l. 21

    'Place' means rank or office; 'blood' lineage. The line rejects aristocratic pride, a theme Tennyson treated in 'Lady Clara Vere de Vere' ('Kind hearts are more than coronets'). 'The civic slander and the spite' extends the criticism to the quarrels of town and public life. The section's social hopes are, typically for Tennyson, moral rather than programmatic: they ask for changed hearts more than for particular measures, and pair the vices of the privileged with the spite of public life.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'place'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, ll. 21-22

  9. old shapes of foul disease l. 25

    Britain suffered cholera epidemics in 1831-32 and 1848-49, and the sanitary reform movement (Chadwick's report of 1842) was arguing that disease came from bad drainage and overcrowding. The hope to 'ring out old shapes of foul disease' is thus a concrete reform agenda as well as a millennial vision. Tennyson's list thus moves from social conflict and personal grief to the material conditions of life, and then to war and peace.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, l. 25; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI

  10. the narrowing lust of gold l. 26

    The line recalls 1 Timothy 6:10, 'the love of money is the root of all evil'. 'Narrowing' makes greed a contraction of sympathy, the opposite of 'The larger heart, the kindlier hand' in the next stanza. In the 1840s the railway speculation mania made money-lust a topical subject. The stanza joins disease, greed and war as evils of the old order, each paired with the hope that will replace it in the final stanza.

    Bible, King James Version, 1 Timothy 6:10; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, l. 26

  11. the thousand years of peace l. 28

    Revelation 20:1-6 foresees Satan bound for a thousand years while the saints reign with Christ; Isaiah 2:4 foresees nations beating swords into ploughshares. The 'thousand wars of old' are rung out and a thousand years of peace rung in, the numbers balanced. Victorian progressive thought often gave the millennium a secular form, a gradual improvement of society. The line prepares the section's final, explicitly Christian hope.

    Bible, King James Version, Revelation 20:2; Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 2:4

  12. the darkness of the land l. 31

    Bradley compares the antithesis with section XXX, where the Christmas Eve singers turn from darkness to the hope of light. The phrase may also recall the darkness 'over all the land' at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:45) and Isaiah's people who walked in darkness and saw a great light (Isaiah 9:2), so that the final line brings dawn after the long night.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI, l. 32; Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 9:2

  13. Ring in the Christ that is to be l. 32

    The Memoir (I.325-326), quoted by Bradley, reports Tennyson's conviction that the forms of the Christian religion would alter but the spirit of Christ would grow 'from more to more'; one of his meanings for this line was a time when Christianity without bigotry would triumph and controversies of creed would vanish. Some readers take the line as orthodox expectation of the Second Coming; others as a liberal, even evolutionary idea of a Christ realised progressively in mankind. Tennyson's 'one of my meanings' allows both.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, pp. 325-326; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. CVI, l. 32; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam CVI, l. 32
