Religion

L

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Religion

Be near me when my light is low,
When the blood creeps, and the nerves prick
And tingle; and the heart is sick,
And all the wheels of Being slow.

Be near me when the sensuous frame
Is rack’d with pangs that conquer trust,
And time, a maniac, scattering dust,
And life, a Fury, slinging flame.

Be near me when my faith is dry,
And men the flies of latter spring,
That lay their eggs, and sting and sing,
And weave their petty cells and die.

Be near me when I fade away,
To point the term of human strife,
And on the low dark verge of life
The twilight of eternal day.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam L: 'Be near me when my light is low' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section L in the standard numbering. Bradley treats L-LVI as a group, united by the poet's new longing for present communion with his friend and by the problem of pain and evil, which here begins with the poet's sense of his own weakness. The 'light' is both vital energy and the lamp of faith. Each stanza begins with the same plea, so the section is a litany.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L-LVI and L; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam L; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. L

  2. the blood creeps, and the nerves prick / And tingle ll. 2-3

    The lines describe depression and anxiety in precise bodily terms: creeping blood, pricking and tingling nerves, a sick heart. The enjambment 'prick / And tingle' lets the sensation spill over the line-end. Bradley, citing Collins, compares Shelley's The Cenci (IV.i), where a character feels the blood run up and down the veins and a fearful pleasure makes it 'prick and tingle'. Bradley also compares Tennyson's The Two Voices, whose speaker longs for more life, not death; and he sees the mood of this section dramatised in the opening of Maud.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L, ll. 2-3 and addenda; P. B. Shelley, The Cenci (1819), IV.i

  3. the wheels of Being l. 4

    The image treats existence as a mechanism, an engine or clock whose wheels slow as the spring winds down. 'Being' with a capital suggests not only the poet's own life but the larger principle of existence, so personal exhaustion is felt as if the universe itself were slowing. The mechanical vocabulary fits a poem preoccupied with whether the world is a purposeful creation or a mere machine.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam L, l. 4; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L

  4. sensuous frame l. 5

    'Sensuous' means relating to the senses, without the later suggestion of sensual pleasure; the OED credits Milton with introducing the word in the 1640s, and it was revived by Coleridge. The 'frame' is the body. The stanza imagines the body racked by pains that 'conquer trust', that is, overcome faith. The point is that suffering of the body can undo belief, an admission which Bradley says opens the group's larger meditation on pain, defect and evil.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sensuous'; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L-LVI

  5. time, a maniac, scattering dust l. 7

    The two personifications of ll. 7-8 have no main verb; they hang on 'Be near me when', as though the sentence itself were breaking. Time as a maniac reverses the stately figure of Father Time; the dust he scatters may suggest the sand of an hourglass and the dust to which human beings return (Genesis 3:19). The violence of the image belongs to the 'wild and wandering cries' the Prologue asks forgiveness for.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:19; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam L, ll. 7-8

  6. life, a Fury l. 8

    The Furies (Greek Erinyes, Latin Furiae) are the avenging goddesses who pursue Orestes in Aeschylus' Eumenides; in classical art and Roman poetry they often carry torches and snakes. Making life itself a Fury, hurling fire, suggests that existence torments the speaker as if he were guilty. With time as a maniac, the two figures make the world a madhouse. The image also anticipates the 'wild and wandering cries' of the Prologue, which asks pardon for such violence of feeling.

    Aeschylus, Eumenides (trans. H. W. Smyth), Perseus Digital Library; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam L, l. 8

  7. Form: the In Memoriam stanza and the litany st. 3

    The In Memoriam stanza is a quatrain of iambic tetrameters rhyming abba; the enclosed rhyme tends to turn each stanza back on itself, a shape suited to brooding. Tennyson thought he had invented it, though it had been used by Ben Jonson and others. Here the repeated 'Be near me when' turns the section into a litany of four occasions: weak vitality, bodily pain, dryness of faith, and death. 'Dry' faith is a term from devotional writing for spiritual aridity.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam, headnote; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. L; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L

  8. the flies of latter spring l. 10

    Bradley identifies the 'summer-fly' of Shakespeare and Milton's Samson Agonistes (c. l. 676), where the common run of men grow up and perish like the summer fly. 'Latter spring' is the season's end, when such insects swarm. The stanza is the most contemptuous in the section: when faith fails, humanity appears as a swarm of insects with no purpose but reproduction and death. 'Petty cells' are the insects' tiny combs or chambers.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L, l. 10; John Milton, Samson Agonistes (1671), ll. 674-676

  9. sting and sing l. 11

    The near-rhyme within the line ('sting and sing') is contemptuous in effect. Bradley suggests an unconscious reminiscence of Pope's Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot (ll. 309-310), on the 'bug with gilded wings' that stinks and stings, Pope's satiric portrait of Lord Hervey. If the echo is there, Tennyson's despair borrows the satirist's tone for the whole species. The sound-play cheapens human activity to buzzing: to sting and to sing are made equivalent, poetry included. The mood is the bitterest in the group, and the next sections retreat from it.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L, l. 11; Alexander Pope, An Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot (1735), ll. 309-310

  10. point the term l. 14

    Bradley glosses 'point the term' as 'point to the limit'. 'Term' keeps its Latin sense of boundary or end (terminus), and the line imagines the dying man as a marker of where human struggle stops. The friend's presence at death would thus mean that the end is also a threshold. The stanza turns from the poet's present weakness to his own death, the last occasion of the litany. He asks his friend to be present at the very border he himself has crossed, so that death is imagined as a meeting rather than a parting.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L, l. 14; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'term'

  11. the low dark verge of life / The twilight of eternal day ll. 15-16

    The last line is an oxymoron: twilight usually precedes night, but this is the twilight of an eternal day. The 'low dark verge' is a horizon, the border of life. Bradley cross-refers to the close of section XCV, where East and West mix their dim lights 'like life and death' to broaden into boundless day. The section that began with failing light ends with a dim, hopeful one.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L, ll. 15-16; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam L, ll. 15-16
