Nature

XCV

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Nature

By night we linger’d on the lawn,
For underfoot the herb was dry;
And genial warmth; and o’er the sky
The silvery haze of summer drawn;

And calm that let the tapers burn
Unwavering: not a cricket chirr’d:
The brook alone far-off was heard
And on the board the fluttering urn:

And bats went round in fragrant skies,
And wheel’d or lit the filmy shapes
That haunt the dusk, with ermine capes
And woolly breasts and beaded eyes;

While now we sang old songs that peal’d
From knoll to knoll, where, couch’d at ease,
The white kine glimmer’d and the trees
Laid their dark arms about the field.

But when those others, one by one,
Withdrew themselves from me and night,
And in the house light after light
Went out, and I was all alone,

A hunger seized my heart; I read
Of that glad year which once had been,
In those fall’n leaves which kept their green,
The noble letters of the dead:

And strangely on the silence broke
The silent-speaking words, and strange
Was love’s dumb cry defying change
To test his worth; and strangely spoke

The faith, the vigour, bold to dwell
On doubts that drive the coward back,
And keen thro’ wordy snares to track
Suggestion to her inmost cell.

So word by word, and line by line,
The dead man touch’d me from the past,
And all at once it seem’d at last
His living soul was flash’d on mine,

And mine in his was wound, and whirl’d
About empyreal heights of thought,
And came on that which is, and caught
The deep pulsations of the world,

Æonian music measuring out
The steps of Time--the shocks of Chance--
The blows of Death. At length my trance
Was cancell’d, stricken thro’ with doubt.

Vague words! but ah, how hard to frame
In matter-moulded forms of speech,
Or ev’n for intellect to reach
Thro’ memory that which I became:

Till now the doubtful dusk reveal’d
The knolls once more where, couch’d at ease,
The white kine glimmer’d, and the trees
Laid their dark arms about the field:

And suck’d from out the distant gloom
A breeze began to tremble o’er
The large leaves of the sycamore,
And fluctuate all the still perfume;

And gathering freshlier overhead,
Rock’d the full-foliaged elms, and swung
The heavy-folded rose, and flung
The lilies to and fro, and said

‘The dawn, the dawn,’ and died away;
And East and West, without a breath,
Mixt their dim lights, like life and death,
To broaden into boundless day.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam XCV: 'By night we linger'd on the lawn' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section XCV in the standard numbering. Bradley's summary: after a peaceful evening in the garden his friend once visited, the poet, left alone, rereads the dead man's letters and his wish for communion is, in some sense, fulfilled. Lawn, brook, sycamore and elms recall the Somersby scene of LXXXIX. Many readers treat the section as the turning point of the elegy.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 1; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. XCV

  2. herb l. 2

    'Herb' in older English can mean grass and green plants in general (as in Genesis 1:11, 'the herb yielding seed'). The dry grass explains why the company could sit out on the lawn. The opening stanzas are deliberately calm and exact, a still evening in which 'tapers burn / Unwavering' because there is no wind; the stillness sets up the later breeze.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'herb'; Bible, King James Version, Genesis 1:11

  3. the fluttering urn l. 8

    The urn is a metal vessel with a tap for heating water for tea, a household object of the period; it 'flutters' because steam lifts its lid or because its flame wavers. The homely detail anchors the mystical experience in a real domestic evening. 'Board' is the table. The only sounds are the brook and the urn. Such details also recall the happy social evenings at Somersby when Hallam was alive.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'urn'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, l. 8

  4. the filmy shapes / That haunt the dusk, with ermine capes ll. 10-11

    Bradley reports the 'authorised interpretation' given in Gatty's Key: the filmy shapes are night-moths, and the ermine moth fits the description. 'Lit' means alighted: the bats either wheel about or settle on the moths, that is, catch them. The precise natural-history detail (capes, woolly breasts, beaded eyes) shows the poet's habit of exact observation even in a visionary section. Darkness and the creatures of the dusk prepare the night of solitude.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 10; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 9-12

  5. kine l. 15

    'Kine' is the old plural of 'cow', biblical and poetic (Genesis 41, Pharaoh's 'kine'). The white cattle glimmering in the dusk and the trees laying 'their dark arms about the field' return almost word for word in ll. 51-52, after the trance. The repetition frames the vision between two identical pictures of the ordinary world, so that the return to earth is felt as a return to the same place, now changed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kine'; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 49

  6. fall'n leaves which kept their green, / The noble letters of the dead ll. 23-24

    The letters are 'leaves' in two senses, sheets of paper and leaves of a tree, fallen like their writer yet still green, that is, living. Bradley notes that 'that glad year', as the metaphor shows, means the whole time of the friendship. Rereading the dead friend's words is the occasion of the trance: the experience begins in reading. The phrase 'kept their green' implies that the friendship remains alive in them.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 22; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 21-24

  7. silent-speaking words ... love's dumb cry ll. 26-27

    Bradley observes that 'silent-speaking' and 'dumb cry' express the strangeness of the dead man seeming to speak, and so prepare for what follows. The repeated 'strangely ... strange ... strangely' builds an incantatory, unsettled rhythm. 'To test his worth' probably refers to love's willingness to be tested by change. The lines thus move from memory toward presence: the letters seem less like records than like voices, and the dead man's love appears to defy the change that death has made.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, ll. 25 f.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 25-28

  8. keen thro' wordy snares to track / Suggestion to her inmost cell ll. 31-32

    The stanza praises Hallam's intellectual courage: the faith and vigour that would 'dwell / On doubts' that frighten cowards away, and the sharpness that could see through 'wordy snares' and follow a suggestion, an idea or hint, into its 'inmost cell', its lair or the innermost chamber of the mind. Hallam was famous among the Cambridge Apostles for his philosophical brilliance.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 29-32; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV

  9. The dead man touch'd me from the past l. 34

    'Word by word, and line by line' marks the slow build. Bradley notes that 'touch'd' takes up a word from section XCIII, where the poet had prayed to the spirit to 'Descend, and touch, and enter'. The plain phrase 'the dead man' is deliberately unconsoling, and makes the following lines more startling. The rhythm quickens here, and the next stanzas run on without a full stop as the trance begins.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 34; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, l. 34

  10. His living soul was flash'd on mine l. 36

    Bradley records that until about 1878 the lines read 'His living soul' and 'mine in his'; Tennyson then altered them to 'The living soul' and 'mine in this', telling James Knowles the soul was 'perchance of the Deity' and that his conscience had been troubled by 'his'. The archive's text has the earlier reading. Bradley thinks Tennyson probably did believe at the moment that his friend's soul was present, but never felt certain afterward. The crux is whether the trance was communion with Hallam or with God, a question the revision deliberately opened.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, ll. 36-37; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 36-37; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. XCV

  11. empyreal l. 38

    From Greek empyros, 'in the fire': in Ptolemaic and medieval cosmology the empyrean was the outermost heaven of pure fire or light, the dwelling of God, as in Dante and Paradise Lost. The two souls are 'wound' and 'whirl'd' together upward, an image of motion and union that suits the dance of the spheres in the next lines. The word makes the ascent a cosmic one.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'empyreal'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, l. 38

  12. that which is l. 39

    Bradley glosses 'that which is' as ultimate reality, distinguished from the half-deceptive appearance we commonly call the real world, and compares The Higher Pantheism and The Ancient Sage. The phrase translates the Platonic to on, 'that which is', and may recall God's name in Exodus 3:14, 'I AM THAT I AM'. Bradley compares Tennyson's experience to Plotinus' account of the soul's ascent.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 39; Bible, King James Version, Exodus 3:14

  13. Æonian music l. 41

    'Aeonian' comes from Greek aion, an age or eternity. In the trance the poet perceives the 'deep pulsations of the world' as music that measures out the steps of Time and the shocks of Chance and Death, recalling the ancient music of the spheres. Bradley says he perceives law and harmony in what at other times seems unintelligible, and compares Milton's 'On Time', which triumphs over 'Death, and Chance, and thee O Time'.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, ll. 41-43; John Milton, 'On Time' (1645)

  14. my trance / Was cancell'd, stricken thro' with doubt ll. 43-44

    In a letter quoted in the Memoir (I.320), Tennyson described a 'waking trance' he had often had since boyhood, in which individuality seemed to dissolve into boundless being, a state beyond words in which death seemed an almost laughable impossibility; he compared St Paul's 'whether in the body ... or out of the body, I cannot tell' (2 Corinthians 12:2-3). Here the trance is 'cancell'd' by doubt: the vision is not allowed to be a certainty, and the poem's honesty about that is part of its authority.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, p. 320; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV; Bible, King James Version, 2 Corinthians 12:2-3

  15. matter-moulded forms of speech l. 46

    Bradley explains that words, moulded by and for sensible experience, are inadequate to a higher experience. The confession 'Vague words!' belongs to the long tradition of mystics declaring their visions unutterable; Bradley, citing Collins, compares Dante's Paradiso XXXIII.55-57, where speech fails before the vision. Bradley also discusses whether 'that which I became' means the state during the trance or after it.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, ll. 45-48; Dante, Paradiso XXXIII.55-57

  16. The knolls once more ... The white kine glimmer'd ll. 50-51

    The stanza repeats ll. 14-16 with small changes. The return of the same words marks the return of the senses after the trance, and the 'doubtful dusk' (half-light, also the light of doubt) sets it at the edge of dawn. Bradley considers the last four stanzas, taken alone, among the most wonderful descriptive passages in poetry. The changed punctuation, a colon instead of a semicolon, drives forward into the dawn stanzas.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 49; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 49-52

  17. A breeze began to tremble o'er / The large leaves of the sycamore ll. 54-55

    Bradley observes that in context the breeze seems to recall the coming and passing of 'the wind of the Spirit' in the trance. The biblical association of wind and Spirit is close: 'The wind bloweth where it listeth' (John 3:8), and the 'rushing mighty wind' of Pentecost (Acts 2:2). The breeze rocks the elms, swings the rose, flings the lilies, and speaks: 'The dawn, the dawn'. 'Fluctuate' is used transitively, to set the scent in waves.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 49; Bible, King James Version, John 3:8; Bible, King James Version, Acts 2:2

  18. East and West ... Mixt their dim lights, like life and death ll. 62-63

    On a short summer night the afterglow in the West and the dawn in the East can meet across the northern sky. Bradley reads the mingling as the meeting of life and death just experienced in the trance, a foretaste of an endless union to come; he compares Herbert's 'The Search' and notes that 'dim lights of life' occurs in Pope's Elegy to the Memory of an Unfortunate Lady. 'Without a breath' contrasts with the breeze: the wind has died, but the light continues. The final 'boundless day' answers the twilight at the close of section L.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. XCV, l. 63; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam XCV, ll. 61-64
