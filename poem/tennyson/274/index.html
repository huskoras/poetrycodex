Mythology & Folklore

The Lady of Shalott

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Poems (1843)

Mythology & Folklore

PART I.

On either side the river lie
Long fields of barley and of rye,
That clothe the wold and meet the sky;
And thro' the field the road runs by
To many-tower'd Camelot:
And up and down the people go,
Gazing where the lilies blow
Round an island there below,
The island of Shalott.

Willows whiten, aspens quiver,
Little breezes dusk and shiver

Thro' the wave that runs for ever
By the island in the river
Flowing down to Camelot.
Four gray walls, and four gray towers,
Overlook a space of flowers,
And the silent isle imbowers
The Lady of Shalott.

By the margin, willow-veil'd,
Slide the heavy barges trail'd
By slow horses; and unhail'd
The shallop flitteth silken-sail'd
Skimming down to Camelot:
But who hath seen her wave her hand?
Or at the casement seen her stand?
Or is she known in all the land,
The Lady of Shalott?

Only reapers, reaping early
In among the bearded barley,
Hear a song that echoes cheerly
From the river winding clearly,
Down to tower'd Camelot:

And by the moon the reaper weary,
Piling sheaves in uplands airy,
Listening, whispers "'Tis the fairy
Lady of Shalott."

PART II.

There she weaves by night and day
A magic web with colours gay.
She has heard a whisper say,
A curse is on her if she stay
To look down to Camelot.
She knows not what the curse may be,
And so she weaveth steadily,
And little other care hath she,
The Lady of Shalott.

And moving thro' a mirror clear
That hangs before her all the year,

Shadows of the world appear.
There she sees the highway near
Winding down to Camelot:
There the river eddy whirls,
And there the surly village-churls,
And the red cloaks of market girls,
Pass onward from Shalott.

Sometimes a troop of damsels glad,
An abbot on an ambling pad,
Sometimes a curly shepherd-lad,
Or long-hair'd page in crimson clad,
Goes by to tower'd Camelot;
And sometimes thro' the mirror blue
The knights come riding two and two:
She hath no loyal knight and true,
The Lady of Shalott.

But in her web she still delights
To weave the mirror's magic sights,
For often thro' the silent nights
A funeral, with plumes and lights
And music, went to Camelot:

Or when the moon was overhead,
Came two young lovers lately wed;
"I am half sick of shadows," said
The Lady of Shalott.

PART III.

A bow-shot from her bower-eaves,
He rode between the barley-sheaves,
The sun came dazzling thro' the leaves,
And flamed upon the brazen greaves
Of bold Sir Lancelot.
A red-cross knight for ever kneel'd
To a lady in his shield,
That sparkled on the yellow field,
Beside remote Shalott.

The gemmy bridle glitter'd free,
Like to some branch of stars we see

Hung in the golden Galaxy.
The bridle bells rang merrily
As he rode down to Camelot:
And from his blazon'd baldric slung
A mighty silver bugle hung,
And as he rode his armour rung,
Beside remote Shalott.

All in the blue unclouded weather
Thick-jewell'd shone the saddle-leather,
The helmet and the helmet-feather
Burn'd like one burning flame together,
As he rode down to Camelot.
As often thro' the purple night,
Below the starry clusters bright,
Some bearded meteor, trailing light,
Moves over still Shalott.

His broad clear brow in sunlight ⟨glow'd;⟩
On burnish'd hooves his war-horse ⟨trode;⟩

From underneath his helmet flow'd
His coal-black curls as on he rode,
As he rode down to Camelot.
From the bank and from the river
He flash'd into the crystal mirror,
"Tirra lirra," by the river
Sang Sir Lancelot.

She left the web, she left the loom,
She made three paces thro' the room,
She saw the water-lily bloom,
She saw the helmet and the plume,
She look'd down to Camelot.
Out flew the web and floated wide;
The mirror crack'd from side to side;
"The curse is come upon me," cried
The Lady of Shalott.

PART IV.

In the stormy east-wind straining,
The pale yellow woods were waning,
The broad stream in his banks complaining,
Heavily the low sky raining
Over tower'd Camelot;
Down she came and found a boat
Beneath a willow left afloat,
And round about the prow she wrote
The Lady of Shalott.

And down the river's dim expanse—
Like some bold seër in a trance
Seeing all his own mischance—
With a glassy countenance
Did she look to Camelot.
And at the closing of the day
She loosed the chain, and down she lay;
The broad stream bore her far away,
The Lady of Shalott.

Lying, robed in snowy white
That loosely flew to left and right—
The leaves upon her falling light—
Thro' the noises of the night
She floated down to Camelot:
And as the boat-head wound along
The willowy hills and fields among,
They heard her singing her last song,
The Lady of Shalott.

Heard a carol, mournful, holy,
Chanted loudly, chanted lowly,
Till her blood was frozen slowly,
And her eyes were darken'd wholly,
Turn'd to tower'd Camelot;
For ere she reach'd upon the tide
The first house by the water-side,
Singing in her song she died,
The Lady of Shalott.

Under tower and balcony,
By garden-wall and gallery,

A gleaming shape she floated by,
A corse between the houses high,
Silent into Camelot.
Out upon the wharfs they came,
Knight and burgher, lord and dame,
And round the prow they read her name,
The Lady of Shalott.

Who is this? and what is here?
And in the lighted palace near
Died the sound of royal cheer;
And they crossed themselves for fear,
All the knights at Camelot:
But Lancelot mused a little space;
He said, "She has a lovely face;
God in his mercy lend her grace,
The Lady of Shalott."

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Lady of Shalott: versions and sources l. 1

    This is one of Tennyson's earliest Arthurian poems (a fragment, 'Sir Launcelot and Queen Guinevere', had appeared in 1830). The first version (20 stanzas) appeared in the volume dated 1833 but issued in December 1832; the revised text printed here (19 stanzas) appeared in 1842. Tennyson is said to have taken the name from an Italian novella, 'La Donna di Scalotta' (Il Novellino, no. 82), in which a maiden dies of love for Lancelot and floats down to Camelot in a boat. 'Shalott' is a form of the Astolat of the romances; Malory tells the related story of Elaine, the Fair Maid of Astolat, which Tennyson retold in 'Lancelot and Elaine' (1859). The isolation in the tower, the web, the mirror and the curse are Tennyson's additions.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; 'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  2. wold l. 3

    'Wold' (Old English wald, forest) came to mean an area of open upland, as in the Cotswolds and the Lincolnshire Wolds, where Tennyson was born (Somersby). The fields of barley and rye 'clothe' the hills up to the skyline, an image of abundant, ordinary, working life that frames the enclosed island. Part I keeps returning to this busy outer world of roads, barges and reapers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wold' n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  3. Camelot l. 5

    Camelot is the court of King Arthur in the romances. RPO notes that Caxton, in his preface to Malory, puts it in Wales; Malory himself identifies it with Winchester. Tennyson keeps it vague, a towered city downstream. Every stanza turns on the rhyme between 'Camelot' (line 5) and 'Shalott' (line 9), so the two places are fixed as the poles of the poem: the world of action and society, and the island of seclusion.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Thomas Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, ed. Caxton (1485), Preface and Book II, Project Gutenberg

  4. stanza form and refrain st. 1

    Each stanza has four lines on one rhyme, a shorter fifth line ending 'Camelot', three more on a new rhyme, and a ninth line ending 'Shalott' (usually 'The Lady of Shalott'). The lines are mostly iambic tetrameter, with trimeter in the refrain lines. The long runs of a single rhyme ('lie / rye / sky / by') and the fixed refrains create a hypnotic, enclosed movement, like the weaving itself, and make any departure, such as the river's 'For ever' or the curse's arrival, felt strongly. The form is a ballad-like lyric with roots in medieval romance and in contemporary ballad imitation.

    'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  5. shallop l. 22

    A shallop (from French chaloupe) is a small light boat. The stanza contrasts heavy barges dragged by 'slow horses' with the quick silken-sailed shallop, but both pass by without contact: 'unhail'd' means not greeted. The three questions that follow ('who hath seen her...?') make the Lady unknown to the passing world, preparing the moment when she herself becomes a boat drifting to Camelot.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shallop' n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  6. the reapers st. 4

    The Lady is known only by sound: reapers at dawn and the tired reaper at night hear her singing and whisper that this is the 'fairy / Lady of Shalott'. The 'bearded barley' (barley has long bristles, or awns) and moonlit sheaves place her among the rhythms of rural work, but she remains invisible. The unusual feminine rhymes ('early / barley / cheerly / clearly') make the stanza sing. Critics who read the poem as an allegory of the artist note that her art reaches the world only indirectly and is misunderstood as enchantment.

    'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  7. the magic web ll. 38-39

    Weaving women are an ancient figure: Penelope at her loom, the Fates spinning destiny, Homer's Helen weaving the battles of Troy (Iliad III.125-128). The Lady's 'magic web' records the world in images, so it is often read as an emblem of art, especially art made in isolation. She weaves 'steadily' because of a curse whose nature she does not know, a condition many readers relate to the Victorian poet's uncertainty about engaging with society. David Staines, cited by Wikipedia, sees in the poem the study of the artist and the dangers of personal isolation; feminist critics read the enclosure as a picture of the confined Victorian woman.

    'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); Homer, Iliad III.125-128 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  8. the mirror ll. 46-48

    Because she must not look directly at Camelot, the Lady watches the road and river in a mirror and weaves what it shows. The phrase 'Shadows of the world' invites comparison with Plato's allegory of the cave, where prisoners see only shadows of real things on a wall (Republic VII, 514a-517a). Tennyson's own later comment, recorded in the Memoir and quoted by RPO, said that her new-born love for someone in the wide world takes her out of the region of shadows into that of realities. The mirror is thus both protection and limitation.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Plato, Republic VII.514a-517a, Perseus Digital Library; Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir, 2 vols (Macmillan, 1897)

  9. pad l. 56

    RPO glosses 'pad' as an easy-paced horse; the OED derives the word from 'pad-nag', a horse for the road (from Dutch or Low German pad, path). 'Ambling' describes its smooth gait, in which both legs on one side move together. The stanza lists the medieval figures who pass the mirror, damsels, an abbot, a shepherd, a page, knights, a social panorama of the world she cannot join.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pad' n.3

  10. churls l. 52

    'Churl' (Old English ceorl, a free man of the lowest rank) came to mean a peasant and then a rude, ill-bred person (OED). 'Surly' strengthens the later sense. The village churls and the 'red cloaks of market girls' bring colour and ordinary life into the mirror, and they 'Pass onward from Shalott': the outside world is always moving, while she remains.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'churl' n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  11. 'I am half sick of shadows' l. 71

    The stanza sets death (a funeral 'with plumes and lights') beside love ('two young lovers lately wed'), the two great events she cannot share. Her words are the first she speaks and the end of Part II. 'Half sick' is exact: she is not yet in revolt, only tired. In the 1832 version the line is the same, which shows it was central from the start. J. W. Waterhouse took it as the title of his 1915 painting, and Tennyson's own gloss on love leading 'out of the region of shadows' (Memoir I, 116-17, quoted by RPO) is usually applied here.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; 'The Lady of Shalott (1832)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto); 'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson)

  12. brazen greaves l. 76

    Greaves are pieces of armour covering the leg below the knee (OED). 'Brazen' (of brass or bronze) makes them recall the bronze-greaved Achaeans of the Iliad, whose standard epithet is 'well-greaved' (euknemides). The sun 'flamed' on them: Part III is a blaze of light and metal after the grey, shadowed world of Parts I-II. 'Bower-eaves' are the eaves of her chamber ('bower', a lady's private room).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'greave' n. and 'bower' n.1; Homer, Iliad I.17 (euknemides Achaioi) (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library)

  13. Sir Lancelot l. 77

    Lancelot du Lac is the pre-eminent knight of the Round Table, whose love for Guinevere eventually destroys Arthur's fellowship (Malory). In Malory's story of the Fair Maid of Astolat (Book XVIII), Elaine loves him, he cannot return her love, and she dies and is sent to Camelot in a barge. Here Lancelot does not know the Lady exists: all of Part III is description of his dazzling appearance as she sees it, which makes her turn an act of desire for something glimpsed rather than a response to any man's attention.

    Thomas Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Book XVIII, chs. 9-20, Project Gutenberg; 'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson)

  14. A red-cross knight for ever kneel'd ll. 78-79

    The device on Lancelot's shield shows a knight wearing the red cross (St George's emblem, and the badge of Spenser's Redcrosse Knight, The Faerie Queene I.i.2) kneeling forever to a lady. It is an image of courtly love and religious chivalry, frozen in art, like the Lady's own woven images. The irony is that Lancelot's devotion belongs to Guinevere, not to the Lady who watches; the 'yellow field' is both the heraldic gold ground of the shield and the ripe barley around him.

    Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene I.i.2 (1590), Project Gutenberg; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  15. Galaxy l. 84

    RPO glosses 'Galaxy' as the Milky Way. The word comes from Greek galaxias, 'milky', from gala, milk, the name for the band of stars across the night sky. The simile is part of the poem's cosmic scale for Lancelot: his bridle is like a branch of stars, his helmet-feather burns like a flame, and he passes like a meteor. The Lady sees him as a celestial apparition rather than a man.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'galaxy' n.

  16. blazon'd baldric l. 87

    A baldric is a belt worn from one shoulder to the opposite hip, used to hang a sword or, here, a 'mighty silver bugle' (a hunting horn). 'Blazon'd' means painted or embroidered with heraldic arms (OED, 'blazon'). The alliteration of b-sounds ('bridle bells', 'blazon'd baldric', 'bugle') and the bells and ringing armour make the stanza noisy and bright, the opposite of the silent island.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'baldric' n. and 'blazon' v.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  17. bearded meteor l. 98

    'Bearded' translates the old way of describing comets by their tails (Greek kometes means 'long-haired'; OED 'comet'). In the nineteenth century 'meteor' could include any bright atmospheric or celestial phenomenon. Comets were traditionally omens: 'Comets, importing change of times and states' opens 1 Henry VI. Lancelot's passing is a portent for the Lady, foreshadowing the catastrophe that follows; the simile also emphasises his remoteness, crossing 'still Shalott' without stopping.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'comet' n. and 'meteor' n.; Shakespeare, 1 Henry VI I.i.2 (Folger Shakespeare Library text)

  18. Tirra lirra l. 107

    RPO notes the source in Shakespeare's The Winter's Tale (IV.iii), where the rogue Autolycus sings of 'The lark, that tirra-lyra chants'. The nonsense refrain marks Lancelot's light-heartedness: he sings as he rides, unaware of the effect he has. It is the only thing the Lady hears from him before she leaves the loom. The double 'river' rhyme ('river / mirror / river') sets him visually and aurally into her mirror world.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale IV.iii (Folger Shakespeare Library text)

  19. the mirror crack'd ll. 109-117

    The stanza moves through five short clauses beginning 'She': left, made, saw, saw, look'd. The repetition makes her action decisive and ritual-like. Then the consequences arrive in impersonal verbs: the web flies out, the mirror cracks, the curse comes. Looking directly at the world destroys both her art (the web) and her means of seeing (the mirror). The line became proverbial (Agatha Christie's novel The Mirror Crack'd from Side to Side, 1962). Readers differ on whether her choice is a fall, a liberation, or both; Christine Poulson, cited in Wikipedia, reads the escape as defiance and female empowerment.

    'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  20. Like some bold seër in a trance ll. 128-131

    The diaeresis in 'seër' marks two syllables: a seer is one who sees visions. The simile compares her to a prophet in a trance who foresees his own 'mischance' (misfortune) and cannot avert it. 'Glassy countenance' suggests a fixed, unblinking look, and also turns her face into a kind of mirror now that the real one is broken. The storm stanza before it (east wind, waning woods, raining sky) is the poem's only bad weather, matching her fate.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seer' n.1 and 'mischance' n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  21. her name on the prow ll. 125-126

    The woman who has been known only as a rumour now identifies herself in writing. The detail completes a pattern in which she becomes her own work of art: robed in white, singing her 'last song', she drifts downstream like the funeral she once watched in the mirror. In the Italian novella the dead maiden carries a letter; Tennyson transfers the message to the boat. At the end the people 'read her name' on the prow, the only thing they can know of her.

    'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  22. corse l. 157

    'Corse' is an older form of 'corpse' (from Latin corpus, body), kept in poetry for its solemn and archaic sound (OED). The stanza shows the boat entering the city 'silent', a 'gleaming shape' passing balcony and gallery. The contrast with the noisy, bright Lancelot of Part III is pointed: she arrives at Camelot only as a body, and the court's festive 'royal cheer' dies at the sight.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'corse' n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  23. Lancelot's last words ll. 169-171

    In the 1832 version the poem ended with the townsfolk finding a parchment on her breast, which announced that the web was woven, the charm broken, and 'this is I, / The Lady of Shalott'. In 1842 Tennyson replaced this with Lancelot's musing, a change that ends the poem on the man she saw rather than on her own statement. Some readers find his words merely aesthetic, a comment on her face that repeats the world's failure to know her; others hear genuine pity in the prayer 'God in his mercy lend her grace'. The irony is that Lancelot, the cause of her death, never learns it.

    'The Lady of Shalott (1832)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Lady of Shalott (1842)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; 'The Lady of Shalott', Wikipedia (citing Il Novellino LXXXII; Staines; Poulson)
