The Eagle

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Poetical Works (1899)

Nature

He clasps the crag with crooked hands:
Close to the sun in lonely lands,
Ring'd with the azure world, he stands.

The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
He watches from his mountain walls,
And like a thunderbolt he falls.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'The Eagle: A Fragment': date and publication l. 1

    The poem first appeared in 1851, when Tennyson added it to the seventh edition of his Poems (Moxon), the collection first published in 1842. It bears the subtitle 'Fragment', which raises the question whether it was conceived as part of something larger or presents itself as a glimpse, a single seen moment. Its composition date is not securely known, and sources disagree. Some readers connect it with Tennyson's memories of eagles in the Pyrenees, where he travelled with Arthur Hallam in 1830, though the setting in the poem is sea cliffs. The first line's hard c/k alliteration (clasps, crag, crooked) is one of the best-known sound effects in Victorian verse.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'The Eagle', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto); text from Poems, 7th edn (Moxon, 1851); 'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  2. crooked hands l. 1

    Calling the talons 'hands' personifies the eagle at once, and the pronoun 'he' does the same: this is a figure of power, like a king gripping a throne or a sceptre. 'Crooked' here means hooked or curved, as of claws, though the word can also suggest something twisted or old. The verb 'clasps' is strong and possessive. Political readings of the poem (the eagle as ruler, the sea as the mass below) take their start from this human grasp; other readers see simply a precise visual description of how a raptor perches.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. crooked, adj.

  3. Close to the sun l. 2

    Literally the line is an effect of perspective: seen from the shore, a bird on a high crag appears to stand against the sun. But it also draws on an old tradition. Ancient natural history held that the eagle alone could look straight at the sun, and Pliny reports that eagles tested their young by making them face its rays, rejecting any that blinked (Natural History, book X). Medieval bestiaries repeated the story. Tennyson does not spell out the legend, but the closeness to the sun adds majesty and isolation to the portrait.

    Pliny the Elder, Natural History X.3 (on the eagle; Perseus Digital Library); 'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia

  4. in lonely lands l. 2

    The phrase sets the bird in high, uninhabited country, far from human life. Solitude and height go together in the Romantic sublime, and the eagle here is a figure for isolated power. The soft l-alliteration of 'lonely lands' contrasts with the hard k-sounds of the line before, so that the first tercet moves from the grip of the bird to the empty space around it. Readers who take the eagle as an emblem of the poet or of the heroic individual find in this solitude a cost as well as a grandeur.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  5. Ring'd with the azure world l. 3

    'Ring'd' means encircled. From the eagle's height the blue of the sky and of the sea seem to merge into a single sphere around it, and the bird is placed at its centre, like a ruler in his domain. 'Azure', the heraldic and poetic word for sky-blue, came into English through Old French from Arabic and Persian 'lazward', the name of the blue stone lapis lazuli. 'World' is significant: the eagle's surroundings are not just a view but a whole cosmos.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. azure, n. and adj.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  6. Two rhymed tercets l. 3

    The poem consists of two triplets, each line rhyming with the others in its stanza ('hands / lands / stands', 'crawls / walls / falls'). The metre is iambic tetrameter, a shorter line than the pentameter usual for Victorian descriptive verse, which gives the poem compression and speed. The structure is carefully balanced: stanza one ends with the eagle standing, stanza two with it falling, so the two verbs of the final rhymes contain the poem's whole action. The repeated rhymes also give each stanza a strong closure that suits a fragment presented as complete in itself.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  7. The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls l. 4

    The line is a celebrated example of Tennyson's visual precision: from a cliff top, the moving surface of the ocean looks creased and slow, like skin, and seems to creep rather than surge. The point of view is the eagle's own, looking down. The verb 'crawls' reverses the usual grandeur of the sea, making it small, old and almost abject beneath the bird. Political readings take the sea as the common mass beneath a ruler; others stress the poem's sheer act of seeing.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  8. his mountain walls l. 5

    'His' makes the cliffs the eagle's property, and 'walls' turns natural rock into architecture: a castle from whose battlements a lord watches his territory. The image continues the poem's royal and military suggestion, from the 'hands' that clasp to the lord on his walls to the 'thunderbolt' that strikes. The verb 'watches' prepares for the swoop: the bird is not merely resting but surveying for prey.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  9. like a thunderbolt l. 6

    In classical myth the eagle is the bird of Zeus (Jupiter), the bearer of his lightning. Horace opens Odes IV.4 with the eagle as 'ministrum fulminis alitem', the winged servant of the thunderbolt. Tennyson's simile thus gives the bird a divine, Jovian force: its fall is both a swift natural plunge and a stroke from heaven. The simile also conveys speed and suddenness, after the slow stillness of the first five lines.

    Horace, Odes IV.4.1 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  10. And ... he falls: stoop or fall? l. 6

    'Falls' most naturally describes a falconer's 'stoop', the raptor's steep dive on its prey, and most readers take it so. But the word also carries a sense of downfall, and the poem stops at the moment of falling without showing the outcome. Readers who allegorise the eagle as a ruler read the close either as predation on those below or as the sudden decline of the mighty. Others insist on the poem as pure description. The subtitle 'Fragment' has been read both as a sign of incompleteness and as an ironic label for a perfectly finished miniature.

    'The Eagle (poem)', Wikipedia; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes
