Love

Mariana

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Poems (1843)

Love

With blackest moss the flower-plots
Were thickly crusted, one and all:
The rusted nails fell from the knots
That held the peach to the garden-wall.
The broken sheds look'd sad and strange:
Unlifted was the clinking latch;
Weeded and worn the ancient thatch
Upon the lonely moated grange.
She only said "My life is dreary,
He cometh not," she said;
She said "I am aweary, aweary;
I would that I were dead!"

Her tears fell with the dews at even;
Her tears fell ere the dews were dried
She could not look on the sweet heaven,
Either at morn or eventide.
After the flitting of the bats,
When thickest dark did trance the sky,
She drew her casement-curtain by,
And glanced athwart the glooming flats.
She only said "The night is dreary,
He cometh not," she said:
She said "I am aweary, aweary,
I would that I were dead!"

Upon the middle of the night.
Waking she heard the night-fowl crow:
The cock sung out an hour ere light:
From the dark fen the oxen's low
Came to her: without hope of change,
In sleep she seem'd to walk forlorn,

Till cold winds woke the gray-eyed morn
About the lonely moated grange.
She only said, "The day is dreary,
He cometh not," she said;
She said, "I am aweary, aweary,
I would that I were dead!"

About a stone-cast from the wall
A sluice with blacken'd waters slept,
And o'er it many, round and small,
The cluster'd marish-mosses crept.
Hard by a poplar shook alway,
All silver-green with gnarled bark:
For leagues no other tree did dark
The level waste, the rounding gray.
She only said, "My life is dreary,
He cometh not," she said;
She said, "I am aweary, aweary,
I would that I were dead!"

And ever when the moon was low,
And the shrill winds were up and away,
In the white curtain, to and fro,
She saw the gusty shadow sway.
But when the moon was very low,
And wild winds bound within their cell,
The shadow of the poplar fell
Upon her bed, across her brow.
She only said, "The night is dreary,
He cometh not," she said;
She said, "I am aweary, aweary,
I would that I were dead!"

All day within the dreamy house,
The doors upon their hinges creak'd;
The blue fly sung i' the pane; the mouse
Behind the mouldering wainscot shriek'd,
Or from the crevice peer'd about.
Old faces glimmer'd thro' the doors,

Old footsteps trod the upper floors,
Old voices call'd her from without.
She only said, "My life is dreary,
He cometh not," she said;
She said, "I am aweary, aweary,
I would that I were dead!"

The sparrow's chirrup on the roof,
The slow clock ticking, and the sound
Which to the wooing wind aloof
The poplar made, did all confound
Her sense; but most she loath'd the hour
When the thick-moted sunbeam lay
Athwart the chambers, and the day
Was sloping toward his western bower.
Then said she, "I am very dreary,
He will not come," she said;
She wept, "I am aweary, aweary,
Oh God, that I were dead!"

Study notes by the editors

  1. Source and date l. 1

    In Measure for Measure Mariana is betrothed to Angelo, and he deserts her when her dowry is lost at sea. The Duke mentions that 'at the moated grange resides this dejected Mariana' (3.1). Tennyson's poem, first published in Poems, Chiefly Lyrical (1830) with the epigraph 'Mariana in the moated grange' (not printed in the archive text), takes only that situation and leaves out the play's plot. Tennyson later said that the grange was no particular place but one that grew out of the music of Shakespeare's words. The poem opens on a landscape of decay with no human figure in it. Mariana herself first appears only in the refrain. J. S. Mill (1835) praised Tennyson's power of 'creating scenery, in keeping with some state of human feeling'.

    Shakespeare, Measure for Measure 3.1 (Folger Shakespeare); Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; Hallam Tennyson (ed.), The Works of Tennyson, Eversley edition (Macmillan, 1907–08), Tennyson's notes on 'Mariana'; J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835)

  2. peach to the garden-wall l. 4

    The archive prints the text of 1830–1850s, in which rusted nails fall from the ties that held a peach tree trained against a garden wall. In 1860 Tennyson revised the line to 'the pear to the gable-wall', a hardier fruit and a humbler building. The usual explanation is that this better fits the Lincolnshire fen country he had in mind. Either way the image of decay is exact. The espaliered tree, once kept in order, now sags loose from its supports, an emblem of a life that has lost its frame.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Mariana', ed. H. M. McLuhan, with notes; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  3. moated grange l. 8

    'Grange' first meant a granary or barn, then an outlying farm belonging to a monastery, and later any country house with farm buildings. A moat is a deep, wide ditch, usually water-filled, around a house. The phrase comes straight from Measure for Measure (3.1), and for Tennyson's readers it already suggested remoteness and confinement. The moat cuts Mariana off, and its stagnant water returns later as the sluice with 'blacken'd waters' (l. 38). The phrase closes the descriptive section of stanzas 1 and 3 (ll. 8 and 32), just before the refrain.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grange, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'moated, adj.'; Shakespeare, Measure for Measure 3.1 (Folger Shakespeare)

  4. Stanza and refrain st. 1–7

    Each stanza has an eight-line descriptive section (ababcddc) and then a refrain (efef) built on 'dreary' / 'said' / 'aweary' / 'dead'. The tetrameter is short and regular, and the refrain keeps coming back, so time seems to circle without moving forward, which is Mariana's experience. Small changes mark the passage of a day and night ('My life', 'The night', 'The day'). The last stanza breaks the pattern: 'very dreary', 'He will not come', 'She wept', 'Oh God'. The descriptive sections move from garden to house to bed and the house's interior, closing in on her.

    Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Mariana', ed. H. M. McLuhan, with notes

  5. He cometh not l. 10

    Mariana's only words in the first six stanzas are the refrain, which never names the absent lover. 'Cometh' is the old third-person form used in the Authorised Version and in ballads, so the plain statement sounds like a lament or a liturgy. The present tense ('cometh not') describes a habitual condition, not a single event. 'Aweary' is an archaic intensive of 'weary' (Macbeth 5.5 has 'I gin to be aweary of the sun'). The wish 'I would that I were dead' turns despair into a formula, repeated until the final stanza's direct cry to God.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aweary, adj.'; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  6. Tears and dews ll. 13–14

    The parallel 'Her tears fell with the dews at even; / Her tears fell ere the dews were dried' makes weeping as regular and impersonal as dew. Grief takes up the whole day. She cannot bear to look at 'the sweet heaven, / Either at morn or eventide'. The pathetic fallacy in this poem goes both ways. The landscape reflects her feeling and her feeling becomes a natural process. Mill singled the poem out for the way scenery carries emotion.

    J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835); Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  7. trance the sky l. 18

    'When thickest dark did trance the sky' uses 'trance' as a transitive verb, which is rare. One line of editors (E. K. Brown) takes it as a form of 'transe' or 'traverse', meaning darkness passes across the sky. Others (Beck and Snow) read it as 'entrance', meaning darkness holds the sky still as if under a spell. The second reading fits the poem's atmosphere of suspended time and Mariana's own half-dreaming state ('In sleep she seem'd to walk forlorn', l. 30). Ricks records the difficulty.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Mariana', ed. H. M. McLuhan, with notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trance, v.1'; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  8. glooming flats l. 20

    'Athwart' is a literary form meaning across, from side to side. 'Glooming' means growing dark and is a favourite Tennysonian participle. The 'flats' are low-lying level land such as fen and marsh. Tennyson grew up at Somersby in Lincolnshire, and the poem's level waste, fen, sluices and lone poplar are often traced to that landscape. It contrasts with the Italianate setting of the later 'Mariana in the South' (1832). Her glance across the flats is a look of waiting, but nothing comes over the horizon.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'athwart, adv. and prep.'; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; Hallam Tennyson (ed.), The Works of Tennyson, Eversley edition (Macmillan, 1907–08), Tennyson's notes on 'Mariana'

  9. night-fowl, cock ll. 25–27

    This stanza opens at midnight ('Upon the middle of the night'; the full stop after it is in the archive text) and moves through sounds Mariana hears in the dark: a crowing 'night-fowl', the cock singing 'an hour ere light', oxen lowing from the fen. 'Night-fowl' may mean a nocturnal bird, or the cock itself heard crowing in the dead of night. The cock's crow before dawn traditionally drives away ghosts, as in Hamlet 1.1. The 'old faces' of l. 66 later come back to haunt her all the same.

    Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.1.150–64 (Folger Shakespeare)

  10. gray-eyed morn l. 31

    Romeo and Juliet 2.3 opens with Friar Laurence's 'The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night'. Tennyson borrows the epithet, but the effect is cold. His morning is woken by 'cold winds' and brings only another dreary day. The Shakespearean echo suits a poem that is grown from a Shakespearean phrase. Here 'gray' (Tennyson's spelling) means pale, colourless dawn light.

    Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet 2.3.1 (Folger Shakespeare); Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  11. Sluice ll. 37–38

    A sluice is an artificial channel for water fitted with a gate, common in drained fen country. 'About a stone-cast from the wall' (a stone's throw) it lies 'blacken'd' and asleep, and the verb 'slept' carries over to the landscape the torpor that Mariana suffers. The blackness picks up the 'blackest moss' of l. 1. Water that ought to flow is held back, as her life is.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sluice, n.'; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  12. marish l. 40

    'Marish' (from Old French mareis) was by 1830 a poetic archaism for marsh. It gives the line a Spenserian, antique colour. The 'cluster'd' mosses, 'many, round and small', that 'crept' over the sluice continue the poem's imagery of slow overgrowth. Everything in Mariana's world moves only by creeping, crusting or decaying. The word belongs to the poem's fenland vocabulary, along with 'sluice', 'flats' and 'fen', and like them it fixes the setting in real, drained marsh country.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'marish, n.'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Mariana', ed. H. M. McLuhan, with notes

  13. The poplar ll. 41–44

    'Hard by' means close by, and 'alway' is an archaic form of 'always'. The poplar, 'silver-green with gnarled bark', is the only vertical feature in the 'level waste'. It returns twice. Its shadow falls 'Upon her bed, across her brow' (ll. 55–56), and its sound in the wind helps 'confound / Her sense' (ll. 76–77). Critics have read it in several ways: as a substitute for the absent male presence (some see a sexual threat in the shadow on the bed), as a figure of unceasing restless motion beside her stillness, or as a plain realist detail of fen country. The poem does not decide between them.

    Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alway, adv.'

  14. Winds bound in their cell ll. 53–56

    In Aeneid 1.52–63 Aeolus, king of the winds, holds the struggling winds in a vast cave and keeps them in chains and prison. Tennyson's phrase 'bound within their cell' may recall that classical picture. When the winds are free ('up and away') the shadow sways in the white curtain. When they are imprisoned and the moon is low, the shadow lies across Mariana. The movement from 'gusty shadow' to a shadow that falls 'Upon her bed, across her brow' is the poem's closest approach to bodily contact. For that reason critics read it as a ghostly stand-in for the absent lover, or a premonition of death.

    Virgil, Aeneid 1.52–63 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  15. Blue fly, mouse ll. 63–65

    The 'blue fly' is the bluebottle, a fly associated with rotting matter, and 'sung i' the pane' gives its buzzing against the glass a mock-musical sound. The mouse 'Behind the mouldering wainscot' (a wainscot is wooden panelling lining the lower walls of a room) shrieks and peers from the crevice. These close-up details made the poem famous for its 'Pre-Raphaelite' precision before the movement existed. Millais's painting Mariana (1851), exhibited with lines from the poem, draws on this world of detail. Mill praised the poem's 'vivid feeling of physical and spiritual dreariness'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wainscot, n.'; J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835); John Everett Millais, Mariana (1851), Tate

  16. Old faces, footsteps, voices ll. 66–68

    The repeated 'Old faces ... Old footsteps ... Old voices' is anaphora, and it fills the empty house with presences. They may be memories, ghosts of earlier inhabitants, or the effects of solitude on the mind. 'Glimmer'd' leaves it unclear whether anything is seen at all. This is the poem's psychological climax before the last stanza. The 'dreamy house' (l. 61) has become an image of her own mind.

    Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835)

  17. Thick-moted sunbeam ll. 77–80

    Motes are specks of dust made visible in a beam of light. The 'thick-moted sunbeam' slanting 'Athwart the chambers' is beautiful in itself, but to Mariana it shows how much time has passed and how much dust has gathered. The day is personified, 'sloping toward his western bower', the sun going to rest in its bower (a lady's chamber or a shady retreat), a pastoral and mythic touch. She 'loath'd' this hour above all, because the evening ends another day of waiting and begins the night.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mote, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bower, n.1'; Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'

  18. The final refrain ll. 81–84

    For the first time the refrain changes its key words. 'He cometh not' (habitual present) becomes 'He will not come' (a future certainty). 'She said' becomes 'She wept'. 'I would that I were dead' becomes 'Oh God, that I were dead'. The poem does not end with an event, only with a recognition. Readers who know Measure for Measure know that the deserted Mariana will in fact be married to Angelo in the end, but Tennyson cuts off any rescue. Critics have found here an early statement of Tennyson's poetry of paralysed waiting (compare 'The Lady of Shalott', 1832).

    Christopher Ricks (ed.), The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn (Longman, 1987), vol. 1, headnote and notes on 'Mariana'; Shakespeare, Measure for Measure 3.1 (Folger Shakespeare); J. S. Mill, review of Tennyson's Poems, London Review 1 (July 1835)
