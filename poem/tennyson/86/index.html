Religion

LVI

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Religion

‘So careful of the type?’ but no.
From scarped cliff and quarried stone
She cries ‘a thousand types are gone:
I care for nothing, all shall go.

Thou makest thine appeal to me:
I bring to life, I bring to death:
The spirit does but mean the breath:
I know no more.’ And he, shall he,

Man, her last work, who seem’d so fair,
Such splendid purpose in his eyes,
Who roll’d the psalm to wintry skies,
Who built him fanes of fruitless prayer,

Who trusted God was love indeed
And love Creation’s final law--
Tho’ Nature, red in tooth and claw
With ravine, shriek’d against his creed--

Who loved, who suffer’d countless ills,
Who battled for the True, the Just,
Be blown about the desert dust,
Or seal’d within the iron hills?

No more? A monster then, a dream,
A discord. Dragons of the prime,
That tare each other in their slime,
Were mellow music match’d with him.

O life as futile, then, as frail!
O for thy voice to soothe and bless!
What hope of answer, or redress?
Behind the veil, behind the veil.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam LVI: 'So careful of the type?' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section LVI in the standard numbering. It quotes the line of the previous section as a question and rejects it: Nature preserves not even the species. Bradley paraphrases the section as Nature producing life and death with equal indifference, so that man's spiritual hopes become a hideous contradiction, with no solution available on earth. It is the darkest section of the poem and contains its most quoted phrase.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. LVI

  2. scarped cliff and quarried stone l. 2

    'Scarped' means cut steeply, so as to show the layers of rock. Bradley explains that cliff and quarry are full of fossils of extinct species. Geology was a popular science in the 1830s and 40s, and Lyell's Principles of Geology, which Tennyson studied in 1837, discussed the extinction of species at length. The stones themselves 'cry' Nature's message. The cliff and quarry are therefore witnesses, and their testimony is given in Nature's own speech in the following lines.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scarp'; Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, 1837 (Lyell's Geology)

  3. a thousand types are gone l. 3

    Extinction had been established by Cuvier and discussed by Lyell; by the 1830s it was clear that many species had vanished entirely. Nature's speech, 'I care for nothing, all shall go', extends the threat from the individual to every kind, including humanity. The quotation marks give Nature a voice of her own, answering the poet's appeal. The geological evidence thus overturns the consolation offered in LV, and the poet's question becomes whether humanity is one more vanishing type.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 1; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, ll. 2-4

  4. I bring to life, I bring to death l. 6

    The balanced line recalls the divine declaration in Deuteronomy 32:39, 'I kill, and I make alive' (compare 1 Samuel 2:6). In Nature's mouth the formula of sovereign power has lost all moral meaning: she does both indifferently. The echo makes Nature a rival or parody of the God to whom the poet appeals. The parallel phrasing, 'I bring to life, I bring to death', gives the two acts equal weight, which is precisely the indifference the poet fears.

    Bible, King James Version, Deuteronomy 32:39; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI

  5. The spirit does but mean the breath l. 7

    Latin spiritus, like Greek pneuma and Hebrew ruach, means both breath and spirit. Nature reduces the higher sense to the physical one: the soul is merely respiration. Bradley takes 'spirit' to include all that the spirit does and promises, as listed in the following stanzas. 'I know no more' ends Nature's speech with a flat denial of anything beyond. The materialist reading of the word reduces the 'living soul' to physiology.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spirit'

  6. And he, shall he, / Man ... ll. 8-20

    The question begun at 'And he, shall he' does not reach its verb until 'Be blown about the desert dust' in stanza 5. In between come relative clauses listing what man has been: beautiful, purposeful, a singer of psalms, a builder of temples, a believer in love, a fighter for justice. The delay makes the possible answer, extinction like any fossil, fall with greater weight. The stanza break after 'shall he,' is one of the poem's most dramatic enjambments.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 21; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, ll. 8-20

  7. fanes l. 12

    'Fane' is a poetic word for temple, from Latin fanum, a consecrated place. 'Roll'd the psalm to wintry skies' imagines hymns sung into a cold, empty heaven. Bradley pairs 'wintry' with 'fruitless', and adds that the apparent fruitlessness of prayer had not shaken man's faith, which is what makes his possible extinction terrible. The clause belongs to the long catalogue of human achievements that delays the main verb.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fane'; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, ll. 11-12

  8. God was love indeed / And love Creation’s final law ll. 13-14

    'God is love' is from 1 John 4:8, 16. The creed is placed against what Nature seems to show. Bradley notes that the whole group L-LVI concerns the doubts that pain and evil cast upon the faith that love is creation's final law. The phrase 'Creation's final law' implies a law-governed universe, the language of science applied to theology. The juxtaposition with the bloody Nature of the next line is the poem's sharpest collision.

    Bible, King James Version, 1 John 4:8; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L-LVI

  9. Nature, red in tooth and claw l. 15

    The image is of Nature as a carnivore whose teeth and claws are red with the blood of its prey; it is set as a parenthesis inside man's creed, shrieking against it. The phrase became a slogan for the Darwinian struggle for existence, but the section was written before The Origin of Species (1859) and, according to the Memoir, the evolution sections were read to friends before the Vestiges (1844). The science behind it is the geology and natural history of the 1830s.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, p. 223 n.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, l. 15; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI

  10. ravine l. 16

    'Ravine' here is the old word for rapine or prey, from Latin rapina, 'plunder'; the KJV uses the form 'ravin' when the lion fills 'his dens with ravin' (Nahum 2:12). The word is commonly said to be stressed on the first syllable here, like 'ravin', to suit the metre. The modern sense of a narrow gorge would be meaningless here.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ravine'; Bible, King James Version, Nahum 2:12

  11. Be blown about the desert dust, / Or seal'd within the iron hills ll. 19-20

    Bradley glosses the 'iron hills' simply: like any other fossil. The two fates are dispersal and petrification: to be blown away like desert dust, or preserved as a specimen in stone, a fate that would put man alongside the extinct types of stanza 1. 'Iron' suggests both the hardness and the metallic ores of the rock. The two lines thus imagine the end of man in the very terms of the geology that opened the section.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 20; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, ll. 19-20

  12. Dragons of the prime, / That tare each other in their slime ll. 22-23

    'The prime' is the first age of the earth. Fossil saurians such as ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and Mantell's Iguanodon (announced 1825) were popular wonders in Tennyson's youth. 'Tare' is an archaic past tense of 'tear'. Bradley compares the 'monstrous eft' of Maud and explains the argument: the brutal dragons were at least in harmony with themselves, whereas man, with his spiritual longings, would be a discord if death ended all. Hence they 'were mellow music match'd with him'.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 21; The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, ll. 21-24

  13. A monster then, a dream, / A discord ll. 21-22

    Bradley stresses that the point is not that man would clash with the rest of Nature, but that he would be in discord with himself: a being made for immortality and doomed to extinction. 'A monster' means an unnatural combination, like the monsters of fable; 'a dream', an incoherent fantasy. The musical word 'discord' prepares the ironic 'mellow music' of the dragons.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 21; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI

  14. O for thy voice to soothe and bless! l. 26

    After the cosmic argument, the poet turns to Hallam. Bradley notes that the group of sections from L to LVI starts from and returns here to the poet's desire for present communion with his friend. 'Redress' means remedy for the injustice of a futile existence, as Bradley glosses it. The exclamations 'O life as futile, then, as frail!' and 'O for thy voice' move from argument to lament. The friend is now the only possible answerer, and the plea prepares the communion that the later sections, especially XCV, will seek.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. L-LVI and LVI, l. 27; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LVI, ll. 25-28

  15. Behind the veil l. 28

    Bradley says the general meaning is clear, that the veil cannot be lifted in this life, but the metaphor is uncertain. It may be the veil of the Temple before the holy of holies (Leviticus 16:2; Hebrews 6:19-20, the hope that 'entereth into that within the veil'), or the inscription said to stand on the veiled statue of Isis at Sais, 'no one has lifted my veil'; Bradley prefers Isis because Maud speaks of 'an Isis hid by the veil'. The doubled phrase ends the section without an answer.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LVI, l. 28; Bible, King James Version, Hebrews 6:19; Bible, King James Version, Leviticus 16:2
