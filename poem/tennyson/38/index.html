Nature

VII

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Nature

Dark house, by which once more I stand
Here in the long unlovely street,
Doors, where my heart was used to beat
So quickly, waiting for a hand,

A hand that can be clasp’d no more--
Behold me, for I cannot sleep,
And like a guilty thing I creep
At earliest morning to the door.

He is not here; but far away
The noise of life begins again,
And ghastly thro’ the drizzling rain
On the bald street breaks the blank day.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam VII: the house and the occasion l. 1

    Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest friend and the fiance of his sister Emily, died suddenly of a brain haemorrhage in Vienna on 15 September 1833, aged twenty-two. Tennyson wrote the lyrics of In Memoriam A. H. H. over the next sixteen years and published them anonymously in 1850. The 'dark house' is the Hallam family home at No. 67 Wimpole Street, where Henry Hallam the historian lived. Section VII belongs to the poem's earliest phase of raw grief, before the long movement toward consolation; it is regularly singled out as one of the finest lyrics in the sequence because its grief is fixed so exactly to a real place, hour and weather. The house is addressed directly (apostrophe), as if it could witness the mourner.

    'In Memoriam A. H. H.: 7', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), glossed note on Hallam's house (text and notes from Hallam Tennyson's 1908 edition); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  2. Wimpole Street l. 2

    Wimpole Street runs north-south through Marylebone in a long regular line of brick terraced houses. Tennyson does not describe it; he judges it, and the negative 'unlovely' withholds any picturesque appeal. The street is unlovely because the beloved is absent from it: the adjective records the mourner's mood as much as the architecture. The same street was later also the home of Elizabeth Barrett (No. 50, from 1838), whom Robert Browning was to court there, a coincidence of literary history rather than a reference in the poem. The phrase itself does not return: section CXIX revisits the street in calmer grief, and 'the long unlovely street' gives way to 'the meadow in the street'.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); 'In Memoriam A. H. H.: 7', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), glossed note on Hallam's house (text and notes from Hallam Tennyson's 1908 edition); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  3. Doors, where my heart was used to beat l. 3

    The line remembers the anticipation of past visits: the poet's heart beat fast as he waited at these doors for Hallam's greeting. The doors are named, not the friend, so that the threshold itself becomes the place of memory and of exclusion. Tennyson deliberately reworks these lines much later in the poem. Section CXIX begins with the same words, 'Doors, where my heart was used to beat / So quickly', but there the speaker comes 'not as one that weeps': the city sleeps, he smells 'the meadow in the street', and he can again imagine the friend's hand. Reading the two sections together shows the sequence's slow movement from desolation to a qualified peace.

    Alfred Tennyson, In Memoriam (Project Gutenberg text); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020)

  4. A hand ... A hand: repetition across the stanza break l. 5

    The first stanza does not close: its sentence runs on, and the last words, 'waiting for a hand', are picked up at the start of stanza two. This figure (anadiplosis, the repetition of the end of one unit at the start of the next) mimics the reaching gesture the speaker can no longer complete. The hand is the most physical token of friendship in the poem, the greeting at the door; that it 'can be clasped no more' turns a social courtesy into the sign of death's finality. The hand motif recurs across In Memoriam, and the image of clasping a hand returns in CXIX, where the speaker imagines it again.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  5. Behold me l. 6

    'Behold' is the language of the Bible and of formal address, and it gives the house a kind of personhood. The speaker cannot be seen by his friend, so he asks the house to see him. The reason given, 'for I cannot sleep', is pointedly ordinary, almost a confession of weakness; the elevated verb and the plain excuse sit side by side. Insomnia and the night watch are recurrent states in the early sections of In Memoriam, where sleep is both longed for and resisted.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  6. Like a guilty thing l. 7

    In Hamlet I.i Horatio describes how the Ghost, at cockcrow, 'started like a guilty thing / Upon a fearful summons', fleeing the dawn. Wordsworth borrowed the phrase in the Intimations Ode, where 'our mortal Nature / Did tremble like a guilty thing surprised'. Tennyson reverses Shakespeare's situation: it is now the living man who haunts a house at dawn, while the dead friend is absent. The guilt is unexplained; readers have taken it as the furtiveness of an unseemly, obsessive visit, or as a survivor's guilt at being alive. The allusion also makes the mourner a ghost of his own former self, returning to a place where he no longer belongs.

    Shakespeare, Hamlet I.i (Folger Shakespeare text); William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality' (1807), stanza IX; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  7. At earliest morning l. 8

    The visit is timed at first light, the hour of the Gospel resurrection narratives: in Luke the women come to the sepulchre 'upon the first day of the week, very early in the morning' (Luke 24:1; compare Mark 16:2). The echo prepares for the next stanza's 'He is not here'. The time also links with the Hamlet allusion, where the Ghost flees at cockcrow. Dawn, the conventional moment of hope, will be emptied of promise in the final line, where the day 'breaks' only as a blank.

    Bible, King James Version, Luke 24:1-6; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  8. He is not here l. 9

    In Luke 24:6 the two men in shining garments (angels, as verse 23 calls them) tell the women at the tomb, 'He is not here, but is risen' (compare Mark 16:6, Matthew 28:6). Tennyson quotes only the first half. The phrase of Easter consolation becomes a bare statement of absence, and the expected 'but' is supplied instead by the city: 'but far away / The noise of life begins again'. The pointed truncation is one of the most discussed effects in the section, since it measures how far the mourner is, at this stage of the poem, from the faith he will later reach. Some readers hear a faint hope that Hallam is 'far away' in heaven, immediately undone by the syntax.

    Bible, King James Version, Luke 24:6; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  9. The noise of life begins again l. 10

    The distant sound of carts, trades and voices marks the start of a London working day. 'Again' points to repetition: each day life recommences as though nothing had happened. The world's continuing is the hardest fact of bereavement in the early sections of In Memoriam, and here it fills the space where resurrection should have been. The verb 'begins' also quietly answers 'He is not here': something does rise, but it is the impersonal noise of the city, not the friend.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  10. ghastly l. 11

    'Ghastly' is etymologically connected with 'ghost' and the old verb 'gast', to terrify; by the nineteenth century it chiefly meant 'deathly pale, like a ghost or corpse' as well as 'horrible'. Both senses work here. The adverbial placement, at the head of the line, makes the whole dawn spectral, continuing the ghost-language of 'like a guilty thing'. The dawn that should bring light brings only a grey, dead pallor through the drizzle.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. ghastly, adj.; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  11. On the bald street breaks the blank day l. 12

    The poem is in regular iambic tetrameter until the last stanza, where the metre roughens. The final line crowds stresses together ('bald street breaks', 'blank day'), so that it reads almost as two pairs of heavy beats; commentators scan it as a sequence of trochees and spondees. The plosive b's add to the effect of something dull and hard. 'Bald' means bare, without ornament or vegetation, and 'blank' (from French blanc, white) means empty, featureless, pale. The rhyme 'away / again / rain / day' is held together by the long a-sound. A day that 'breaks' usually promises renewal; here the verb sounds like damage.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. blank, adj.

  12. The In Memoriam stanza l. 4

    Each stanza has four eight-syllable lines rhymed abba. Tennyson did not invent this envelope quatrain (it appears earlier, for example in Lord Herbert of Cherbury's 'Ode upon a Question Moved', and Tennyson himself later learned that Ben Jonson and Sidney had used it), but his use made it known as the In Memoriam stanza. Because the fourth line returns to the first rhyme, the stanza seems to circle rather than advance, which suits the static brooding of this section, where the speaker literally returns 'once more' to the same door. Here, unusually, the first stanza runs on into the second without a full stop, so the form is strained by the speaker's urgency.

    Eric W. Nye, English 3500 course notes on In Memoriam (University of Wyoming), on the 'ABBA tetrameter quatrain'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Dark House, by Which Once More I Stand"', Interesting Literature (2020)
