Time & Brevity

Tears, Idle Tears

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Victorian Songs (1895)

Time & Brevity

Tears, idle tears, I know not what they mean,
Tears from the depth of some divine despair
Rise in the heart, and gather to the eyes,
In looking on the happy Autumn-fields,
And thinking of the days that are no more.

Fresh as the first beam glittering on a sail,
That brings our friends up from the underworld,
Sad as the last which reddens over one
That sinks with all we love below the verge;
So sad, so fresh, the days that are no more.

Ah, sad and strange as in dark summer dawns
The earliest pipe of half-awakened birds
To dying ears, when unto dying eyes
The casement slowly grows a glimmering square;
So sad, so strange, the days that are no more.

Dear as remembered kisses after death,
And sweet as those by hopeless fancy feigned
On lips that are for others; deep as love,
Deep as first love, and wild with all regret;
O Death in Life, the days that are no more.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song in The Princess title, l. 1

    The lyric appeared in the first edition of The Princess: A Medley (1847), a long narrative poem about a princess who founds a university for women. In Part IV a maid sings it among Ida's party; Ida answers by dismissing such backward-looking feeling and urging that the past be let go in favour of the future. Tennyson said the song came to him in autumn at Tintern Abbey, a place 'full for me of its bygone memories' (Memoir I, 253). Read in context, the song is a dramatic utterance whose melancholy is challenged; read alone, as it nearly always is, it becomes one of the purest lyrics of loss in English. Hill's 1971 anthology of Tennyson describes it as among his most Virgilian poems.

    Alfred Tennyson, The Princess: A Medley (1847), Part IV, ll. 1-60 (Wikisource text); Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir by His Son, 2 vols (Macmillan, 1897); Frederick L. Gwynn, 'Tennyson's "Tithon," "Tears, Idle Tears," and "Tithonus"', PMLA 67 (1952), citing Memoir I.253 and Knowles, Nineteenth Century 33 (Jan. 1893), 170; 'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  2. Why the tears? 'the passion of the past' l. 1

    Tennyson told James Knowles that the poem expressed a lifelong sense he called 'the passion of the past', the abiding in the transient. Cleanth Brooks, in a famous New Critical reading, argued that the poem dramatises the paradox of tears that are 'idle' (purposeless) yet spring from a 'divine' source, and that the speaker discovers their meaning as the poem proceeds. Graham Hough answered that the grief is deliberately undifferentiated, which also explains the absence of rhyme. Biographical critics have related the sorrow to Hallam's death (he was buried not far across the Severn at Clevedon) or, for the last stanza, to Rosa Baring, but the poem itself refuses a specific cause.

    James Knowles, 'Aspects of Tennyson II: A Personal Reminiscence', The Nineteenth Century 33 (Jan. 1893), as cited by Gwynn (p. 170); Frederick L. Gwynn, 'Tennyson's "Tithon," "Tears, Idle Tears," and "Tithonus"', PMLA 67 (1952), citing Memoir I.253 and Knowles, Nineteenth Century 33 (Jan. 1893), 170; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947); Graham Hough, 'Tears, Idle Tears', Hopkins Review 4 (1951), repr. in Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960); 'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough)

  3. idle l. 1

    The older senses of 'idle' (OED) include 'empty, vain, worthless' and 'without ground or foundation', as in 'idle words'. The tears are idle because nothing in the present scene, the 'happy Autumn-fields', explains them, and because weeping cannot recover what is lost. Brooks made the word central to his reading: the poem's task is to show that tears called idle in the first line are in fact deeply motivated, so that the adjective is ironically undone by what follows.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'idle, adj.'; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947)

  4. divine despair l. 2

    In Christian tradition despair, the loss of hope in God's mercy, is a grave sin, so 'divine despair' is a striking paradox. Readers have taken 'divine' to mean 'god-like, exalted', 'mysterious in origin', or 'springing from the soul's divine part', a longing that no earthly object can satisfy. Brooks treats the phrase as the first of the poem's paradoxes, holding together purposelessness and profundity. The tears 'rise' from 'the depth', an image of a spring welling upward, which anticipates the vertical images of ships rising and sinking in stanza 2.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'despair, n.'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  5. the happy Autumn-fields l. 4

    'Happy' carries its older sense of 'fortunate, prosperous' (compare Latin felix, used of fertile land). Autumn traditionally marks both fruition and decline, so the harvest scene holds together completeness and ending. Tennyson associated the song with an autumn visit to Tintern Abbey, the ruined Cistercian house in the Wye valley that Wordsworth had made a site of memory in 'Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey' (1798); critics compare the two poems' treatment of revisiting the past, Wordsworth's consoling, Tennyson's mournful.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir by His Son, 2 vols (Macmillan, 1897); 'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough); William Wordsworth, 'Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey' (1798)

  6. Refrain and unrhymed stanzas ll. 5, 10, 15, 20

    The poem is in unrhymed iambic pentameter, divided into four five-line stanzas; the closing phrase 'the days that are no more' acts as a refrain. Most of the songs in The Princess rhyme (though 'Now sleeps the crimson petal' and 'Come down, O maid' do not), and Hough asked why this one is unrhymed, arguing that rhyme would give too definite a shape to an indefinite grief. The refrain is varied by what precedes it: 'So sad, so fresh', 'So sad, so strange', and finally 'O Death in Life', so that a fixed phrase gathers new meaning. The phrase 'no more' was a favourite Tennysonian cadence of finality.

    Graham Hough, 'Tears, Idle Tears', Hopkins Review 4 (1951), repr. in Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; 'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough)

  7. Fresh ... Sad: the ship simile ll. 6-10

    Stanza 2 is built on a double simile. The first light catches the sail of a ship coming in, bringing friends back; the last light of sunset reddens over a ship that disappears over the horizon, carrying away 'all we love'. Memory is simultaneously vivid (fresh) and grievous (sad), because what it presents so vividly is gone. Brooks noted that the images are of arrivals and departures, of the past as both returning and receding. The pairing of 'first' and 'last' beams frames the stanza within a single day.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  8. underworld l. 7

    The OED records an older sense of 'underworld' as the region below the horizon or the antipodes, as well as the classical abode of the dead (Hades). Literally the ship climbs into view from beyond the curve of the earth. Yet the word inevitably suggests that the returning friends come back from death, which gives the line its poignancy: the image of reunion is shadowed by the impossibility of the dead returning. Editors gloss both senses; the doubleness is characteristic of the poem's method.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'underworld, n.'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  9. verge l. 9

    'Verge' means the edge or rim of something (OED), and was used poetically of the horizon. 'Sinks' describes a ship disappearing over the horizon, but it also suggests drowning, and 'all we love' turns the image towards death. Readers have connected the image with the ship that brought Hallam's body home from Trieste, which Tennyson mourns elsewhere (compare In Memoriam IX, 'Fair ship, that from the Italian shore').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'verge, n.1'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Alfred Tennyson, In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), section IX

  10. The deathbed dawn ll. 11-14

    The third stanza moves from sea to sickroom. In the dark of a summer dawn the first 'pipe' (piping song) of birds not yet fully awake reaches someone who will not see the day out. 'Strange' is the key word: the ordinary becomes unfamiliar because it is perceived for the last time. The speaker's relation to the past is thus likened to the dying person's relation to the present, sharpening the idea that the past is a kind of death within life, which the last line will name. Brooks admired the precision of the visual detail that follows.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  11. casement l. 14

    A casement is a window that opens on hinges (OED). The line records exactly how a window appears before sunrise in a dark room: not as a view but as a pale, glimmering shape. The verb 'grows' makes the light seem to develop slowly, matching the slow fading of the dying eyes. The monosyllabic, heavily stressed line enacts slowness. The image is frequently praised as an instance of Tennyson's exact observation used for emotional effect.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'casement, n.'; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947)

  12. hopeless fancy feigned / On lips that are for others ll. 17-18

    The final stanza compares the past to two kinds of kisses: those remembered after a loved one's death, and those merely imagined ('feigned', invented) by a lover without hope, on lips 'that are for others', that is, promised to someone else. 'Fancy' here means imagination, with a suggestion of love-fancy. Biographical critics, following Ralph Rader's work on Maud, have connected the lines with Tennyson's disappointed attachment to Rosa Baring in the mid-1830s; others caution against reducing the poem's deliberately universal grief to a single episode.

    'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough); Ralph Wilson Rader, Tennyson's 'Maud': The Biographical Genesis (Univ. of California Press, 1963); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  13. O Death in Life l. 20

    The final refrain replaces 'so sad, so strange' with an apostrophe: the days that are no more are 'Death in Life', a death experienced while living. Coleridge's Ancient Mariner (Part III) personifies 'The Night-mare LIFE-IN-DEATH', a condition of living on in suffering; Tennyson's reversal makes memory a presence of death within life. The phrase gathers the poem's paradoxes (idle/divine, fresh/sad, dear/deep) into one, and is often read as Tennyson's summary of the elegiac mood shared by In Memoriam and 'Tithonus'.

    S. T. Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798/1817), Part III; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Motivation of Tennyson's Weeper', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  14. Virgilian tears l. 3

    In Aeneid 1.462 Aeneas, seeing the Trojan War depicted on the walls of Juno's temple at Carthage, says 'sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt' ('there are tears for things, and mortal matters touch the mind'). Tennyson revered Virgil (see his ode 'To Virgil', 1882, which hails the Roman poet as majestic in his sadness at human fate), and the description of the song as among his most Virgilian poems rests on this shared sense of a sorrow belonging to existence rather than to one event. The line itself traces the physical movement of tears from 'heart' to 'eyes', unifying feeling and body.

    Virgil, Aeneid 1.459-63 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); 'Tears, Idle Tears', Wikipedia (summarising Hill 1971, Brooks, Hough); Alfred Tennyson, 'To Virgil' (1882)
