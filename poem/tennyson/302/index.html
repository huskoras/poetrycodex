Death & Dying

Crossing the Bar

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From The Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912)

Death & Dying

Sunset and evening star,
And one clear call for me!
And may there be no moaning of the bar,
When I put out to sea,

But such a tide as moving seems asleep,
Too full for sound and foam,
When that which drew from out the boundless deep
Turns again home.

Twilight and evening bell,
And after that the dark!
And may there be no sadness of farewell,
When I embark;

For tho' from out our bourne of Time and Place
The flood may bear me far,
I hope to see my Pilot face to face
When I have crost the bar.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Crossing the Bar: occasion l. 1

    Hallam Tennyson's Memoir records that his father wrote the poem in October 1889 on the way from Aldworth to Farringford on the Isle of Wight, after a serious illness; he had 'the moaning of the bar' in mind and showed the poem after dinner, saying it 'came in a moment' (Memoir II, 366, as quoted by Sexton). It was published in Demeter and Other Poems (1889). Shortly before his death in 1892 he told his son to put it at the end of all editions of his poems, and editors have done so since. Musical settings include those by Barnby, Parry and Stanford.

    Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366; 'Crossing the Bar', Wikipedia (citing R. W. Hill, ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton, 1971); Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir, 2 vols (Macmillan, 1897)

  2. one clear call l. 2

    The 'call' is a summons to depart, as a ship is called to sail; Sexton's notes gloss it as a summons to duty, here that of God. The opening lines name signs of ending (sunset, evening star, call) in short exclamatory phrases without verbs. The evening star (Venus seen after sunset, Hesperus) is the counterpart of the morning star that guides the Dawn in 'Tithonus'; here it signals the close of day.

    Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'evening star' n.

  3. the moaning of the bar l. 3

    A harbour bar is a ridge of sand or shingle across the mouth of a harbour or estuary, dangerous to cross in rough water or at low tide. Folklore made its moaning an omen of death at sea, as in Charles Kingsley's ballad 'The Three Fishers' (1851), whose refrain returns to the moaning of the harbour bar. Tennyson wishes for no such moaning: no lament, and no struggle in the crossing. The bar is the line between life (the harbour) and the open sea of death and eternity.

    Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366; Charles Kingsley, 'The Three Fishers' (1851), Representative Poetry Online; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bar' n.1 (bank of sand across a harbour mouth)

  4. such a tide as moving seems asleep ll. 5-6

    At high water the tide is deep enough over the bar for a ship to cross without the turbulence of breaking waves. The paradox 'moving seems asleep' and the phrase 'Too full for sound and foam' make death a gentle passage carried by a force larger than oneself. The tide that comes in from the ocean and then 'Turns again home' is the poem's model of life: it comes from the boundless deep and returns there.

    Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  5. the boundless deep ... Turns again home ll. 7-8

    'That which drew from out the boundless deep' is the life or soul, imagined as water drawn from the ocean that now returns. The idea that we come from and go back to God recalls Wordsworth's Ode: 'trailing clouds of glory do we come / From God, who is our home'. Tennyson often used 'the deep' for the mystery from which life comes (e.g. 'From the great deep to the great deep he goes' in 'The Coming of Arthur'). 'Turns again home' is set as a short line, giving it the weight of arrival.

    William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality' (1807), st. 5, Poetry Foundation text; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  6. stanza 3: parallel structure ll. 9-12

    Each half of the poem opens with a two-stress noun phrase ('Sunset and evening star' / 'Twilight and evening bell'), then an exclamation ('And one clear call' / 'And after that the dark!'), then a wish ('And may there be no...'). The poem alternates long and short lines, and its rhymes run abab. Critics have connected the swelling and shrinking line lengths with the motion of the tide. The 'evening bell' suggests the curfew or a church bell for evening prayer or for a death, deepening the funeral tone, and 'the dark' is the only plain statement of fear in the poem.

    'Crossing the Bar', Wikipedia (citing R. W. Hill, ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton, 1971); Robert W. Hill Jr., ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1971); Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366

  7. bourne l. 13

    'Bourne' (from French borne, boundary) means a limit or frontier (OED). Here 'our bourne of Time and Place' is the boundary of earthly life, the conditions of space and time that define mortal existence. The word inevitably recalls Hamlet's soliloquy on death, 'The undiscover'd country from whose bourn / No traveller returns' (Hamlet III.i), but Tennyson reverses Hamlet's dread: the flood carries him beyond the boundary, with hope.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bourn' n.2; Shakespeare, Hamlet III.i (Folger Shakespeare Library text)

  8. my Pilot face to face l. 15

    Tennyson explained the Pilot as 'that Divine and Unseen Who is always guiding us' (Memoir II, 366, as quoted by Sexton). The capital letter marks the divine reference. 'Face to face' recalls 1 Corinthians 13:12: 'For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known.' The conclusion is a statement of hope rather than certainty ('I hope'), which suits Tennyson's characteristically tentative faith, familiar from In Memoriam.

    The Bible, King James Version, 1 Corinthians 13:12; Melissa Sexton, ed., English Literature: Victorians and Moderns (open textbook), 'Crossing the Bar', notes quoting Memoir II, 366; 'Crossing the Bar', Wikipedia (citing R. W. Hill, ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton, 1971)

  9. When I have crost the bar l. 16

    A harbour pilot normally boards to guide a ship across the bar and disembarks once it reaches open sea, so some readers find the image inverted: the speaker expects to see the Pilot only after crossing. Tennyson's own explanation, as reported, was that the Pilot 'has been on board all the while, but in the dark I have not seen him' (quoted in Hill's Norton edition). On this reading the image is not about navigation at all but about recognition: the guide has been present throughout life and will be seen only beyond it. 'Crost' is an old spelling of 'crossed'.

    'Crossing the Bar', Wikipedia (citing R. W. Hill, ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton, 1971); Robert W. Hill Jr., ed., Tennyson's Poetry, Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1971); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Tennyson's "Crossing the Bar"', Interesting Literature
