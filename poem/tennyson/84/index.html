Religion

LIV

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Religion

Oh yet we trust that somehow good
Will be the final goal of ill,
To pangs of nature, sins of will,
Defects of doubt, and taints of blood;

That nothing walks with aimless feet;
That not one life shall be destroy’d,
Or cast as rubbish to the void,
When God hath made the pile complete;

That not a worm is cloven in vain;
That not a moth with vain desire
Is shrivel’d in a fruitless fire,
Or but subserves another’s gain.

Behold! we know not anything;
I can but trust that good shall fall
At last--far off--at last, to all,
And every winter change to spring.

So runs my dream: but what am I?
An infant crying in the night:
An infant crying for the light:
And with no language but a cry.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam LIV: 'Oh yet we trust' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section LIV in the standard numbering. Bradley summarises it as a trust that good will be the final goal of all evil and that no life is wasted, followed by the admission that we know nothing and have only blind longing. The 'Oh yet' answers section LIII, which had cautioned against the doctrine that evil may lead to good. The tentative 'somehow' is the key word: the hope has no account of its own grounds.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 1; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. LIV

  2. the final goal of ill l. 2

    The hope that 'good / Will be the final goal of ill' restates a long tradition of theodicy, the attempt to reconcile evil with a good God. Pope's Essay on Man (I.291-294) had asserted confidently that all 'partial evil' is 'universal good'. Tennyson's version is no assertion but a trust, and it is immediately extended to include animals as well as people, which Bradley notes is unusual in Tennyson.

    Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man (1733-34), Epistle I, ll. 289-294; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, ll. 9-12

  3. pangs of nature, sins of will, / Defects of doubt, and taints of blood ll. 3-4

    Bradley links each phrase to the preceding sections: bodily pain (L), sins of the will (LI, LIII), the weakness of doubt (L, LI), and inherited taint. 'Taints of blood' means hereditary defects, a phrase that may glance at the melancholy and illness of Tennyson's own family, which he feared he had inherited. The balanced pairs (noun 'of' noun) give the list the formality of a catechism, which the last stanza will undo.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, ll. 3-4; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV, ll. 3-4

  4. nothing walks with aimless feet l. 5

    The figure of creatures walking 'with aimless feet' expresses the fear of a purposeless universe, and its denial the hope of design. The 'That ... That ... That' construction of stanzas 2 and 3 piles up the content of the trust in clauses all dependent on 'we trust', so the articles of faith are grammatically subordinate to a feeling. The phrase sets the tone for the section's catalogue of hopes.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV, l. 5; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV

  5. cast as rubbish to the void l. 7

    The word 'rubbish' is startlingly blunt in an elegy: the fear is not only death but annihilation as waste. The poet is returning here to the question of life beyond the grave. 'Void' is empty space, the nothing that precedes creation. The whole stanza turns on waste: life destroyed, cast away, discarded, the vocabulary of a universe without economy or care.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'void'

  6. When God hath made the pile complete l. 8

    'Pile' can mean a large building or a heap. Following 'rubbish', the more natural sense is a builder's: when the edifice of creation is complete, no stone will have been wasted. The image makes God an architect working over time, consistent with the poem's interest in long, progressive processes. The implication that creation is still unfinished is one of the poem's hopeful ideas.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pile'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV, l. 8

  7. not a worm is cloven in vain l. 9

    The stanza recalls Christ's saying that not one sparrow 'shall fall on the ground without your Father' (Matthew 10:29). 'Cloven' means split, as by a spade or plough. Bradley notes that this stanza implies a trust that good will come to animals as well as men, an idea he could find nowhere else in Tennyson's poems. The worm and moth are deliberately humble cases to test the hope.

    Bible, King James Version, Matthew 10:29; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, ll. 9-12

  8. a moth with vain desire / ... shrivel'd in a fruitless fire ll. 10-11

    The moth and flame was an emblem of destructive desire; Shelley's lyric 'One word is too often profaned' speaks of 'The desire of the moth for the star', a parallel rather than a proven source. Here the moth's death is 'fruitless' unless it serves some end. 'Or but subserves another's gain': Bradley glosses 'but' as 'merely'; the hope is that the creature's suffering is not merely a means to some other creature's benefit, but somehow good for it too.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 12; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'subserve'

  9. Behold! we know not anything l. 13

    'Behold!' announces the reversal. Bradley cross-refers to the Prologue, 'We have but faith: we cannot know', written later but placed first. The thought also recalls St Paul, 'For we know in part' (1 Corinthians 13:9, 12). The speaker shrinks from 'we trust' to 'I can but trust', from the communal to the solitary. The confession prepares the section's famous closing image, in which even the language of hope collapses into a wordless cry.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 13; Bible, King James Version, 1 Corinthians 13:12

  10. At last--far off--at last, to all l. 15

    The repeated 'at last' and the parenthetical 'far off' make the line stall. Bradley connects 'at last' to the 'one far-off divine event' of the poem's Epilogue, toward which the whole creation moves. 'To all' extends salvation universally, an idea Tennyson later called 'the larger hope' (LV). The closing 'every winter change to spring' gives the hope a seasonal image, appropriate to a poem structured by its returning Christmases and springs.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 15; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV, ll. 14-16

  11. An infant crying in the night l. 18

    'So runs my dream' demotes everything before to a dream. The speaker then sees himself as an infant crying in the dark, and the Latin root of 'infant', infans, means 'unable to speak', which the last line spells out: 'with no language but a cry'. Bradley glosses the line as not even knowing what one cries for and compares the end of Herbert's 'The Collar', where the rebel hears a voice call 'Child!' The image also resembles St Paul's 'groanings which cannot be uttered' (Romans 8:26). It ironises the poem's own eloquence.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LIV, l. 20; Bible, King James Version, Romans 8:26; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LIV, ll. 17-20
