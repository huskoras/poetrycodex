Nature

The Kraken

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From The Early Poems of Alfred Lord Tennyson

Nature

Below the thunders of the upper deep;
Far, far beneath in the abysmal sea,
His antient, dreamless, uninvaded sleep
The Kraken sleepeth: faintest sunlights flee
About his shadowy sides: above him swell
Huge sponges of millennial growth and height;
And far away into the sickly light,
From many a wondrous grot and secret cell
Unnumber’d and enormous polypi
Winnow with giant arms the slumbering green.
There hath he lain for ages and will lie
Battening upon huge seaworms in his sleep,
Until the latter fire shall heat the deep;
Then once by man and angels to be seen,
In roaring he shall rise and on the surface die.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Kraken l. 4

    The kraken belongs to Scandinavian sailors' lore of a vast creature off the Norwegian coast, so huge it could be mistaken for islands. Pontoppidan, Bishop of Bergen, described it in his natural history of Norway (1752-53; English translation 1755), and Tennyson's own note points readers to Pontoppidan's account and to the Biographie Universelle. The OED dates the word in English from 1755. The poem appeared in Poems, Chiefly Lyrical (1830), when Tennyson was twenty. Ricks notes that he could also have met the creature in Leyden's 'The Mermaid' in Scott's Minstrelsy and in Croker's Fairy Legends; Paden argued that only the name comes from Pontoppidan, the apocalyptic shape from G. S. Faber's mythography. Tennyson's monster is no ship-swallower: it only sleeps.

    Farringford (Tennyson's house), 'The Kraken', Tennyson poems blog, quoting the author's note; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kraken'

  2. the thunders of the upper deep l. 1

    The poem opens by placing us below the world we know: the 'upper deep' is the stormy top layer of the sea, and its thunder is what the Kraken lies beneath. 'Deep' as a noun for the sea is biblical and Miltonic (Genesis 1:2, 'darkness was upon the face of the deep'). The spatial plan of the whole poem is set at once: the reader is drawn downward through 'Far, far beneath' to a place of stillness, and only at the end does the movement reverse, when the monster rises to the surface. The opening line also supplies the key rhyme ('deep') to which the last lines return.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 1:2; The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', l. 1

  3. abysmal l. 2

    'Abysmal' here keeps its root sense: the abyss is the bottomless deep, from Greek abyssos, 'without bottom'. In the Greek Old Testament abyssos translates the Hebrew tehom, the primeval waters of Genesis, so the word carries a hint of the chaos that preceded creation. The colloquial sense 'extremely bad' arrived only in the twentieth century. Paired with 'upper deep', it marks the double world of the poem, storm above and timeless dark below.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abysmal'; Bible, King James Version, Genesis 1:2

  4. antient, dreamless, uninvaded l. 3

    The archive keeps the archaic spelling 'antient' found in the 1830 text; it adds an antiquarian flavour suited to a creature older than history. The three adjectives slow the line and make the sleep itself the subject before we learn whose sleep it is: the grammatical subject, 'The Kraken', is held back until the next line, so the syntax enacts the long wait. 'Dreamless' denies the creature any inner life; 'uninvaded' implies a world no diver, ship or human knowledge has reached, a point that matters for the ending, where it will be seen only once.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ancient'; The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', l. 3

  5. faintest sunlights flee l. 4

    The plural 'sunlights' is unusual and suggests separate shafts or flickers of light rather than daylight as a whole. They 'flee', as though even light avoided the creature. Tennyson builds the Kraken entirely by what surrounds it: sunlight, sponges and polyps are described, the creature itself barely at all. A. W. Thomson, cited on the Victorian Web, observes that the monster is only inferred from its surroundings. The method is suggestion rather than description, and the 'shadowy sides' of the next line keep the creature indistinct.

    The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', ll. 4-5

  6. millennial growth l. 6

    'Millennial' means lasting or measured in thousands of years (Latin mille, 'thousand'). The sponges are as old as the sleep. The word also carries the apocalyptic sense of the millennium of Revelation 20, which the poem's close takes up with 'the latter fire'. In 1830, when Lyell's Principles of Geology was beginning to appear, such vast time-scales were newly in question; the Victorian Web notes on the poem set it against the geological debates that challenged the short biblical chronology. Tennyson places a mythic creature inside something like geological time.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'millennial'; The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Bible, King James Version, Revelation 20:1-6

  7. grot l. 8

    'Grot' is a shortened, poetic form of 'grotto' (Italian grotta, from Latin crypta), common in eighteenth-century and Romantic verse. Joined with 'secret cell' it gives the seabed a hidden architecture of caves and chambers, and 'cell' also suggests the cells of living organisms, appropriate to sponges and polyps. The line prepares the strange, half-animal, half-vegetable life of ll. 9-10. The diction is consciously poetic, part of the Romantic inheritance of the 1830 volume.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grot'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', l. 8

  8. polypi l. 9

    Greek polypous means 'many-footed', and in older usage 'polypus' named the octopus or cuttlefish; naturalists also used it for small tube-like animals such as coral polyps. Representative Poetry Online glosses the word here as squids, which suits 'giant arms' in the next line. Tennyson's choice sits ambiguously between the two senses, the tiny polyp made 'enormous'. Some have connected the kraken legend itself to sightings of giant squid, though that is a later rationalisation. 'Unnumber'd' (countless) stresses their profusion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'polypus'; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Kraken', gloss on 'polypi' (squids)

  9. Winnow with giant arms l. 10

    To winnow is to fan grain so the wind blows away the chaff; by extension, to beat the air or move as a fan does. Transferring the word to the undersea arms of the polypi gives their motion a slow, rhythmic, agricultural character and makes the water itself a kind of crop: 'the slumbering green' is the green-lit sea, sleeping like the Kraken. The image prepares the 'battening' of l. 12, another word that makes the deep a feeding ground.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'winnow'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', l. 10

  10. There hath he lain for ages and will lie l. 11

    The line looks back over 'ages' and forward ('will lie') to an end, and so begins the movement from description to prophecy. Commentators have compared the sonnet's structure to the Petrarchan turn, though the poem's rhyme pattern is irregular. The vastness of the time involved belongs to the same years as Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830-33), which made deep time a pressing subject, while the prophecy that follows comes from Christian apocalyptic. Thomson, cited on the Victorian Web, reads the poem as holding scientific and Christian time in balance.

    The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', ll. 11-15

  11. Battening l. 12

    To batten is to feed gluttonously, to grow fat on something. The best-known earlier use is Hamlet's taunt to his mother, 'batten on this moor' (III.iv), and the word has kept a pejorative tone of gross, parasitic feeding. That the creature feeds 'in his sleep' makes it more uncanny: it is passive and yet devouring. The 'huge seaworms' may recall the sea-serpent tradition and the biblical Leviathan, called 'that crooked serpent' in Isaiah 27:1.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'batten'; William Shakespeare, Hamlet, III.iv; Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 27:1

  12. the latter fire l. 13

    'Latter' means last, as in 'the latter days'. The image draws on New Testament apocalyptic: in 2 Peter 3:10-12 the heavens pass away and the elements 'melt with fervent heat', and in Revelation 8:8-9 a burning mountain is cast into the sea and a third of the creatures in it die; Revelation 21:1 promises a new earth where 'there was no more sea'. Ricks records Paden's argument that Tennyson combined the kraken with G. S. Faber's mythological theories, in which a great serpent is bound up with world catastrophe; this would explain the end by fire. The fire that heats the deep is the only thing strong enough to break the Kraken's sleep.

    Bible, King James Version, 2 Peter 3:10; Bible, King James Version, Revelation 8:8-9; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken', l. 13; The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks)

  13. once by man and angels to be seen l. 14

    The monster has been 'uninvaded' and unseen; it will be seen only once, at the end of time, by both human beings and angels. The line recalls Revelation 13:1, 'I ... saw a beast rise up out of the sea', and the Leviathan whom God will slay 'in that day' (Isaiah 27:1). Milton had already joined the Leviathan to the Norwegian seas: in Paradise Lost I the sleeping beast 'on the Norway foam' is mistaken by a sailor for an island. Tennyson's revelation is brief and fatal: being seen is the creature's end.

    Bible, King James Version, Revelation 13:1; Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 27:1; John Milton, Paradise Lost (1667), I.192-208 (Leviathan); The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks)

  14. Form: an irregular fifteen-line sonnet l. 15

    The Kraken has fifteen lines rather than fourteen, rhymed in an irregular pattern (given by Thomson as ABABCDDCEFEAAFE) closer to Petrarchan than Shakespearean models. The last lines return to the opening rhyme on 'deep' and 'sleep', so the poem circles back on itself before the final release. The closing line has six stresses instead of five, an alexandrine that slows the reading for the monster's death. Thomson, cited on the Victorian Web, called it one of Tennyson's few good sonnets. Some later readers have taken the sleeping creature as a figure for the buried imagination or the unconscious; that reading is modern, not Tennyson's.

    The Victorian Web, notes on 'The Kraken' (citing A. W. Thomson and Ricks); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Kraken'
