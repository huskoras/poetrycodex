The Lotos Eaters

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From The Early Poems of Alfred Lord Tennyson

Nature

This charming poem is founded on Odyssey, ix., 82 seq.

“On the tenth day we set foot on the land of the lotos-eaters who eat a
flowery food. So we stepped ashore and drew water.... When we had
tasted meat and drink I sent forth certain of my company to go and make
search what manner of men they were who here live upon the earth by
bread.... Then straightway they went and mixed with the men of the
lotos-eaters, and so it was that the lotos-eaters devised not death for
our fellows but gave them of the lotos to taste. Now whosoever of them
did eat the honey-sweet fruit of the lotos had no more wish to bring
tidings nor to come back, but there he chose to abide with the
lotos-eating men ever feeding on the lotos and forgetful of his
homeward way. Therefore I led them back to the ships weeping and sore
against their will ... lest haply any should eat of the lotos and be
forgetful of returning.” (Lang and Butcher’s translation.) But in the
details of his poem Tennyson has laid many other poets under
contribution, notably Moschus, Idyll, v.; Bion, Idyll, v.; Spenser,
Faerie Queen, II. vi. (description of the Idle Lake), and Thomson’s
Castle of Indolence.

“Courage!” he said, and pointed toward the land,
“This mounting wave will roll us shoreward soon.”
In the afternoon they came unto a land,
In which it seemed always afternoon.
All round the coast the languid air did swoon,
Breathing like one that hath a weary dream.
Full-faced above the valley stood the moon;
And like a downward smoke, the slender stream
Along the cliff to fall and pause and fall did seem.

A land of streams! some, like a downward smoke,
Slow-dropping veils of thinnest lawn, did go;
And some thro’ wavering lights and shadows broke,
Rolling a slumbrous sheet of foam below.
They saw the gleaming river seaward flow
From the inner land: far off, three mountain-tops,
Three silent pinnacles of aged snow,
Stood sunset-flush’d: and, dew’d with showery drops,
Up-clomb the shadowy pine above the woven copse.

The charmed sunset linger’d low adown
In the red West: thro’ mountain clefts the dale
Was seen far inland, and the yellow down
Border’d with palm, and many a winding vale
And meadow, set with slender galingale;
A land where all things always seem’d the same!
And round about the keel with faces pale,
Dark faces pale against that rosy flame,
The mild-eyed melancholy Lotos-eaters came.

Branches they bore of that enchanted stem,
Laden with flower and fruit, whereof they gave
To each, but whoso did receive of them,
And taste, to him the gushing of the wave
Far far away did seem to mourn and rave
On alien shores; and if his fellow spake,
His voice was thin, as voices from the grave;
And deep-asleep he seem’d, yet all awake,
And music in his ears his beating heart did make.

They sat them down upon the yellow sand,
Between the sun and moon upon the shore;
And sweet it was to dream of Father-land,
Of child, and wife, and slave; but evermore
Most weary seem’d the sea, weary the oar,
Weary the wandering fields of barren foam.
Then some one said, “We will return no more”;
And all at once they sang, “Our island home
Is far beyond the wave; we will no longer roam”.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The editor's headnote and the archive text headnote

    This text comes from J. Churton Collins's annotated edition of Tennyson's early poems (1900). His headnote quotes the Odyssey passage (Book 9) in the Butcher and Lang prose version and lists the poets Tennyson borrowed from. The poem first came out in Poems (published December 1832, dated 1833) and was heavily revised for Poems (1842). In both versions these five narrative stanzas lead into an eight-part 'Choric Song' sung by the mariners. That song is missing from the archive text, which therefore ends just as the men begin to sing. Students should read the full poem in a standard edition such as Ricks's. In the full poem the two parts set narrative against lyric, and a shared epic action against the private wish of each man to give up.

    J. Churton Collins, ed., The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson (Methuen, 1900), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; 'The Lotos-Eaters', Wikipedia (composition, 1832 and 1842 texts)

  2. 'Courage!' he said l. 1

    Tennyson never names Odysseus. The leader's cry of 'Courage!' and his gesture toward land are all we hear from him, and it is the last firm act of will in the poem. Everything after it slows, softens and dissolves. In Homer the episode takes a few lines. Odysseus sends three men inland, they eat the lotus and forget their homecoming, and he drags them back weeping and ties them under the benches. Tennyson keeps the arrival and the eating but drops the rescue. The poem ends with the men's decision, not their leader's. Read beside 'Ulysses' (written 1833), where the aged hero again urges his mariners onward, the two poems make a pair: in one the will gives out, in the other it persists.

    Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; J. Churton Collins, ed., The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson (Methuen, 1900), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  3. 'a land / In which it seemed always afternoon' l. 4

    Line 3 ends 'unto a land', rhyming with 'toward the land' in line 1: Tennyson rhymes a word with itself, a small formal sign of a world where nothing moves on. The verb 'seemed' matters too. The poem keeps saying how things 'seem' (ll. 9, 24, 32, 35, 41), so the enchantment works on the men's perception before it works on their will. Critics often read the landscape as the outward form of a mood, a kind of Romantic 'indolence' like Keats's in his 'Ode on Indolence'.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  4. 'seem' and the drugged perception l. 9

    Count the verbs of appearance: the stream 'did seem' to fall and pause (l. 9); 'all things always seem'd the same' (l. 24); the wave 'did seem to mourn and rave' (l. 32); the eater 'seem'd' asleep yet awake (l. 35); the sea 'Most weary seem'd' (l. 41). The narrative never says what the land is, only how it strikes the senses. This ties the descriptive stanzas to the drug. After the lotos, home and labour sound far off and the body's own heartbeat becomes 'music' (l. 36). Critics have linked this to Tennyson's lifelong interest in trance states. His Memoir reports his account of a waking trance he could bring on by repeating his own name.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (1897), vol. I, Tennyson's own account of his waking trance

  5. 'like a downward smoke' l. 8; repeated l. 10

    The slender stream falls so lightly that it looks like smoke moving the wrong way, downward. The paradox matches the poem's slack, reversed energies. The phrase comes back in the opening line of stanza 2 ('A land of streams! some, like a downward smoke'), a repetition that copies the pause-and-fall movement of the water. Commentators have linked the mountain scenery here, as in 'Oenone', to the summer of 1830, when Tennyson and Arthur Hallam went to the Pyrenees to carry money to Spanish rebels. That link is general, and the poem's landscape is a composite. The next line turns the falling water into gauze or 'lawn' (l. 11).

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (1897), vol. I, on the 1830 journey to the Pyrenees with Arthur Hallam; J. Churton Collins, ed., The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson (Methuen, 1900), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  6. lawn l. 11

    'Lawn' here is the cloth, not the grass: a fine, nearly transparent linen. The OED's suggested origin is the town of Laon in France, a centre of linen weaving. The metaphor turns the waterfalls into 'slow-dropping veils', delicate and slow. The next lines set this against streams that 'broke' through light and shadow into 'a slumbrous sheet of foam'. Even the violent water is drowsy ('slumbrous'). The veil image also prepares for the poem's interest in screening and half-seeing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lawn' (the fabric); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  7. three mountain-tops ... of aged snow ll. 15-17

    The view travels inland from the shore to 'three silent pinnacles of aged snow', lit red by the sunset. 'Aged' makes the snow ancient and permanent, outside the drowsy present of the valley. The mountains are silent, as the whole landscape is muffled. Some readers see Alpine or Pyrenean memory here; there is no snow-capped triple peak in Homer's brief episode, so this is Tennyson's addition. The scene is a painterly, Turner-like composition of moonlight, sunset and mist at once. The coexistence of sun and moon becomes explicit at l. 38.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (1897), vol. I, on the 1830 journey to the Pyrenees with Arthur Hallam

  8. Up-clomb l. 18

    'Clomb' descends from the Old English strong verb climban (past clamb) and was already archaic in Tennyson's time. The OED records it surviving in dialect and poetry, notably in Milton (Paradise Lost 4.192, of Satan: 'So clomb this first grand thief into God's fold'). With the prefix 'Up-' it gives a deliberately antique, Spenserian colouring to the stanza. That suits a poem whose form and diction look back to The Faerie Queene. The 'woven copse' is a dense thicket whose branches interlace.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'climb, v.'; John Milton, Paradise Lost 4.192; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  9. galingale l. 23

    The word first named an aromatic root from the East (Alpinia, used as a spice), and only later the native English sedge Cyperus longus, whose root smells similar. Chaucer's Cook uses 'galyngale' as a spice in the General Prologue. Tennyson's 'slender galingale' is clearly a plant growing in the meadow, so the sedge is meant. The word's sound and Eastern origin still add perfume and remoteness. Ricks's notes discuss the word. Tennyson cared about naming plants exactly, and his botanical terms are rarely only decorative.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'galingale'; Geoffrey Chaucer, General Prologue, Canterbury Tales, l. 381 (the Cook); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  10. 'A land where all things always seem'd the same!' l. 24

    The line is built from sameness: 'all', 'always', 'same', with alliterating l- and s- sounds. Change is what makes a journey, a story or a homecoming possible. A land without it offers rest, but also the end of the Odyssey's narrative of return (nostos). Victorian readers often read the poem as a debate about duty and withdrawal; for 1842 Tennyson added a Choric Song stanza on the wives and homes left behind and rewrote the last stanza around Lucretian gods careless of mankind. Critics disagree on how far Tennyson condemns the mariners. Some read the poem as moral warning; others, as a sympathetic statement of a real longing for rest.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; 'The Lotos-Eaters', Wikipedia (composition, 1832 and 1842 texts)

  11. the Lotos-eaters l. 27

    In Odyssey 9 the Lotus-eaters mean no harm: they simply share their food, and whoever tastes it forgets the way home. Herodotus (4.177) puts a people of this name on the coast of Libya and describes the lotus fruit as about the size of a mastic berry and as sweet as a date. Botanists often identify it with the jujube shrub Ziziphus lotus. Tennyson's eaters are 'mild-eyed' and 'melancholy', with 'dark faces pale' against the sunset. They are less tempters than living examples of what the mariners will become. The sadness is in them already.

    Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919); Herodotus, Histories 4.177 (trans. A. D. Godley, Loeb); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  12. Branches ... of that enchanted stem l. 28

    Flower and fruit together on one branch is an old mark of the earthly paradise or enchanted garden. Spenser's Garden of Adonis (Faerie Queene 3.6) has 'continuall spring, and haruest' at once. Homer's lotus is simply a 'flowery food'. Tennyson makes the gift ritual: 'whereof they gave / To each'. The syntax then spills over the line ending ('whoso did receive of them, / And taste'), so the reader slides into the effect as the eater does. Note the biblical cadence of 'whoso did receive of them'.

    Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene 3.6.42; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  13. thin voices and the beating heart ll. 34-36

    The drug turns attention inward. The sea seems to 'mourn and rave / On alien shores', so the real world is now foreign. A friend's voice is 'thin' like the voices of the dead; in Homer (Odyssey 11, and Iliad 23.101) the shades speak in thin, gibbering cries. The paradox 'deep-asleep he seem'd, yet all awake' describes a trance. The last line hands the poem's music over to the body: 'music in his ears his beating heart did make'. Some critics treat this as an image of the lyric poet's own self-absorption.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Homer, Iliad 23.100-101 (the shade of Patroklos departs 'gibbering'); Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919)

  14. 'Between the sun and moon upon the shore' l. 38

    Stanza 1 set a 'full-faced' moon above the valley, and stanza 3 a sunset 'linger'd low adown'. A full moon does rise as the sun sets, so the picture is accurate, but Tennyson uses it symbolically. The mariners sit on the shore between elements and times, neither at sea nor inland, neither working nor yet asleep. The yellow sand and yellow down repeat a golden palette. The line is the stillest in the poem, the moment before the decision.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; J. Churton Collins, ed., The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson (Methuen, 1900), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  15. Father-land ... child, and wife, and slave ll. 39-40

    'Father-land', with its hyphen, translates the Greek idea of patris, the land of one's fathers. The list 'child, and wife, and slave' names the Homeric household (oikos), in which slaves were part of the family. Note that home is now an object of reverie, pleasant to dream of, not a goal. The adversative 'but evermore' turns at once to weariness, with 'weary' said three times (ll. 41-42). Penelope and Telemachus wait at the end of the epic; these men choose the dream over the return.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919)

  16. 'the wandering fields of barren foam' l. 42

    Homer often calls the sea atrygetos, usually translated 'barren' or 'unharvested' (for instance Iliad 1.316). The epithet sets the sea against the fruitful ploughland. Tennyson turns it into a metaphor: the sea is a field, but 'wandering' and made of foam, so it yields nothing for all the rowing. The line closes the triple 'weary', then strikes the main theme: labour without harvest. The Choric Song (not printed here) develops the contrast between toiling men and resting nature.

    Homer, Iliad 1.316 (halos atrygetoio); LSJ, s.v. atrygetos; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  17. Spenserian stanza and the long last line l. 9

    Tennyson borrows the nine-line stanza of The Faerie Queene, rhymed ababbcbcc, whose final line has twelve syllables. Its length makes each stanza close with a long, sinking cadence, suited to drowsiness: 'Along the cliff to fall and pause and fall did seem'. The choice also announces his debts: Spenser's Idle Lake and Thomson's Castle of Indolence, itself in Spenserian stanzas, are the main English models for lands of seductive idleness. In the Choric Song (not printed here) Tennyson moves to irregular lines and rhymes, so the contrast of forms shapes the whole poem.

    Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene (the Spenserian stanza, ababbcbcc with a final alexandrine); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; James Thomson, The Castle of Indolence (1748), canto 1

  18. Spenser, Thomson, Moschus and Bion headnote

    In Faerie Queene 2.6 the wanton Phaedria ferries Guyon across the Idle Lake to an island of ease and idleness. In Thomson's Castle of Indolence (1748) the wizard Indolence lures pilgrims into a land of 'drowsy-head'. The short poems of the Greek bucolic poets Moschus (Idyll 5) and Bion (Idyll 5 in some numbering) contrast the toil of the sailor's life with the peace of the land. Collins, an energetic hunter of sources, documented such parallels at length; Tennyson disliked having his poems traced to sources in this way. Ricks's notes give a fuller, more cautious account.

    J. Churton Collins, ed., The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson (Methuen, 1900), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene 2.6; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'

  19. 'We will return no more' l. 43

    'Some one said' keeps the speaker anonymous: the decision becomes collective without anyone owning it. The men then sing together, 'Our island home / Is far beyond the wave'. Their island is Ithaca; 'home' is still theirs, but given up. Here the speaking voice shifts from the narrator to the chorus, and the full poem goes on to their song. Readers differ over whether Tennyson endorses the mariners' longing (a reading encouraged by the Choric Song's beauty) or exposes it as moral collapse (as many Victorian reviewers and the 1842 additions suggest). Many modern critics see the poem as holding both views in tension.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed. (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to 'The Lotos-Eaters'; 'The Lotos-Eaters', Wikipedia (composition, 1832 and 1842 texts); Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919)
