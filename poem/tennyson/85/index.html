LV

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From In memoriam

Religion

The wish, that of the living whole
No life may fail beyond the grave;
Derives it not from what we have
The likest God within the soul?

Are God and Nature then at strife,
That Nature lends such evil dreams?
So careful of the type she seems,
So careless of the single life;

That I, considering everywhere
Her secret meaning in her deeds,
And finding that of fifty seeds
She often brings but one to bear;

I falter where I firmly trod,
And falling with my weight of cares
Upon the great world’s altar-stairs
That slope thro’ darkness up to God;

I stretch lame hands of faith, and grope,
And gather dust and chaff, and call
To what I feel is Lord of all,
And faintly trust the larger hope.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In Memoriam LV: 'The wish, that of the living whole' l. 1

    In Memoriam A. H. H. (published anonymously in 1850) mourns Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend and his sister Emily's fiancé, who died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833. Its 131 sections and frame poems were written over some seventeen years. This is section LV in the standard numbering. Bradley's paraphrase: the wish that no life should fail beyond the grave seems a divine instinct, yet Nature is so careless of individual life that she gives it no support, and the poet can scarcely keep a dumb trust. 'The living whole' means all living things together.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LV; Susan Shatto and Marion Shaw, eds, Tennyson: In Memoriam (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), commentary on sect. LV

  2. The likest God within the soul l. 4

    The first stanza is a question: does not the wish for universal survival come from what is most like God in the soul? The thought recalls the creation of humanity 'in the image of God' (Genesis 1:27), and suggests an argument from desire: so strong and unselfish a longing must have a divine origin. The question form leaves the argument hopeful but not secure.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 1:27; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV

  3. Are God and Nature then at strife l. 5

    Nature is personified as a female agent with 'evil dreams' and 'secret meaning'. The opposition of a loving God to an indifferent Nature was sharpened by the geology Tennyson read in 1837, notably Lyell's Principles of Geology, according to his son's Memoir. The Memoir also notes that the 'evolution' sections were read to friends some years before Chambers's Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation (1844).

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, 1837 (Lyell) and p. 223 n. (sections on evolution read before the Vestiges); A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LV

  4. So careful of the type ... So careless of the single life ll. 7-8

    'Type' means the species or kind. Bradley quotes Buffon's remark that nature cares little for individuals and concerns herself only with the species. The antithesis 'careful ... careless' balances the two lines on one root. Section LVI will take up the line and reject even this consolation, since fossils show species too are destroyed. The opposition between species and individual was a commonplace of eighteenth-century natural history before it became Darwinian. For a mourner the individual is everything, so Nature's preference is a direct wound.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, ll. 7-8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'type'

  5. of fifty seeds / She often brings but one to bear ll. 11-12

    Bradley records Tennyson's own note that 'fifty' should have been 'myriad', though he never altered the text. The image of profuse seed and few survivors was a commonplace of natural history and political economy (Malthus's argument on population had made overproduction a familiar idea). It may also call up, ironically, the parable of the sower, where most seed falls on unfruitful ground (Matthew 13:3-8). Darwin later made such superabundance central to natural selection, after this poem was written.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, addenda on l. 11; Bible, King James Version, Matthew 13:3-8

  6. I falter where I firmly trod, / And falling ll. 13-14

    Bradley compares Sophocles' Antigone (853-855), where the chorus tells Antigone that, going to the extreme of daring, she has stumbled against the high throne or step of Justice. Tennyson transfers the image to faith: the poet stumbles on stairs he once climbed securely. 'Weight of cares' explains the fall; doubt is felt as a burden. The movement from firm treading to faltering and falling is the section's turning point, from argument to helpless gesture.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, ll. 14-15; Sophocles, Antigone 853-855, trans. R. C. Jebb (1891), Perseus Digital Library

  7. the great world's altar-stairs / That slope thro' darkness up to God ll. 15-16

    The image makes creation a sanctuary and life an ascent toward an altar hidden in the dark. It recalls Jacob's ladder reaching to heaven (Genesis 28:12), and Bradley compares a passage in The Princess (VII) about scaling 'the shining steps of Nature' with man. Here, though, the steps slope 'thro' darkness': the way to God is real but unlit.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, l. 16; Bible, King James Version, Genesis 28:12; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LV, ll. 15-16

  8. lame hands of faith l. 17

    'Lame' transfers the weakness of legs to hands. The phrase may recall Hebrews 12:12, 'lift up the hands which hang down, and the feeble knees', an exhortation to the faint-hearted. The verbs 'stretch', 'grope', 'gather', 'call' describe a blind man's search, the bodily form of doubt. Each verb is weaker than the last, so the syntax itself loses strength as the section approaches its faint close. The hands that once clasped Hallam's (section VII) now grope at nothing.

    Bible, King James Version, Hebrews 12:12; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LV, l. 17

  9. gather dust and chaff l. 18

    Bradley glosses the phrase as what the poet gets 'in trying to reason', and compares The Two Voices, 'a dust of systems and of creeds'. Chaff is the husk winnowed from grain and blown away, a biblical image of the worthless (Psalm 1:4). The reasoning mind finds no nourishment; only feeling, 'what I feel is Lord of all', remains. Section LVI will make Nature's own speech an answer to this faint trust.

    A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, l. 18; Bible, King James Version, Psalm 1:4

  10. the larger hope l. 20

    Hallam Tennyson's Memoir (I.321) says the poet meant by 'the larger hope' that the whole human race would, perhaps through ages of suffering, be purified and saved, a hope wider than the orthodox doctrine of salvation for some and eternal punishment for others. The phrase became a watchword in Victorian debates over eternal punishment; Tennyson's friend F. D. Maurice lost his professorship at King's College London in 1853 partly over this question. Bradley wonders whether this is also the 'far-off divine event' of the Epilogue. The adverb 'faintly' keeps the trust weak.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir (London: Macmillan, 1897), vol. I, p. 321; A. C. Bradley, A Commentary on Tennyson's In Memoriam, 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1910), note on sect. LV, l. 20; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to In Memoriam LV, l. 20
