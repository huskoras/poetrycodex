Death & Dying

Break, Break, Break

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Poems (1843)

Death & Dying

Break, break, break,
On thy cold gray stones, O Sea!
And I would that my tongue could utter
The thoughts that arise in me.

O well for the fisherman's boy,
That he shouts with his sister at play!
O well for the sailor lad,
That he sings in his boat on the bay!

And the stately ships go on
To their haven under the hill;
But O for the touch of a vanish'd hand,
And the sound of a voice that is still!

Break, break, break,
At the foot of thy crags, O Sea!
But the tender grace of a day that is dead
Will never come back to me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date l. 1

    Tennyson's closest Cambridge friend, Arthur Hallam, died suddenly in Vienna on 15 September 1833, aged twenty-two. This lyric belongs to the first wave of grief that also produced the earliest sections of In Memoriam (1850), 'Ulysses' and 'Tithonus'. Tennyson's own note, printed in the Memoir, says it was 'made in a Lincolnshire lane at five o'clock in the morning, between blossoming hedges'; biographers place it in 1834 or early 1835, Martin linking it to a stay at Mablethorpe on the Lincolnshire coast. The archive's heading (1843) refers to the second edition of the 1842 Poems. The poem names no one: its power lies in withholding the dead man's identity, so that the grief is general as well as personal.

    Hallam Tennyson, Alfred Lord Tennyson: A Memoir by His Son, 2 vols (Macmillan, 1897); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Break, break, break', editorial note quoting Tennyson's annotation; Robert Bernard Martin, Tennyson: The Unquiet Heart (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem

  2. Metre and the opening spondees ll. 1, 13

    The poem is in four quatrains rhymed abcb, the pattern of the ballad stanza, but the lines are irregular trimeters mixing anapaests and iambs (for example 'O well for the fisherman's boy'). The first line is exceptional: three stressed syllables separated by pauses, so that each 'break' takes the time of a whole foot and mimics the separate fall of three waves. The command is addressed to the sea, but the word also points to the speaker's breaking heart and to the break in his life. The line returns at l. 13, giving the poem a refrain-like frame; the second time the waves break 'At the foot of thy crags', marking a hardening from stones to cliffs.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to the poem; W. David Shaw, Tennyson's Style (Cornell UP, 1976)

  3. The inexpressible ll. 3-4

    'I would that' is an archaic optative, 'I wish that'. The paradox, familiar in elegy, is that a poem announces its own inability to say what it feels. In Memoriam V develops the same idea ('I sometimes hold it half a sin / To put in words the grief I feel'). Critics have noted that the sea, which ceaselessly 'breaks' and makes sound, contrasts with the speaker's blocked utterance; nature can express itself, the mourner cannot. The poem's own extreme plainness of diction is a form of this reticence.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Alfred Tennyson, In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), section V (Wikisource)

  4. O well for ... ll. 5-8

    The construction is an old exclamatory form of congratulation, close to the biblical beatitude ('Blessed are ...'). The fisherman's boy shouting with his sister and the sailor lad singing are images of unselfconscious voice, set against the speaker who cannot 'utter'. They also represent ordinary life continuing indifferently around a private grief, a motif Tennyson returns to in In Memoriam VII, where 'on the bald street breaks the blank day'. The brother-and-sister pair may glance at Hallam's engagement to Tennyson's sister Emily, though this is a biographical inference, not something the poem states.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'well, adj.'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Robert Bernard Martin, Tennyson: The Unquiet Heart (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980)

  5. The stately ships ll. 9-10

    The ships 'go on' to their destination, a phrase that stresses continuation; the world's business proceeds. Their 'haven under the hill' is a sheltered harbour beneath a headland, but the phrase also carries the old association of the haven as the soul's rest after the voyage of life. Some readers hear in 'under the hill' a hint of burial; Hallam was buried in January 1834 in the church at Clevedon, Somerset, on a hill above the Bristol Channel, though there is no evidence Tennyson intended the reference. The contrast is between purposeful motion and the speaker's static lament.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Psalm 107:23-30 (KJV); Robert Bernard Martin, Tennyson: The Unquiet Heart (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980)

  6. haven l. 10

    'Haven' is an old English word for a harbour or port (OED), long used figuratively for a place of rest and safety. The Prayer Book and King James versions of Psalm 107 describe those who 'go down to the sea in ships' and are brought, after the storm, to 'their desired haven' (v. 30); the psalm was familiar from the Prayer Book, including its forms of prayer at sea. The allusion, if felt, deepens the gap: the ships reach their haven, the speaker finds no comfort.

    Psalm 107:23-30 (KJV); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'haven, n.'

  7. The vanished hand and the still voice ll. 11-12

    'O for' expresses longing ('would that I had'). Touch and voice are the two signs of Hallam's bodily presence that Tennyson repeatedly mourns: in In Memoriam VII he waits at Hallam's door in Wimpole Street 'A hand that can be clasp'd no more'. 'Vanish'd' (the archive keeps Tennyson's elided spelling) suggests disappearance rather than death, a sudden removal. A voice 'that is still' means silent; it may also hint at 'still' in the sense of 'continuing', a double meaning some readers find in Tennyson's usage, though most editors take it simply as 'silenced'. The line is among the best-known expressions of Victorian bereavement.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Alfred Tennyson, In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), section VII; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to the poem

  8. crags l. 14

    The Lincolnshire coast near Mablethorpe, where the poem has been associated, is flat and sandy, so the 'crags' are not a literal description; editors treat the seascape as composite or imagined. Within the poem, the change from the 'cold gray stones' of l. 2 to 'crags' raises the scale of the obstacle: the sea breaks against something it cannot alter, as the speaker's grief beats against the fact of death. The return of the opening line with a variation is a typical device of song.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; Robert Bernard Martin, Tennyson: The Unquiet Heart (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980)

  9. The tender grace of a day that is dead ll. 15-16

    'Grace' here means beauty of a delicate kind and the favour of a happier time; the religious sense of divine grace may hover, given the absence of any explicit Christian comfort. The personification of a 'day that is dead' transfers death from the friend to time itself. The closing line, 'Will never come back to me', refuses the movement from grief to hope that In Memoriam would later make. The poem shares this sense of the lost past with 'Tears, idle tears', whose refrain is 'the days that are no more'.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and notes to the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to the poem
