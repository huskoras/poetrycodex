The Charge of the Light Brigade

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Poetical Works (1899)

History & Politics

HALF a league, half a league,
Half a league onward,
All in the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.
'Forward, the Light Brigade!
Charge for the guns!' he said:
Into the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.

'Forward, the Light Brigade!'
Was there a man dismay'd?
Not tho' the soldier knew
Some one had blunder'd:
Their's not to make reply,
Their's not to reason why,
Their's but to do and die:
Into the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.

Cannon to right of them,
Cannon to left of them,
Cannon in front of them
Volley'd and thunder'd;
Storm'd at with shot and shell,
Boldly they rode and well,
Into the jaws of Death,
Into the mouth of Hell
Rode the six hundred.

Flash'd all their sabres bare,
Flash'd as they turn'd in air
Sabring the gunners there,
Charging an army, while
All the world wonder'd:
Plunged in the battery-smoke
Right thro' the line they broke
Cossack and Russian
Reel'd from the sabre-stroke
Shatter'd and sunder'd.
Then they rode back, but not
Not the six hundred.

Cannon to right of them,
Cannon to left of them,
Cannon behind them
Volley'd and thunder'd;
Storm'd at with shot and shell,
While horse and hero fell,
They that had fought so well
Came thro' the jaws of Death,
Back from the mouth of Hell,
All that was left of them,
Left of six hundred.

When can their glory fade?
O the wild charge they made!
All the world wonder'd.
Honour the charge they made!
Honour the Light Brigade,
Noble six hundred!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: the charge at Balaclava, 25 October 1854 st. 1

    During the Crimean War, at the Battle of Balaclava (25 October 1854), the British Light Brigade charged down a valley at a Russian battery, under fire from three sides, after an order was misunderstood. Tennyson, Poet Laureate since 1850, wrote the poem at Farringford in a few minutes on 2 December after reading the Times; his note says the first report he read mentioned only 607 sabres and that the charge lasted about twenty-five minutes. It appeared in The Examiner on 9 December signed 'A. T.' He revised it heavily for Maud and Other Poems (1855), then produced a version printed for soldiers in the Crimea at Lady Franklin's suggestion. He recorded it on a wax cylinder in 1890.

    Tennyson's own note, Poems, annotated by the author, ed. Hallam Tennyson (London: Macmillan, 1908), II, as given by Representative Poetry Online; History Today, 'Publication of The Charge of the Light Brigade' (9 Dec. 1854); Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports); Edgar F. Shannon Jr and Christopher Ricks, '"The Charge of the Light Brigade": The Creation of a Poem', Studies in Bibliography 38 (1985)

  2. Half a league: dactylic metre ll. 1-2

    The opening repetition measures the length of the ride down the valley, roughly a mile and a half. The metre is the poem's chief effect: Tennyson called it dactylic (stressed syllable followed by two unstressed, 'HALF a league'), with short lines of two stresses, and most lines end in a falling cadence. His note denies that Drayton's ballad of Agincourt, with a similar short-lined stanza, was in his mind, and says the poem was founded on the phrase 'Some one had blunder'd'. The insistent beat, repeated phrases and refrains make the poem easy to memorise and recite.

    Tennyson's own note, Poems, annotated by the author, ed. Hallam Tennyson (London: Macmillan, 1908), II, as given by Representative Poetry Online; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'league'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade'

  3. the valley of Death l. 3

    The North Valley at Balaclava, along which the brigade rode, becomes a biblical landscape. The Psalmist walks 'through the valley of the shadow of death' fearing no evil because God is with him (Psalm 23:4). Tennyson drops the 'shadow': this valley is death itself. The phrase recurs as a refrain in the first three stanzas, then changes to 'the jaws of Death' and 'the mouth of Hell', and is finally passed through and left behind in stanza 5, so its variations chart the ride in and out.

    Bible, King James Version, Psalm 23:4; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', l. 3

  4. the six hundred l. 4

    Tennyson's note explains that the first account he read mentioned only 607 sabres; the figure is now usually put at around 670 men, of whom some 110 were killed and many more wounded or captured. The round number gives the brigade a collective, almost epic identity, as with Leonidas's three hundred at Thermopylae, and its refrain-like repetition ('the six hundred', 'Not the six hundred', 'Left of six hundred', 'Noble six hundred') tracks the losses without ever giving a casualty figure.

    Tennyson's own note, Poems, annotated by the author, ed. Hallam Tennyson (London: Macmillan, 1908), II, as given by Representative Poetry Online; Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004); Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports)

  5. 'Charge for the guns!' he said l. 6

    Lord Raglan, the commander, sent a written order to prevent the Russians carrying away captured guns on the heights; it reached Lord Lucan, commanding the cavalry, through Captain Nolan, and Lucan ordered Lord Cardigan's Light Brigade to attack the Russian battery at the end of the valley instead. Nolan, apparently trying to redirect the charge, was killed at its start. Historians still argue over where the blame lies. Tennyson's anonymous 'he said' compresses this chain of command into one voice and keeps the poem's attention on the men rather than the generals.

    Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004); Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M); Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports)

  6. Some one had blunder'd st. 2

    A Times article of November 1854 spoke of soldiers as victims of 'some hideous blunder', and Tennyson took the last words as the germ of his line; his own note says the poem was founded on the phrase. The compression into 'Some one had blunder'd', with its pointed anonymity, is Tennyson's own. The line was cut from the 1855 Maud volume, apparently in response to friends' criticism, and then restored within weeks. Its vagueness is pointed: it records blame without naming anyone, an awkward note for a laureate's celebration.

    Tennyson's own note, Poems, annotated by the author, ed. Hallam Tennyson (London: Macmillan, 1908), II, as given by Representative Poetry Online; Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M); History Today, 'Publication of The Charge of the Light Brigade' (9 Dec. 1854); Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports)

  7. Their's not to make reply, / Their's not to reason why st. 2

    'Their's', with the apostrophe, was an accepted older spelling of the possessive pronoun and appears in early printings; the archive keeps it. The three-line anaphora (make reply, reason why, do and die) states the code of unquestioning obedience. Readers divide on its tone: it can be read as straightforward praise of discipline, or as a more troubled acknowledgement that the men died for an error they had no means to question. Perry discusses the poem's mixed feelings about making heroic poetry from a military failure. 'Do and die' became proverbial, often misquoted as 'do or die'.

    Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'theirs'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', ll. 13-15

  8. Cannon to right of them st. 3

    The Light Brigade rode under fire from Russian guns on the Fedioukine Hills on one side, the Causeway Heights on the other, and the battery ahead. The triple repetition, right, left, front, makes the reader feel the enclosure. In stanza 5 the pattern returns with one change, 'Cannon behind them', the single word that tells us the survivors are now riding back. 'Volley'd and thunder'd', with its apostrophised past tenses, gives the guns a heavy double-stressed close.

    Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', ll. 18-21, 39-42

  9. Storm'd at with shot and shell st. 3

    'Shot' here means solid round shot fired from cannon; 'shell' a hollow projectile filled with powder that bursts. The alliterating pair became a stock phrase for heavy fire. 'Storm'd at' makes the bombardment a weather, a storm through which the riders pass, consistent with the thunder of l. 21. The line is repeated in stanza 5, where it is joined to 'While horse and hero fell', as the riders return through the same fire.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shot'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shell'

  10. the jaws of Death ... the mouth of Hell st. 3

    'Out of the jaws of death' occurs in Shakespeare's Twelfth Night (III.iv), and the devouring jaws of death are a much older commonplace. The 'mouth of Hell' recalls the hellmouth of medieval painting and mystery plays, a monstrous open maw into which the damned are driven. Together they turn the valley of stanza 1 into a living, swallowing creature. In stanza 5 the survivors come 'thro' the jaws of Death' and 'Back from the mouth of Hell', a descent and return like an epic katabasis.

    William Shakespeare, Twelfth Night, III.iv; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', ll. 24-25

  11. Flash'd all their sabres bare st. 4

    Stanza 4 is the climax and the only place where the brigade acts rather than suffers. The repeated 'Flash'd' and the rhymes bare / air / there quicken the pace. 'Sabring the gunners' records that the survivors did reach the battery and fought the artillerymen at their guns. 'Charging an army, while' ends on a weak word, so the line runs on breathlessly into the next. The stanza is longer than the others, as though the charge had broken the poem's pattern.

    Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', st. 4

  12. All the world wonder'd st. 4

    'Wonder'd' holds both admiration and stunned disbelief. The words exactly echo the King James Revelation 13:3, where 'all the world wondered after the beast', a resonance that, if felt, makes the wonder more ambivalent. The famous contemporary verdict of the French general Pierre Bosquet, 'C'est magnifique, mais ce n'est pas la guerre' (it is magnificent, but it is not war), expresses the same double response. The phrase returns in stanza 6 as a refrain, now unambiguously a call to admiration.

    Bible, King James Version, Revelation 13:3; Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M); Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004)

  13. Cossack and Russian st. 4

    Cossacks were frontier horsemen of the Don and other regions, serving as light cavalry in the Russian army; Cossack troops were among the Russian forces defending the battery at the end of the valley. Naming them alongside 'Russian' gives the enemy its only appearance in the poem, and it is at the moment of being broken: 'Reel'd' and 'Shatter'd and sunder'd' make the attackers' brief success vivid. The alliterating 'sabre-stroke' and 'sunder'd' bind the stanza's sounds together.

    Terry Brighton, Hell Riders: The Truth about the Charge of the Light Brigade (London: Viking, 2004); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Cossack'

  14. Then they rode back, but not / Not the six hundred st. 4

    Up to here each stanza has closed with 'Rode the six hundred'. Now the refrain is negated. The line ends on 'not', and the next line repeats it, a stammer that sounds like the poet catching his breath at the losses. The poem never states numbers of dead; it lets the altered refrain carry the fact. The device prepares the further change in stanza 5, 'Left of six hundred'.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', ll. 37-38; Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M)

  15. Cannon behind them st. 5

    Stanza 5 mirrors stanza 3 almost line for line, so the poem's shape imitates the ride down the valley and back. The variation 'behind' (for 'in front of') is the hinge. 'While horse and hero fell' replaces 'Boldly they rode and well', and 'Came thro' the jaws of Death' replaces 'Into the jaws of Death'. Readers who know the earlier stanza feel each loss as an alteration of the familiar.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', st. 5

  16. Refrain and its variations st. 5

    The poem is built on refrains whose small changes tell the story. 'Rode the six hundred' (stanzas 1-3) gives the advance; 'Not the six hundred' (4) the loss; 'All that was left of them, / Left of six hundred' (5) the remnant; 'Noble six hundred' (6) the commemoration, which restores the full number in honour. The short, indented lines (in some printings) work like the drumbeat of a ballad. Tennyson's own recording of 1890 stresses this incantatory quality.

    Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade'

  17. When can their glory fade? st. 6

    The question and exclamations shift the poem from narrative to the laureate's public voice: 'Honour the charge they made!' The poem chooses the soldiers' courage, not the command's error, as what must be remembered. Some readers find the shift a resolution; others feel it smooths over the 'blunder' of stanza 2. The poem's later life complicates the commemoration: Kipling's 'The Last of the Light Brigade' (1890) describes surviving veterans living in poverty while the poem was recited in their praise.

    Seamus Perry, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade: Making Poetry from War', in Victorian Poetry (open textbook, Texas A&M); Wikipedia, 'The Charge of the Light Brigade (poem)' (summarising Shannon and Ricks 1985 and the Times reports); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd ed., 3 vols (Harlow: Longman, 1987), notes to 'The Charge of the Light Brigade', st. 6
