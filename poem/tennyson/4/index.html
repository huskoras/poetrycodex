Time & Brevity

Tithonus

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From Enoch Arden, &c.

Time & Brevity

The woods decay, the woods decay and fall,
The vapors weep their burthen to the ground,
Man comes and tills the field and lies beneath,
And after many a summer dies the swan.
Me only cruel immortality
Consumes: I wither slowly in thine arms,
Here at the quiet limit of the world,
A white-hair'd shadow roaming like a dream
The ever-silent spaces of the East,
Far-folded mists, and gleaming halls of morn.

Alas! for this gray shadow, once a man--
So glorious in his beauty and thy choice,
Who madest him thy chosen, that he seem'd
To his great heart none other than a God!
I ask'd thee, 'Give me immortality.'
Then didst thou grant mine asking with a smile,
Like wealthy men who care not how they give.
But thy strong Hours indignant work'd their wills,
And beat me down and marr'd and wasted me,
And tho' they could not end me, left me maim'd
To dwell in presence of immortal youth,
Immortal age beside immortal youth,
And all I was, in ashes. Can thy love,
Thy beauty, make amends, tho' even now,
Close over us, the silver star, thy guide,
Shines in those tremulous eyes that fill with tears
To hear me? Let me go: take back thy gift:
Why should a man desire in any way
To vary from the kindly race of men,
Or pass beyond the goal of ordinance
Where all should pause, as is most meet for all?

A soft air fans the cloud apart; there comes
A glimpse of that dark world where I was born.
Once more the old mysterious glimmer steals
From thy pure brows, and from thy shoulders pure,
And bosom beating with a heart renew'd.
Thy cheek begins to redden thro' the gloom,
Thy sweet eyes brighten slowly close to mine,
Ere yet they blind the stars, and the wild team
Which love thee, yearning for thy yoke, arise,
And shake the darkness from their loosen'd manes,
And beat the twilight into flakes of fire.

Lo! ever thus thou growest beautiful
In silence, then before thine answer given
Departest, and thy tears are on my cheek.

Why wilt thou ever scare me with thy tears,
And make me tremble lest a saying learnt,
In days far-off, on that dark earth, be true?
'The Gods themselves cannot recall their gifts.'

Ay me! ay me! with what another heart
In days far-off, and with what other eyes
I used to watch--if I be he that watch'd--
The lucid outline forming round thee; saw
The dim curls kindle into sunny rings;
Changed with thy mystic change, and felt my blood
Glow with the glow that slowly crimson'd all
Thy presence and thy portals, while I lay,
Mouth, forehead, eyelids, growing dewy-warm
With kisses balmier than half-opening buds
Of April, and could hear the lips that kiss'd
Whispering I knew not what of wild and sweet,
Like that strange song I heard Apollo sing,
While Ilion like a mist rose into towers.

Yet hold me not for ever in thine East:
How can my nature longer mix with thine?
Coldly thy rosy shadows bathe me, cold
Are all thy lights, and cold my wrinkled feet
Upon thy glimmering thresholds, when the steam
Floats up from those dim fields about the homes
Of happy men that have the power to die,
And grassy barrows of the happier dead.
Release me, and restore me to the ground;
Thou seest all things, thou wilt see my grave:
Thou wilt renew thy beauty morn by morn;
I earth in earth forget these empty courts,
And thee returning on thy silver wheels.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Tithonus: myth, date and companion poem l. 1

    Tithonus, a Trojan prince, was loved by Eos (Aurora), goddess of the dawn. She obtained immortality for him but forgot to ask for eternal youth, so he aged without end. Tennyson began the poem as 'Tithon' in 1833, in the months after Arthur Hallam's death, as a pendant to 'Ulysses': where Ulysses longs to go on, Tithonus longs only to end. When Thackeray asked him for a contribution to the new Cornhill Magazine, Tennyson revised the old draft (finishing it in November 1859) and it appeared in February 1860. RPO records the family tradition that his sister Emily, Hallam's fiancee, prompted it through her grief. It is a dramatic monologue in blank verse addressed to the goddess at dawn.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Tithonus', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; 'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  2. the opening cadence ll. 1-6

    The poem opens with a dying fall: woods decay and fall, vapours weep their burden to the ground, man tills the field and then 'lies beneath' it, the swan dies 'after many a summer'. Each line completes a natural cycle, and the repetition 'the woods decay, the woods decay' makes the rhythm itself sound like slow decline. Line 5 then breaks the pattern: 'Me only cruel immortality / Consumes', with the emphatic object pronoun placed first. The whole poem is built on this contrast between natural mortality and Tithonus's unnatural exception. Aldous Huxley took the title of his novel After Many a Summer (1939) from line 4.

    'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  3. burthen l. 2

    'Burthen' is an older spelling of 'burden', a load (Old English byrthen). The vapours 'weep' their load of water to the ground, a personification that makes nature itself mourn. The OED also records 'burden' in the sense of the refrain or recurring theme of a song, and the opening lines do work like a refrain, repeating the idea of falling and ending.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'burden' n. (load; refrain of a song); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  4. after many a summer dies the swan l. 4

    Swans were believed to live very long, so the line chooses the longest-lived creature to make the point that every life has its term. The swan was also traditionally said to sing before death: in Plato's Phaedo Socrates says swans sing most beautifully when they know they are about to die, out of gladness at going to the god they serve (Phaedo 84e-85b). Tithonus's whole speech is in a sense a swan-song he is denied the right to finish.

    Plato, Phaedo 84e-85b, Perseus Digital Library; 'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others)

  5. cruel immortality ll. 5-6

    The fullest ancient account is the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite (ll. 218-238): Eos asks Zeus to make Tithonus deathless, but foolishly does not ask that he stay young. When his hair turns grey she leaves his bed though she still feeds and clothes him, and when he can no longer move she shuts him in a chamber, where his voice babbles on endlessly. Tennyson changes the story in two ways: Tithonus himself asks for immortality (l. 15), and the goddess grants it herself. 'Consumes' makes immortality a slow fire or disease eating him away.

    Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 218-238 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Perseus Digital Library); 'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Tithonus', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  6. the quiet limit of the world ll. 7-10

    Eos lives at the eastern rim of the world; in the Homeric Hymn she and Tithonus dwell 'by the streams of Ocean, at the ends of the earth' (l. 227). Tennyson's phrasing makes this a liminal place, at the 'limit' and in 'ever-silent spaces', between the human world and the divine. Tithonus is a 'white-hair'd shadow roaming like a dream', a ghost-like figure who belongs to neither world. 'Far-folded mists' and 'gleaming halls of morn' set the poem's colour scheme of grey and gold.

    Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 218-238 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  7. 'Give me immortality' ll. 15-17

    Unlike the Homeric Hymn, where Eos makes the request to Zeus, Tennyson makes Tithonus the petitioner, so the poem becomes a study of a wish fulfilled to the speaker's ruin. The simile 'Like wealthy men who care not how they give' catches the goddess's careless generosity: what costs her nothing costs him everything. William E. Cain, summarised in the Wikipedia article, reads Tithonus as receiving the curse of having his wish granted. The phrase 'that he seem'd / To his great heart none other than a God' shows the original fault as pride.

    Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 218-238 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Perseus Digital Library); 'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others)

  8. thy strong Hours l. 18

    The Horae (Hours or Seasons) keep the gates of heaven in the Iliad (V.749-751) and attend the chariot of the sun and dawn in later art and poetry. RPO explains that they are 'indignant' because the gift of immortality interfered with their ordinary work: they cannot end Tithonus, so they 'beat me down and marr'd and wasted me'. The personification turns time into a group of hostile agents, and the strong verbs make ageing an act of violence.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Tithonus', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Homer, Iliad V.749-751 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library)

  9. Immortal age beside immortal youth l. 22

    The line repeats the end of l. 21 ('immortal youth') and sets 'Immortal age' against it with 'beside' as a fulcrum. Its symmetry mirrors the couple's fixed, endless juxtaposition: the goddess renewed each morning, the man forever older. The next phrase, 'And all I was, in ashes', breaks off the rhythm with a mid-line pause, a reminder that 'Consumes' (l. 6) is a metaphor of burning.

    Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes; 'Tithonus (poem)', Wikipedia (summarising Markley, Cain and others)

  10. the silver star, thy guide l. 25

    The planet Venus seen before sunrise was the 'light-bringer' (Greek Phosphoros, Latin Lucifer), and so it 'guides' the Dawn. Here the star is reflected in the goddess's 'tremulous eyes that fill with tears', which places the scene at the moment before sunrise and makes her pity part of the daily cycle. Her tears are also the dew, an old association of Eos with dew and weeping (she mourns her son Memnon in later myth).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'morning star' and 'Lucifer'; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  11. kindly l. 29

    The older sense of 'kindly' (OED) is natural, in accordance with one's nature or kind; it comes from 'kind' meaning race or nature. Tithonus asks why anyone should wish to differ from his own natural species, mankind, which is mortal. The warmer modern sense (gentle, benevolent) is also present, so the phrase means both the race of men to whom he belongs and the race whose mortality now seems a kindness.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kindly' adj. (natural; of one's kind); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  12. goal of ordinance l. 30

    RPO glosses the phrase as 'the limit ordained'. 'Goal' keeps its older meaning of the post or boundary marking the end of a race (like the Latin meta), so life is pictured as a course whose end is set by decree ('ordinance'). Tithonus has passed beyond it into a place where no one 'should' go. The phrase 'as is most meet for all' (most fitting) presents mortality as justice rather than loss.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Tithonus', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'goal' n. (boundary, winning-post) and 'ordinance' n.

  13. the wild team ll. 39-42

    Homer names the Dawn's horses as Lampus and Phaethon ('Shining' and 'Blazing') in Odyssey XXIII.244-246, where Athena holds them back to lengthen the night for Odysseus and Penelope. Tennyson's horses 'love thee, yearning for thy yoke', and 'beat the twilight into flakes of fire', an image in which their hooves strike sparks from the dark and the first light breaks into flecks of cloud. The sensuous description of dawn makes the goddess beautiful and unreachable at the same moment she leaves him.

    Homer, Odyssey XXIII.241-246 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  14. The Gods themselves cannot recall their gifts l. 49

    Tithonus fears that a saying learnt long ago on earth is true. The idea goes back to Homer: Paris tells Hector that 'the glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside' (Iliad III.65). In the Homeric Hymn, Aphrodite cites Tithonus's case to explain why she will not ask immortality for her own lover Anchises. The line explains why the goddess weeps: she cannot undo her gift even if she wishes to.

    Homer, Iliad III.64-66 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 218-238 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Perseus Digital Library)

  15. if I be he that watch'd l. 52

    The parenthesis questions whether personal identity survives such change. Similar formulas of recognition and disbelief run through epic: Aeneas sees Hector's ghost 'how changed from that Hector' (Aeneid II.274, 'quantum mutatus ab illo'), and Satan addresses Beelzebub 'If thou beest he; but O how fallen! how changed' (Paradise Lost I.84), itself an echo of Virgil. The memories that follow (ll. 53-63), of the goddess's dawn transformation and her kisses, are among the most sensuous lines Tennyson wrote, and they belong to a self he no longer recognises.

    Virgil, Aeneid II.274, Perseus Digital Library; Milton, Paradise Lost I.84 (1674), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room

  16. Apollo's song and the walls of Ilion ll. 62-63

    Tithonus was a son of Laomedon and brother of Priam (Iliad XX.237). Laomedon's walls of Troy were built by Poseidon and Apollo (Iliad VII.452-453, XXI.441-457); later tradition said they rose to the music of Apollo's lyre, as in Ovid's Heroides, where Paris promises Helen she will see Ilion's walls 'built to the music of Apollo's lyre' (XVI.179-180). Tennyson used the same legend in 'Oenone'. The simile 'like a mist rose into towers' links the city's magical birth with the dawn mists of the poem.

    Homer, Iliad XX.237 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Ovid, Heroides XVI.179-180, Perseus Digital Library; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  17. cold my wrinkled feet / Upon thy glimmering thresholds ll. 67-68

    The 'thresholds' and 'portals' (l. 57) of the Dawn's palace are places of passage that Tithonus can never cross. He looks down at the 'steam' rising from fields around 'the homes / Of happy men that have the power to die', the mist of the human world at dawn. The repetition of 'cold' (three times in ll. 66-67) contrasts with the 'glow' of his remembered youth. In the Homeric Hymn Eos shuts him away behind shining doors; Tennyson keeps him at the door, looking out.

    Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 218-238 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  18. barrows l. 71

    A barrow (Old English beorg, hill, mound) is an earth mound raised over a grave, especially an ancient one. The comparative grades the human condition: men who can die are 'happy', the dead are 'happier', and the immortal Tithonus is the most wretched. The grass on the mounds suggests the earth reclaiming the body peacefully, which is what he asks for in the last lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'barrow' n.1; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  19. I earth in earth l. 75

    Tithonus asks to become what every mortal becomes: 'dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19), echoed in the Book of Common Prayer burial service ('earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust'). In the grave he will forget the goddess's 'empty courts' and her return each morning 'on thy silver wheels'. The poem ends with that return, so the cycle he cannot share is the last thing we see; the death he asks for is never granted within the poem.

    The Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:19; Book of Common Prayer (1662), The Order for the Burial of the Dead
