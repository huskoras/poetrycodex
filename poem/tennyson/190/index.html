Nature

Ulysses

by Alfred, Lord Tennyson 1809–1892

From The Early Poems of Alfred Lord Tennyson

Nature

This noble poem, which is said to have induced Sir Robert Peel to give
Tennyson his pension, was written soon after Arthur Hallam’s death,
presumably therefore in 1833. “It gave my feeling,” Tennyson said to
his son, “about the need of going forward and braving the struggle of
life perhaps more simply than anything in In Memoriam.” It is not the
Ulysses of Homer, nor was it suggested by the Odyssey. The germ,
the spirit and the sentiment of the poem are from the twenty-sixth
canto of Dante’s Inferno, where Ulysses in the Limbo of the Deceivers
speaks from the flame which swathes him. I give a literal version of
the passage:—

“Neither fondness for my son nor reverence for my aged sire nor the due
love which ought to have gladdened Penelope could conquer in me the
ardour which I had to become experienced in the world and in human vice
and worth. I put out into the deep open sea with but one ship and with
that small company which had not deserted me.... I and my companions
were old and tardy when we came to that narrow pass where Hercules
assigned his landmarks. ‘O brothers,’ I said, ‘who through a hundred
thousand dangers have reached the West deny not to this the brief vigil
of your senses that remain, experience of the unpeopled world beyond
the sun. Consider your origin, ye were not formed to live like Brutes
but to follow virtue and knowledge.... Night already saw the other pole
with all its stars and ours so low that it rose not from the ocean
floor’” (Inferno, xxvi., 94-126).

But if the germ is here the expansion is Tennyson’s; he has added
elaboration and symmetry, fine touches, magical images and magical
diction. There is nothing in Dante which answers to—

Yet all experience is an arch wherethro’
Gleams that untravell’d world, whose margin fades
For ever and for ever when I move.

or

It may be that the gulfs will wash us down:
It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles,
And see the great Achilles, whom we knew.

Of these lines well does Carlyle say what so many will feel: “These
lines do not make me weep, but there is in me what would till whole
Lacrymatorics as I read”.

It little profits that an idle king,
By this still hearth, among these barren crags,
Match’d with an aged wife, I mete and dole
Unequal laws unto a savage race,
That hoard, and sleep, and feed, and know not me.
I cannot rest from travel: I will drink
Life to the lees: all times I have enjoy’d
Greatly, have suffer’d greatly, both with those
That loved me, and alone; on shore, and when
Thro’ scudding drifts the rainy Hyades
Vext the dim sea: I am become a name;
For always roaming with a hungry heart
Much have I seen and known; cities of men
And manners, climates, councils, governments,
Myself not least, but honour’d of them all;
And drunk delight of battle with my peers,
Far on the ringing plains of windy Troy.
I am a part of all that I have met;
Yet all experience is an arch wherethro’
Gleams that untravell’d world, whose margin fades
For ever and for ever when I move.
How dull it is to pause, to make an end,
To rust unburnish’d, not to shine in use!
As tho’ to breathe were life. Life piled on life
Were all too little, and of one to me
Little remains: but every hour is saved
From that eternal silence, something more,
A bringer of new things; and vile it were
For some three suns to store and hoard myself,
And this gray spirit yearning in desire
To follow knowledge, like a sinking star,
Beyond the utmost bound of human thought.
This is my son, mine own Telemachus,
To whom I leave the sceptre and the isle—
Well-loved of me, discerning to fulfil
This labour, by slow prudence to make mild
A rugged people, and thro’ soft degrees
Subdue them to the useful and the good.
Most blameless is he, centred in the sphere
Of common duties, decent not to fail
In offices of tenderness, and pay
Meet adoration to my household gods,
When I am gone. He works his work, I mine.
There lies the port: the vessel puffs her sail:
There gloom the dark broad seas. My mariners,
Souls that have toil’d and wrought, and thought with me—
That ever with a frolic welcome took
The thunder and the sunshine, and opposed
Free hearts, free foreheads—you and I are old;
Old age hath yet his honour and his toil;
Death closes all; but something ere the end,
Some work of noble note, may yet be done,
Not unbecoming men that strove with Gods.
The lights begin to twinkle from the rocks:
The long day wanes: the slow moon climbs: the deep
Moans round with many voices. Come, my friends,
’Tis not too late to seek a newer world.
Push off, and sitting well in order smite
The sounding furrows; for my purpose holds
To sail beyond the sunset, and the baths
Of all the western stars, until I die.
It may be that the gulfs will wash us down:
It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles,
And see the great Achilles, whom we knew.
Tho’ much is taken, much abides; and tho’
We are not now that strength which in old days
Moved earth and heaven; that which we are, we are;
One equal temper of heroic hearts,
Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will
To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ulysses: occasion and date l. 1

    Tennyson drafted the poem within weeks of hearing (October 1833) that his closest friend Arthur Henry Hallam had died in Vienna. He later said it 'gave my feeling about the need of going forward, and braving the struggle of life' more simply than In Memoriam (Memoir I, 196). It was first printed in Poems (1842). The speaker is Ulysses (the Latin name of Homer's Odysseus), old and home again on Ithaca, but the poem's main source is not the Odyssey; it is Dante's Inferno XXVI, where Ulysses tells how he left home again to sail beyond the known world. Homer's own prophecy of a last journey (Odyssey XI, Tiresias) lies behind both. The poem is one of the founding examples of the Victorian dramatic monologue: a single voice, an implied audience, and a character revealed partly against his own intentions.

    Farringford (Tennyson's home), 'To Strive, to Seek, and Not to Yield', citing Memoir I, 196; H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  2. Ithaca l. 2

    The 'still hearth' and 'barren crags' are Ithaca, the island Ulysses rules. Homer's Odysseus calls it rugged, 'but a good nurse of young men', and longs for it through twenty years of war and wandering (Odyssey IX.27-28). Tennyson reverses that longing: the homecoming that is the climax of Homer's epic becomes the starting point of restlessness. 'Still' carries both 'quiet' and 'motionless', and 'idle' both 'unoccupied' and 'futile', so the opening line already frames domestic kingship as a kind of paralysis. Critics who read Ulysses as heroic stress this sense of confinement; those who read him ironically (e.g. Chiasson) note how contemptuous his account of home and people is.

    Homer, Odyssey IX.21-28 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; E. J. Chiasson, 'Tennyson's "Ulysses": A Re-Interpretation', University of Toronto Quarterly 23.4 (1954), 402-409; repr. in J. Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960)

  3. Penelope l. 3

    In the Odyssey, the reunion with Penelope is the emotional climax of the poem (Odyssey XXIII). Tennyson's Ulysses mentions her only as 'an aged wife' he is 'match'd' with, a phrase that has drawn much comment. The coldness comes from Dante: his Ulysses says that neither tenderness for his son, nor duty to his old father, nor the love owed to Penelope could overcome his ardour to gain experience of the world (Inferno XXVI.94-99). Readers who see the poem as a straightforward expression of courage tend to pass over the line; readers like E. J. Chiasson take it as evidence that Tennyson shows a man who neglects duty and the 'softer affections'.

    Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867); Homer, Odyssey XXIII (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); E. J. Chiasson, 'Tennyson's "Ulysses": A Re-Interpretation', University of Toronto Quarterly 23.4 (1954), 402-409; repr. in J. Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960)

  4. mete and dole l. 3

    'Mete' (Old English metan, to measure) and 'dole' (from Old English dal, a portion, as in 'dole out') both mean to allot in measured amounts. Paired, the near-synonyms make the verse itself sound like routine administration. 'Unequal laws' (l. 4) means laws that are uneven or unsuited to their subjects, or perhaps laws the people are not equal to; the phrase suggests Ulysses' impatience rather than tyranny. The line's heavy monosyllables and the enjambment across 'dole / Unequal' slow it down, enacting the boredom he complains of.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mete' v.1 and 'dole' v.1; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  5. hoard, and sleep, and feed l. 5

    The list of verbs recalls Hamlet's soliloquy after seeing Fortinbras's army: 'What is a man, / If his chief good and market of his time / Be but to sleep and feed? a beast, no more' (Hamlet IV.iv, Second Quarto passage). Hamlet goes on to say that God did not give us 'that capability and god-like reason / To fust in us unused', which anticipates Ulysses' refusal to 'rust unburnish'd' (l. 23). The 'savage race' are his own subjects, whose merely bodily life he contrasts with his hunger for knowledge; the line ends with the self-regarding 'know not me', a hint of the egotism critics have found in the speaker.

    Shakespeare, Hamlet IV.iv.33-39 (Folger Shakespeare Library text); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  6. drink / Life to the lees ll. 6-7

    'Lees' (OED) are the sediment that settles at the bottom of a cask or cup of wine. To drink 'to the lees' is to consume everything, even the last bitter part, and old age is precisely that last part. Shakespeare's Macbeth uses the same image despairingly after Duncan's murder: 'The wine of life is drawn, and the mere lees / Is left this vault to brag of' (Macbeth II.iii). Tennyson's speaker turns Macbeth's despair into appetite. The metaphor of drinking returns in 'drunk delight of battle' (l. 16), linking experience with intoxication.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lees' n.; Shakespeare, Macbeth II.iii (Folger Shakespeare Library text)

  7. the rainy Hyades l. 10

    The Hyades are a cluster of stars in the head of Taurus. Ancient writers linked their name with the Greek verb huein, 'to rain', because their rising and setting coincided with the rainy season (RPO note: they 'rise with the sun in spring at the rainy season'). Virgil twice gives them the epithet 'pluvias' (rainy) in the Aeneid (I.744, III.516), and Homer names them among the constellations on Akhilleus' shield (Iliad XVIII.486). 'Scudding drifts' are wind-driven sheets of rain and spray; 'vext' (vexed) means stirred up, agitated.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Virgil, Aeneid I.744 and III.516 ('pluviasque Hyadas'), Perseus Digital Library; Homer, Iliad XVIII.486 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library)

  8. I am become a name l. 11

    The phrase means both 'I have become famous' and 'I am now only a name', a reputation that has outlived the active self. Homer's Odysseus introduces himself to the Phaeacians as 'Odysseus, son of Laertes, known among men for all manner of wiles, and my fame reaches heaven' (Odyssey IX.19-20). The archaic perfect 'am become' (rather than 'have become') gives the line a biblical, ceremonial weight. The ambiguity matters: the speech that follows is an attempt to prove that he is still more than his legend.

    Homer, Odyssey IX.19-20 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'be' v. (perfect formed with be)

  9. cities of men / And manners ll. 13-14

    The proem of the Odyssey says of its hero that 'many were the men whose cities he saw and whose mind he learned' (Odyssey I.3). Horace rendered the line in the Ars Poetica as 'qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes' (he who saw the manners and cities of many men, l. 142), and Tennyson's 'manners' follows Horace's 'mores' (customs, ways of life). The catalogue that follows, 'climates, councils, governments', extends Homer's line into a Victorian survey of political and natural knowledge, and 'Myself not least' quietly places the speaker at the centre of what he has seen.

    Homer, Odyssey I.1-5 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Horace, Ars Poetica 141-142, Perseus Digital Library

  10. ringing plains of windy Troy l. 17

    'Windy' translates the Homeric epithet enemoessa, used of Ilios several times in the Iliad (e.g. III.305). 'Ringing' suggests the clash of bronze on the Trojan plain, and 'drunk delight of battle with my peers' recalls the Iliad's picture of the Greek chieftains as equals at war. The line is the only place where Ulysses looks back to the Trojan War itself, and its Homeric colouring sets up the closing reference to 'the great Achilles, whom we knew' (l. 64).

    Homer, Iliad III.305 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  11. I am a part of all that I have met l. 18

    The sentence can mean that Ulysses has been formed by all his encounters, or that he has entered into and changed them, or both. A related Romantic idea appears in Byron's Childe Harold III.72: 'I live not in myself, but I become / Portion of that around me'. Tennyson's version is less mystical: identity is the sum of experience, which is why stopping feels like dying. The next lines (the 'arch') then turn this accumulated past into an opening onto the future.

    Byron, Childe Harold's Pilgrimage III.72 (1816), Project Gutenberg; Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  12. experience is an arch ll. 19-21

    The image makes experience a frame or gateway: what one has lived through lets one glimpse what has not yet been lived, but the 'margin' of that untravelled world fades 'for ever and for ever' as one moves, as a horizon always recedes. The key word comes from Dante, whose Ulysses tells his crew not to deny themselves 'l'esperienza' of the unpeopled world behind the sun, and speaks of his ardour 'to become experienced of the world' (Inferno XXVI.98, 116-117). The image of experience as an arch is Tennyson's own; Dante supplies only the word and the appetite for it. The repetition 'For ever and for ever' turns infinite desire into rhythm.

    Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867)

  13. To rust unburnish'd l. 23

    'Burnish' means to polish metal by rubbing. Unused armour rusts; used, it 'shines'. The image strikingly matches a speech by Ulysses in Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida, urging Akhilleus back to action: perseverance 'keeps honour bright', while past deeds hang 'like a rusty mail / In monumental mockery' (III.iii). The same idea appears in the Hamlet passage echoed in l. 5 (reason must not 'fust in us unused'). The next sentence, 'As tho' to breathe were life', dismisses mere survival: life, for this speaker, is activity.

    Shakespeare, Troilus and Cressida III.iii (Folger Shakespeare Library text); Shakespeare, Hamlet IV.iv (Folger Shakespeare Library text); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'burnish' v.

  14. like a sinking star l. 31

    Grammatically, 'like a sinking star' can attach to 'knowledge' (to follow knowledge as one follows a star sinking in the west) or to 'this gray spirit' (the old man is himself a sinking star, near his end but still moving). Both readings are active in the line, and they combine the poem's two directions: decline toward death and pursuit of the unknown. The westward star prepares for the closing resolve 'To sail beyond the sunset' (l. 60). Dante's Ulysses, sailing south-west, sees the stars of the other pole while 'ours' sink so low they no longer rise above the sea (Inferno XXVI.127-129). 'Beyond the utmost bound of human thought' echoes the Pillars of Hercules, the 'bounds' Dante's Ulysses passes.

    Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867); Christopher Ricks, ed., The Poems of Tennyson, 2nd edn, 3 vols (Longman, 1987), headnote and line notes

  15. Telemachus ll. 33-43

    In Homer Telemachus grows up during his father's absence and helps him kill the suitors. Here Ulysses hands him 'the sceptre and the isle'. The praise ('Most blameless', 'decent not to fail / In offices of tenderness') can be read as sincere recognition that a different, quieter virtue suits the kingdom, or as faintly patronising: Telemachus is 'centred in the sphere / Of common duties', a phrase that contrasts with the father's boundless striving. Critics who stress irony, such as Chiasson, see here the speaker's contempt for ordinary duty; others read a genuine, if distant, blessing. 'He works his work, I mine' may echo Christ's 'My Father worketh hitherto, and I work' (John 5:17), giving the division of labour a scriptural ring.

    E. J. Chiasson, 'Tennyson's "Ulysses": A Re-Interpretation', University of Toronto Quarterly 23.4 (1954), 402-409; repr. in J. Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960); Homer, Odyssey XVI, XXII (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); The Bible, King James Version, John 5:17

  16. household gods l. 42

    'Household gods' translates the Latin Lares and Penates, the protective spirits of the Roman home and its store-cupboard; Aeneas famously carries the Penates out of burning Troy (Aeneid II.717). Applying them to Ithaca is a Romanising touch consistent with the Latin name 'Ulysses'. 'Meet adoration' means fitting worship: the dutiful son will keep up the domestic piety his father leaves behind, which marks the contrast between the household world and the open sea.

    Virgil, Aeneid II.717 (Penates), Perseus Digital Library; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'household god'

  17. My mariners ll. 45-49

    The address to 'My mariners' / 'Souls that have toil'd ... with me' is inconsistent with the Odyssey, where Odysseus' last companions drown after eating the cattle of the Sun and he alone reaches Ithaca (Odyssey XII.417-419). Tennyson takes the faithful, ageing crew from Dante: 'I and my companions were old and slow' when they reached the Pillars of Hercules (Inferno XXVI.106-107), and Ulysses rouses them with a short speech (the 'orazion picciola'). The final paragraph is thus a version of that speech. The change also opens a question: is Ulysses speaking to actual men on a shore, or to an imagined audience? Some critics note the poem never tells us.

    Homer, Odyssey XII.403-419 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  18. men that strove with Gods l. 53

    In Iliad V, Diomedes, with Athena's help, wounds Aphrodite and then Ares on the battlefield before Troy; Odysseus also endured the long enmity of Poseidon in the Odyssey. The line therefore reminds the mariners of a heroic age in which mortals could contend with divine powers. It also carries a note of hubris that Dante's version makes explicit, since his Ulysses is punished for exceeding the limits set for men.

    Homer, Iliad V.330-351, 855-863 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Homer, Odyssey I.19-21, 68-75 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867)

  19. smite / The sounding furrows ll. 58-59

    The Odyssey repeatedly describes the crew sitting in order at the benches and striking the grey sea with their oars (e.g. IX.103-104, XII.146-147). Tennyson keeps the formulaic order ('sitting well in order') and adds the agricultural metaphor of the sea's 'furrows', cut by the ship as a plough cuts earth; the plural also suggests the troughs between waves. Compare the slow, monosyllabic lines just before ('The long day wanes: the slow moon climbs'), where heavy stresses and pauses delay the action that this command finally sets going.

    Homer, Odyssey IX.103-104 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Homer, Odyssey XII.146-147 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library)

  20. the baths / Of all the western stars ll. 60-61

    Homer's cosmos is ringed by the river Ocean, into which the stars sink at their setting; the Great Bear alone 'has no part in the baths of Ocean' because it never sets (Iliad XVIII.489; Odyssey V.275). RPO's note glosses 'the baths' as the place where the stars seem to plunge into the ocean. To sail 'beyond the sunset' and these baths is therefore to go past the western boundary of the known world, where Greek myth also put the entrance to the underworld; 'until I die' makes clear that the voyage and the death are the same horizon.

    Homer, Iliad XVIII.487-489 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Homer, Odyssey V.272-275 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  21. the gulfs will wash us down l. 62

    RPO notes the Homeric idea that the Ocean river plunges into a vast western chasm leading toward Hades. In Dante's account the voyage ends exactly so: after five months, a mountain appears, a whirlwind strikes, and the sea closes over the ship 'as pleased Another' (Inferno XXVI.133-142). Tennyson's Ulysses names this possibility calmly, paired against the opposite hope in the next line. The double 'It may be' keeps both outcomes open and makes the courage of the speech depend on not knowing.

    H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; Dante, Inferno XXVI.90-142, trans. H. W. Longfellow (1867)

  22. the Happy Isles l. 63

    Hesiod describes the 'islands of the blessed' by deep-swirling Ocean at the ends of the earth, where some heroes of the Theban and Trojan wars live untouched by sorrow (Works and Days 167-173). Pindar places the virtuous dead on the Island of the Blessed and names Akhilleus among them, brought there by his mother Thetis (Olympian 2.68-80). RPO locates the isles beyond the Pillars of Hercules, in the Atlantic. The hope of reaching them thus doubles as a hope of a heroic afterlife.

    Hesiod, Works and Days 167-173, Perseus Digital Library; Pindar, Olympian 2.68-80, Perseus Digital Library; H. M. McLuhan, ed., 'Ulysses', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes

  23. the great Akhilleus, whom we knew l. 64

    Akhilleus died at Troy. In Odyssey XI Odysseus meets his ghost in the underworld, where Akhilleus famously says he would rather be a hired labourer on earth than king of all the dead (XI.488-491). Tennyson's Ulysses, by contrast, imagines meeting him in the Happy Isles, following the tradition (Pindar, Olympian 2) that Akhilleus was taken there. The quiet 'whom we knew' turns myth into personal memory and makes the comrades of Troy into the speaker's own dead, an echo, for many readers, of Tennyson's grief for Hallam.

    Homer, Odyssey XI.467-540 (Greek text and A. T. Murray trans., Perseus Digital Library); Pindar, Olympian 2.79-80, Perseus Digital Library; Farringford (Tennyson's home), 'To Strive, to Seek, and Not to Yield', citing Memoir I, 196

  24. To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield l. 70

    After a poem of hesitations and enjambments, the last line is metrically regular and end-stopped, a series of infinitives building to the negative 'not to yield'. 'Tho' much is taken, much abides' and 'that which we are, we are' prepare it with balanced, almost proverbial phrasing. The line has had a long public life: it was carved on the memorial cross to Scott's polar party on Observation Hill, Antarctica (1913), and has been used as a motto by schools and in public ceremonies. Readers divided over the speaker's character still often agree that this ending is the poem's most purely affirmative moment; ironic readers note that its resolution is directed toward death.

    Farringford (Tennyson's home), 'To Strive, to Seek, and Not to Yield', citing Memoir I, 196; Royal Museums Greenwich, Maritime Memorials: Scott memorial cross, Observation Hill (line chosen by Apsley Cherry-Garrard); E. J. Chiasson, 'Tennyson's "Ulysses": A Re-Interpretation', University of Toronto Quarterly 23.4 (1954), 402-409; repr. in J. Killham, ed., Critical Essays on the Poetry of Tennyson (1960)
