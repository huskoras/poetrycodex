The Mind

The Passionate Shepherd to His Love

by Christopher Marlowe 1564-1593

The Mind

Come live with me and be my love,
And we will all the pleasures prove
That hills and vallies, dales and fields,
Woods or steepy mountain yields.

And we will sit upon the rocks,
Seeing the shepherds feed their flocks
By shallow rivers to whose falls
Melodious birds sing madrigals.

And I will make thee beds of roses
And a thousand fragrant posies,
A cup of flowers and a kirtle
Embroidered all with leaves of myrtle.

A gown made of the finest wooll
Which from our pretty lambs we pull;
Fair-linËd slippers for the cold,
With buckles of the purest gold.

A belt of straw and ivy-buds,
With coral clasps and amber studs;
An if these pleasures may thee move,
Come live with me, and be my love.

The shepherd-swains shall dance and sing
For thy delight each May-morning:
If these delights thy mind may move,
Then live with me, and be my love.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Text, date and attribution l. 1

    The song circulated widely in manuscript and print. An incomplete four-stanza version appeared in The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), a miscellany published under Shakespeare's name. The fuller text, signed 'Chr. Marlow', appeared in Englands Helicon (1600), followed by the reply later ascribed to Ralegh. Izaak Walton, in The Compleat Angler (1653), has a milkmaid sing 'that smooth Song which was made by Kit Marlow', in a six-stanza text like the archive's. The archive's six stanzas correspond broadly to the Helicon text, with the final 'shepherd-swains' stanza. Editors such as Bowers and Maclure accept Marlowe's authorship. The lyric is among the most imitated poems in English, as Forsythe's survey shows.

    Englands Helicon (London, 1600), Bodleian 'Digital Miscellanies' records: the Shepherd song signed 'Chr. Marlow', the reply signed 'Ignoto'; Izaak Walton, The Compleat Angler (1653), ch. 2 of the Project Gutenberg text: the milkmaid sings 'that smooth Song which was made by Kit Marlow' and her mother 'an answer to it, which was made by Sir Walter Raleigh in his younger days' (Project Gutenberg); Thomas Percy, Reliques of Ancient English Poetry (1765), headnote to 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love' and the Nymph's reply; Fredson Bowers, ed., The Complete Works of Christopher Marlowe, 2nd edn (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1981), vol. 2, text and notes on 'The Passionate Shepherd'; R. S. Forsythe, 'The Passionate Shepherd and English Poetry', PMLA 40 (1925)

  2. Come live with me: the pastoral invitation l. 20

    The 'invitation to love' with a list of rustic gifts descends from Theocritus' eleventh Idyll, where the Cyclops Polyphemus woos the nymph Galatea, and from its imitations in Virgil's second Eclogue (Corydon to Alexis) and Ovid's Metamorphoses 13, where Polyphemus offers Galatea fruits, flocks and pets. In each case the classical lover is rejected, a pattern that gives the genre an undercurrent of irony which Ralegh's reply exploits. Marlowe strips the form of its comic monster and makes it smooth and seductive.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.789-869 (Polyphemus' wooing-song to Galatea), trans. A. S. Kline; Virgil, Eclogue 2 (Corydon's invitation to Alexis), trans. A. S. Kline; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  3. Form: tetrameter couplets st. 1

    Each stanza consists of two rhyming couplets in iambic tetrameter (eight syllables, four stresses), a song metre that suits a poem meant to be sung and its persona's simple speech. The refrain-like return of 'Come live with me and be my love' at the end of stanza 5 and its variant in stanza 6 frame the poem. Walton called it 'smooth', and the regularity is part of the persuasion: nothing in the metre hints at time or difficulty.

    Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'; Izaak Walton, The Compleat Angler (1653), ch. 2 of the Project Gutenberg text: the milkmaid sings 'that smooth Song which was made by Kit Marlow' and her mother 'an answer to it, which was made by Sir Walter Raleigh in his younger days' (Project Gutenberg)

  4. prove l. 2

    'Prove' here has its older sense, to test by experience, to try (OED, prove, v.), as in 'the proof of the pudding'. The verb has an erotic undertone of trying pleasures together. Ralegh's reply picks up the vocabulary of pleasure and moving ('might me move') to question whether such pleasures last. The first-person plural 'we will' also makes the speaker's proposal sound like a shared plan already agreed, a quiet piece of persuasion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'prove, v.'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  5. steepy l. 4

    'Steepy' (OED, steepy, adj.) is a poetic variant of steep, used for its extra syllable. The catalogue of 'hills and vallies, dales and fields' is not a real landscape but an idealised pastoral world, Arcadia rather than England, offered entirely as a source of pleasure ('yields'). Ralegh's reply restores time and weather to this landscape. The lines name landscape features in pairs, a rhetorical habit of the song that gives its catalogue an effect of abundance and balance.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'steepy, adj.'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  6. shallow rivers ... madrigals ll. 7-8

    These two lines were famous enough to be quoted, garbled, by the nervous Welsh parson Sir Hugh Evans in The Merry Wives of Windsor (3.1), who mixes them with a metrical psalm. 'Falls' are small waterfalls, and the birds' song is imagined as human art. Ralegh's reply counters with rivers that 'rage' and a silenced nightingale. 'Shallow' is a soothing epithet here: the rivers are safe, clear and pleasant, quite unlike the flooding rivers of the reply.

    Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor 3.1, Sir Hugh Evans's garbled singing of the song (Folger Shakespeare text); Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  7. madrigals l. 8

    The madrigal, an Italian form of secular part-song for several voices, often on pastoral or amorous themes, became fashionable in England with the publication of Musica Transalpina (1588) and the works of Morley and others in the 1590s. Making birds sing madrigals turns nature into a courtly concert, a sign of the poem's sophisticated urban pastoral. The word is up-to-date, not rustic.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'madrigal, n.'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  8. beds of roses l. 9

    Stanzas 3-5 list the gifts the shepherd will make: beds of roses, posies, a cap of flowers, a kirtle, a gown, slippers, a belt. Most are made from flowers and leaves, hence short-lived, and some (gold buckles, coral clasps and amber studs) are luxuries no shepherd could afford. The mixture of floral and precious goods shows the fantasy at work. 'Beds of roses' combines flower-beds with beds to lie on. Ralegh's reply lists these gifts again one by one and declares that they 'Soon break, soon wither, soon forgotten'.

    Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'; R. S. Forsythe, 'The Passionate Shepherd and English Poetry', PMLA 40 (1925)

  9. posies l. 10

    'Posy' (OED, posy, n.) is a contracted form of 'poesy': a nosegay of flowers, and also a short verse inscription, especially inside a ring. Both senses fit a love-gift, and the second links flowers with verse, so the shepherd's posies are also his poems. Elizabethan posy-rings carried brief amorous mottoes, so a reader could hear in the word a promise of constancy as well as a gift of blossoms. The flowers, however, are as short-lived as the promise may prove, a point Ralegh's nymph makes in 'soon forgotten'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'posy, n.'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  10. kirtle l. 11

    A kirtle (OED, kirtle, n.1) was a woman's gown or skirt worn over a smock, sometimes under an outer gown. Here it is to be decorated ('Embroidered') with myrtle leaves, so even the textile gift is botanical. The 'cup of flowers' in the same line is a cap or garland of flowers, sometimes printed 'cap'. Ralegh's reply lists 'Thy cap, thy kirtle, and thy posies'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kirtle, n.1'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  11. myrtle l. 12

    In classical tradition the myrtle was Venus' plant (Ovid, Fasti 4, describes her bathing crowned with myrtle; Virgil's Eclogue 7 assigns the myrtle to 'lovely Venus'). Embroidering the beloved's dress with myrtle thus clothes her in the emblem of love. Contemporary readers would recognise the hint that the gift is an invitation to sexual love, not merely companionship. Renaissance mythographers repeated the association, and myrtle garlands appear in Elizabethan wedding and love imagery.

    Virgil, Eclogue 7.61-62 (myrtle sacred to Venus), trans. A. S. Kline; cf. Ovid, Fasti 4.139-144; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  12. A gown made of the finest wooll l. 13

    Wool was the basis of English wealth in the sixteenth century, and the shepherd's world was in reality one of enclosure and commerce. Marlowe's line ('Which from our pretty lambs we pull') presents wool-gathering as an idyllic act, without shearing or labour. The possessive 'our' already assumes the beloved shares his life. Ralegh's nymph mentions only 'Thy gown', but her reply's realism about seasons and age counters this idealised economy.

    Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'; R. S. Forsythe, 'The Passionate Shepherd and English Poetry', PMLA 40 (1925)

  13. purest gold ... coral clasps and amber studs ll. 17-18

    These ornaments, gold, red coral from the Mediterranean and amber from the Baltic, are expensive imported goods. Set beside the 'belt of straw and ivy-buds', they mix rustic simplicity with aristocratic luxury, revealing that the shepherd's world is an urban or courtly dream. Ralegh's nymph repeats the line almost word for word ('Thy coral clasps, and amber studs') before rejecting it.

    Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'; Michael Rudick, ed., The Poems of Sir Walter Ralegh: A Historical Edition (Tempe, AZ: Arizona Center for Medieval and Renaissance Studies / English Renaissance Text Society, 1999), text of the poem and textual apparatus

  14. An if l. 19

    'An' is an Elizabethan form of 'and' meaning 'if' (OED, an, conj.), and 'an if' is a pleonastic reinforced form, 'if indeed'. The conditional is the hinge of the poem's persuasion: the pleasures are offered on condition that they 'move' her. Ralegh's reply turns the condition back on him, beginning with its own 'If'. The same idiom is frequent in Elizabethan drama, including Shakespeare, and it gives the line a colloquial, spoken quality.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'an, conj.'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  15. May-morning; shepherd-swains ll. 21-22

    May-morning celebrations, with dancing, garlands and the gathering of greenery, were a traditional festival of courtship and fertility in England. 'Swains' are young country men or lovers (OED, swain, n.). The stanza, found in Englands Helicon, sets the invitation in a perpetual spring and ends with the varied refrain 'Then live with me, and be my love'. Its absence of time is precisely what the Nymph's reply exposes ('Time drives the flocks from field to fold').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swain, n.'; Englands Helicon (London, 1600), Bodleian 'Digital Miscellanies' records: the Shepherd song signed 'Chr. Marlow', the reply signed 'Ignoto'; Millar Maclure, ed., Christopher Marlowe: The Poems (London: Methuen, 1968), text and commentary on 'The Passionate Shepherd to his Love'

  16. Replies and imitations l. 24

    The poem became a model for answer-poems. The most famous, 'The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd', printed after it in Englands Helicon and ascribed to Ralegh by Walton, rejects the gifts point by point. John Donne's 'The Bait' recasts the invitation as fishing, and later poets (Herrick, C. Day Lewis and others) continued the game; Forsythe's essay surveys this tradition. The dialogue between the poems turns a simple lyric into a debate about pastoral idealism, time and desire.

    R. S. Forsythe, 'The Passionate Shepherd and English Poetry', PMLA 40 (1925); Izaak Walton, The Compleat Angler (1653), ch. 2 of the Project Gutenberg text: the milkmaid sings 'that smooth Song which was made by Kit Marlow' and her mother 'an answer to it, which was made by Sir Walter Raleigh in his younger days' (Project Gutenberg); Englands Helicon (London, 1600), Bodleian 'Digital Miscellanies' records: the Shepherd song signed 'Chr. Marlow', the reply signed 'Ignoto'; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Nymph's Reply' (first printed anonymously in Englands Helicon, 1600; ascribed to Ralegh by Walton, 1653)
