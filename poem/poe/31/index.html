Nature

To Helen (1831)

by Edgar Allan Poe 1809–1849

Nature

Helen, thy beauty is to me
Like those Nicean barks of yore,
That gently, o'er a perfumed sea,
The weary, wayworn wanderer bore
To his own native shore.

On desperate seas long wont to roam,
Thy hyacinth hair, thy classic face,
Thy Naiad airs have brought me home
To the glory that was Greece,
To the grandeur that was Rome.

Lo! in yon brilliant window niche,
How statue-like I see thee stand,
The agate lamp within thy hand!
Ah, Psyche, from the regions which
Are Holy Land!

1831.

* * * * *

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and text st. 1, l. 1

    Mabbott traces the poem to Poe's boyhood devotion to Jane Stith Stanard (d. 1824), mother of his schoolfriend Robert Stanard, whom Poe later called 'Helen' in a letter to Sarah Helen Whitman; he calls the story that Poe wrote it at fourteen absurd. It appeared in Poems (1831) and was revised over some twelve years; Mabbott uses the 1845 Raven and Other Poems text, which the archive follows under its first date. The 'Helen' of the title is Helen of Troy, the classical type of supreme beauty. This is not the 'To Helen' of 1848 ('I saw thee once'), addressed to Mrs Whitman.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, eapoe.org: texts of 'To Helen'

  2. Nicean barks of yore st. 1, l. 2

    A 'bark' is a ship. Mabbott thinks 'Nicean' most likely means 'victorious', from Greek Nike (Victory), noting that the spelling 'Nice' for Nike was common in the period; he also reports suggestions that it refers to Nice in France, and W. P. Trent's idea that Poe meant 'Phaeacian', the seafaring people whose ships carried Odysseus home asleep (Odyssey 13), which Mabbott finds weakened by the difference between the two words. No identification is certain, and Poe may have chosen the word chiefly for its sound and its aura of antiquity. The barks bear the wanderer home as Helen's beauty bears the speaker home to the classical past.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; W. P. Trent, ed., Poe, Poems and Tales (1897-98), note on 'To Helen', at eapoe.org; Homer, Odyssey 13.70-125 (the Phaeacian ship)

  3. a perfumed sea st. 1, l. 3

    The perfumed sea makes the journey not an epic struggle but a reward after struggle; Mabbott surveys proposed explanations of it, but no identification is needed. The sound of the stanza ('weary, wayworn wanderer', with its w-alliteration) slows the line into a lullaby. The contrast with the 'desperate seas' of stanza 2 organises the poem. The sea seems to take on the beloved's own perfume, so that woman and landscape merge.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'perfumed'

  4. the weary, wayworn wanderer st. 1, l. 4

    Mabbott lists the candidates: W. M. Rossetti proposed Bacchus (1885), citing Paradise Lost 4 and Albert Pike's 1830 'Hymn to Bacchus', and Mabbott judges this the only fully consistent reading; Mabbott himself suggested Menelaus, who sailed home with the recovered Helen (drawing on Stesichorus and Euripides' Helen); W. P. Trent tentatively offered Ulysses; an 1863 Notes and Queries correspondent proposed Alexander the Great; others have offered Catullus, Nice in France, and an everyman. Odysseus is the reading many students reach first, since the Phaeacians carried him 'to his own native shore', and he is the archetypal wanderer longing for home, but the case for him is not strong. Whoever the figure is, he is the speaker's double: the speaker too has roamed 'desperate seas' and is brought 'home'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Homer, Odyssey 13 (Odysseus carried home by the Phaeacians)

  5. Stanza and metre st. 1, l. 5

    Each stanza is built mainly of tetrameter lines (stanza 1 has four, stanzas 2 and 3 three each) and closes with a short line ('To his own native shore', 'To the grandeur that was Rome', 'Are Holy Land!'), so the movement is a voyage that ends in arrival. The rhyme schemes vary from stanza to stanza (ababb, ababa, abbab), and the third stanza uses the bold rhyme 'niche / which'. The poem's economy contrasts with the expansiveness of Poe's later lyrics such as 'Ulalume'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  6. desperate seas ... long wont st. 2, l. 1

    'Wont' means accustomed (OED); 'desperate' keeps its root sense, without hope (Latin desperare). The line is syntactically ambiguous: it is the speaker who has roamed, but the participle is attached to the list of Helen's attributes that 'have brought me home'. The seas are the troubles of the poet's youth (Poe was orphaned and later estranged from his foster father), though the poem universalises them.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wont'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'desperate'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  7. hyacinth hair st. 2, l. 2

    In the Odyssey Athena makes Odysseus' hair fall in curls 'like the hyacinth flower' (6.231, 23.158), and Milton gives Adam 'hyacinthin locks' (Paradise Lost 4.301), as Frank Durham pointed out. Mabbott stresses that Poe means the clustering, curling form rather than a colour (Mrs Stanard's hair was, he notes, brown). The myth of Hyacinthus, the beautiful youth loved by Apollo and turned into a flower, adds a hint of beauty touched by death.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Homer, Odyssey 6.229-35; Milton, Paradise Lost 4.301 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  8. classic face st. 2, l. 2

    'Classic' in Poe's day meant of the first rank and, specifically, belonging to Greek and Roman antiquity (OED). The face, the hair and the 'Naiad airs' together make Helen a living antique statue, preparing the 'statue-like' figure of stanza 3.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'classic'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  9. Naiad airs st. 2, l. 3

    Naiads were the nymphs of fresh water in Greek myth (OED). Mabbott notes the contrast with the 'desperate seas': the salt sea of wandering against the sweet water of home. 'Airs' means manner or bearing, and perhaps also melodies or breezes, which bring the wanderer home. The three possessives of the stanza ('Thy hyacinth hair, thy classic face, / Thy Naiad airs') pile up like a blazon, the Renaissance catalogue of a beloved's beauties, in compressed classical form.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'naiad'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  10. 'the glory that was Greece' st. 2, ll. 4-5

    Mabbott records that the 1831 text read 'To the beauty of fair Greece, / And the grandeur of old Rome' and that the familiar couplet was achieved later (it first appears in 1843). The revision gives parallel syntax ('the glory that was ... the grandeur that was'), alliteration (glory/Greece, grandeur/Rome) and a sense of loss in the past tense 'was'. The pairing is now proverbial for the two great civilisations of antiquity: Greek achievement and Roman power. Beauty brings the speaker 'home' not to a place but to the classical ideal.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  11. window niche st. 3, l. 1

    A niche is a recess in a wall for a statue. Mabbott records the variants: 'that little window-niche' (1831), 'that shadowy' (1841), and finally 'yon brilliant', which makes the scene glow with lamplight. Biographically, readers have imagined Mrs Stanard seen at a lit window; the poem itself turns the woman into an icon in a shrine. 'Lo!' and 'yon' are deliberately archaic, the language of vision, as if the speaker points to an apparition.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'niche'

  12. statue-like st. 3, l. 2

    The simile completes the transformation of a living woman into a classical artwork; Mabbott records Wilbur's comparison with the statuary described in Poe's tale 'The Assignation'. Readers note the cost of the idealisation: the beloved is admired as an image at a distance, not met as a person, a pattern common in Poe's poems of ideal women. The stillness of the third stanza contrasts with the motion of the first two (barks, seas, roaming): the voyage ends in contemplation of a fixed image.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  13. the agate lamp st. 3, l. 3

    Agate is a variegated, semi-translucent quartz, which would glow when lit from within. Mabbott records that the lamp replaced a 'folded scroll' in a late revision, and mentions a suggested link between 'agate' and Virgil's 'faithful Achates', the companion of Aeneas. The lamp identifies Helen with Psyche, who held a lamp over the sleeping Cupid. The light is in her hand, so she is both illuminated and the source of illumination, a figure of beauty as guide.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'agate'

  14. Psyche st. 3, l. 4

    In the story of Cupid and Psyche (Apuleius, Metamorphoses 5.22-23), Psyche, forbidden to look at her unseen lover, brings a lamp to his bed, sees that he is the god Cupid, and lets a drop of oil fall that wakes him. Mabbott connects the lamp with that scene. The renaming moves the poem from physical beauty (Helen) to spiritual beauty (Psyche, soul), and earlier versions read 'A Psyche'. Poe returns to Psyche in 'Ulalume'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; 'Cupid and Psyche' (Apuleius, Metamorphoses 4.28-6.24), Wikipedia

  15. the regions which / Are Holy Land st. 3, ll. 4-5

    The phrase transfers the Christian term for Palestine to the realm from which Helen/Psyche comes. Mabbott reads it as both Greece, the holy land of art, and Richmond, the holy land of Poe's heart. Other readers take it as the Platonic realm of ideal beauty, or as the afterlife, since Jane Stanard was dead when the poem was written. The exclamation closes the poem's voyage: the wanderer has arrived, not home to a place, but at a shrine.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'To Helen' [1831], introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org
