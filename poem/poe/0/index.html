Death & Dying

The Raven

by Edgar Allan Poe 1809–1849

Death & Dying

Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary,
Over many a quaint and curious volume of forgotten lore--
While I nodded, nearly napping, suddenly there came a tapping,
As of some one gently rapping--rapping at my chamber door.
"'Tis some visitor," I muttered, "tapping at my chamber door--
Only this and nothing more."

Ah, distinctly I remember, it was in the bleak December,
And each separate dying ember wrought its ghost upon the floor.
Eagerly I wished the morrow;--vainly I had sought to borrow
From my books surcease of sorrow--sorrow for the lost Lenore--
For the rare and radiant maiden whom the angels name Lenore--
Nameless here for evermore.

And the silken sad uncertain rustling of each purple curtain
Thrilled me--filled me with fantastic terrors never felt before;
So that now, to still the beating of my heart, I stood repeating
"'Tis some visitor entreating entrance at my chamber door--
Some late visitor entreating entrance at my chamber door;--
This it is and nothing more."

Presently my soul grew stronger; hesitating then no longer,
"Sir," said I, "or Madam, truly your forgiveness I implore;
But the fact is I was napping, and so gently you came rapping,
And so faintly you came tapping--tapping at my chamber door,
That I scarce was sure I heard you"--here I opened wide the door:--
Darkness there and nothing more.

Deep into that darkness peering, long I stood there wondering,
fearing,
Doubting, dreaming dreams no mortal ever dared to dream before;
But the silence was unbroken, and the darkness gave no token,
And the only word there spoken was the whispered word, "Lenore!"
This I whispered, and an echo murmured back the word, "Lenore!"
Merely this and nothing more.

Back into the chamber turning, all my soul within me burning,
Soon I heard again a tapping, somewhat louder than before.
"Surely," said I, "surely that is something at my window lattice;
Let me see, then, what thereat is, and this mystery explore--
Let my heart be still a moment, and this mystery explore;--
'Tis the wind and nothing more."

Open here I flung the shutter, when, with many a flirt and flutter,
In there stepped a stately Raven of the saintly days of yore;
Not the least obeisance made he: not an instant stopped or stayed he;
But, with mien of lord or lady, perched above my chamber door--
Perched upon a bust of Pallas just above my chamber door--
Perched, and sat, and nothing more.

Then this ebony bird beguiling my sad fancy into smiling,
By the grave and stern decorum of the countenance it wore,
"Though thy crest be shorn and shaven, thou," I said, "art sure no
craven,
Ghastly grim and ancient Raven wandering from the Nightly shore--
Tell me what thy lordly name is on the Night's Plutonian shore!"
Quoth the Raven, "Nevermore."

Much I marvelled this ungainly fowl to hear discourse so plainly,
Though its answer little meaning--little relevancy bore;
For we cannot help agreeing that no living human being
Ever yet was blessed with seeing bird above his chamber door--
Bird or beast upon the sculptured bust above his chamber door,
With such name as "Nevermore."

But the Raven, sitting lonely on that placid bust, spoke only
That one word, as if his soul in that one word he did outpour.
Nothing further then he uttered--not a feather then he fluttered--
Till I scarcely more than muttered, "Other friends have flown before--
On the morrow he will leave me, as my hopes have flown before."
Then the bird said, "Nevermore."

Startled at the stillness broken by reply so aptly spoken,
"Doubtless," said I, "what it utters is its only stock and store,
Caught from some unhappy master whom unmerciful Disaster
Followed fast and followed faster till his songs one burden bore--
Till the dirges of his Hope the melancholy burden bore
Of 'Never--nevermore.'"

But the Raven still beguiling all my sad soul into smiling,
Straight I wheeled a cushioned seat in front of bird and bust and
door;
Then, upon the velvet sinking, I betook myself to linking
Fancy unto fancy, thinking what this ominous bird of yore--
What this grim, ungainly, ghastly, gaunt, and ominous bird of yore
Meant in croaking "Nevermore."

This I sat engaged in guessing, but no syllable expressing
To the fowl whose fiery eyes now burned into my bosom's core;
This and more I sat divining, with my head at ease reclining
On the cushion's velvet lining that the lamp-light gloated o'er,
But whose velvet violet lining with the lamp-light gloating o'er,
She shall press, ah, nevermore!

Then, methought, the air grew denser, perfumed from an unseen censer
Swung by Seraphim whose foot-falls tinkled on the tufted floor.
"Wretch," I cried, "thy God hath lent thee--by these angels he hath
sent thee
Respite--respite aad nepenthé from thy memories of Lenore!
Quaff, oh quaff this kind nepenthé, and forget this lost Lenore!"
Quoth the Raven, "Nevermore."

"Prophet!" said I, "thing of evil!--prophet still, if bird or devil!--
Whether Tempter sent, or whether tempest tossed thee here ashore,
Desolate yet all undaunted, on this desert land enchanted--
On this home by Horror haunted--tell me truly, I implore--
Is there--is there balm in Gilead?--tell me--tell me, I implore!"
Quoth the Raven, "Nevermore."

"Prophet!" said I, "thing of evil!--prophet still, if bird or devil!
By that Heaven that bends above us--by that God we both adore--
Tell this soul with sorrow laden if, within the distant Aidenn,
It shall clasp a sainted maiden whom the angels name Lenore--
Clasp a rare and radiant maiden whom the angels name Lenore."
Quoth the Raven, "Nevermore."

"Be that word our sign of parting, bird or fiend!" I shrieked,
upstarting--
"Get thee back into the tempest and the Night's Plutonian shore!
Leave no black plume as a token of that lie thy soul hath spoken!
Leave my loneliness unbroken!--quit the bust above my door!
Take thy beak from out my heart, and take thy form from off my door!"
Quoth the Raven, "Nevermore."

And the Raven, never flitting, still is sitting, still is sitting
On the pallid bust of Pallas just above my chamber door;
And his eyes have all the seeming of a demon's that is dreaming,
And the lamp-light o'er him streaming throws his shadow on the floor;
And my soul from out that shadow that lies floating on the floor
Shall be lifted--nevermore!

Published, 1845.

* * * * *

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and the opening st. 1 (l. 1)

    The poem was printed in the Evening Mirror on 29 January 1845, in advance of the American Review's February issue, where it appeared under the pseudonym '---- Quarles' (after Francis Quarles, author of the popular Emblems, 1635). It was an instant success and was much reprinted and parodied. Mabbott dates the composition to 1844. The fairy-tale opening 'Once upon a' sets a deliberately familiar, story-telling tone. Mabbott notes that it resembles the opening of an ode in the Anacreontea (no. 33), in which Love knocks at the poet's door at midnight, a parallel noticed by several scholars including Gilbert Highet.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Library of Congress, 'From the Catbird Seat': Edgar Allan Poe and 'The Raven' (2015); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846

  2. Metre, stanza and internal rhyme st. 1

    Each stanza has five long trochaic lines (eight feet, the alternate lines lacking the final syllable) and a short refrain line; Poe described the scheme in 'The Philosophy of Composition' and claimed originality for the stanza's combination rather than for its lines. The middle of each long line rhymes internally ('dreary' / 'weary', 'napping' / 'tapping'), and lines 2, 4, 5 and 6 share the '-ore' rhyme. Mabbott identifies Elizabeth Barrett's 'Lady Geraldine's Courtship' (1844) as the 'cardinal source' of the form; Poe dedicated The Raven and Other Poems (1845) to her. The prefatory note in the American Review commented on the poem's 'unique rhyming' and alliteration.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  3. quaint ... forgotten lore st. 1 (l. 2)

    'Quaint' here means curiously old-fashioned or ingeniously odd, and 'lore' is learning, especially traditional or esoteric knowledge. The speaker is a solitary student, as Poe said in 'The Philosophy of Composition' (which also gives his reason for the bust of Pallas: it suits the lover's scholarship). In stanza 2 we learn that he has sought in books 'surcease of sorrow'. Mabbott records that Poe asked Susan Talley whether 'many a' volume (rather than one) was proper, and defends it: a scholar turns from one book to another.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quaint', adj., and 'lore', n.1

  4. the bleak December st. 2 (l. 7)

    The memory is fixed in December, at night, with each 'dying ember' casting its 'ghost' (the shadowy glow or shape of the coal) on the floor. Mabbott suggests an echo of Keats's lyric beginning 'In a drear-nighted December', available in American reprints of the Galignani edition, and of a December knocking in William Wirt's Old Bachelor (1814). The dying fire and its ghost quietly anticipate the poem's concern with loss and haunting.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); John Keats, 'Stanzas' ('In a drear-nighted December')

  5. surcease st. 2 (l. 10)

    'Surcease' means cessation or stopping (from Old French sursis, past participle of surseoir, 'to delay, suspend'). Already archaic in Poe's time, it is best known from Macbeth 1.7.4, 'catch, / With his surcease, success', where it means Duncan's death. Poe's phrase, alliterating on 's', names what the speaker seeks throughout the poem, an end to sorrow, which the bird will repeatedly deny, most directly in the nepenthe stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'surcease', n.; Shakespeare, Macbeth 1.7.1-7 (Folger Shakespeare); T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  6. Lenore st. 2 (l. 10)

    Lenore is the 'rare and radiant maiden' whose death the speaker mourns. Poe used the name in 'Lenore' (1843), and the type recurs in his tales ('Ligeia', 'Eleonora'). In 'The Philosophy of Composition' he argued that the death of a beautiful woman is 'the most poetical topic in the world', voiced by her bereaved lover. Mabbott thinks her almost surely imaginary, though some biographers see Poe's young wife Virginia, then ill with tuberculosis, as a prototype. The long 'o' and 'r' of 'Lenore' chime with the refrain 'Nevermore'.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  7. Nameless here st. 2 (l. 12)

    Mabbott glosses 'Nameless here' as 'not called on by name or spoken to in this world'. The angels 'name' her in heaven; on earth her name has fallen silent, except when the speaker whispers it into the darkness in stanza 5. 'For evermore' anticipates the 'Nevermore' that will dominate the poem. The rhyme-word joins the '-ore' chain that runs through every stanza ('lore', 'door', 'floor', 'Lenore'), so that her name is woven into the poem's sound even while she is said to be nameless.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846

  8. the purple curtain st. 3 (l. 13)

    The line 'the silken sad uncertain rustling of each purple curtain' closely echoes Elizabeth Barrett's 'With a rushing stir, uncertain, in the air, the purple curtain' in 'Lady Geraldine's Courtship' (1844). Mabbott records a report that Poe said the suggestion of his poem lay wholly in that line; the debt was noticed in 1845 by Thomas Dunn English. The sound of the line imitates rustling through its sibilants. Mabbott adds that purple may symbolise both wealth and mourning.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846

  9. 'Sir ... or Madam' st. 4 (l. 20)

    The speaker addresses the unseen knocker with exaggerated courtesy. Mabbott notes that a parrot is addressed as 'Sir, or Madam' in John Wilson's Noctes Ambrosianae (Blackwood's, 1829), with a talking raven on the following page. He also records Poe's remark that he wrote the poem 'to see how near to the absurd' he could come without crossing the line. The American Review introduction similarly mentioned 'ludicrous touches' amid the serious; the comedy heightens the eeriness.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  10. ''Tis the wind' st. 6 (l. 36)

    Each early stanza ends with a reassuring dismissal, 'nothing more', which grows less convincing as it recurs. Mabbott compares Othello 4.3, where Desdemona hears a knock and Emilia answers 'It's the wind', and Poe's own 'Tell-Tale Heart', 'It is nothing but the wind in the chimney'. The refrain prepares for its own replacement by 'Nevermore' from stanza 8. Here the explanation is half true: when the shutter is opened, the wind is indeed there, but so is the Raven.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Shakespeare, Othello 4.3 (Folger Shakespeare)

  11. flirt st. 7 (l. 37)

    'Flirt' here is a sudden jerk or flick, the bird's abrupt motion of wings and tail, not flirtation. Mabbott cites a reviewer of 1850 who pointed to Johnson's Dictionary for the sense 'a quick, elastic motion'. Mabbott also records Poe's view that 'shutter' in the same line was too commonplace. The reviewer, who had once owned a raven, praised the bird's movements as true to life.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'flirt'

  12. saintly days of yore st. 7 (l. 38)

    The bird is 'of the saintly days of yore' ('yore': long ago), an ironic halo for a black bird of ill omen. In Genesis 8:7 Noah sends out a raven from the ark; in 1 Kings 17:6 ravens bring bread and flesh to the prophet Elijah. Ravens also feed hermit saints in later legend. The phrase looks forward to the speaker's calling the bird 'Prophet!'. Mabbott adds that ravens were proverbially long-lived (Pliny), supporting the bird's 'ancient' character in stanza 8.

    The Bible, King James Version, 1 Kings 17:6; The Bible, King James Version, Genesis 8:7; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  13. Pallas st. 7 (l. 41)

    Pallas is an epithet of Athena, goddess of wisdom and patron of scholars. In 'The Philosophy of Composition' Poe said he chose the bust for its contrast with the black plumage, because it suited the lover's scholarship, and because 'Pallas' itself is sonorous. Busts of Minerva were popular ornaments; Mabbott records a story that one stood above a door in the Brennan farmhouse where Poe stayed in 1844. The image of the raven settled on wisdom's head is often read as grief or irrational dread mastering reason. Athena's own bird is the owl, and Mabbott notes Poe's reported first plan of an owl.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  14. shorn and shaven ... craven st. 8 (l. 45)

    The speaker teases the bird, whose head feathers look flattened, by saying that despite its 'shorn and shaven' crest it is 'sure no craven' (coward). Mabbott explains that a cowardly knight might have his head shaved as a mark of disgrace, and thinks the pun on 'knightly' and 'Nightly' (next line) deliberate, one of the poem's humorous touches. He also notes the legend in Don Quixote that King Arthur was turned into a raven.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'craven', adj. and n.

  15. Plutonian shore st. 8 (l. 47)

    Pluto (Greek Plouton) ruled the classical underworld, reached by crossing the Styx or Acheron. The 'Plutonian shore' is the bank of the river of the dead, and 'Night's' makes it the realm of darkness. Mabbott compares Horace, Odes 1.4.16-17, 'domus exilis Plutonia' ('the meagre house of Pluto'), which he says is the passage from which the Latin adjective is cited. The phrase recurs in stanza 17, when the speaker orders the bird back there.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Horace, Odes 1.4 (The Latin Library)

  16. The refrain 'Nevermore' st. 8 (l. 48)

    In 'The Philosophy of Composition' Poe explained that he wanted a one-word refrain, that the long 'o' was 'the most sonorous vowel' and 'r' the most easily prolonged consonant, and that 'Nevermore' presented itself at once. He chose a non-reasoning creature to speak it so that the repetition would be natural, and a raven rather than a parrot for its ill-omened tone. The poem's design is that the same word acquires changing meanings as the speaker's questions grow more desperate. 'Quoth' is the archaic past tense of 'quethe', to say. Mabbott notes that 'nevermore' was common in English poetry, for instance in Shelley.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  17. burden st. 11 (ll. 64-66)

    The speaker rationalises the word as the bird's 'only stock and store', learned from an 'unhappy master' pursued by Disaster until his songs 'one burden bore'. 'Burden' means the refrain of a song (from the French bourdon, a drone), and also a weight; both senses apply. 'Followed fast and followed faster' echoes, as Mabbott notes, Midsummer Night's Dream 3.2.416, 'I followed fast, but faster did he fly'. Mabbott also records that Poe removed a 'bad rhyme' from this eleventh stanza within a week of first publication.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'burden', n. (sense 'refrain'); Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream 3.2 (Folger Shakespeare)

  18. velvet violet lining st. 13 (ll. 76-78)

    The cushion's lining, lit by the lamp, prompts the thought that Lenore will never sit there again. Mabbott notes that 'gloated' here has the rare sense 'reflected light from', though in Poe it usually carries something sinister; that Poe regarded 'lining' as a blunder but would not sacrifice the stanza; and that he had used 'velvet violet' before in a parody. Here the speaker himself, not the bird, pronounces 'nevermore' of Lenore, a turn in his own understanding.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  19. censer and Seraphim st. 14 (ll. 79-80)

    A censer is a vessel for burning incense, swung in worship. The Seraphim are the angels seen by Isaiah about God's throne (Isaiah 6:2). The speaker imagines the air thickened with heavenly incense, a sign that God has sent him relief. The 'foot-falls' that 'tinkled on the tufted floor' were criticised in Poe's time; Mabbott reports that Thomas Dunn English asked whether the angels had bells on their toes, and that F. W. Thomas reported Poe had referred him to Isaiah 3:16, where the daughters of Zion make 'a tinkling with their feet'. Mabbott compares similar censers in 'Ligeia' and 'Eleonora'.

    The Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 6:2; The Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 3:16; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  20. nepenthe st. 14 (ll. 82-83)

    In Odyssey 4.219-221 Helen puts into the wine a drug from Egypt, nepenthes (Greek ne-, 'not', and penthos, 'grief'), which banishes sorrow and anger. Mabbott also cites Spenser's Faerie Queene IV.iii.43 and Milton's Comus. 'Respite' is relief or pause. The speaker interprets the perfume as heaven sending him forgetfulness; the bird's 'Nevermore' denies it, making the bird a figure of unending memory. (The archive's text has a misprint, 'aad', for 'and' in the preceding line.)

    Homer, Odyssey 4.219-232 (Perseus Digital Library); T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene IV.iii.43

  21. 'Prophet!' st. 15 (l. 85)

    From here the speaker asks the bird questions as if it could foretell the future. Mabbott hears an echo of Agamemnon's abuse of the seer Calchas in Pope's Iliad (1.106), 'Augur accurst! denouncing mischief still', and notes the ancient lore of ravens as prophetic birds sacred to Apollo, recorded by Pliny. Poe had complained in reviewing Dickens's Barnaby Rudge (1842) that Grip the raven's croakings were not made prophetic; here he does it himself. 'Tempter' suggests Satan; the bird may be 'bird or devil'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846

  22. balm in Gilead st. 15 (l. 89)

    Gilead, a region east of the Jordan, was famed for an aromatic healing resin (Genesis 37:25). Jeremiah's lament, 'Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?' (Jeremiah 8:22), became proverbial for asking whether any cure exists for affliction. Mabbott notes that Poe's use is figurative, as elsewhere in his work. The question is the first of the two great questions the speaker puts, and the bird answers it with despair. The 'desert land enchanted' and the 'home by Horror haunted' (where Mabbott says the old pronunciation of 'haunted' means the rhyme was almost surely not imperfect for Poe) intensify the desolation.

    The Bible, King James Version, Jeremiah 8:22; The Bible, King James Version, Genesis 37:25; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  23. Aidenn st. 16 (l. 93)

    Mabbott explains 'Aidenn' as Poe's unusual spelling of Eden (Arabic 'Adn), used also in 'The Conversation of Eiros and Charmion' and 'The Power of Words'. The second great question is whether the speaker's soul will meet Lenore in Paradise; 'Nevermore' answers that even the hope of reunion is denied. Mabbott compares the solemn adjuration 'by that God whom thou didst adore' in Cary's translation of Dante's Inferno 1.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Eden', n.

  24. beak in the heart st. 17 (l. 101)

    In 'The Philosophy of Composition' Poe identified 'from out my heart' as the poem's first metaphorical expression, designed, with the answer 'Nevermore', to make the reader seek a moral or meaning in all that has been narrated. The bird, which has been a literal raven, becomes an image of grief tearing at the speaker, like the eagle at Prometheus's liver. The speaker's furious command is met by the unchanging refrain.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  25. The emblematic ending st. 18

    The last stanza shifts to the present tense: the Raven 'still is sitting' on the bust, and the speaker's soul 'Shall be lifted - nevermore!'. Poe wrote that only here is the bird's emblematic meaning, 'Mournful and Never-ending Remembrance', permitted to be seen. Mabbott quotes C. Alphonso Smith that there is no trace of remorse in the ending, against critics who find guilt. John M. Daniel's 1849 introduction (perhaps reflecting Poe's talk) called the last stanza an image of settled despair added afterwards. Mabbott quotes Poe's letter to Eveleth explaining the lamp-light by a bracket candelabrum high above the door and bust.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore)

  26. the shadow on the floor st. 18 (ll. 106-108)

    The poem ends with the speaker's soul trapped in the shadow cast by the bird, an image of despair which completes the opening stanza's ember casting 'its ghost upon the floor'. Mabbott records that Poe explained to a correspondent he imagined a bracket candelabrum above the door. The final 'nevermore!' is spoken by the speaker of himself, and so differs from the bird's quoted refrain.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., The Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. I: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'The Raven', pp. 350-374 (online at the Poe Society of Baltimore); Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846
