Death & Dying

Ulalume

by Edgar Allan Poe 1809–1849

Death & Dying

The skies they were ashen and sober;
The leaves they were crisped and sere--
The leaves they were withering and sere;
It was night in the lonesome October
Of my most immemorial year;
It was hard by the dim lake of Auber,
In the misty mid region of Weir--
It was down by the dank tarn of Auber,
In the ghoul-haunted woodland of Weir.

Here once, through an alley Titanic.
Of cypress, I roamed with my Soul--
Of cypress, with Psyche, my Soul.
These were days when my heart was volcanic
As the scoriac rivers that roll--
As the lavas that restlessly roll
Their sulphurous currents down Yaanek
In the ultimate climes of the pole--
That groan as they roll down Mount Yaanek
In the realms of the boreal pole.

Our talk had been serious and sober,
But our thoughts they were palsied and sere--
Our memories were treacherous and sere--
For we knew not the month was October,
And we marked not the night of the year--
(Ah, night of all nights in the year!)
We noted not the dim lake of Auber--
(Though once we had journeyed down here)--
Remembered not the dank tarn of Auber,
Nor the ghoul-haunted woodland of Weir.

And now as the night was senescent
And star-dials pointed to morn--
As the sun-dials hinted of morn--
At the end of our path a liquescent
And nebulous lustre was born,
Out of which a miraculous crescent
Arose with a duplicate horn--
Astarte's bediamonded crescent
Distinct with its duplicate horn.

And I said--"She is warmer than Dian:
She rolls through an ether of sighs--
She revels in a region of sighs:
She has seen that the tears are not dry on
These cheeks, where the worm never dies,
And has come past the stars of the Lion
To point us the path to the skies--
To the Lethean peace of the skies--
Come up, in despite of the Lion,
To shine on us with her bright eyes--
Come up through the lair of the Lion,
With love in her luminous eyes."

But Psyche, uplifting her finger,
Said--"Sadly this star I mistrust--
Her pallor I strangely mistrust:--
Oh, hasten!--oh, let us not linger!
Oh, fly!--let us fly!--for we must."
In terror she spoke, letting sink her
Wings till they trailed in the dust--
In agony sobbed, letting sink her
Plumes till they trailed in the dust--
Till they sorrowfully trailed in the dust.

I replied--"This is nothing but dreaming:
Let us on by this tremulous light!
Let us bathe in this crystalline light!
Its Sibyllic splendor is beaming
With Hope and in Beauty to-night:--
See!--it flickers up the sky through the night!
Ah, we safely may trust to its gleaming,
And be sure it will lead us aright--
We safely may trust to a gleaming
That cannot but guide us aright,
Since it flickers up to Heaven through the night."

Thus I pacified Psyche and kissed her,
And tempted her out of her gloom--
And conquered her scruples and gloom;
And we passed to the end of a vista,
But were stopped by the door of a tomb--
By the door of a legended tomb;
And I said--"What is written, sweet sister,
On the door of this legended tomb?"
She replied--"Ulalume--Ulalume--
'Tis the vault of thy lost Ulalume!"

Then my heart it grew ashen and sober
As the leaves that were crisped and sere--
As the leaves that were withering and sere;
And I cried--"It was surely October
On this very night of last year
That I journeyed--I journeyed down here--
That I brought a dread burden down here!
On this night of all nights in the year,
Ah, what demon has tempted me here?
Well I know, now, this dim lake of Auber--
This misty mid region of Weir--
Well I know, now, this dank tarn of Auber,--
This ghoul-haunted woodland of Weir."

1847.

* * * * *

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and publication st. 1, l. 1

    Mabbott dates the finished poem to about October 1847 and records two prompts: a walk to Mamaroneck, New York, where Poe saw a family tomb, and the plot of Elizabeth Oakes Smith's poem 'The Summons Answered'. Poe then asked N. P. Willis to reprint it in the Home Journal (1 January 1848) 'with a word of inquiry as to who wrote it', a publicity game for his literary comeback. Virginia Clemm Poe had died on 30 January 1847, and readers since Sarah Helen Whitman have taken the poem as autobiographical allegory, though Mabbott treats the correspondence as ideal rather than literal. The archive's text is a later revised version, without the original last stanza.

    Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Ulalume': manuscripts and printings; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  2. crisped and sere st. 1, l. 2

    'Sere' (also 'sear') means dry and withered, especially of leaves (OED); its literary locus is Macbeth's 'my way of life / Is fall'n into the sear, the yellow leaf' (5.3). 'Crisped' is curled or crinkled by dryness. The adjective pair is repeated and varied ('withering and sere', 'palsied and sere', 'treacherous and sere'), so that the autumn landscape and the speaker's mind decay together. In the last stanza the heart grows 'ashen and sober' like the leaves, closing the frame.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sere'; Shakespeare, Macbeth 5.3.22-23 (Folger Shakespeare); T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  3. Metre, repetition and refrain st. 1

    The lines are mainly anapaestic (da-da-DUM) trimeter, with heavy feminine rhymes ('sober / October', 'Titanic / volcanic') and many internal echoes. Almost every line is immediately repeated with a slight change ('The leaves they were crisped and sere-- / The leaves they were withering and sere'), so the stanzas advance by incremental repetition, as in the folk ballad (hence the subtitle 'A Ballad'). In 'The Philosophy of Composition' (1846) Poe argued for the refrain varied in application. The effect has been admired as music and mocked as mere sound (Aldous Huxley found it vulgar); either way the repetition mimics a mind circling something it refuses to remember.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846, at eapoe.org; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Aldous Huxley, 'Vulgarity in Literature' (1930), on Poe's metre

  4. the lonesome October / my most immemorial year st. 1, ll. 4-5

    'Immemorial' normally means so old that memory does not reach it (OED); applied to a single year it is a typical Poe paradox: the year is both supremely memorable and blotted out, which is exactly the speaker's state. Mabbott reads the surface plot as a walk on Hallowe'en night, the 'night of all nights in the year', to the tomb of a lost love. Biographical readers point out that Virginia died in January, not October, so the poem is not a literal anniversary; Mrs Whitman asked Mrs Clemm in 1859 whether Virginia had died in January, and was told the poem's timing was purely ideal, so the question was raised early.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'immemorial'

  5. Auber st. 1, l. 6

    Mabbott suggests that Poe took the name from Auber (1782-1871), whose ballet-opera Le Lac des fees (The Lake of the Fairies) was performed in New York in 1845: a fairy lake behind a dim, haunted one. The name's sound matters more than its reference: 'Auber' and 'October' chime, and the vowel is dark. A 'tarn' (l. 8) is a small mountain lake, a northern English word (from Old Norse tjorn) beloved of Gothic writers; Poe's 'House of Usher' stands over a 'black and lurid tarn'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tarn'

  6. Weir st. 1, l. 7

    Mabbott identifies the name with Robert Walter Weir (1803-89), the landscape painter who taught drawing at West Point (where Poe was briefly a cadet), and notes the further suggestions of 'weir' (a dam or fish-trap) and 'weird' (from Old English wyrd, fate). 'Mid region' is an old term for the middle layer of the air, between earth and heaven, appropriate to a landscape that is half real, half dream. Together, a composer and a painter: the dream country is made of music and painting.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weir'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weird'

  7. ghoul-haunted st. 1, l. 9

    'Ghoul' came into English from Arabic ghul through translations of the Arabian Nights in the eighteenth century and William Beckford's Vathek (1786); a ghoul is an evil spirit that feeds on corpses (OED). Poe had used ghouls before (they ring the bells in 'The Bells', 1849). The compound is a key part of the refrain that returns in stanzas 1, 3 and 9 of this text, each time a little changed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ghoul'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  8. an alley Titanic / Of cypress st. 2, ll. 1-2

    An 'alley' here is a walk or avenue bordered by trees (OED). 'Titanic' means vast, like the Titans, the giant elder gods of Greek myth; the word did not yet have its later associations with the ship. The cypress, sacred to Pluto and planted in graveyards, has been the tree of mourning since antiquity (Ovid tells how Cyparissus was turned into a cypress for his grief, Metamorphoses 10.106-42), and Mabbott glosses it so. The inverted word order ('alley Titanic') is a poetic licence for rhyme with 'volcanic'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alley'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.106-42 (Cyparissus)

  9. Psyche st. 2, l. 3

    Greek psyche means 'breath, soul' and also 'butterfly', which is why she has wings and plumes that 'trailed in the dust' (st. 5). In Apuleius' tale (Metamorphoses 4-6) Psyche is the mortal girl loved by Cupid and persecuted by Venus; Mabbott suggests that her quarrel with Venus explains why she mistrusts the star of Venus (Astarte) in this poem, and he insists she is the speaker's soul, not a double. Poe also ends 'To Helen' with 'Psyche'. The dialogue of a man with his own soul is an old form (the medieval debate of body and soul).

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; 'Cupid and Psyche' (Apuleius, Metamorphoses 4.28-6.24), Wikipedia

  10. scoriac st. 2, l. 5

    Scoriae are the cindery, jagged fragments of cooled lava (Latin from Greek skoria, refuse of metal). Mabbott knew of no earlier use of the adjective than Poe's. The volcanic heart is a Byronic hyperbole for passion, and it is set against the cold, sere October landscape: inside fire, outside ash. 'Sulphurous currents' adds the brimstone of hell, so the passion is also torment.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scoria'

  11. Mount Yaanek st. 2, ll. 7-10

    Ross's Antarctic expedition sighted and named the active volcano Mount Erebus in January 1841, and the report of fire amid polar ice caught the public imagination. H. P. Lovecraft first pointed out the identification (in The Outsider, 1939), and Mabbott accepts it while noting that the reason for the name remains unresolved. Poe calls the pole 'boreal' (northern), which does not fit Erebus; whether this is a slip, a rhyme-led choice or a deliberately non-geographical dream pole is debated. 'Ultimate climes' means the farthest regions, echoing the classical Ultima Thule that Poe used in 'Dream-Land'.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; 'Mount Erebus', Wikipedia

  12. the forgotten anniversary st. 3

    The third stanza is built on negatives: 'we knew not', 'we marked not', 'we noted not', 'Remembered not'. Their memories are 'treacherous'. The point of the poem's drama is that the speaker has walked unknowingly back to the scene of his loss; the reader is told, in parentheses, 'once we had journeyed down here', before the speaker himself knows it. This is a psychological plot of repression and return, and readers have long seen Poe here anticipating later accounts of the unconscious.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Ulalume': manuscripts and printings

  13. senescent ... liquescent st. 4, ll. 1, 4

    'Senescent' is from Latin senescere, to grow old; 'liquescent' from liquescere, to become liquid (OED). Poe chose the Latinate rhyme pair for its sound and its strangeness, and the slowly dissolving light prepares the appearance of the star. 'Nebulous lustre' (misty brightness) makes the light both beautiful and indistinct. The night's old age is also the speaker's: the whole landscape, from the sere leaves to the senescent night, is in decline, and the new light seems a miraculous rebirth against it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'senescent'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'liquescent'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  14. star-dials st. 4, l. 2

    Mabbott explains the stars as heavenly clocks and notes that no such instrument actually existed: Poe coins 'star-dials' by analogy with 'sun-dials', which follows in the next line, and the doubling is typical of the poem's paired restatements. The time is the darkest hour before dawn, when the morning star rises. The image makes the heavens a timepiece counting down to the anniversary hour, and its oddity, a dial that reads by the stars, suits the poem's dream-cosmos.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  15. Astarte st. 4, l. 8

    Astarte (Ashtoreth in the Old Testament, 1 Kings 11:5) was the Semitic goddess of love and fertility, identified with the planet Venus. Mabbott glosses the 'crescent' as the planet (Venus does show phases, though not to the naked eye) and 'bediamonded' as its diamond-like brilliance, citing a Chateaubriand passage about the morning star. The 'duplicate horn' is the crescent's two points. The star is the poem's central symbol and its meaning is disputed: the speaker sees it as warm love that has come to console him; Psyche distrusts it.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Bible (KJV), 1 Kings 11:5

  16. Dian st. 5, l. 1

    Dian is Diana, Roman goddess of the moon and of chastity (Greek Artemis). Mabbott notes that the moon is set against the planet of love: the speaker welcomes a warm, passionate consolation instead of chaste coldness. 'An ether of sighs' (ether being the pure upper air of the heavens) makes the sky itself sympathetic to lovers. The speaker's preference is telling: he wants a goddess who has 'seen that the tears are not dry' and comes to comfort him, warmth rather than purity, which is exactly what Psyche will distrust.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ether'

  17. where the worm never dies st. 5, l. 5

    The phrase comes from Isaiah 66:24 and Mark 9:44-48, where hell is a place 'where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not quenched' (KJV). Mabbott cites both and notes a parallel in Wordsworth's 'Guilt and Sorrow' ('Say that the worm is on my cheek'). Poe uses it as hyperbole for a grief that eats at the mourner without end, and it also hints at the worm in the grave.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 66:24; Mark 9:44-48; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org

  18. the stars of the Lion st. 5, ll. 7, 9, 11

    The star has come 'past the stars of the Lion', 'in despite of the Lion', 'through the lair of the Lion'. Mabbott explains that astrologers read Venus in Leo as a sign of danger or passion in love, citing a gloss in Spenser's Shepheardes Calender, and adds that Venus was in Virgo, not Leo, at the time of the poem's 1847 setting, so the reference is symbolic. The speaker takes the star's passage through the Lion as triumph over danger; the reader may suspect the omen is darker. 'Lethean peace' (l. 8) is the peace of forgetfulness: Lethe is the river of oblivion in the classical underworld (Virgil, Aeneid 6.703-15).

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Virgil, Aeneid 6.703-15 (Lethe), Perseus Digital Library

  19. Psyche's mistrust: what does the star mean? st. 6

    Mrs Whitman, who knew Poe, read the star as a hope of new love that is defeated when it rises over the tomb of the old. Many later readers take Astarte as sensual or erotic love that the soul (Psyche) rightly fears, so that the poem dramatises a conflict between passion and spiritual fidelity; Mabbott resists a purely sensual reading and sees the star as love and hope. Psyche's 'pallor I strangely mistrust' and her trailing wings make her a figure of premonition. Mabbott calls the poem 'wholly explicable' yet inexplicably powerful: the allegory can be decoded, but its effect lies in the incantation.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Ulalume': manuscripts and printings

  20. Sibyllic st. 7, l. 4

    The Sibyls were women inspired by Apollo to prophesy (most famously the Cumaean Sibyl who guides Aeneas to the underworld, Aeneid 6); Mabbott glosses 'Sibyllic' as 'prophetic'. The speaker reads the light as a prophecy of hope and beauty; the irony is that it leads him straight to the underworld of his memory, as the Sibyl led Aeneas. 'Splendor' and 'beaming' keep the speaker's tone ecstatic just before the discovery of the tomb.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sibylline'

  21. legended tomb st. 8, l. 6

    Mabbott glosses 'legended' as inscribed with something legible. A 'legend' was originally a writing to be read (Latin legenda), and the word is still used of inscriptions on coins and maps (OED). The 'vista' (avenue, view) ends at the door of the tomb, and the soul, calling him 'sweet sister' and answering for him, reads the inscription he does not want to read.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'legend'

  22. Ulalume st. 8, l. 9

    Mabbott calls the name a sorrowful form of 'Eulalie' (Poe's poem 'Eulalie', 1845) and records that Poe pronounced it 'You-la-loom'. Several etymologies have been proposed, for instance from Latin ululare (to wail, howl) and lumen (light), giving 'light of sorrow'; none is certain, and Mabbott lists them as conjectures. The long 'u' sounds and the liquid 'l's embody Poe's view, in 'The Philosophy of Composition', that melancholy is the most legitimate poetic tone and the death of a beautiful woman the most poetical topic.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org; Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846, at eapoe.org

  23. The missing final stanza st. 9, l. 12

    The December 1847 and January 1848 printings ended with a further stanza in which speaker and soul wonder whether the 'merciful ghouls' had drawn the star up to bar their way. The Providence Journal text of 22 November 1848 omitted it, and Mrs Whitman later told J. H. Ingram (30 March 1874) that Poe had cut it at her suggestion; the Literary World restored it in March 1849. Mabbott thought the cut stanza's sentiment ungraceful for a man then engaged to her, and judged that Poe came to agree. The return of the stanza-1 refrain, now as recognition ('Well I know, now'), makes the shorter version close in a circle.

    Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Ulalume': manuscripts and printings; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Ulalume', introduction and line notes, at eapoe.org
