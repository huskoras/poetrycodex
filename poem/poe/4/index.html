Love

Annabel Lee

by Edgar Allan Poe 1809–1849

Love

It was many and many a year ago,
In a kingdom by the sea,
That a maiden there lived whom you may know
By the name of ANNABEL LEE;
And this maiden she lived with no other thought
Than to love and be loved by me.

I was a child and she was a child,
In this kingdom by the sea:
But we loved with a love that was more than love--
I and my ANNABEL LEE;
With a love that the winged seraphs of heaven
Coveted her and me.

And this was the reason that, long ago,
In this kingdom by the sea,
A wind blew out of a cloud, chilling
My beautiful ANNABEL LEE;
So that her highborn kinsmen came
And bore her away from me,
To shut her up in a sepulchre
In this kingdom by the sea.

The angels, not half so happy in heaven,
Went envying her and me--
Yes!--that was the reason (as all men know,
In this kingdom by the sea)
That the wind came out of the cloud by night,
Chilling and killing my ANNABEL LEE.

But our love it was stronger by far than the love
Of those who were older than we--
Of many far wiser than we--
And neither the angels in heaven above,
Nor the demons down under the sea,
Can ever dissever my soul from the soul
Of the beautiful ANNABEL LEE.

For the moon never beams without bringing me dreams
Of the beautiful ANNABEL LEE;
And the stars never rise but I see the bright eyes
Of the beautiful ANNABEL LEE;
And so, all the night-tide, I lie down by the side
Of my darling, my darling, my life and my bride,
In her sepulchre there by the sea--
In her tomb by the side of the sea.

* * * * *

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and occasion l. 1

    Poe made several fair copies in mid-1849 (for Griswold, John Moore, Henry Hirst, John Sartain and, on 26 September, John R. Thompson), and the Poe Society lists six primary printings and dozens of newspaper reprints in 1849-50. The Tribune text of 9 October 1849, in Rufus Griswold's obituary two days after Poe died in Baltimore, is probably the first to reach readers (the Society also records Richmond Examiner proofs of about September 1849); the first authorised printing was in Sartain's Union Magazine, January 1850. Mabbott's edition prints two texts (from the Griswold and Thompson manuscripts) because the copies differ in small but telling ways. The opening is a storyteller's formula, the 'once upon a time' of fairy tale and ballad, which sets the love outside ordinary history.

    Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Annabel Lee': manuscripts, printings and texts; New-York Daily Tribune, 9 October 1849, p. 2 (Griswold's 'Ludwig' obituary), transcription at eapoe.org; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  2. Metre, stanza and refrain l. 2

    The lines run mostly in triple rhythm (da-da-DUM), alternating four-beat and three-beat lines as in ballad measure, but the stanzas lengthen irregularly (six, six, eight, six, seven and eight lines). One rhyme sound dominates: 'sea' and 'Lee' with 'me' and 'we' end nearly every short line, so the poem sounds like a single incantation. Poe had argued in 'The Philosophy of Composition' (1846) that a refrain gains force when the sound stays the same while its application varies; here 'kingdom by the sea' moves from fairy-tale setting to the place of death to the site of the tomb. The Sartain manuscript is titled 'Annabel Lee: A Ballad', which confirms the genre Poe had in mind.

    Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes; Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Annabel Lee': manuscripts, printings and texts

  3. 'kingdom by the sea' l. 8

    The phrase recurs five times and changes its weight each time. Biographers have tried to locate it (Charleston and Sullivan's Island, where Poe served as a soldier, or the Baltimore of Virginia's girlhood), but Mabbott and most critics prefer to read it as an imagined space, like the 'misty mid region of Weir' in 'Ulalume'. The sea in Poe is often a boundary between the living and the dead, as in 'The City in the Sea'. By the last stanza the speaker lies 'by the side of the sea', and the kingdom has shrunk to a tomb.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  4. Annabel Lee l. 4

    Like 'Lenore' and 'Ulalume', the name is built from l, n and open vowels, the sounds Poe valued for melancholy. Several women claimed to be the original: Sarah Helen Whitman, Frances Sargent Osgood, Estelle Anna Lewis, and his first sweetheart Elmira Royster (by 1849 the widowed Mrs Shelton, to whom he was then engaged). Most biographers, including Quinn and Silverman, think first of his wife Virginia Clemm, who married him at thirteen and died of tuberculosis in January 1847. Mabbott warned that the figure is imaginary and that no single model should be pressed. The capitals reproduce the typography of the archive's source text.

    T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes; Arthur Hobson Quinn, Edgar Allan Poe: A Critical Biography (1941), on the last poems; Kenneth Silverman, Edgar A. Poe: Mournful and Never-Ending Remembrance (HarperCollins, 1991), on the last poems and Virginia's death

  5. Child lovers l. 7

    Readers have linked the line to Virginia, Poe's cousin, whom he married in 1836 when she was thirteen. But the motif of child-lovers is also literary, part of the Romantic idealisation of first love. Some critics take the speaker's explanation of her death (the envy of the angels) as itself childish, a sign that the speaker has never grown beyond the loss. Others read the insistence on childhood as shielding the love from sexuality, which the closing image of a bride in a sepulchre then complicates.

    Kenneth Silverman, Edgar A. Poe: Mournful and Never-Ending Remembrance (HarperCollins, 1991), on the last poems and Virginia's death; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  6. seraphs l. 11

    In Isaiah 6:2 the seraphim stand above the throne of God, each with six wings. Christian angelology (pseudo-Dionysius) ranked them first among the nine orders, burning with love of God. Poe turns this upside down: the angels who should embody perfect love are the ones who 'coveted' a human love. A related idea appears in Poe's 'Israfel' (1831), where the speaker imagines changing places with an angel. The English plural 'seraphs', instead of Hebrew 'seraphim', was common in the period.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 6:2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seraph'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  7. Coveted l. 12

    'Thou shalt not covet' (Exodus 20:17) forbids desiring what belongs to a neighbour. By giving the verb to the seraphs Poe makes the angels guilty of envy, one of the seven deadly sins, and the speaker's grief becomes a charge against heaven. The verb opens a short line of its own, which isolates the accusation. The theme returns in stanza 4, 'Went envying her and me', where the reason is stated a second time with emphatic certainty.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 20:17; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'covet'; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  8. The chilling wind l. 15

    The cause of death is natural and supernatural at once: a wind from a cloud, sent (the speaker believes) by envious angels. Readers who take Virginia as the model connect the chill with the tuberculosis that killed her after years of illness, with repeated haemorrhages and fevers that Poe's letters describe. The line ends on the falling word 'chilling', and in stanza 4 the word returns paired with its rhyme, 'Chilling and killing', the climax of the poem's internal rhyming.

    Kenneth Silverman, Edgar A. Poe: Mournful and Never-Ending Remembrance (HarperCollins, 1991), on the last poems and Virginia's death; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  9. 'highborn kinsmen' l. 17

    Read literally, the 'highborn kinsmen' are her aristocratic family, who part the lovers as in a ballad of feuding houses and bury her. But 'highborn' can be heard as 'born on high', and many readers take them to be the seraphs, now acting as her heavenly kin and carrying her off. The ambiguity suits the poem's double world: the same event is a funeral on earth and an abduction by heaven. Hoffman reads kinsmen and angels alike as figures of everything that intrudes on the closed world of the couple.

    Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'high-born'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  10. sepulchre l. 19

    The word is the King James term for the tomb of Christ (Matthew 27:60), which lends the burial solemnity. 'Shut her up' suggests imprisonment, as if the family had locked away a living girl; Poe's fiction often dwells on burial alive ('The Fall of the House of Usher', 'The Premature Burial'). The sepulchre returns in the final stanza, where the speaker lies down beside it every night.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sepulchre'; Bible (KJV), Matthew 27:60; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  11. 'not half so happy' l. 21

    The claim is bold theology: earthly love surpasses heavenly bliss. The Poe Society notes that many reprints substitute 'so merry in Heaven' for 'so happy in Heaven', a variant students will meet in anthologies. The following parenthesis, '(as all men know', is a rhetorical bluff that claims common knowledge for a private conviction, and the dashes and 'Yes!' make the speaker's voice more agitated than in the opening stanzas.

    Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Annabel Lee': manuscripts, printings and texts; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  12. 'Chilling and killing' l. 26

    Stanza 3 had ended a line on 'chilling'; now the word is paired with 'killing' inside a single line. The device is typical of Poe's late style ('The Raven', 'Ulalume', 'The Bells') and of his belief, set out in 'The Poetic Principle', that music lies close to the essence of poetry. The jingle is the poem's first plain statement that she died, delivered in a sound almost like a nursery rhyme, which makes it all the more disturbing.

    Poe, 'The Poetic Principle' (lecture 1848-49; printed 1850); T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  13. Older and wiser l. 28

    The two lines beginning 'Of' rhyme on 'we' and run on into the 'sea' and 'Lee' rhymes that close the stanza. The contrast between child-lovers and older, wiser people repeats the poem's claim that this love was 'more than love'. Critics divide over whether this is Romantic defiance (love stronger than death, as in the Song of Songs 8:6, 'love is strong as death') or a grieving man's refusal to accept adult reality.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 8:6; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  14. Angels and demons l. 31

    The pairing recalls Romans 8:38-39, where neither death nor life nor angels can separate the faithful from the love of God; Poe transfers the promise to human lovers. The demons under the sea give the 'kingdom by the sea' a hell beneath it. Critics have noted that, unlike 'The Raven', whose speaker's soul shall be lifted 'nevermore', this poem insists on the lovers' union beyond death.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 8:38-39; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  15. dissever l. 32

    The OED records 'dissever' from Middle English in the sense of separating or dividing. Poe uses it for its weight and for its chime with 'ever', another internal rhyme. The claim that soul cannot be parted from soul is the poem's thesis, and it prepares for the final stanza, where the dead woman's presence is felt every night through the moon and stars.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dissever'; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes

  16. 'night-tide' and the internal rhymes l. 38

    'Tide' in the old sense of time or season (as in 'eventide' and 'Christmastide') gives 'night-tide', the whole night; the sea setting adds the second sense of a tide that rises and falls. The last stanza is crowded with internal rhymes (beams/dreams, rise/eyes, night-tide/side, life/bride), so the music thickens as the speaker approaches the tomb. Some texts print the word without a hyphen.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tide'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'night-tide'; Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Annabel Lee': manuscripts, printings and texts

  17. 'my life and my bride' l. 39

    Poe's own theory held that the death of a beautiful woman is, as he put it, 'the most poetical topic in the world' ('The Philosophy of Composition'). The ending takes that idea to its limit: the lover sleeps beside the tomb each night. Marie Bonaparte's psychoanalytic study (1933) read such poems as necrophile fantasy rooted in the early loss of Poe's mother; later critics such as Hoffman see a worship that becomes possible only once the beloved is dead. Others read 'bride' as simple conjugal fidelity beyond death.

    Poe, 'The Philosophy of Composition', Graham's Magazine, April 1846; Marie Bonaparte, Edgar Poe: Étude psychanalytique (1933); English trans. 1949; Daniel Hoffman, Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe Poe (Doubleday, 1972), on 'Annabel Lee'

  18. The last line and its variant l. 41

    The final line is the most famous variant in the poem. The Poe Society's comparative text shows that the first four fair copies of 1849 (Griswold, Moore, Hirst, Sartain) read 'In her tomb by the sounding sea', a conventional poetic epithet, and Sartain's Union Magazine printed that reading in January 1850. Poe's last manuscript, the Thompson copy of 26 September 1849, reads 'In her tomb by the side of the sea', which repeats 'side' from two lines before and keeps the flat, exhausted cadence of grief. Mabbott prints both the Griswold and Thompson texts, so the reading a student meets depends on the edition.

    Edgar Allan Poe Society of Baltimore, 'Annabel Lee': comparative text of the five 1849 manuscripts; T. O. Mabbott, ed., Collected Works of Edgar Allan Poe, vol. 1: Poems (Harvard UP, 1969), 'Annabel Lee', introduction and notes; Wikipedia, 'Annabel Lee' (versions and variants, citing the Poe Society)
