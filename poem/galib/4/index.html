Love

The Song of Love's Nurse

by Şeyh Galip 1757–1799

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

LoveLiving

O Moon! sleep, sleep thou, for this night
The cry 'O Lord!' upon thine ear shall smite;
Though formed, its purpose is yet hid from sight,
It shall be seen — the stars' potential might.
Thou'lt be the roast upon the spit of pain!

O Rosebud! sleep thou then this little while;
The Sphere's design against thee sooth is vile.
For pitiless is it and strong in guile;
Ah! never trust it, even though it smile.
Thou'lt have, I fear me, reason oft to plain!

O Love's Narcissus! sleep the sleep of peace!
Fall at the skirt of Fate and beg surcease;
Thy soul's eye ope — and, lo! thy fears increase!
Guard thee against the end of woe, nor cease.
Thou'lt be as plaything by Misfortune la'en!

Come, in the cradle of repose thee rest
A few short nights, by sorrow undistrest;
Bid care and all it brings leave thee unprest;
In place of milk, blood shall be thy bequest.
Thou'lt need the goblet of despite to drain!

O Jasmine-breast! within the cradle lie;
Thus will not long remain the rolling Sky:
The stars do not aye in one circle hie;
See what they'll do to thee, Love, by-and-bye.
Thou'lt be the mill on sorrow's torrent's train!

From slumber do not thou thine eyelids keep.
If aid can reach thee, it will come through sleep;
The Sphere will give a draught of poison deep.
Then will thy work, like Galib's, be to weep.
Thou'lt be the rebeck at the feast of pain!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A lullaby sung by Love's nurse in Galib's Husn ü Aşk, in which the child beauties, the Moon, the Rosebud and the Narcissus, are warned of the cruelty of Fate that is to come.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: the nurse's lullaby in Hüsn ü Aşk st. 1

    Şeyh Galib (1757-1799), a Mevlevi dervish and later sheikh of the Galata Mevlevihane in Istanbul, wrote Hüsn ü Aşk at about twenty-six, after friends claimed that Nâbî's romance Hayrâbâd could not be equalled; he finished it in some six months, in 1197/1783. The mesnevi tells of two children, Hüsn (Beauty, a girl) and Aşk (Love, a boy), born on the same night in the tribe of Benî Mahabbet ('Sons of Love'). To win Beauty, Love must journey to the City of the Heart and suffer many trials, until he learns that he and Beauty are one. Gibb's heading gives the opening as 'Ey mâh uyu uyu ki bu şeb'. Gibb says this is the lullaby that Love's nurse sings over his cradle.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131 and note 252, pp. 261-262; Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999); Şeyh Galip, Beauty and Love, trans. Victoria Rowe Holbrook (New York: Modern Language Association, 2005); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), pp. 180-195 (on Hüsn ü Aşk); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), the lullaby as piece [403], pp. 198-199

  2. Stanza form: a tardiyye inside the mesnevi every stanza

    The romance itself is in rhyming couplets (mesnevi) in the metre mef'ûlü mefâilün feûlün, the metre of Leylâ and Mecnun romances. To vary the long narrative Galib inserted four songs headed 'tardiyye', each of six stanzas; Okçu's article counts 2,041 couplets of narrative and 2,101 with the songs. This lullaby has the six stanzas of such a song, and Gibb's English keeps its five-line stanza: four lines on one rhyme and a fifth line that returns to a rhyme shared by all six stanzas. Each fifth line is a prophecy beginning 'Thou'lt be' or 'Thou'lt need', so the lullaby turns into a horoscope of a lover's sorrows.

    Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999); E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131

  3. The heart roasted on the spit l. 5

    In Ottoman and Persian poetry the lover's heart, or liver (ciğer, the seat of feeling), is burnt by the fire of love and becomes kebab on the skewer of grief. Each stanza ends with a similar picture of a lover's fate: plaything of misfortune, drinker from the goblet of spite, mill turned by a torrent of tears, rebeck at the feast of pain. The images are stock (mazmun), but Galib's achievement, in the eyes of critics from Gibb to Tanpınar, was to renew the old images with fresh fancy in the manner of the 'Indian style' (sebk-i Hindî).

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; TDV contributor, 'Mazmun', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999)

  4. 'O Lord!': the lover's cry l. 2

    The night-long cry of 'yâ Rab!' is the sound of the lover's grief in divan poetry, uttered at night or at dawn, when sighs were thought most effective. The nurse foresees that the child will hear that cry tonight, as if the destiny of a lover begins at once. The irony is that a lullaby, meant to send a child to sleep, is filled with the sleeplessness of lovers.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), p. 95, n. (the sigh of dawn)

  5. Fate already formed ll. 3-4

    Hüsn and Aşk are born on the same night, so their horoscope is shared, and the idea that a child's fortune is fixed by the stars at birth runs through the song (the 'Sphere', the 'rolling Sky', the stars that 'do not aye in one circle hie'). Gibb's lines are difficult: 'Though formed, its purpose is yet hid from sight, / It shall be seen' seems to mean that destiny is already decided (in Islamic thought, written in pre-eternity) but not yet revealed, and that the stars' 'potential' (latent) power will become actual. Readers should treat the precise sense as uncertain; Holbrook's modern translation can be compared.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; Şeyh Galip, Beauty and Love, trans. Victoria Rowe Holbrook (New York: Modern Language Association, 2005); Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999)

  6. Moon, Rosebud, Narcissus, Jasmine-breast sts. 1-5

    Moon, rosebud (the small closed mouth), narcissus (the eye) and jasmine (white skin) are the beloved's attributes in divan poetry, not the lover's. By giving them to the baby Aşk, the nurse's song quietly anticipates the end of the romance, where Love learns from Sühan (Speech) that 'Love is Beauty and Beauty is Love', and that there is no room for twoness in unity. In the Sufi allegory, Okçu explains, Beauty stands for Absolute Beauty (God) and Love for the seeker. The endearments are also natural in a lullaby, and the poem works on both levels.

    Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999); Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994); E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 252, pp. 261-262

  7. The treacherous Sphere ll. 7-9

    In divan poetry felek, the turning sphere of heaven, is the agent of fate and nearly always the lover's enemy: it separates lovers, overturns fortunes and is fickle by nature. Gibb's notes on another poem explain the Ptolemaic spheres. The warning 'never trust it, even though it smile' is a commonplace of the poetry of complaint (şikâyet). In Galib's allegory the dangers that follow, the well, the witch, the sea of fire, are the obstacles the seeker must pass, so the complaint against fate is also a description of the mystic path.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 84, p. 240 (the spheres); Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999)

  8. The narcissus-eye l. 11

    The narcissus (nergis) is the standard figure for the eye, especially a languid, drowsy or love-sick eye, because of its shape and its drooping head. To bid the 'Narcissus' to sleep is therefore apt. The stanza contrasts the bodily eye, which may sleep, with the 'soul's eye', which on opening sees what is coming. The suggestion is that spiritual insight brings more suffering, not less, until the end of the path.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  9. Clasping the skirt l. 12

    Seizing or falling at the hem of a person's robe was a gesture of humble petition, made to a king, a saint or a beloved; Bâkî's qasida uses the 'skirt of aid and grace' of God in its closing prayer. Here the petition is addressed to Fate itself, and 'surcease' (an old word for cessation or respite, in the OED) is what the child is to ask for. The advice is hopeless: the following line already says the fears will grow.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'surcease'; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131

  10. Blood instead of milk l. 19

    Gibb's notes explain that 'to drink one's own blood' means to suffer intense sorrow, and 'to shed tears of blood' to weep in bitter anguish. In a lullaby sung by a nurse the contrast with milk is especially sharp: the infant's first nourishment is replaced by the drink of lovers. The next line continues the drinking image with the 'goblet of despite', a bitter inversion of the wine-cup of the feast.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 77, p. 239; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131

  11. The turning stars ll. 22-23

    The heavens are a wheel (çarh) that never stops, and their turning is the image of changing fortune. 'Hie' is the old verb for 'hasten' or 'go'. The nurse reasons from astronomy to fate: since the stars move, the configuration that now protects the sleeping child will alter, and then 'see what they'll do to thee'. Readers who know Galib's Mevlevi background may hear in the turning heavens the cosmic whirling that his sema ghazel celebrates, though here the motion is threatening.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hie'

  12. The mill turned by sorrow l. 25

    The water-mill turned by a stream is a figure for ceaseless motion and for the lover's eye that pours tears. Galib's contemporary readers knew the water-wheel image well (Necâtî compares himself to a water-wheel watering the beloved's garden with his tears in a ghazel Gibb translated). Here the torrent is sorrow itself and the lover is the mill, ground and turning for ever.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 130-131; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), Necâtî, ghazel [91] 'a-turn a-turn', with n. 3

  13. Help through sleep l. 27

    The line explains why the song is a lullaby at all. Sleep, the nurse's gift, is the one refuge before the trials of waking life. In the romance, help in fact comes from Sühan (Speech, or the Word), who appears in many disguises to rescue Love; Okçu reads Sühan as the perfect spiritual guide. Whether the line also suggests the Sufi idea that the unseen world is reached in sleep and dream is a matter of interpretation; it is safest to read it first as the nurse's simple, sad advice.

    Naci Okçu, 'Hüsn ü Aşk', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (1999); Şeyh Galip, Beauty and Love, trans. Victoria Rowe Holbrook (New York: Modern Language Association, 2005)

  14. The poet's name l. 29

    Songs set into a mesnevi, like ghazels, could carry the poet's pen-name in the final stanza. Galib's pen-name means 'victorious', which sits ironically beside a life of weeping. The identification of the poet with Love, the hero, fits the romance's allegory of the seeker's path, and the poet's preface on his own times and his rivalry with Nâbî's admirers; Holbrook has argued for reading the romance as reflecting on poetry and its own making. Galib was born in 1757 near the Yenikapı Mevlevihane and died on 4 January 1799.

    M. Muhsin Kalkışım, 'Şeyh Galib', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 39 (2010), pp. 54-57; Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994)

  15. The rebeck l. 30

    The rebab is a bowed instrument of the Middle East; the English 'rebeck' (OED: a medieval fiddle) comes ultimately from Arabic rabāb. Its sound was heard as weeping, like that of the reed-flute, and both rebab and ney were part of Mevlevi music. To be the rebeck at the 'feast of pain' (bezm-i gam) is to be the voice of lament itself. The image closes the lullaby with music, the art of Galib's own father's convent world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rebeck'; TDV contributor, 'Ney', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; M. Muhsin Kalkışım, 'Şeyh Galib', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 39 (2010), pp. 54-57
