While the Lovers Circle Here in Mystic Dance

by Şeyh Galip 1757–1799

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1905) · From A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (1905)

Religion

While the lovers circle here in mystic dance,
Sun and moon traverse the sphere in mystic dance.

Love's deep secret made the spheric heavens reel;
All the worlds do still appear in mystic dance.

E'en as 'twere a whirlpool shows the weeping eye,
Casting pearls and spray as clear in mystic dance.

Youthful Magians sweet about the Tavern go,
Like as pilgrims round in fere in mystic dance.

Lo, the reed ecstatic thrills, the Heavenly Birds
Dove-like beat their plumy gear in mystic dance.

I am yonder vagrant Qays upon whose head
Whiles the nests do leap and rear in mystic dance.

Ghalib, while the Sun of Love doth radiance pour,
Mevlevis will mote-like veer in mystic dance.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A ghazel on the sema, the mystic whirling dance of the Mevlevi dervishes, in which the heavens and the lovers turn together. Gibb's notes explain the sema and the 'Heavenly Birds' who are moved by the Mevlevi reed-flute.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: a ghazel on the Mevlevi sema couplet 1

    Gibb printed this as Ghazel [408] in his History of Ottoman Poetry, the first of four ghazels he gave from Galib's Divan, noting that it refers to the sema, which led Europeans to call the Mevlevis 'Dancing' or 'Whirling Dervishes'. Galib (1757-1799) was born into a Mevlevi family, entered the order, and in 1791 became sheikh of the Galata Mevlevihane, where Sultan Selim III, himself a composer, rebuilt the semahane. The poem appears to be the seven-couplet ghazel with the refrain 'eyler semâ' ('performs the sema') on which Demirbağ (2024) has published a commentary, reading it as a picture of all existence drawn towards divine love.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), Ghazel [408], pp. 203-204, with Gibb's notes; M. Muhsin Kalkışım, 'Şeyh Galib', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 39 (2010), pp. 54-57; Abdulsamet Demirbağ, 'A Commentary on Sheikh Galib's Ghazal with "Eyler Semâ" Rhymed', Bingöl Üniversitesi Sosyal Bilimler Enstitüsü Dergisi 27 (2024), pp. 122-128

  2. The refrain every couplet

    A ghazel keeps one rhyme and, often, a repeated word or phrase after the rhyme (redif) in both lines of the first couplet and the second line of every other. Gibb reproduces the effect with 'in mystic dance' and an English rhyme on '-ere' (here, sphere, appear, clear...). The refrain makes the ghazel itself turn: lovers, sun and moon, the heavens, the weeping eye, the tavern boys, the angels, Mecnun's birds and finally the Mevlevis are each caught up in the same motion. If the original redif is 'eyler semâ', as seems likely, each couplet literally ends 'performs the sema'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), pp. 203-204; Abdulsamet Demirbağ, 'A Commentary on Sheikh Galib's Ghazal with "Eyler Semâ" Rhymed', Bingöl Üniversitesi Sosyal Bilimler Enstitüsü Dergisi 27 (2024), pp. 122-128

  3. The lovers: the dervishes l. 1

    The Sufi vocabulary of love makes the dervish an âşık (lover) and God the ma'şûk (beloved). Gibb's note identifies the 'lovers' circling here as the Mevlevi dervishes performing the sema. The dervishes, the semazens, turn on their own axis, the right hand raised to receive grace and the left lowered to pass it on, while moving round the hall; the poem's next line makes the sun and moon do the same in their spheres. The ritual (also called mukabele) is the best-known form of Sufi sema.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 203, n. 3; TDV contributor, 'Semâ', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 36 (2009), pp. 455-457; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  4. Cosmic sema l. 2

    Mevlevi tradition explained the sema as an imitation of the turning of the heavens and of all created things around their centre. Galib's couplet states the correspondence directly: the lovers in the hall and the planets in the sky perform the same dance. The idea that love moves the heavens is also old in philosophy (the heavens move out of desire for the First Mover), and English readers may compare the last line of Dante's Paradiso, 'the love that moves the sun and the other stars', though there is no direct link.

    TDV contributor, 'Semâ', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 36 (2009), pp. 455-457; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 203

  5. The secret of love ll. 3-4

    Love (aşk) is in Sufi thought the cause of creation: the famous saying in which God says 'I was a hidden treasure and I wished to be known, so I created the world' was read as making love the motive of all existence. So the turning of the spheres is a kind of intoxication with that secret. Demirbağ's commentary, as summarized in its abstract, reads the ghazel as showing all existence drawn to divine love and links this attraction to the sema. 'All the worlds' (âlemler) are the many levels of being, from the earthly to the divine.

    Abdulsamet Demirbağ, 'A Commentary on Sheikh Galib's Ghazal with "Eyler Semâ" Rhymed', Bingöl Üniversitesi Sosyal Bilimler Enstitüsü Dergisi 27 (2024), pp. 122-128; TDV contributor, 'Kenz-i Mahfî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  6. The weeping eye as whirlpool ll. 5-6

    Tears are pearls in the poetic code, and the eye that pours them is a sea or a spring. Galib adds motion: a whirlpool spins, so the weeping eye too 'dances'. The image shows his taste for joining stock figures in surprising ways, the hallmark of the 'Indian style' (sebk-i Hindî) of which he is the great Ottoman representative. Every couplet finds a new thing that turns.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 204; TDV contributor, 'Mazmun', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  7. Young Magians round the Tavern, like pilgrims ll. 7-8

    In Persian and Turkish poetry the wine-tavern was imagined as kept by Zoroastrian 'Magians', whose young servants, the mugbeçe, poured wine; the Magian elder, pîr-i mugân, became a name for the Sufi master. Galib compares their circling to the pilgrims' tawaf, the sevenfold circumambulation of the Kaaba at Mecca. The daring parallel of tavern and Kaaba is a commonplace of Sufi poetry: the true pilgrimage is round the place where the wine of divine love is served. Gibb's 'in fere' is an old phrase meaning 'together, in company' (OED, s.v. 'fere').

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 204; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fere'; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  8. The reed-flute l. 9

    The ney is the central instrument of Mevlevi music, and its player (neyzen) leads the sema. Jalal al-Din Rumi's Mesnevi, the order's sacred book, opens with the complaint of the reed cut from its reed-bed, which became the emblem of the soul longing to return to its origin. When Galib says the reed 'thrills' in ecstasy, every Mevlevi reader would hear that opening. The ney's music is what sets the sema, and in the next line the angels, in motion.

    TDV contributor, 'Ney', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; TDV contributor, 'Mevleviyye', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  9. The angels as birds ll. 9-10

    Gibb explains that the angels are meant, and that they are moved by the Mevlevian reed-flute to break into the sema themselves. Doves turning in flight over a courtyard are a familiar sight, and the beating of wings matches the spreading of the dervishes' skirts as they turn. The couplet moves the dance from the hall up to the heavens, completing the poem's vertical scheme of earth and sky.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 204, n. 1

  10. Qays, that is Mecnun, with birds in his hair ll. 11-12

    Qays ibn al-Mulawwah, called Mecnun ('madman'), loved Leylâ and, parted from her, wandered the desert with the wild beasts. Gibb's note recalls that the birds built their nests in his long unkempt hair. The story came to Ottoman readers through Nizami and through Fuzûlî's Turkish Leylâ vü Mecnûn (1535). Galib's twist is that the lover's ecstasy shakes his head so that the very nests on it 'leap and rear' in the dance. Gibb's 'whiles' is an old word for 'at times'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 204, n. 2; TDV contributor, 'Leylâ vü Mecnûn', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  11. The Sun of Love: a hint of Şems l. 13

    The mote dancing in a sunbeam is one of Rumi's favourite images for the soul's ecstatic movement in the light of God. Shams-i Tabrizi ('Sun of Tabriz'), the wandering dervish whose friendship transformed Rumi in 1244, gave his name, which means 'sun', to a whole cycle of Rumi's lyrics. A Mevlevi poet saying 'Sun of Love' could hardly avoid the association, though the main sense is God as the source of love's light. The image ends the poem on the smallest and the greatest at once: the mote and the sun.

    Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); TDV contributor, 'Mevleviyye', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 204

  12. The Mevlevis l. 14

    The Mevlevi order takes its name from Mevlânâ ('our master'), Jalal al-Din Rumi (d. 1273), and was organized by his son Sultan Veled; its mother house is at Konya, and its convents (mevlevîhâne) were centres of music and poetry across the Ottoman lands. Galib, as sheikh of the Galata Mevlevihane from 1791, presided over its sema. In the signature couplet the poet names himself, 'Ghalib', and his order together, so that the ghazel ends with the community whose ritual it has described from the first line.

    TDV contributor, 'Mevleviyye', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; M. Muhsin Kalkışım, 'Şeyh Galib', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 39 (2010), pp. 54-57
