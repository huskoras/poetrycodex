Love

LXXXV: Half I hate, half love. How so? one haply requireth

by Gaius Valerius Catullus c. 84 – c. 54 BC

Translated by Robinson Ellis (1871) · From The Poems and Fragments of Catullus (1871)

Love

Half I hate, half love. How so? one haply requireth.
Nay, I know not; alas feel it, in agony groan.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Half I hate, half love' l. 1

    Poem 85 is an elegiac couplet, a single hexameter and pentameter, on the poet's feeling for Lesbia. It opens odi et amo, 'I hate and I love': two full verbs joined by et, so that both feelings are total and simultaneous. Ellis's 'Half I hate, half love' makes them two halves of one divided state, an interpretation rather than a translation. Ellis's commentary links the couplet to the ancient idea that violent love turns into violent hate (Theophrastus, quoted by Plutarch). He dates it rather early in the affair. Merrill instead dates it with poems 72 and 75, which voice disillusion with her. Ovid imitates the opening at Amores 2.4.5 and 3.11b.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 85, pp. 461; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 85; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  2. Love and liking: the context in poems 72 and 75 l. 1

    The paradox of poem 85 is spelled out in two related epigrams. In poem 72 Catullus says that Lesbia's wrongs force a lover 'to love more, but to wish well less' (amare magis, sed bene velle minus). In poem 75 his mind has been brought so low that he cannot wish her well even if she becomes perfect, nor stop loving her whatever she does. Amare there means sexual passion, and bene velle the affection and good will of friendship. Read with these poems, odi et amo describes the loss of affection and the survival of desire. Merrill places 85 with 72 and 75 for this reason.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 85; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Kenneth Quinn, ed., Catullus: The Poems (London: Macmillan, 1970; 2nd edn 1973), note on the poem

  3. The couplet as question and answer l. 1

    The couplet is built as a small dialogue. The hexameter gives the claim and then the imagined objection: quare id faciam, fortasse requiris ('why do I do it, perhaps you ask'). The pentameter replies: nescio, sed fieri sentio et excrucior ('I do not know, but I feel it happening, and I am tortured'). The poem is almost all verbs: odi, amo, faciam, requiris, nescio, fieri, sentio, excrucior. Ellis imitates the elegiac couplet with an accentual hexameter and pentameter. His terse 'How so?' compresses quare id faciam, and so he loses the verb faciam ('I do'), which the Latin sets against fieri ('it is done to me').

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 85, pp. 461; D. F. S. Thomson, ed., Catullus (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1997), commentary on the poem

  4. 'one haply requireth' l. 1

    The Latin fortasse requiris means 'perhaps you ask', addressing the reader, or an imagined friend, in the second person. Ellis's 'one haply requireth' makes the questioner impersonal and puts the phrase into archaic English. 'Haply' means 'perhaps, by chance' (OED, haply adv.). 'Requireth' uses require in its old sense of 'to ask, enquire' (OED, require v.), which is the sense of Latin requirere here. The archaic third-person ending -eth adds distance. In Catullus the question is intimate and challenging, and the answer is given to 'you'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'haply'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'require'; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  5. 'Nay, I know not' and its imitators l. 2

    The pentameter opens with the flat admission nescio. Merrill compares Martial 1.32, non amo te, Sabidi, nec possum dicere quare ('I do not love you, Sabidius, nor can I say why'). He also notes its English imitation by Tom Brown, 'I do not love thee, Doctor Fell'. Martial's couplet turns Catullus's anguish into a social joke: the reason is unknown, but the dislike is simple. The archive holds Martial's epigram in Ker's prose (martial/368) and Fletcher's verse of 1656 (martial/11), so the contrast can be read directly. Ellis's 'Nay' adds an emphatic refusal that the bare Latin verb does not need.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 85; Martial, Epigrams 1.32, trans. W. C. A. Ker (Loeb, 1919), archive.org

  6. 'alas' l. 2

    The Latin line runs nescio, sed fieri sentio et excrucior. Sed ('but') sets what the poet cannot know against what he can feel: he cannot explain, but he can perceive. Ellis replaces this logical turn with the lament 'alas', which tells the reader how to feel and gives the line a Victorian pathos that Catullus avoids. The plainness of the Latin, which has no adjectives, no names and no images, is often taken to be the source of its power. Students comparing translations should note how many of them, like Ellis, add an emotional word here.

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 85, pp. 461; C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  7. 'feel it': fieri sentio l. 2

    The question in line 1 was why 'I do' (faciam) this. The answer uses fieri, the passive of facere: 'I feel it being done', or 'happening'. The shift from active to passive is the centre of the poem. The speaker is not the agent of his hatred and love but the place where they occur, and he can only register them (sentio). Commentators stress how much depends on these two forms of one verb. Ellis's 'feel it' keeps sentio but drops fieri, so the poised contrast between doing and being done to is lost in the English.

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); D. F. S. Thomson, ed., Catullus (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1997), commentary on the poem; Kenneth Quinn, ed., Catullus: The Poems (London: Macmillan, 1970; 2nd edn 1973), note on the poem

  8. 'in agony groan' l. 2

    The poem ends on excrucior, 'I am tormented'. The verb is formed from ex and cruciare, from crux, the cross on which slaves were executed. It is used in comedy for a lover's torment, but here, as the final word of so spare a couplet, it carries its full physical sense. Like fieri it is passive: suffering is done to the speaker. Ellis's 'in agony groan' makes the pain audible and active ('groan'), where the Latin keeps it internal and passive. Ellis's commentary sets the poem beside Ovid's imitations, where the paradox becomes witty rather than agonised.

    Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. excrucio; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 85, pp. 461; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)
