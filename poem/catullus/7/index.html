VIII: Ah poor Catullus, learn to play the fool no more

by Gaius Valerius Catullus c. 84 – c. 54 BC

Translated by Robinson Ellis (1871) · From The Poems and Fragments of Catullus (1871)

Love

Ah poor Catullus, learn to play the fool no more.
Lost is the lost, thou know'st it, and the past is past.

Bright once the days and sunny shone the light on thee,
Still ever hasting where she led, the maid so fair,
By me belov'd as maiden is belov'd no more.

Was then enacting all the merry mirth wherein
Thyself delighted, and the maid she said not nay.
Ah truly bright and sunny shone the days on thee.

Now she resigns thee; child, do thou resign no less,
Nor follow her that flies thee, or to bide in woe
Consent, but harden all thy heart, resolve, endure.

Farewell, my love. Catullus is resolv'd, endures,
He will not ask for pity, will not importune.

But thou'lt be mourning thus to pine unask'd alway.
O past retrieval faithless! Ah what hours are thine!
When comes a likely wooer? who protests thou'rt fair?

Who brooks to love thee? who decrees to live thine own?
Whose kiss delights thee? whose the lips that own thy bite?
Yet, yet, Catullus, learn to bear, resolve, endure.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Catullus 8: the poem and this translation l. 1

    In poem 8 Catullus speaks to himself as miser Catulle, 'wretched Catullus', and tells himself to give up a mistress who no longer wants him. Merrill dates it to about 59 BC, during a quarrel, and is sure the girl is Lesbia. Ellis's commentary notes how often Catullus addresses himself and uses his own name. The metre is the choliambus or scazon ('limping iambic'), an iambic line whose last foot is reversed, so that it seems to stumble at the close. Robinson Ellis's translation (1871) uses long iambic lines, many ending in a heavy pair of stresses ('no more', 'is past'). The archive keeps his indentations, which mark the poem's sections.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 25-27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  2. 'learn to play the fool no more' l. 1

    The Latin desinas ineptire means 'you are to stop playing the fool'. Merrill points out that ineptire is colloquial: it appears twice in Terence, never in Plautus, and in no classical author after Catullus. Ellis's commentary reads desinas as a hortatory subjunctive laying down a rule of conduct, rather than a plain command. The vocabulary of the comic stage matters to the poem's tone, since lovesick young men in Plautus and Terence are told the same things by their slaves. Ellis's 'learn to play the fool no more' is close, and the added 'learn' anticipates the poem's final line.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. ineptio

  3. 'Lost is the lost' l. 2

    The Latin is et quod vides perisse perditum ducas, 'and what you see has perished, count as lost'. Merrill and Ellis both trace it to Plautus, Trinummus 1026, quin tu quod periit periisse ducis?, a commonplace of comic common sense. Ellis notes that perire and perdere were often set side by side as passive and active. His translation, 'Lost is the lost', keeps the jingle of the two forms and makes it proverbial. The second half, 'and the past is past', is Ellis's own doubling. It fits the poem's theme of past happiness set against present loss.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  4. 'Bright once the days and sunny shone the light on thee' l. 3

    Catullus writes fulsere quondam candidi tibi soles, 'once the bright suns shone for you'. Merrill explains candidi soles as days of good fortune and contrasts Horace's 'black sun' of a bad day (Satires 1.9.72). Ellis cites Aeschylus's 'white day' (Persians 301) and Sophocles' use of a 'white' day for a lucky one. The line is repeated as a refrain at line 8 with vere ('truly') replacing quondam ('once'). Ellis's English spends a whole long line on it, with both 'Bright' and 'sunny' standing for candidi.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  5. 'Still ever hasting where she led' l. 4

    The Latin cum ventitabas quo puella ducebat means 'when you used to keep going wherever the girl led'. Ventitare is a frequentative, so the action was repeated. Merrill says it refers not to a particular house but to the lover's whole submission to her guidance. Ellis agrees that ducebat ('led the way') implies Catullus's surrender to her will, not any trick on her part. The image of the lover as a follower recurs at line 10, where he is told not to follow one who runs away. Ellis's 'Still ever hasting' gives the repetition well. 'Hasting', an archaic form of 'hastening', adds eagerness.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'haste'

  6. 'By me belov'd as maiden is belov'd no more' l. 5

    The Latin amata nobis quantum amabitur nulla means 'loved by us as much as none will be loved'. Merrill uses this line to identify the girl, since Catullus makes the same claim for Lesbia in poems 37 and 87. The plural nobis ('by us') is a common poetic plural for 'by me'. The future amabitur makes the claim reach into all time. Ellis keeps the doubled verb ('belov'd ... belov'd') and uses the archaic 'maid' for puella throughout. His 'no more' gives the Latin future a backward look, as though the speaker already knows the love is over.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  7. 'the maid she said not nay' l. 7

    Lines 6-7 describe the playful love of the past: ibi illa multa cum iocosa fiebant, / quae tu volebas nec puella nolebat, 'then those many playful things were done, which you wanted and the girl did not refuse'. The figure is litotes, assertion by a denied negative: volebas ... nec nolebat. Merrill compares Ovid's description of his own Muse as iocosa. Ellis renders iocosa, with its erotic undertone, as 'the merry mirth'. He keeps the litotes in 'she said not nay', an old ballad idiom, while 'enacting' is a slightly theatrical choice for fiebant ('were done').

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 26-27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  8. The refrain l. 8

    Line 8 repeats line 3 with one word changed: vere ('truly') for quondam ('once'). Merrill describes the poem's structure: the past is framed by these two lines, and the resolve for the future is framed by line 11 (obstinata mente perfer, obdura) and the last line (at tu, Catulle, destinatus obdura). He notes the small variation in each pair, quondam/vere and obstinata/destinatus. Ellis keeps both refrains clearly. His layout sets the refrain lines at the left margin and many of the lines between them indented, so the ring structure can be seen.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 25-27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  9. 'child, do thou resign no less' l. 9

    Line 9 reads nunc iam illa non volt: tu quoque impotens noli, 'now she no longer wants; you too, powerless one, must not want'. Impotens is the reading of the manuscripts, not an emendation; the question is what it means here. It can mean 'weak, without power over oneself', a man ruled by his passions, and that is the sense most editors give it, though it is a stronger word than a plain 'weakling'. The play between volt ('she wills') and noli ('do not will') answers the volebas ... nolebat of line 7. Ellis's 'child' renders impotens as helpless immaturity. His 'resigns ... resign' substitutes his own wordplay on giving up for Catullus's willing and unwilling.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 27; C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  10. 'Nor follow her that flies thee' l. 10

    The Latin is nec quae fugit sectare, nec miser vive, 'do not chase her who flees, and do not live in misery'. Merrill compares Theocritus 11.75, where the Cyclops asks himself why he chases the one who runs from him. He thinks Catullus may have had that passage in mind, though in Theocritus the words keep more of their literal meaning. The Cyclops, a ridiculous lovelorn monster, is an ironic model for a poet trying to be firm. The line also reverses the image of line 4, where Catullus went where the girl led. Ellis's 'or to bide in woe consent' turns nec miser vive into a refusal to accept misery.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Theocritus, Idyll 11 (The Cyclops), Greek text, Perseus Digital Library

  11. 'harden all thy heart, resolve, endure' l. 11

    The Latin is sed obstinata mente perfer, obdura, 'but with a stubborn mind, bear it out, be hard'. Merrill notes that the asyndeton (no 'and' between the imperatives) gives the line a rugged determination. He also notes that Ovid reuses the phrase (Tristia 5.11.7, perfer et obdura) and that Horace has persta atque obdura (Satires 2.5.39). Obdurare is literally 'to harden'. Ellis translates it twice, in 'harden' and 'endure', and adds 'resolve'. His triad then comes back in the next line and at the end of the poem, so it becomes a refrain of its own.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 27; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. obduro

  12. The shift to the third person l. 12

    Line 12 reads vale, puella. iam Catullus obdurat, 'goodbye, girl. Now Catullus is hard'. Merrill draws attention to the change of person: the poet has been addressing himself as 'you' and now speaks of 'Catullus' in the third person, before returning to 'you' at line 19. Ellis lists the many places where Catullus names himself. Readers disagree whether the third person here sounds like real self-mastery or like a man acting a part and watching himself do it. Ellis's 'Catullus is resolv'd, endures' keeps the switch. Note that in his version 'Farewell, my love' is warmer than the Latin's plain vale, puella.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 25; D. F. S. Thomson, ed., Catullus (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1997), commentary on the poem

  13. 'will not importune' l. 13

    The Latin is nec te requiret nec rogabit invitam, 'he will not seek you or ask you when you are unwilling'. Rogare ('to ask') is used, as often in love poetry, for asking a woman's favours. Merrill notes that in line 14 rogaberis is used in the same way, without saying what is asked for. That line turns the word round: she will grieve when nobody asks her (rogaberis nulla). Ellis renders the second verb as 'importune', 'to pester with requests' (OED). His clause 'He will not ask for pity' adds a sentiment that is not in the Latin.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'importune'; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  14. 'O past retrieval faithless! Ah what hours are thine!' l. 15

    Line 15 reads scelesta, vae te, quae tibi manet vita?, 'wretch, woe to you, what life remains for you?' Merrill notes that vae is usually followed by a dative, but an accusative of exclamation is also found in comedy. He takes tibi manet as 'will continue to be yours', not 'awaits you'. He observes that Catullus 'fans his anger' but at once dwells on details of past happiness, as if to tempt her back. Ellis renders scelesta as 'past retrieval faithless', a moral judgement stronger than the Latin, and he turns the question about her life into 'what hours are thine'.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  15. The string of questions ll. 16-18

    Lines 16-18 run: quis nunc te adibit? cui videberis bella? / quem nunc amabis? cuius esse diceris? / quem basiabis? cui labella mordebis?, 'who will come to you now? to whom will you seem pretty? whom will you love? whose will you be called? whom will you kiss? whose lips will you bite?' Merrill reads the questions as showing that Catullus does not yet suspect a rival, and he cites love-bites in Plautus, Horace (Odes 1.13.12) and Tibullus. The questions move from the social to the erotic, and the poet's resolve seems to weaken as he goes. Ellis's renderings are free: 'who decrees to live thine own?' is a loose version of cuius esse diceris ('whose will you be called?').

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  16. The last line l. 19

    The last line is at tu, Catulle, destinatus obdura, 'but you, Catullus, be determined and hard'. Merrill notes that this is the first use of destinatus in the sense of obstinatus ('resolute'), and that it varies the obstinata of line 11, just as vere varies quondam in the first refrain. The return to 'you' after the third-person 'Catullus' of line 12 suggests that the resolve has to be made again. Ellis's 'Yet, yet' doubles the adversative at, and his closing triad 'bear, resolve, endure' completes the series he began at line 11.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 8, pp. 27; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  17. How serious is the poem? whole poem

    Much of the poem's vocabulary (ineptire, perisse perditum, vae te) comes, as Merrill's notes show, from Plautus and Terence. There the lovesick young man is a stock comic figure who is told to be firm and never manages it. Merrill himself thought the poet 'ill able' to bear Lesbia's coldness with dignity and saw him keeping one eye open for reconciliation. A. L. Wheeler, following E. P. Morris, took the poem as a comic exercise, and Marilyn Skinner (1971) read the speaker as a comic lover ironically aware of his role. Fordyce, Fraenkel and Quinn instead treat it as a serious study of inner conflict, as P. J. Connor's survey (1974) sets out. Ellis's earnest, archaic English leans towards the serious reading.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 8; P. J. Connor, 'Catullus 8: The Lover's Conflict', Antichthon 8 (1974); Marilyn B. Skinner, 'Catullus 8: The Comic Amator as Eiron', Classical Journal 66 (1971), 298-305; Kenneth Quinn, ed., Catullus: The Poems (London: Macmillan, 1970; 2nd edn 1973), note on the poem
