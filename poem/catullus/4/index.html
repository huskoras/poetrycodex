Love

V: Living, Lesbia, we should e'en be loving

by Gaius Valerius Catullus c. 84 – c. 54 BC

Translated by Robinson Ellis (1871) · From The Poems and Fragments of Catullus (1871)

Love

Living, Lesbia, we should e'en be loving.
Sour severity, tongue of eld maligning,
All be to us a penny's estimation.

Suns set only to rise again to-morrow.
We, when sets in a little hour the brief light,
Sleep one infinite age, a night for ever.

Thousand kisses, anon to these an hundred,
Thousand kisses again, another hundred,
Thousand give me again, another hundred.

Then once heedfully counted all the thousands,
We'll uncount them as idly; so we shall not
Know, nor traitorous eye shall envy, knowing
All those myriad happy many kisses.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Catullus 5: the poem and this translation l. 1

    Catullus 5 is among the first poems to Lesbia, the name he gives his mistress, usually identified with Clodia, the wife of Metellus Celer. Merrill dates it, with poems 2 and 3, to the early days of the affair, when the poet trusted her fully. Its companion is poem 7, which asks how many kisses would be enough. Ovid and Martial both cite it. The archive holds Robinson Ellis's version (1871), which keeps the original metre: the Phalaecean hendecasyllable, an eleven-syllable line ('Living, Lesbia, we should e'en be loving'). Ellis also sets his thirteen English lines in groups of three, three, three and four, an arrangement that is his own, since the Latin runs on unbroken. As Merrill notes, the first word, vivamus, sets the key: 'let us live' in the full sense of enjoying life.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 18-20; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  2. 'we should e'en be loving' l. 1

    In Latin the line is two hortatory subjunctives joined: vivamus ... atque amemus, 'let us live and let us love'. Ellis puts both into participles and a 'should' ('Living ... we should e'en be loving'), which makes the line sound like advice or a rule of life rather than an invitation. The pairing of living and loving survives in the English tradition that grew from this poem, most famously in Thomas Campion's 'My sweetest Lesbia, let us live and love' (A Book of Airs, 1601) and Ben Jonson's 'Come, my Celia, let us prove' in Volpone (1606), which Ellis's own commentary cites. 'E'en' is the poetic monosyllabic form of 'even' (OED, even adv.), used here to fill the metre.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 18-19; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'even'

  3. 'tongue of eld maligning' l. 2

    The Latin is rumores senum severiorum, 'the talk of over-strict old men'. Merrill explains rumores here as remarks on what people have actually seen, not mere rumour, and notes that old men are the standing censors of youth in Roman comedy and in Horace (Ars Poetica 174, castigator censorque minorum). Ellis glosses severiorum as 'censorious'. His English abstracts the old men into 'Sour severity' and a 'tongue of eld': eld is an archaic noun for old age, or the old collectively (OED, eld n.). 'Maligning' adds a hint of slander that the Latin only implies. The comparative severiorum ('rather too severe') is a Catullan mannerism, like venustiorum in poem 3.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eld'

  4. 'a penny's estimation' l. 3

    The Latin is omnes unius aestimemus assis, 'let us value them all at one as'. The as was the basic copper coin, worth very little by Catullus's time, so the phrase means 'count as nothing'. Ellis points out that the contrast omnes ... unius ('all ... at one') sharpens a phrase that would otherwise be commonplace. Merrill notes that Catullus is the first writer to use assis and pili ('a hair') in such phrases, where Plautus and Terence say nauci or nihili. The language of valuation (aestimare) begins the money imagery that returns at the end of the poem with counting and bankruptcy.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. as

  5. 'Suns set only to rise again' l. 4

    Catullus writes soles occidere et redire possunt, 'suns can set and come back'. The plural 'suns' (meaning days) is noted by Ellis as a Grecism. The thought, that nature renews itself but man does not, was a commonplace of Greek lament. Both Merrill and Ellis quote the Lament for Bion attributed to Moschus (3.106 ff.), where mallows and parsley die and grow again but men sleep an endless sleep. Horace develops the same contrast with the moons in Odes 4.7.13 ff. Ellis's English keeps the plural and the bare statement. His 'only to rise again' is a fair rendering of possunt: they can, as we cannot.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Horace, Odes 4.7, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  6. 'the brief light' l. 5

    Catullus ends his fifth line with nobis cum semel occidit brevis lux, 'for us, when once the brief light has set'. Merrill calls a monosyllable at the end of this metre 'very unusual' and compares cum tacet nox in poem 7. He also notes the pointed opposition of lux at the end of one line and nox at the start of the next. Ellis's commentary adds that lux means 'our short day of life', since 'light' was a common Latin figure for being alive. His translation keeps 'the brief light' as the last words of the line, so the abrupt monosyllabic close is partly preserved, though 'a little hour' is his own addition.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  7. 'one infinite age, a night for ever' l. 6

    The Latin reads nox est perpetua una dormienda, 'there is one everlasting night to be slept'. The gerundive dormienda gives the sense of compulsion: the sleep must be undergone. Ellis's commentary praises the rhythm of the line and its repeated vowel sound as an image of a sleep that never breaks, and collects Greek parallels from Callimachus and the Greek Anthology. He also quotes Ben Jonson's adaptation in Volpone, where suns that set may rise again but, once we lose this light, ''Tis with us perpetual night'. Ellis's own English doubles the idea ('one infinite age, a night for ever') to fill the hendecasyllable.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Ben Jonson, Volpone (1606), Act III, the song 'Come, my Celia' (Project Gutenberg)

  8. 'Thousand kisses' l. 7

    Catullus says da mi basia mille, 'give me a thousand kisses'. Merrill notes that basium is first found here. It later displaced both savium and the more formal osculum in everyday speech and passed into the Romance languages (Italian bacio, French baiser). Merrill thought it might be a word from the Po valley, Catullus's own region, and Ellis quotes Landor to the same effect. Martial clearly read this poem and poem 7 as the classic kiss poems: he jokes that Rome gave a returning friend more kisses than Lesbia gave Catullus (12.59). Ellis drops 'give me' from this line and saves the imperative for 'Thousand give me again'.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. basium

  9. The count of kisses ll. 7-9

    Lines 7-9 run: da mi basia mille, deinde centum, / dein mille altera, dein secunda centum, / deinde usque altera mille, deinde centum. Thousands alternate with hundreds, joined by deinde or dein ('then'), so the total climbs to 3,300 before the poet stops counting. Merrill notes that the rapid succession already performs the 'confusion' of the count that the next lines promise. Ellis explains mille altera as 'a second set of a thousand', and usque as going straight on. In his translation Ellis keeps the pattern: each of the three lines ends with 'hundred', and 'Thousand' stands at the head of each, which makes the repetition visible on the page.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  10. 'Thousand give me again' l. 9

    In the Latin the command da mi ('give me') comes once, at the start of line 7, and governs the whole sequence. Ellis spreads the sense: the first two lines are mere noun phrases ('Thousand kisses, anon to these an hundred'), and only the third makes the demand ('Thousand give me again'). 'Anon' is an archaic word meaning 'soon, straight away' (OED, anon adv.), standing for deinde. The effect suits Ellis's grouping into three-line stanzas more than Catullus's single unbroken rush. Readers comparing the translations in the archive should note that this layout is the translator's, not the poet's.

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'anon'

  11. 'heedfully counted all the thousands' l. 10

    Catullus writes cum milia multa fecerimus, 'when we have made up many thousands'. Ellis's commentary glosses fecerimus as 'made up the number' and cites parallels for facere used of reaching a sum (Juvenal 14.326, Nepos). Merrill notes the unusual long -i- in fecerimus, an old quantity the poets sometimes kept. Ellis's English adds 'heedfully', which brings out the deliberate, book-keeping care of the counting before the lovers throw it all into disorder. Catullus turns the lovers' kisses into a ledger so that he can then wreck the accounts.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 19; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5

  12. 'We'll uncount them as idly' l. 11

    The Latin conturbabimus illa means 'we will confuse them'. Ellis's commentary explains it as short for conturbare rationes, 'to throw the accounts into confusion'. He notes that Cicero uses conturbare by itself for 'to become bankrupt' (Pro Plancio 68; Letters to Atticus 4.7). The lovers will deliberately spoil the count so that neither they nor anyone else can know the total. Commentators weigh the bankruptcy sense differently: Ellis puts the money idiom to the fore, while Merrill stresses only the hurried jumble of numbers. Ellis's 'uncount ... as idly' is a bold coinage. It catches the undoing of the sum but not the commercial idiom, and 'idly' is his own addition.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 20; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. conturbo

  13. The evil eye ll. 12-13

    Catullus gives a second reason for confusing the count: aut ne quis malus invidere possit, / cum tantum sciat esse basiorum, 'or so that no evil person can cast the evil eye, when he knows there are so many kisses'. Invidere (from in + videre, 'look upon') carries its old sense of bewitching by a glance. Merrill compares a line of Accius quoted by Cicero, and notes the widespread belief that a spell needs an exact count of what it attacks. Ellis cites a French proverb that the wolf eats counted sheep. Ellis's 'traitorous eye' keeps the literal eye of invidere, though 'traitorous' is a stronger word than malus ('wicked').

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 5, pp. 20; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. invideo

  14. 'All those myriad happy many kisses' l. 13

    The last Latin line is cum tantum sciat esse basiorum, 'when he knows there are so many kisses'. Merrill glosses tantum as 'just so many', the exact sum the envious would need. Catullus ends on the word basiorum, returning to the 'kisses' of line 7. Ellis's version spreads the Latin over a fourth, four-line group, and he fills the close with three stacked adjectives: 'myriad happy many'. 'Myriad' is from Greek myrias, ten thousand, so it suits the theme of numbers, but the cluster is the translator's, not the poet's. Students should treat it as an English flourish.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'myriad'
