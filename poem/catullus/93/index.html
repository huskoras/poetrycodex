Death & Dying

CI: Borne o'er many a land, o'er many a level of ocean

by Gaius Valerius Catullus c. 84 – c. 54 BC

Translated by Robinson Ellis (1871) · From The Poems and Fragments of Catullus (1871)

Death & Dying

Borne o'er many a land, o'er many a level of ocean,
Here to the grave I come, brother, of holy repose,
Sadly the last poor gifts, death's simple duty, to bring thee;
Unto the silent dust vainly to murmur a cry.

Since thy form deep-shrouded an evil destiny taketh
From me, O hapless ghost, brother, O heavily ta'en,
Yet this bounty the while, these gifts ancestral of usance
Homely, the sad slight store piety grants to the tomb;
Drench'd in a brother's tears, and weeping freshly, receive them;
Yea, take, brother, a long Ave, a timeless adieu.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Catullus 101: occasion l. 1

    Catullus's brother died in the Troad, the region of Troy, and was buried there far from home; the poet mourns him in poems 65 and 68. Ellis's commentary places this poem at Rhoeteum, on the coast of the Troad, and argues that Catullus would have visited the grave at the start of his journey to Bithynia with the governor Memmius (57-56 BC), not on his return. Merrill reads the exaggerated opening ('through many peoples and over many seas') as a measure of the grief caused by the distance. Many readers hear in the line an echo of the opening of the Odyssey, whose hero saw many cities and suffered much at sea.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  2. 'o'er many a level of ocean' l. 1

    Latin aequor (plural aequora) comes from aequus, 'level', and means a flat surface: a plain, or more often the sea. Ellis's 'many a level of ocean' gives the etymological sense rather than simply 'many seas'. Ellis's commentary observes that vectus ('carried') belongs most closely with the seas, since Catullus made most of the journey by ship. The phrasing is balanced: multas per gentes et multa per aequora, 'through many peoples and through many seas'. Ellis keeps the double 'many a' to match.

    Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. aequor; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  3. 'Here to the grave I come' l. 2

    Line 2 reads advenio has miseras, frater, ad inferias, 'I come, brother, to these sad funeral offerings'. Ellis glosses advenio as 'I am come', a solemn present of arrival. Inferiae were the rites and gifts offered to the dead; Merrill quotes Servius's definition, that they are 'paid to those below'. Merrill suggests Catullus may now be performing the ninth-day funeral meal that no member of the family had been there to give. Ellis's English names 'the grave' where the Latin names the rite. 'Here' stresses that the poet is at last present in person.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  4. 'of holy repose' l. 2

    The poem is in elegiac couplets, the metre of Greek and Latin epitaph and lament. Ellis imitates it in accentual English hexameters and pentameters. To fill the second half of his pentameter he adds 'of holy repose', which describes the grave as a place of sacred rest. Catullus's line has nothing of this: he calls the rites miseras, 'wretched' or 'pitiful'. The addition introduces a note of Christian consolation that the Latin lacks, since the poem offers no hope beyond the farewell. Readers should note such additions, which are typical of Victorian translation.

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480; C. J. Fordyce, Catullus: A Commentary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1961), note on the poem

  5. The repeated 'brother' ll. 2, 6, 9, 10

    The brother is never named. Instead the word frater recurs as an address at lines 2, 6 and 10, and as the adjective fraterno ('a brother's') at line 9. The repetitions sound like a call at the graveside. Ellis keeps all four, at the same points ('brother, of holy repose'; 'brother, O heavily ta'en'; 'a brother's tears'; 'take, brother'). In poem 68 Catullus repeats the cry frater adempte mihi ('brother taken from me'), and line 6 here repeats it word for word. The brother's death is the main subject of poems 65, 68 and 101.

    Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101

  6. 'the last poor gifts, death's simple duty' l. 3

    The Latin is ut te postremo donarem munere mortis, 'to present you with the last gift of death'. Ellis's commentary explains munere mortis as 'death-gift', the gift that belongs to death and is therefore the last. Munus means both a gift and a duty, and was used especially of offerings to the dead. The line and the next are heavy with m-sounds (munere mortis, mutam), which many readers hear as a murmur of mourning. Ellis divides the sense into 'last poor gifts' and 'death's simple duty', keeping both meanings of munus.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. munus; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  7. 'Unto the silent dust' l. 4

    Line 4 reads et mutam nequiquam alloquerer cinerem, 'and speak in vain to the mute ashes'. The brother was cremated, so what remains is cinis, ash. Catullus treats the word as feminine (mutam) here and in poem 68, an unusual gender. Ellis's commentary compares a Greek epitaph by Antipater on the 'deaf dust' left to the mourner. He also compares Aeneas greeting his father's ashes nequiquam ('in vain') at Aeneid 5.81. Ellis's 'silent dust' is close, though 'dust' loses the specific reference to cremation.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 480-1; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  8. 'vainly to murmur a cry' l. 4

    Alloquerer means 'that I might address', the formal word for speaking to someone face to face. It was also used of consoling the bereaved. The address is nequiquam, 'in vain', since the ashes cannot reply. The whole poem, then, is an address that knows it will not be heard. Merrill links alloquerer to the final farewell in line 10. Ellis's 'murmur a cry' is a mixed phrase that combines quiet speech and lament. It is less exact than the Latin, where the speaker formally addresses his brother.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. alloquor; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  9. 'an evil destiny taketh' l. 5

    Line 5 reads quandoquidem fortuna mihi tete abstulit ipsum, 'since fortune has taken you, yourself, from me'. Tete is an emphatic form of te, strengthened by ipsum: the brother himself is gone, while the rites remain possible. Ellis's commentary takes lines 5-6 as the protasis ('since ...') to which lines 7-10 are the main clause, comparing a passage in the Ciris. Ellis's 'Since thy form deep-shrouded an evil destiny taketh' adds the shroud. His 'evil destiny' makes fortuna ('chance, fortune') more sinister and more fated than the Latin.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  10. 'O hapless ghost, brother, O heavily ta'en' l. 6

    Line 6 reads heu miser indigne frater adempte mihi, 'alas, brother, wretchedly taken from me, undeservedly'. Ellis's commentary notes that frater adempte mihi repeats poem 68 (lines 20 and 92). He explains indigne as 'wrongfully', because the death came too early, and quotes inscriptions that use the word of youthful deaths. Miser may go with the brother ('poor brother') or with the speaker. Ellis's 'hapless ghost' takes it with the brother and adds the idea of a 'ghost'. 'Heavily ta'en' renders indigne as grief rather than injustice.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  11. 'Yet this bounty the while' l. 7

    Line 7 begins nunc tamen interea haec, 'now, nevertheless, meanwhile, these things'. Tamen marks the offerings as a poor compensation for the brother's loss. Ellis's commentary argues that interea implies a promise of some richer offering later. He suggests a cenotaph in Italy, and compares the Ciris, where a poet offers a slight gift 'meanwhile'. Merrill takes interea with the imperative to mean that the poet sets aside his grief for a while. Ellis's English renders interea as 'the while', an old phrase meaning 'meanwhile' (OED, while n.), and haec as 'this bounty'.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'while'

  12. 'these gifts ancestral of usance' ll. 7-8

    The Latin is prisco quae more parentum / tradita sunt tristi munere ad inferias, 'which by the ancient custom of our fathers are handed down as a sad gift for the rites of the dead'. Ellis's commentary explains tradita as 'handed down' as traditional usage. He lists the usual offerings to the dead: wine, milk, blood, honey and flowers. Merrill suggests the dishes of the ninth-day meal. Ellis's English uses 'usance', an old word for custom or habitual practice (OED, usance n.), and 'ancestral' for parentum. Both choices give the line an antique air, which fits Catullus's emphasis on inherited rite.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'usance'

  13. 'Drench'd in a brother's tears' l. 9

    Line 9 reads accipe fraterno multum manantia fletu, 'receive them, streaming much with a brother's weeping'. Manare means to flow or drip. Ellis's commentary argues that it implies solid offerings, perhaps garlands wet with tears, as in Tibullus and Ovid. Merrill thinks the phrase fits the food of a funeral meal better than libations. The adjective fraterno, 'a brother's', keeps the brother present in the grief itself. Ellis's 'Drench'd ... and weeping freshly' doubles the image.

    Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Catullus, Carmina, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  14. 'a long Ave, a timeless adieu' l. 10

    The last line reads atque in perpetuum, frater, ave atque vale, 'and for ever, brother, hail and farewell'. Merrill explains the words as the final farewell (conclamatio) that should have been spoken at the burial. Ellis's commentary cites funeral inscriptions from Spain with have vale and ave et vale, and Aeneas's farewell to Pallas at Aeneid 11.97. In perpetuum ('for ever') answers the eternal night of poem 5. Ellis splits the two greetings into 'a long Ave' and 'a timeless adieu', keeping the Latin word. Tennyson's 'Frater Ave atque Vale' (1883), written at Catullus's Sirmione, borrows the phrase as its title.

    E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 101; Robinson Ellis, A Commentary on Catullus, 2nd edn (Oxford, 1889), on poem 101, pp. 481-2; Alfred Tennyson, 'Frater Ave atque Vale', in Tiresias and Other Poems (1885), Wikisource
