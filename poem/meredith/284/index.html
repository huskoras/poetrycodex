Love

Modern Love: I

by George Meredith 1828–1909

From Modern Love (1862)

Love

BY this he knew she wept with waking eyes:
That, at his hand’s light quiver by her head,
The strange low sobs that shook their common bed
Were called into her with a sharp surprise,
And strangled mute, like little gaping snakes,
Dreadfully venomous to him. She lay
Stone-still, and the long darkness flowed away
With muffled pulses. Then, as midnight makes
Her giant heart of Memory and Tears
Drink the pale drug of silence, and so beat
Sleep’s heavy measure, they from head to feet
Were moveless, looking through their dead black years,
By vain regret scrawled over the blank wall.
Like sculptured effigies they might be seen
Upon their marriage-tomb, the sword between;
Each wishing for the sword that severs all.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Modern Love (1862) and its occasion l. 1

    Modern Love, published in 1862, is a sequence of fifty sixteen-line poems that follows a husband and wife through jealousy, infidelity, attempted reconciliation and the wife's death. It is widely read against Meredith's marriage to Mary Ellen Nicolls, a widow and daughter of the novelist Thomas Love Peacock; she left him in 1857/8 for the painter Henry Wallis and died in 1861. Critics warn against simple biography, since Meredith invented much (the husband's mistress, for instance). Its frank treatment of marital sex and unhappiness shocked reviewers, and the poem was not reprinted until the early 1890s. Sonnet I opens in the middle of the crisis, at night, in the marriage bed.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. H. Kerpneck), 'Modern Love: I', notes; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; Open Letters Monthly, 'A Shuddering Heap of Pain: George Meredith's Modern Love' (January 2014)

  2. By this he knew ll. 1-4

    The opening is abrupt and grammatically forward-pointing: 'this' is explained only in the next lines. As the husband's hand trembles near his wife's head, the sobs that have been shaking their bed are suddenly drawn back into her and stifled, and from that reaction he knows she has been awake, pretending to sleep. The poem thus begins with mutual concealment and a kind of detective inference, typical of a sequence where husband and wife read each other's signs suspiciously. The third-person 'he' will later alternate with a first-person 'I' and with a commenting narrator, which makes point of view an issue throughout the sequence.

    EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  3. wept with waking eyes l. 1

    'Waking eyes' contrasts with the sleep she pretends to and with the 'Sleep's heavy measure' that both partners later only simulate. Both lie wakeful 'looking through their dead black years'. The poem thus stages a marriage in which two conscious people share a bed in silence, each aware of the other's pain and unable to speak. The alliterating w-sounds ('wept with waking') muffle the line, appropriately.

    Open Letters Monthly, 'A Shuddering Heap of Pain: George Meredith's Modern Love' (January 2014); Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  4. common bed l. 3

    'Common' primarily means shared: this is the marital bed. But the word also carries connotations of the ordinary or vulgar, and in a sequence about adultery the bed's 'commonness' is pointed. Reviewers objected precisely that Meredith brought the marriage bed into poetry; Hutton in the Spectator thought he meddled with a painful subject to no good purpose. The opening sonnet makes the bed the poem's stage and anticipates its transformation into a 'marriage-tomb' at the close.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'common'; Ioan Williams, ed., Meredith: The Critical Heritage (Routledge, 1971), R. H. Hutton's Spectator review and Swinburne's reply

  5. strangled mute, like little gaping snakes ll. 5-6

    The simile is startling: sobs that are swallowed back ('strangled mute') become 'little gaping snakes', open-mouthed, silent, yet 'Dreadfully venomous to him'. Her suppressed grief poisons him because it reveals what she conceals. Snakes recur many times in Modern Love; one critic counts nearly a dozen, with sexual overtones and an echo of Eden after the Fall, when the serpent has entered the first marriage (Genesis 3). The diminutive 'little' makes the image intimate and grotesque at once.

    Open Letters Monthly, 'A Shuddering Heap of Pain: George Meredith's Modern Love' (January 2014); Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:1; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  6. Stone-still l. 7

    The line break 'She lay / Stone-still' suspends the verb before the compound adjective falls heavily at the start of the next line. 'Stone-still' prepares for the 'sculptured effigies' of line 14, so that the image of the couple as tomb carvings grows out of the wife's rigid pretence of sleep. Meredith's syntax throughout the sonnet runs across lines and quatrains, creating a pressure that the rhyme scheme contains.

    Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love; Open Letters Monthly, 'A Shuddering Heap of Pain: George Meredith's Modern Love' (January 2014)

  7. the long darkness flowed away / With muffled pulses ll. 7-8

    The night 'flows away' with 'muffled pulses', the heartbeat each partner can hear in the silence, and perhaps the muffled drums of a funeral procession, in keeping with the poem's funereal ending. Nothing is said aloud: the only sounds are bodily ones, the suppressed sobs and the pulse, so that the marriage is registered through the involuntary signs of the body rather than through speech. The phrase begins an extended bodily metaphor developed in the next lines, where midnight has a 'giant heart' that beats 'Sleep's heavy measure'.

    Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  8. midnight ... Her giant heart of Memory and Tears ll. 8-10

    A dense personification: Midnight, feminine, 'makes / Her giant heart of Memory and Tears / Drink the pale drug of silence'. The night's heart, swollen with the couple's memories and sorrow, is quieted by silence as by an opiate, and so beats with the slow rhythm of sleep. The capitalised abstractions, Memory and Tears, give the image an allegorical grandeur that contrasts with the domestic setting; Meredith's mixing of the abstract and the physical is a hallmark of his difficult style, which reviewers found obscure.

    Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love; Ioan Williams, ed., Meredith: The Critical Heritage (Routledge, 1971), R. H. Hutton's Spectator review and Swinburne's reply

  9. the pale drug of silence l. 10

    Opium and laudanum (opium dissolved in alcohol) were widely used in Victorian households to procure sleep. Silence works on the night like such a drug, numbing feeling into apparent rest. 'Pale' transfers to the drug the colour of the sleepless faces and of death, and the silence it brings is not peace but suppression, as the couple's 'moveless' bodies show.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'drug'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  10. Sleep's heavy measure l. 11

    The night's heart beats 'Sleep's heavy measure', the slow regular rhythm of sleep, which the couple imitate while lying awake. 'Measure' also means metre, and the poem's own iambic pentameter is quietly involved: the heavy stresses of 'Sleep's heavy measure, they from head to feet' slow the line. The phrase 'from head to feet' makes them rigid from end to end, preparing for the effigy image three lines later.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'measure'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  11. dead black years, / By vain regret scrawled over the blank wall ll. 12-13

    Lying motionless, the couple stare at the blank wall of the dark room and 'see' their 'dead black years' written there by 'vain regret' (useless, as well as self-regarding). The image of an accusing script on a wall may recall Belshazzar's feast in Daniel 5, where a hand writes the king's doom on the palace wall, though Meredith's 'scrawled' is untidier and more private. The years are 'dead' and 'black' both because they are past and because the marriage has died.

    Bible (KJV), Daniel 5:5; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  12. sculptured effigies ... Upon their marriage-tomb ll. 14-15

    Medieval and Renaissance church monuments often show a knight and his lady lying side by side, hands joined in prayer, sometimes with a sword. Meredith's couple resemble such effigies: still, parallel, and dead to each other while alive. Critics have contrasted Larkin's 'An Arundel Tomb' (written 1956, collected 1964), where the dead couple's stone hands suggest love survives; here the partners are alive but their love is dead. The 'marriage-tomb' is the marriage bed turned into a grave: the marriage itself has become the monument to its own death.

    Open Letters Monthly, 'A Shuddering Heap of Pain: George Meredith's Modern Love' (January 2014); Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  13. marriage-tomb l. 15

    The hyphenated compound collapses the poem's two spaces, bed and grave, into one, completing the movement from 'common bed' (l. 3) through 'Stone-still' (l. 7) and 'moveless' (l. 12). It also announces the sequence's subject: not adultery as romance, but the slow death of a marriage that neither partner can leave. Modern Love ends, in fact, with the wife's suicide by poison, so the opening tomb proves prophetic.

    EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  14. the sword between l. 15

    In the Tristan legend, King Mark discovers the lovers asleep in the forest with a drawn sword lying between their bodies and takes it as a sign of innocence (Béroul's twelfth-century Romance of Tristran; Gottfried's version has a similar scene). A sword between a sleeping man and woman was a traditional symbol of chastity, as in other medieval tales. Meredith inverts the motif: the adulterous lovers of legend had the sword to protect their secret, while this lawful married couple lie divided by an invisible blade of mistrust. Some commentators see the husband initially imagining the couple as a Tristan and Iseult in ruin. The sword may also suggest the knightly effigy's weapon.

    Béroul, The Romance of Tristran, trans. Alan S. Fedrick (Penguin Classics, 1970), the forest of Morrois episode; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  15. the sword that severs all l. 16

    The final line turns the sword from a symbol of division into a wish for death, 'the sword that severs all' bonds, including the marriage itself. The repetition 'the sword between; / Each wishing for the sword' produces a grim pun: the sword already between them is not enough; each wants the decisive one. Victorian divorce, made somewhat more accessible by the Matrimonial Causes Act of 1857, remained difficult and scandalous, so death could seem the only release. The ending is not a resolution but a deadlock, and the sequence begins from it.

    Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'

  16. The sixteen-line 'sonnet' ll. 1-16

    Each poem of Modern Love has sixteen lines of iambic pentameter in four quatrains with enclosed rhyme: abba cddc effe ghhg (here eyes/head/bed/surprise, snakes/lay/away/makes, and so on). The form is a variation on the sonnet, which Meredith extended by two lines; it lacks the Shakespearean couplet and the Petrarchan volta, so poems tend to end in a tense rather than a resolved cadence. Sentences constantly run across quatrain boundaries ('She lay / Stone-still'; 'as midnight makes / Her giant heart'), so syntax strains against the stanza. The extra length lets each poem be a scene as well as a reflection, suiting the sequence's novelistic narrative.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. H. Kerpneck), 'Modern Love: I', notes; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; Phyllis B. Bartlett, ed., The Poems of George Meredith, 2 vols (Yale UP, 1978), notes to Modern Love

  17. Reception: Hutton and Swinburne l. 6

    Contemporary reviewers were shocked by the sequence's subject and treated its pain as morbid; the Spectator's reviewer, R. H. Hutton, found it meddling with a painful theme, and the Saturday Review thought it a moral mistake. Swinburne replied in a letter to the Spectator dated 7 June 1862, defending the poem as a serious work of art on a real subject. Swinburne's defence was echoed by later admirers. Modern critics regard it as a forerunner of modern psychological fiction and poetry; its venomous imagery, as in this line, was part of what offended.

    Ioan Williams, ed., Meredith: The Critical Heritage (Routledge, 1971), R. H. Hutton's Spectator review and Swinburne's reply; EBSCO Research Starters, 'Modern Love by George Meredith'; University of Iowa, Victorian poetry study questions on Modern Love (quoting contemporary reviews)
