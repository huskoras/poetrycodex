The Mind

Echoes

by Charles Baudelaire 1821–1867

Translated by Cyril Scott (1909) · From Les Fleurs du Mal (1857)

The Mind

In Nature's temple, living columns rise,
Which oftentimes give tongue to words subdued,
And Man traverses this symbolic wood,
Which looks at him with half familiar eyes,

Like lingering echoes, which afar confound
Themselves in deep and sombre unity,
As vast as Night, and like transplendency,
The scents and colours to each other respond.

And scents there are, like infant's flesh as chaste,
As sweet as oboes, and as meadows fair,
And others, proud, corrupted, rich and vast,

Which have the expansion of infinity,
Like amber, musk and frankincense and myrrh,
That sing the soul's and senses' ecstasy.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its title and translator title, l. 1

    'Correspondances' is the fourth poem of Les Fleurs du mal (1857 and 1861 editions), in the opening section 'Spleen et Idéal', and has long been read as a statement of Baudelaire's poetics and a founding text of French Symbolism. The archive's translation is by Cyril Scott (1879-1970), the English composer, whose Baudelaire: The Flowers of Evil appeared in 1909 (London: Elkin Mathews). Scott replaces the doctrinal title 'Correspondences' with 'Echoes', taken from line 5, which shifts the stress from a metaphysical theory to a sensory, musical effect, perhaps natural for a composer.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; 'L'idéal et la théorie des correspondances dans Les Fleurs du mal', Schoolmouv (French course notes on Swedenborg and the poem)

  2. Nature as temple: the doctrine of correspondences l. 1

    The Swedish mystic Emanuel Swedenborg (1688-1772) taught that every natural thing corresponds to a spiritual reality, so that the world is a language in which heaven speaks. Baudelaire knew the doctrine through Balzac's Swedenborgian novels (Louis Lambert, Séraphîta) and wider Romantic illuminism, and the opening quatrain applies it: nature is a 'temple' whose living pillars utter confused words. Critics usually distinguish two kinds of correspondence in the sonnet: 'vertical', between the material and the spiritual worlds (quatrain 1), and 'horizontal', among the senses themselves (from line 8). Scott's 'In Nature's temple' gently converts Baudelaire's statement ('Nature is a temple') into a setting.

    Emanuel Swedenborg, Heaven and Hell (De Caelo et Inferno, 1758), the chapters on correspondence between heaven and the natural world; Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); 'L'idéal et la théorie des correspondances dans Les Fleurs du mal', Schoolmouv (French course notes on Swedenborg and the poem)

  3. living columns l. 1

    The image fuses a forest with a temple: tree trunks become pillars, the canopy a vault. The comparison of Gothic churches to forests was famous in French Romanticism through Chateaubriand's Génie du christianisme (1802), which argued that Gothic architecture imitated the forests of the Gauls. Baudelaire reverses the direction: nature itself is the sacred building. 'Vivants' (living) makes the pillars animate, preparing for the 'confused words' they let fall and the 'eyes' of line 4. Scott's 'rise' adds an upward movement not in the French ('où de vivants piliers / Laissent parfois sortir de confuses paroles').

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Chateaubriand, Génie du christianisme (1802), Part III, Book 1, ch. 8, 'Des églises gothiques'; Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  4. words subdued l. 2

    The pillars 'sometimes let out confused words'. The French adjective 'confuses' means indistinct, mingled, hard to make out, and it prepares for 'se confondent' (blend, merge) in line 5: nature's language is a blending, and its meaning is not plain. Scott's 'subdued' suggests low volume rather than mixed or obscure sense, and 'give tongue', an idiom also used of hounds baying, is more vigorous than Baudelaire's 'laissent sortir' (let out). The point for students is that Baudelaire's nature is a text that must be deciphered, which is why the poem was later read as a manifesto of Symbolism.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tongue, n.'

  5. symbolic wood l. 3

    Man passes 'à travers des forêts de symboles', through forests of symbols, which watch him 'avec des regards familiers'. The forest is the temple of line 1 seen from within, and the phrase implies that everything in it signifies something beyond itself. It became a key phrase for the generation of Mallarmé, Verlaine and Rimbaud, and Jean Moréas's 'Symbolist manifesto' (1886) named the movement in Baudelaire's wake. Scott's singular 'symbolic wood' is compact but loses the overwhelming plural of 'forêts'.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); 'L'idéal et la théorie des correspondances dans Les Fleurs du mal', Schoolmouv (French course notes on Swedenborg and the poem); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  6. half familiar eyes l. 4

    In Baudelaire the symbols observe man 'avec des regards familiers', with familiar glances, a reversal in which nature looks at man rather than the reverse. 'Familier' suggests a knowledge already shared, as if man had once belonged to this world and forgotten it, a Platonic idea of recognition. Scott's 'half familiar' introduces a note of uncanny half-recognition, perhaps reasonable as interpretation but not in the text. 'Regards' (gazes) is plural and active, whereas 'eyes' are organs; the French insists on the act of looking.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  7. Like lingering echoes l. 5

    The second quatrain is a single simile: as long echoes from afar blend 'in a dark and deep unity', so scents, colours and sounds answer each other. The echo is apt: it is a sound reflected and returned, so it already embodies 'response', and long echoes heard from far away lose their separate outlines and merge. Scott's 'lingering' renders 'longs' (long, drawn out) well, and his phrase 'afar confound / Themselves' keeps the reflexive verb 'se confondent', though 'confound' in English now usually means bewilder rather than blend. This line gave Scott his title.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'confound, v.'

  8. deep and sombre unity l. 6

    'Ténébreuse' (shadowy, obscure) links the unity to night, and the next line compares it to 'la nuit' and 'la clarté': it is as vast as both darkness and light, embracing opposites. The idea of an underlying unity behind diverse sensations is Romantic and mystical; it implies that sensory experience, rightly attended to, gives access to a hidden order. Scott reverses the adjectives ('deep and sombre' for 'ténébreuse et profonde'), which is harmless, but 'sombre' is gloomier than 'ténébreuse', which means not sad but dark and mysterious.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  9. transplendency l. 7

    Baudelaire's line is simple: 'Vaste comme la nuit et comme la clarté', vast as night and as light. Scott chooses the unusual 'transplendency', built from Latin trans- (beyond, through) and splendere (to shine), as in the rare adjective 'transplendent', 'shining through or beyond'. The word heightens what in French is a plain noun, a habit typical of Scott's ornate 1909 manner. It also blurs the balance of Baudelaire's line, where night and daylight stand side by side as equal, opposite immensities.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  10. The scents and colours to each other respond l. 8

    Line 8 states the principle of synaesthetic correspondence: perfumes, colours and sounds answer one another. Scott's version drops 'sounds', presumably to fit the line, which is a significant loss in a poem whose next tercet compares scents to oboes. Baudelaire had already put the idea in prose in his Salon de 1846, quoting a passage from E. T. A. Hoffmann's Kreisleriana about the analogy between colours, sounds and scents. In 'Richard Wagner et Tannhäuser à Paris' (1861) he quoted the two quatrains of this sonnet to describe the effect of Wagner's music. Critics debate whether 'Correspondances' describes literal synaesthesia or a poetic doctrine of analogy; the poem supports both readings.

    Baudelaire, 'Richard Wagner et Tannhäuser à Paris' (1861), where he quotes the two quatrains of 'Correspondances'; Baudelaire, Salon de 1846, ch. III 'De la couleur' (quoting Hoffmann's Kreisleriana on the analogy of colours, sounds and scents); Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  11. like infant's flesh as chaste l. 9

    The first tercet gives examples of correspondence across the senses: a scent is fresh like children's skin (touch), sweet like oboes (sound), green like meadows (sight). Baudelaire's adjective is 'frais' (fresh, cool); Scott's 'chaste' gives the scent a moral quality and rhymes with 'vast' in line 11, but it shifts the analogy from sensation to virtue. 'Chairs' (flesh) is bodily and unsentimental; the innocence of these scents is physical. The tercet opposes these innocent scents to the 'corrupted' ones of the next lines.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  12. As sweet as oboes l. 10

    This line performs the correspondence that Scott's line 8 partly omitted: a scent is sweet (soft) as the oboe's tone and green as the meadows. 'Hautbois' (literally 'high wood') is the oboe, an instrument associated in French with pastoral music; Baudelaire's chain therefore moves from the oboe's pastoral sound to the green fields. Scott's 'meadows fair' replaces Baudelaire's colour, 'verts' (green), with a general aesthetic adjective, so the visual sense also vanishes; what remains is mainly scent and sound.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'oboe'

  13. corrupted ... rich l. 11

    The sonnet turns from fresh, innocent scents to 'others, corrupt, rich and triumphant'. The dash in the French marks the shift. This second class is characteristic of Baudelaire, for whom corruption and splendour are often bound together, as the title Les Fleurs du mal suggests. Many readers think the sonnet's sympathy lies with these heavy, intoxicating scents, since they are given the final, expansive tercet. Scott adds 'proud' and moves 'vast' (for 'triomphants') to the rhyme. 'Corrupted' keeps the moral charge of 'corrompus', which can mean both decayed and morally depraved.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  14. the expansion of infinity l. 12

    The last tercet gives these rich scents a metaphysical scope: they have 'the expansion of infinite things'. 'Expansion' describes the physical spreading of a scent through a room and also an expansion of the soul, so the line moves from physics to spirit, a movement that answers the 'vertical' correspondence of the first quatrain. Scott translates closely, though 'infinity' turns Baudelaire's plural 'choses infinies' (infinite things) into an abstraction. Commentators link this to Baudelaire's essays on intoxication, Les Paradis artificiels (1860), where perfume and drugs expand perception.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  15. amber, musk and frankincense and myrrh l. 13

    Ambergris (ambre), musk, benzoin and incense are animal and resin scents used in perfume and in worship; together they join the erotic boudoir to the church, a typically Baudelairean mixture. Scott keeps 'amber' and 'musk' and renders 'encens' as 'frankincense', but replaces 'benjoin' (benzoin, a balsamic resin from Southeast Asian trees) with 'myrrh', perhaps for metre. 'Frankincense and myrrh' inevitably recalls the gifts of the Magi in Matthew 2:11, so Scott makes the line more biblical and sacred than Baudelaire's, which balances sensual and liturgical scents.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Bible (KJV), Matthew 2:11; Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'benzoin'

  16. the soul's and senses' ecstasy l. 14

    The sonnet ends by joining 'esprit' (mind, spirit) and 'sens' (senses) in one rapture, so that the 'vertical' correspondence of the opening (between the material and the spiritual) and the 'horizontal' correspondence among the senses are reunited. 'Chantent' (sing) brings sound back into a tercet about smell, completing the synaesthesia. 'Transports' means rapture, being carried out of oneself. Scott's 'ecstasy' (from Greek ekstasis, standing outside oneself) is an apt equivalent; the order 'soul's and senses'' keeps the French pairing.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ecstasy'

  17. Sonnet form ll. 9-14

    Baudelaire's sonnet uses twelve-syllable alexandrines. Its quatrains rhyme abba abba (piliers/paroles/symboles/familiers; confondent/unité/clarté/répondent), with the sestet rhyming cdc dee (enfants/prairies/triomphants; infinies/encens/sens). Scott keeps the octave-sestet division, with the sestet printed as two tercets, but writes ten- or eleven-syllable lines, rhymes the quatrains abba cddc with his own sounds ('confound/respond' is a loose rhyme), and arranges the tercets as efe gfg. The sonnet's traditional turn after line 8 here marks a move from statement of principle (octave) to examples (sestet).

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); Baudelaire, The Flowers of Evil, trans. Cyril Scott (London: Elkin Mathews, 1909), Project Gutenberg ebook 36098

  18. Influence l. 3

    Later French poets read the sonnet as a programme: the world as a forest of symbols to be deciphered, and the senses as interchangeable languages. Rimbaud's sonnet 'Voyelles' (c. 1871), assigning colours to vowels, pushes horizontal correspondence to provocation; Mallarmé's poetics of suggestion, and the Symbolist movement named in 1886, are also indebted to it. In English, Arthur Symons's The Symbolist Movement in Literature (1899) helped bring these ideas to Yeats and later modernists. Scott's 1909 translation belongs to that period of English enthusiasm for the French Symbolists.

    Baudelaire, Œuvres complètes, vol. 1, ed. Claude Pichois (Paris: Gallimard, Pléiade, 1975), notes to 'Correspondances' (Les Fleurs du mal, IV); 'L'idéal et la théorie des correspondances dans Les Fleurs du mal', Schoolmouv (French course notes on Swedenborg and the poem); Arthur Symons, The Symbolist Movement in Literature (London: Heinemann, 1899)
