Truth (Balade de Bon Conseyl)

by Geoffrey Chaucer c. 1343–1400

From Short Poems

LivingReligion

Flee fro the prees, and dwelle with sothfastnesse,
Suffyce unto thy good, though hit be smal;
For hord hath hate, and climbing tikelnesse,
Prees hath envye, and wele blent overal;
Savour no more than thee bihove shal;
Werk wel thy-self, that other folk canst rede;
And trouthe shal delivere, hit is no drede.

Tempest thee noght al croked to redresse,
In trust of hir that turneth as a bal:
Gret reste stant in litel besinesse;
And eek be war to sporne ageyn an al;
Stryve noght, as doth the crokke with the wal.
Daunte thy-self, that dauntest otheres dede;
And trouthe shal delivere, hit is no drede.

That thee is sent, receyve in buxumnesse,
The wrastling for this worlde axeth a fal.
Her nis non hoom, her nis but wildernesse:
Forth, pilgrim, forth! Forth, beste, out of thy stal!
Know thy contree, look up, thank God of al;
Hold the hye wey, and lat thy gost thee lede:
And trouthe shal delivere, hit is no drede.

Envoy.

Therfore, thou vache, leve thyn old wrecchednesse
Unto the worlde; leve now to be thral;
Crye him mercy, that of his hy goodnesse
Made thee of noght, and in especial
Draw unto him, and pray in general
For thee, and eek for other, hevenlich mede;
And trouthe shal delivere, hit is no drede.

Source Geoffrey Chaucer, The Complete Works of Geoffrey Chaucer, edited from numerous manuscripts, I, Second edition (Oxford: At the Clarendon Press, 1899). Digital text: Project Gutenberg 43089.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Chaucer’s best-known moral balade: shun the crowd, be content with what you have, and trust that ‘trouthe shal delivere’, the refrain of every stanza. The closing envoy, which bids the reader leave ‘old wrecchednesse’, survives in only one manuscript. Text from W. W. Skeat’s Oxford edition (1899).
