Nature

The Garden of Proserpine

by Algernon Charles Swinburne 1837–1909

From Poems & Ballads (First Series)

Nature

Here, where the world is quiet;
Here, where all trouble seems
Dead winds' and spent waves' riot
In doubtful dreams of dreams;
I watch the green field growing
For reaping folk and sowing,
For harvest-time and mowing,
A sleepy world of streams.

I am tired of tears and laughter,
And men that laugh and weep;
Of what may come hereafter
For men that sow to reap:
I am weary of days and hours,
Blown buds of barren flowers,
Desires and dreams and powers
And everything but sleep.

Here life has death for neighbour,
And far from eye or ear
Wan waves and wet winds labour,
Weak ships and spirits steer;
They drive adrift, and whither
They wot not who make thither;
But no such winds blow hither,
And no such things grow here.

No growth of moor or coppice,
No heather-flower or vine,
But bloomless buds of poppies,
Green grapes of Proserpine,
Pale beds of blowing rushes
Where no leaf blooms or blushes
Save this whereout she crushes
For dead men deadly wine.

Pale, without name or number,
In fruitless fields of corn,
They bow themselves and slumber
All night till light is born;
And like a soul belated,
In hell and heaven unmated,
By cloud and mist abated
Comes out of darkness morn.

Though one were strong as seven,
He too with death shall dwell,
Nor wake with wings in heaven,
Nor weep for pains in hell;
Though one were fair as roses,
His beauty clouds and closes;
And well though love reposes,
In the end it is not well.

Pale, beyond porch and portal,
Crowned with calm leaves, she stands
Who gathers all things mortal
With cold immortal hands;
Her languid lips are sweeter
Than love's who fears to greet her
To men that mix and meet her
From many times and lands.

She waits for each and other,
She waits for all men born;
Forgets the earth her mother,
The life of fruits and corn;
And spring and seed and swallow
Take wing for her and follow
Where summer song rings hollow
And flowers are put to scorn.

There go the loves that wither,
The old loves with wearier wings;
And all dead years draw thither,
And all disastrous things;
Dead dreams of days forsaken,
Blind buds that snows have shaken,
Wild leaves that winds have taken,
Red strays of ruined springs.

We are not sure of sorrow,
And joy was never sure;
To-day will die to-morrow;
Time stoops to no man's lure;
And love, grown faint and fretful,
With lips but half regretful
Sighs, and with eyes forgetful
Weeps that no loves endure.

From too much love of living,
From hope and fear set free,
We thank with brief thanksgiving
Whatever gods may be
That no life lives for ever;
That dead men rise up never;
That even the weariest river
Winds somewhere safe to sea.

Then star nor sun shall waken,
Nor any change of light:
Nor sound of waters shaken,
Nor any sound or sight:
Nor wintry leaves nor vernal,
Nor days nor things diurnal;
Only the sleep eternal
In an eternal night.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and place in Poems and Ballads (1866) l. 1

    The poem appeared in Swinburne's scandalous Poems and Ballads (1866), placed between 'Dolores', a litany to a goddess of cruel pleasure, and 'Hesperia', a poem of gentler love. When reviewers attacked the book (John Morley called Swinburne 'the libidinous laureate of a pack of satyrs'), Swinburne replied in Notes on Poems and Reviews that he had deliberately slipped this poem in between the two, to express a moment when the spirit, without hope or fear, 'hungers and thirsts only after the perfect sleep'. So the speaker's weariness is, in part, the exhaustion that follows passion. The opening 'Here' places us at once inside Proserpine's garden in the underworld, a world beyond 'trouble', 'riot' and dreams.

    A. C. Swinburne, Notes on Poems and Reviews (1866), Wikisource; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'; Wikipedia, 'The Garden of Proserpine' (on form, sources and reception)

  2. Stanza and metre st. 1

    Each stanza has eight short lines of three stresses, rhyming ababcccb. The a and c rhymes are feminine (two syllables: 'quiet/riot', 'growing/sowing/mowing'), the b rhymes masculine ('seems/dreams/streams'). The triple feminine rhyme creates a lulling, falling movement, and the delayed b rhyme at line 8 closes each stanza with a firm masculine stress, as if dropping into sleep. Heavy alliteration ('Dead winds' and spent waves' riot', 'Wan waves and wet winds labour') is characteristic of Swinburne, whose music critics have alternately praised and found monotonous. The poem's form enacts its theme: incantatory repetition, little forward motion, a steady descent toward the final 'eternal night'.

    Wikipedia, 'The Garden of Proserpine' (on form, sources and reception); Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'

  3. Proserpine l. 28

    Proserpina, the Latin form of Persephone, was the daughter of Ceres (Demeter), goddess of grain. Gathering flowers in Sicily, she was seized by Pluto (Dis, Hades) and taken to the underworld as his queen; because she ate pomegranate seeds there, she must return below for part of each year, which explains winter (Ovid, Metamorphoses 5; Homeric Hymn to Demeter). Milton recalls her as the flower-gatherer 'herself a fairer Flowre' (Paradise Lost 4.269-72). Swinburne omits the springtime return and the grieving mother: his Proserpine is wholly the queen of death, who 'gathers all things mortal' instead of flowers. The 'green grapes' are unripened, never-maturing fruit, appropriate to a realm where nothing grows to completion.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.385-571 (Perseus Digital Library); Homeric Hymn to Demeter (Perseus Digital Library); Representative Poetry Online (ed. P. F. Morgan), 'The Garden of Proserpine', notes; John Milton, Paradise Lost 4.268-272 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  4. doubtful dreams of dreams l. 4

    The first sentence is grammatically elusive: in this place all trouble 'seems' to be only the riot of dead winds and spent waves, recalled in 'doubtful dreams of dreams'. The doubling removes experience twice over from reality, the way storms seem when one is half asleep. 'Doubtful' carries both 'uncertain' and 'faint, indistinct'. The surrounding scene of a 'green field growing / For reaping folk and sowing' is seen from a distance, a 'sleepy world of streams', as though the speaker watches the living world from the borders of death.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'doubtful'; Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972)

  5. tears and laughter ... sow to reap ll. 9-12

    Stanza 2 lists what the speaker is tired of: 'tears and laughter', recalling Ecclesiastes 3:4 ('A time to weep, and a time to laugh'), and 'what may come hereafter / For men that sow to reap'. The last phrase echoes Galatians 6:7, 'whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap', a text about moral consequence and judgement after death. Swinburne turns from the Christian promise of reward or punishment to a desire for 'nothing but sleep'. 'Blown buds of barren flowers' (blown = blossomed, also blown away) gives the image of beauty without fruit that runs through the poem.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 3:4; Bible (KJV), Galatians 6:7; Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990)

  6. Weak ships and spirits steer l. 20

    The voyage image is ambiguous: ships and spirits are steered (or steer) through 'wan waves and wet winds', drifting to an unknown end. It suggests both mortal life as a storm-tossed sea and the passage of souls toward death, perhaps recalling the classical crossing of the rivers of the underworld. The point of the stanza is the contrast between 'thither' (where the drifting go, unknowingly) and 'hither' (the speaker's calm garden, where 'no such winds blow'). The very sound of the line, all w-alliteration and weak stresses, mimics aimless drift.

    Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'

  7. wot not l. 22

    'Wot' is an archaic present tense of 'wit' (Old English witan, to know), familiar to Swinburne's readers from the King James Bible (e.g. Abimelech's 'I wot not who hath done this thing', Genesis 21:26). The line means: those who sail there do not know where they are going. The archaism lends the poem a scriptural cadence that it then uses against Christian hope, a typical Swinburnean move.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. P. F. Morgan), 'The Garden of Proserpine', notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wot'; Bible (KJV), Genesis 21:26

  8. coppice l. 25

    A coppice is a thicket of small trees and shrubs, originally woodland periodically cut back to encourage new growth (Old French copeiz, from couper, to cut). Swinburne lists what does not grow in Proserpine's garden: moorland, woodland, heather (northern, British) and the vine (southern, classical). In their place come only poppies, unripe grapes and rushes. The stanza replaces a living landscape with a botanical inventory of sleep and death.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. P. F. Morgan), 'The Garden of Proserpine', notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coppice'

  9. bloomless buds of poppies l. 27

    The poppy was associated in antiquity both with Ceres (Demeter), Proserpine's mother, and with sleep and death: Ovid places poppies at the door of the cave of Sleep, where Night gathers their sleep-bringing juice (Metamorphoses 11.605-7). Victorian readers also knew the poppy as the source of opium and laudanum. Swinburne's buds are 'bloomless', never flowering, like the 'blown buds of barren flowers' of stanza 2: the garden's beauty is sterile. The stanza ends with Proserpine crushing fruit 'For dead men deadly wine', an inversion of Bacchus's life-giving vintage.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.592-615 (Perseus Digital Library); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'; Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990)

  10. For dead men deadly wine l. 32

    The only plant that 'blooms or blushes' in the garden is the one Proserpine crushes into wine for the dead. The polyptoton 'dead ... deadly' makes the drink both for the dead and death-bringing. Wine in classical myth is Bacchus's gift of life and ecstasy, and in Christianity the communion wine promises eternal life; Swinburne's underworld vintage offers only forgetfulness, perhaps recalling Lethe, the river of oblivion. Readers may compare the pomegranate seeds Proserpine ate in Hades, which bound her there, though the poem itself never mentions the pomegranate.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'; Homer, Odyssey 10.508-512 (Perseus Digital Library); Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990)

  11. fruitless fields of corn l. 34

    'Corn' is the British term for grain generally (wheat, barley), not maize. The dead, 'without name or number', bow like ripe ears and sleep in fields that bear nothing. Homer describes the grove of Persephone at the edge of the underworld, with tall poplars and 'willows that lose their fruit' (Odyssey 10.509-10), an ancient image of sterile vegetation in the realm of the dead. Ceres was the goddess of corn: her daughter's fields are its negation.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. P. F. Morgan), 'The Garden of Proserpine', notes; Homer, Odyssey 10.508-512 (Perseus Digital Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'corn'

  12. strong as seven ... fair as roses ll. 41-48

    The stanza runs through the gifts that might seem to defy death: strength ('strong as seven', a proverbial-sounding superlative), beauty ('fair as roses'), and love. Each is cancelled. 'His beauty clouds and closes' compresses fading and the closing of a flower or of eyes. The last two lines pivot on 'well': though love may rest content, in the end 'it is not well'. The phrase reverses the consoling proverb 'All's well that ends well' (the title of Shakespeare's play) and, implicitly, Christian assurances that all shall be well.

    Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'

  13. Nor wake with wings ... Nor weep for pains in hell ll. 43-44

    These lines state plainly what the poem implies elsewhere: there is no heavenly reward (the winged soul of popular piety) and no hellish torment. For Victorian readers this was among the most shocking aspects of the poem, which offers comfort precisely in the absence of an afterlife. Louis reads Swinburne's poetry as an evolving agnostic theology, here hostile to the Christian God and drawn to classical imagery of death as rest. Compare stanza 11, where the speaker thanks the gods 'That dead men rise up never'.

    Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990); Wikipedia, 'The Garden of Proserpine' (on form, sources and reception)

  14. She who gathers all things mortal / With cold immortal hands ll. 51-52

    Stanza 7 offers the poem's most famous portrait of Proserpine, standing 'beyond porch and portal' (the gates of the underworld) and 'Crowned with calm leaves'. In the myth she was seized while gathering flowers; Swinburne makes her the gatherer of all mortal things, a reaper rather than a picker of blossoms. The chiming 'mortal / immortal' sets human transience against divine permanence, and 'cold' gives her touch the chill of death. Her 'languid lips' are said to be sweeter than those of Love, who fears her: death is eroticised, in keeping with the poem's place after 'Dolores'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.385-571 (Perseus Digital Library); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'; A. C. Swinburne, Notes on Poems and Reviews (1866), Wikisource

  15. Forgets the earth her mother l. 59

    In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter the mother's grief for her lost daughter makes the earth barren until Persephone is restored for part of each year, bringing spring. Swinburne's Proserpine 'Forgets the earth her mother', and 'the life of fruits and corn'. Instead of spring following her up, 'spring and seed and swallow' (the swallow, herald of spring) follow her down into the dark. The poem thus cancels the myth's promise of seasonal renewal, which Christian interpreters had long read as a type of resurrection.

    Homeric Hymn to Demeter (Perseus Digital Library); Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.385-571 (Perseus Digital Library); Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990)

  16. Red strays of ruined springs l. 72

    Stanza 9 is a catalogue of all that drifts 'thither' to Proserpine: withered loves, dead years, 'disastrous things' (ill-starred, from Latin astrum, star), dead dreams, blind buds shaken by snow, wild leaves taken by wind. 'Strays' are things that have wandered or been scattered, here probably red leaves or petals torn from 'ruined springs'. The alliteration of r and the anaphora on 'And all' give the sense of an endless, melancholy procession, the dead of the natural year joining the human dead.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stray'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'disastrous'; Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972)

  17. Time stoops to no man's lure l. 76

    In falconry a hawk 'stoops' when it dives on its prey, and the 'lure' is the bundle of feathers and meat swung by the falconer to call the bird back. Time is a hawk that no human lure can recall or control. The image sharpens the commonplace that time waits for no man. The stanza around it denies certainty even to sorrow ('We are not sure of sorrow, / And joy was never sure') and ends with love itself weeping that 'no loves endure'.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. P. F. Morgan), 'The Garden of Proserpine', notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stoop'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lure'

  18. From too much love of living ... Whatever gods may be ll. 81-88

    Freed from 'too much love of living' and from hope and fear, the speakers offer 'brief thanksgiving' not to God but to 'Whatever gods may be', an agnostic formula, for the fact that no life lasts for ever and 'dead men rise up never'. The consolation is close to the Epicurean argument, best known from Lucretius, that death is nothing to us because we will not exist to feel it (De rerum natura 3.830 and following); Swinburne knew the classics well, though the poem does not cite Lucretius. W. E. Henley's 'Invictus' (1888) uses the same phrase in 'I thank whatever gods may be', with a defiant rather than a weary turn. The stanza was widely quoted and set to music.

    Lucretius, De rerum natura 3.830-869 (Perseus Digital Library); Margot K. Louis, Swinburne and His Gods: The Roots and Growth of an Agnostic Poetry (McGill-Queen's UP, 1990); Wikipedia, 'Invictus' (text of Henley's poem)

  19. the weariest river / Winds somewhere safe to sea ll. 87-88

    The image of life as a river ending in the sea is ancient, and the Preacher of Ecclesiastes uses the endless flow of rivers into the sea as an emblem of the weary cycle of things (Ecclesiastes 1:7). Swinburne makes the sea not a place of further labour but safety and rest; 'winds' (meanders) suggests the slow, tired course of a long life. The line is among the most quoted in Victorian poetry. Its relief is the reverse of the drifting ships of stanza 3, which do not know where they are going.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:7; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'

  20. diurnal l. 94

    'Diurnal' (Latin diurnalis, from dies, day) means daily, or belonging to the day. Paired with 'wintry leaves nor vernal' (vernal = of spring, Latin ver), it abolishes both the daily and the yearly cycles of time. English readers would recall Wordsworth's 'Rolled round in earth's diurnal course' ('A slumber did my spirit seal'), another poem of death as absorption into the impersonal. The Latinate words lend the final stanza a liturgical solemnity.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'diurnal'; William Wordsworth, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (1800), l. 7

  21. the sleep eternal / In an eternal night ll. 95-96

    The poem ends with the eternal sleep its speaker has longed for since stanza 2. The cadence recalls Catullus 5.4-6, where the poet urges Lesbia to love because suns may set and rise again, but for us, once our brief light has set, there is one perpetual night to be slept (nox est perpetua una dormienda). Catullus uses the thought to urge love; Swinburne, after 'too much love of living', finds rest in it. The repetition 'eternal ... eternal' and the last masculine rhyme 'night' (with 'light' and 'sight') close the poem with finality, the culmination of its slow descent.

    Catullus, Carmina 5 (Perseus Digital Library); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'The Garden of Proserpine'; Jerome J. McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (University of Chicago Press, 1972)
