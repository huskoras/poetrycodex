Mythology & Folklore

Hymn to Proserpine

by Algernon Charles Swinburne 1837–1909

From The Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912)

Mythology & Folklore

I have lived long enough, having seen one thing, that
love hath an end;
Goddess and maiden and queen, be near me now and
befriend.
Thou art more than the day or the morrow, the seasons
that laugh or that weep;
For these give joy and sorrow; but thou, Proserpina,
sleep.
Sweet is the treading of wine, and sweet the feet of the dove;
But a goodlier gift is thine than foam of the grapes or love.
Yea, is not even Apollo, with hair and harpstring of gold,
A bitter God to follow, a beautiful God to behold?
I am sick of singing: the bays burn deep and chafe:

I am fain
To rest a little from praise and grievous pleasure and pain.
For the Gods we know not of, who give us our daily
breath,
We know they are cruel as love or life, and lovely as death.
O Gods dethroned and deceased, cast forth, wiped out
in a day!
From your wrath is the world released, redeem'd from
your chains, men say.
New Gods are crown'd in the city; their flowers have
broken your rods;
They are merciful, clothed with pity, the young compassionate Gods.

But for me their new device is barren, the days are bare;
Things long past over suffice, and men forgotten that
were.
Time and the Gods are at strife; ye dwell in the midst
thereof,
Draining a little life from the barren breasts of love.
I say to you, cease, take rest; yea, I say to you all, be at peace,
Till the bitter milk of her breast and the barren bosom shall cease.
Wilt thou yet take all, Galilean? but these thou shalt
not take,
The laurel, the palms and the paean, the breasts of the nymphs in the brake;
Breasts more soft than a dove's, that tremble with
tenderer breath;
And all the wings of the Loves, and all the joy before
death;
All the feet of the hours that sound as a single lyre,
Dropp'd and deep in the flowers, with strings that flicker like fire.
More than these wilt thou give, things fairer than all
these things?
Nay, for a little we live, and life hath mutable wings.
A little while and we die; shall life not thrive as it may?
For no man under the sky lives twice, outliving his day.
And grief is a grievous thing, and a man hath enough of his tears:
Why should he labour, and bring fresh grief to blacken
his years?
Thou hast conquer'd, O pale Galilean; the world - has
grown grey from thy breath;
We have drunken of things Lethean, and fed on the
fullness of death.

Laurel is green for a season, and love is sweet for a day;
But love grows bitter with treason, and laurel outlives not May.
Sleep, shall we sleep after all? for the world is not sweet in the end;
For the old faiths loosen and fall, the new years ruin and rend.
Fate is a sea without shore, and the soul is a rock that abides;
But her ears are vex'd with the roar and her face with
the foam of the tides.
O lips that the live blood faints in, the leavings of racks and rods!

O ghastly glories of saints, dead limbs of gibbeted Gods!
Though all men abase them before you in spirit, and all knees bend,
I kneel not, neither adore you, but standing, look to the end.
All delicate days and pleasant, all spirits and sorrows are cast
Far out with the foam of the present that sweeps to the surf of the past:
Where beyond the extreme sea-wall, and between the
remote sea-gates,
Waste water washes, and tall ships founder, and deep
death waits:
Where, mighty with deepening sides, clad about with the seas as with wings,
And impell'd of invisible tides, and fulfill'd of unspeakable things,
White-eyed and poisonous-finn'd, shark-tooth'd and
serpentine-curl'd,
Rolls, under the whitening wind of the future, the wave of the world.

The depths stand naked in sunder behind it, the storms
flee away;
In the hollow before it the thunder is taken and snared as a prey;
In its sides is the north-wind bound; and its salt is of all men's tears;
With light of ruin, and sound of changes, and pulse of
years:
With travail of day after day, and with trouble of hour upon hour;
And bitter as blood is the spray; and the crests are as fangs that devour:
And its vapour and storm of its steam as the sighing of spirits to be;
And its noise as the noise in a dream; and its depth as the roots of the sea:
And the height of its heads as the height of the utmost stars of the air:
And the ends of the earth at the might thereof tremble, and time is made bare.
Will ye bridle the deep sea with reins, will ye chasten the high sea with rods?
Will ye take her to chain her with chains, who is older than all ye Gods?
All ye as a wind shall go by, as a fire shall ye pass and be past;
Ye are Gods, and behold, ye shall die, and the waves be upon you at last.
In the darkness of time, in the deeps of the years, in the changes of things,
Ye shall sleep as a slain man sleeps, and the world shall forget you for kings.
Though the feet of thine high priests tread where thy
lords and our forefathers trod,
Though these that were Gods are dead, and thou being
dead art a God,
Though before thee the throned Cytherean be fallen,
and hidden her head,
Yet thy kingdom shall pass, Galilean, thy dead shall go down to thee dead.
Of the maiden thy mother men sing as a goddess with
grace clad around;
Thou art throned where another was king; where another
was queen she is crown'd.
Yea, once we had sight of another: but now she is queen, say these.
Not as thine, not as thine was our mother, a blossom of flowering seas,
Clothed round with the world's desire as with raiment,
and fair as the foam,
And fleeter than kindled fire, and a goddess, and mother of Rome.
For thine came pale and a maiden, and sister to sorrow 5 but ours,
Her deep hair heavily laden with odour and colour of
flowers,
White rose of the rose-white water, a silver splendour, a flame,
Bent down unto us that besought her, and earth grew
sweet with her name.
For thine came weeping, a slave among slaves, and
rejected; but she
Came flush'd from the full-flush'd wave, and imperial,
her foot on the sea.
And the wonderful waters knew her, the winds and the
viewless ways,
And the roses grew rosier, and bluer the sea-blue stream of the bays.

Ye are fallen, our lords, by what token? we wist that
ye should not fall.
Ye were all so fair that are broken; and one more fair
than ye all.
But I turn to her still, having seen she shall surely abide in the end;
Goddess and maiden and queen, be near me now and
befriend.

O daughter of earth, of my mother, her crown and
blossom of birth,
I am also, I also, thy brother; I go as I came unto
earth.

In the night where thine eyes are as moons are in heaven, the night where thou art,
Where the silence is more than all tunes, where sleep
overflows from the heart,
Where the poppies are sweet as the rose in our world, and the red rose is white,
And the wind falls faint as it blows with the fume of the flowers of the night,
And the murmur of spirits that sleep in the shadow of
Gods from afar
Grows dim in thine ears and deep as the deep dim soul
of a star,
In the sweet low light of thy face, under heavens untrod by the sun,
Let my soul with their souls find place, and forget what is done and undone.
Thou art more than the Gods who number the days of
our temporal breath;
For these give labour and slumber; but thou, Proserpina, death.
Therefore now at thy feet I abide for a season in silence.

I know
I shall die as my fathers died, and sleep as they sleep even so.
For the glass of the years is brittle wherein we gaze for a span;
A little soul for a little bears up this corpse which is man.
So long I endure, no longer; and laugh not again, neither weep.
For there is no God found stronger than death; and
death is a sleep.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, subtitle and epigraph opening and title

    Swinburne gives the poem to a Roman pagan, a poet ('I am sick of singing'), speaking after Christianity has become the state religion of the empire in the fourth century. The epigraph quotes the words traditionally put into the mouth of the Emperor Julian ('the Apostate', d. 363) as he died fighting the Persians. Julian had tried to restore the old cults. Theodoret's Ecclesiastical History (3.20) is the source of the story, which is probably legend. The archive text, from the Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912), leaves out both subtitle and epigraph, and its line-breaks do not follow Swinburne's long lines.

    'Hymn to Proserpine', Wikipedia (publication, epigraph, Theodoret, influence); Theodoret, Ecclesiastical History 3.20 (Julian's dying words); 'Hymn to Proserpine (After the Proclamation in Rome of the Christian Faith)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

  2. 'I have lived long enough' first line

    Macbeth, facing defeat, says 'I have lived long enough: my way of life / Is fall'n into the sere, the yellow leaf'. Swinburne's speaker begins with the same exhausted perspective. The thing he has 'seen' is personal (love ends) as well as historical (his gods end). The poem fuses the two throughout: the end of a love and the end of a religion are both experiences of 'treason' and loss, and both send him to the goddess of sleep and death.

    Shakespeare, Macbeth 5.3.22-23; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  3. 'Goddess and maiden and queen' second line; repeated near the end

    Persephone was worshipped as Kore, 'the Maiden', daughter of Demeter, and as queen of the dead beside Hades. Snatched from Sicily and made to spend part of each year below, she joins spring and death. The speaker does not ask her for life but for help in dying. The line is repeated almost word for word later ('Goddess and maiden and queen, be near me now and befriend'), giving the poem a ritual refrain and showing that she, unlike the Olympians, 'shall surely abide in the end'.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Homeric Hymn to Demeter

  4. Metre and rhyme opening couplets

    Each of Swinburne's lines (often broken in two in this anthology text) has six strong beats in a swinging triple rhythm and a strong caesura. Lines rhyme in couplets (end / befriend, weep / sleep), and the first half of a line often rhymes internally with the first half of the next ('the morrow' / 'joy and sorrow'). Some critics hear an adaptation of the classical hexameter. The effect is incantatory: hymn-like repetition and alliteration ('Thou hast conquer'd, O pale Galilean'). McGann and others discuss how the music itself enacts the pagan 'sleep' the poem longs for.

    Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; 'Hymn to Proserpine (After the Proclamation in Rome of the Christian Faith)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

  5. 'but thou, Proserpina, sleep' ... 'death' opening; varied near the end

    At the start, other powers give joy and sorrow but Proserpina gives sleep. Near the end, the gods who 'number the days' give 'labour and slumber' but 'thou, Proserpina, death'. The variation moves from sleep to death, and the final line closes the circle: 'there is no God found stronger than death; and death is a sleep'. The thought is Epicurean and Lucretian: death is not punishment but rest (compare Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 3). It sets the poem against the Christian promise of resurrection.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 3.830-1094; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972)

  6. the treading of wine, the feet of the dove first verse paragraph

    Grapes were trodden underfoot in the vat, the vintage of Dionysus (Bacchus). Doves drew the chariot of Aphrodite and were her sacred birds. The two lines name the gods of wine and love, the pagan pleasures that the speaker, now weary, puts below sleep. The 'foam of the grapes' anticipates the foam of the sea from which Aphrodite was born later in the poem. Later still the speaker defends the same delights against the 'Galilean'.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Hesiod, Theogony 188-206 (birth of Aphrodite from sea-foam)

  7. Apollo, 'a bitter God to follow' first verse paragraph

    The speaker is a poet worn out by his own art. Apollo's myths are full of cruelty to those near him: Marsyas flayed, Daphne turned to laurel, Hyacinthus killed. The bays (laurel leaves, Daphne's tree) were the crown of poets and victors; here the crown 'burns deep and chafe[s]'. 'I am fain' means I wish. The rest he wants is from 'praise and grievous pleasure and pain'. The phrasing puts Apollo, beautiful to behold and bitter to follow, in the same ambivalent terms as love and death.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (Daphne) and 6.382-400 (Marsyas)

  8. the old gods dethroned; the new 'compassionate Gods' second verse paragraph

    The speaker reports the Christian view with irony: 'men say' the world is released from the old gods' chains. The new gods are 'crown'd in the city', meaning Rome. 'Their flowers have broken your rods' sets the new religion's flowers against the rods (fasces, or punishment) of the old order. The plural 'Gods' for the Christian powers, Christ, the saints and the Virgin, is the pagan's perspective, refusing Christian monotheism. Swinburne's own stance, hostile to Christianity and close to the speaker's, was part of the scandal of Poems and Ballads.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); Margot K. Louis, 'Gods and Mysteries: The Revival of Paganism and the Remaking of Mythography through the Nineteenth Century', Victorian Studies 47 (2005)

  9. 'Time and the Gods are at strife' third verse paragraph

    Gods, the speaker says, live 'in the midst' of a struggle with Time and drain 'a little life from the barren breasts of love'. Time's victory over all gods is the poem's real theology, worked out in the great sea-passage. The image of a nursing mother whose milk is bitter and whose breasts are barren reverses the Christian Madonna. The line had a long afterlife; Lord Dunsany named his book of invented mythology Time and the Gods after it.

    'Hymn to Proserpine', Wikipedia (publication, epigraph, Theodoret, influence); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  10. the Galilean third paragraph and passim

    Julian, in his writings against the Christians, called them 'Galileans' to make the new faith sound provincial. Swinburne's speaker uses the same word for Christ. 'Wilt thou yet take all?' asks whether the new god will take every pagan joy. The answer ('but these thou shalt not take') lists what he keeps. Later comes the most famous line: 'Thou hast conquer'd, O pale Galilean; the world has grown grey from thy breath'. Pale and grey suggest asceticism, death and joylessness. Hardy's Sue Bridehead quotes it in Jude the Obscure (1895).

    'Hymn to Proserpine', Wikipedia (publication, epigraph, Theodoret, influence); Theodoret, Ecclesiastical History 3.20 (Julian's dying words); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  11. laurel, palms, paean, nymphs third paragraph

    The laurel was Apollo's; palms were the prize of victory in Greek games (though palms were also Christian symbols of martyrdom, a double meaning here). The paean was a hymn of praise or thanksgiving to Apollo. The 'breasts of the nymphs in the brake' (thicket) and 'all the wings of the Loves' (Erotes, Cupids) are pagan eroticism. 'All the feet of the hours that sound as a single lyre' turns the Horai, goddesses of the seasons, into dancers whose steps make music. 'Dropp'd and deep in the flowers' keeps the pastoral setting.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'paean, n.'

  12. 'Thou hast conquer'd, O pale Galilean' end of third paragraph

    The line rewrites Julian's dying words as an indictment: 'the world has grown grey from thy breath'. The Christian world is one of 'things Lethean', drinks of forgetfulness, and 'the fullness of death'. Readers disagree about how far the speaker's view is Swinburne's own. Some treat the poem as Swinburne's attack on Christianity; others stress that it is a dramatic monologue in a historical voice and note that the speaker's real god is death. The archive text prints 'the world - has / grown grey'; the dash is an artefact of the anthology text, not Swinburne's punctuation.

    'Hymn to Proserpine', Wikipedia (publication, epigraph, Theodoret, influence); Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  13. things Lethean end of third paragraph

    In Virgil (Aeneid 6.713-15) souls drink Lethe's water to forget their former lives before rebirth. Swinburne applies it to cultural memory: to have 'drunken of things Lethean' is to forget the pagan past. The phrase also belongs to Proserpina's own realm, so it fits the speaker's longing for sleep. Note the ambiguity: the forgetfulness he condemns in the Christians is the forgetfulness he wishes for himself at the end ('forget what is done and undone').

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.713-15; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  14. 'O ghastly glories of saints, dead limbs of gibbeted Gods!' fourth paragraph

    A gibbet is a gallows. To call the crucified Christ a 'gibbeted God', and saints' relics 'dead limbs', is deliberately shocking. The 'lips that the live blood faints in' are drained, pale lips of martyrs and crucifixes. Victorian reviewers, notably John Morley in the Saturday Review (1866), attacked Poems and Ballads for blasphemy and sensuality. The pagan speaker refuses to kneel: 'I kneel not, neither adore you, but standing, look to the end.'

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gibbet, n.'

  15. the wave of the world fourth and fifth paragraphs

    The long periodic sentence ('Where beyond the extreme sea-wall ... Rolls ... the wave of the world') holds its subject back until the end, like a wave gathering height. The wave is a sea-monster, 'White-eyed and poisonous-finn'd, shark-tooth'd and serpentine-curl'd'. The next stanza lists its powers in biblical anaphora ('And ... and ... and'), echoing Psalm and Job language for the sea and Leviathan (Job 41). Its 'salt is of all men's tears'. The point is that Time, figured as the sea, is older than all gods and will drown Christ as it drowned Apollo.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 41:1; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972)

  16. 'Will ye bridle the deep sea ... chasten the high sea with rods?' fifth paragraph

    When a storm broke his bridge, the Persian king Xerxes ordered the Hellespont to be given three hundred lashes and a pair of fetters thrown into it (Herodotus 7.35). The scene became a classic example of hubris. Swinburne's rhetorical questions also recall God's challenge in Job: 'who shut up the sea with doors ... and said, Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further' (Job 38:8-11). The irony is pointed: the biblical God's own language is used to say that the sea, Time, is 'older than all ye Gods'.

    Herodotus, Histories 7.35; The Bible (KJV), Job 38:8-11; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  17. 'Ye are Gods, and behold, ye shall die' fifth paragraph

    In Psalm 82 God judges the unjust 'gods' (rulers or divine beings): 'I have said, Ye are gods ... But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.' Christ quotes the verse in John 10:34. The pagan speaker borrows the biblical sentence and passes it on every deity: 'as a wind shall go by, as a fire shall ye pass'. The following lines prophesy that Christ too will be forgotten 'for kings', and that 'thy kingdom shall pass, Galilean, thy dead shall go down to thee dead'. The phrase 'thou being dead art a God' points to the paradox of a crucified deity.

    The Bible (KJV), Psalm 82:6-7; The Bible (KJV), John 10:34; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  18. the Cytherean, 'mother of Rome' fifth paragraph

    Hesiod tells how Aphrodite was born from the foam and stepped ashore first at Cythera, then Cyprus (Theogony 188-200). Hence 'Cytherean' and the poem's sea-imagery: 'a blossom of flowering seas', 'flush'd from the full-flush'd wave ... her foot on the sea'. As mother of Aeneas, she was ancestress of the Romans; Lucretius opens his poem with 'Aeneadum genetrix', mother of Aeneas's people. Swinburne sets this radiant, imperial goddess against the Virgin Mary, the new 'queen' who is 'pale and a maiden, and sister to sorrow'.

    Hesiod, Theogony 188-200; Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 1.1; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  19. Mary against Venus fifth paragraph

    'Thou art throned where another was king; where another was queen she is crown'd': the Christian pair, Christ and Mary, occupy the thrones of Jupiter and Venus (or Juno). Mary 'came weeping, a slave among slaves, and rejected', recalling her own words, 'the handmaid of the Lord' (Luke 1:38; compare 1:48), and the Mater Dolorosa of devotion. Venus came 'imperial'. The archive text prints 'sister to sorrow 5 but ours'; the '5' is a stray character in the anthology text, not Swinburne's punctuation (probably a semicolon). The passage is a pagan reversal of the medieval hymns that set Mary above Eve.

    The Bible (KJV), Luke 1:38; Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Margot K. Louis, 'Gods and Mysteries: The Revival of Paganism and the Remaking of Mythography through the Nineteenth Century', Victorian Studies 47 (2005)

  20. 'I am also, I also, thy brother' closing section

    Demeter, mother of Persephone, was identified with the earth and its harvests. By calling Earth 'my mother' the speaker becomes Proserpina's brother. That kinship replaces the Christian relation to God the Father. 'I go as I came unto earth' echoes the burial formula 'earth to earth' and Job 1:21, 'Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither'. The speaker accepts death as natural return, not judgment.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 1:21; Book of Common Prayer (1662), The Burial of the Dead ('earth to earth'); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'

  21. Proserpina's night: poppies and white roses closing section

    The poppy, source of opium, was sacred to Demeter and the gods of sleep (Hypnos) and death; in ancient art Demeter holds poppies. In this night 'the red rose is white', passion drained of colour, as in Swinburne's companion poem 'The Garden of Proserpine'. Silence is 'more than all tunes', a final release for the weary singer of the opening lines. The prayer 'forget what is done and undone' asks for Lethe. Compare the late, hushed close of the companion poem.

    Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); A. C. Swinburne, 'The Garden of Proserpine', Poems and Ballads (1866)

  22. 'A little soul ... bears up this corpse which is man' penultimate lines

    Marcus Aurelius records the saying 'as Epictetus used to say' (Meditations 4.41): man is a little soul carrying a corpse. Swinburne added a note acknowledging this. The Stoic phrase makes the body a burden briefly held up by a weak soul, 'for a little' while. It fits the line before, where the 'glass of the years is brittle' (a mirror, or the hour-glass): life is short and fragile. The pagan's last thoughts thus turn to Stoic resignation, not Christian hope.

    Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.41 (quoting Epictetus: 'a little soul carrying a corpse'); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'; 'Hymn to Proserpine (After the Proclamation in Rome of the Christian Faith)', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

  23. 'death is a sleep' last line

    'So long I endure, no longer': the speaker will live only as long as he must, neither laughing nor weeping, the joy and sorrow of the opening both laid aside. 'There is no God found stronger than death' turns Christian language ('O death, where is thy sting?', 1 Corinthians 15:55) on its head: the victor is death, not Christ. Yet the death he accepts is gentle, a sleep. Some critics read the ending as nihilistic; others hear it, with McGann, as a calm acceptance of natural process, the poem's real alternative to both the old and the new religion.

    The Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:55; Jerome McGann, Swinburne: An Experiment in Criticism (U of Chicago P, 1972); Kenneth Haynes, ed., Swinburne: Poems and Ballads & Atalanta in Calydon (Penguin Classics, 2000), notes to 'Hymn to Proserpine'
