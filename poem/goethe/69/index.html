The Wanderer's Night-Song (The Same)

by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe 1749–1832

Translated by Edgar Alfred Bowring (1853) · From Songs

Nature

HUSH'D on the hill
Is the breeze;
Scarce by the zephyr
The trees

Softly are press'd;
The woodbird's asleep on the bough.
Wait, then, and thou
Soon wilt find rest.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion title, l. 1

    Goethe wrote these eight lines on the wooden wall of a hunter's lookout hut on the Kickelhahn, a hill above Ilmenau in Thuringia; the inscription bore the date 7 September 1780, and the poem is usually placed on the evening of the 6th. It was printed without authority in 1800 and with Goethe's consent in his Werke of 1815, under the title 'Ein Gleiches', 'the same again', because it follows the earlier 'Wandrers Nachtlied' ('Der du von dem Himmel bist', 1776). Bowring's '(The Same)' translates that title literally, and the archive's previous poem (goethe/68) is the 1776 lyric. On 27 August 1831, some seven months before his death, Goethe climbed back to the hut and reread the lines, as he reported to Zelter on 4 September 1831. Bowring's version is from The Poems of Goethe (1853), which claims to render poems 'in the original metres'.

Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  2. on the hill l. 1

    Goethe's opening sweeps outward: 'Über allen Gipfeln / Ist Ruh' ('Over all the summits / is rest'). The word 'allen' (all) gives the stillness a cosmic range, and the early transcripts by Herder and Luise von Göchhausen even read 'Gefilden' (fields, realms) for 'Gipfeln', a still wider word. Bowring's 'on the hill' makes the scene local and singular, and 'Hush'd' turns Goethe's noun of state (Ruh, rest) into a participle, as if something had been silenced. The loss matters because the German poem moves downward through the order of nature: summits, then treetops, then birds, then the human addressee. Bowring keeps that order but blurs its first, widest step.

    Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource; 'Wandrers Nachtlied', Wikipedia (German), on the Kickelhahn inscription, the 1831 visit and the printings; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  3. Is the breeze l. 2

    Goethe's second line is the two-syllable 'Ist Ruh', a stark statement that rest is there. Bowring fills the line with a subject, 'the breeze', borrowing from Goethe's fifth line, 'Kaum einen Hauch' ('scarcely a breath'). This anticipates and so weakens Goethe's gradual movement from the mineral stillness of the peaks to the barely felt breath in the treetops. It also changes the syntax: Goethe's lines 3-5 address the reader directly ('In allen Wipfeln / Spürest du / Kaum einen Hauch', 'in all the treetops you feel scarcely a breath'), whereas Bowring removes this first 'thou', so the only address left is the final 'Wait, then, and thou'. Readers comparing the two should notice how much of Goethe's effect rests on the early, quiet 'du'.

    Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  4. zephyr l. 3

    Zephyros was the Greek god of the west wind, a son of Eos (Dawn) in Hesiod's Theogony, and in English poetry since the Renaissance 'zephyr' had become a stock word for a soft, mild breeze (OED). Goethe's line has nothing like it: 'In allen Wipfeln / Spürest du / Kaum einen Hauch' uses everyday words, and that plainness is the source of the poem's celebrated calm. Bowring's mythological word is a typical early-Victorian heightening of diction, and it makes the weather something personified and literary rather than simply felt. Set beside the bare German, the choice shows how hard the poem is to translate: its power lies almost wholly in sound and position, not in imagery.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'zephyr'; Hesiod, Theogony 378-80 (Zephyros among the winds born of Eos and Astraios); Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource

  5. The trees ... Softly are press'd ll. 4-5

    The German poem's first four lines rest on a rich rhyme, Gipfeln/Wipfeln (summits/treetops), which sounds the step from rock to trees, and on the short pair Ruh/du (rest/you), which quietly links the stillness of nature to the person addressed. Bowring keeps the rhyme scheme but changes the words: his rhymes are 'breeze'/'trees', 'press'd'/'rest' and 'bough'/'thou', while 'hill' and 'zephyr' are left unrhymed, so the pairing of the opening lines (Gipfeln/Wipfeln) is lost. The archive's text puts a blank line after 'The trees', so the clause 'Scarce by the zephyr / The trees // Softly are press'd' runs across a stanza gap; whether this follows Bowring's printed layout or comes from the transcription, it gives a pause where Goethe has none. The indented short lines imitate the German's alternation of longer and very short lines (Ist Ruh; Spürest du).

    Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  6. The woodbird's asleep l. 6

    This is the longest line in both versions, and in Goethe it marks the arrival of animal life after peaks and trees. 'Vögelein' is a diminutive (little birds), giving a note of tenderness; the early transcripts read the plainer 'Vögel'. Goethe's verb is 'schweigen', 'are silent', which keeps the poem's theme of hush; Bowring's 'asleep' moves the poem towards night-rest and so prepares a reading of the last line as sleep rather than death. 'Woodbird' is a poetic compound for a bird of the woods, used by Victorian poets. The singular 'woodbird' and the added 'bough' make the line more pictorial than the German.

    'Wandrers Nachtlied', Wikipedia (German), on the Kickelhahn inscription, the 1831 visit and the printings; Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  7. Wait, then, and thou l. 7

    The final couplet is the turn of the poem. After the stillness of peaks, trees and birds, Goethe addresses 'du' directly: 'Warte nur, balde / Ruhest du auch' ('only wait, soon you too will rest'). 'Balde' is an older, poetic form of 'bald' (soon), chosen for the rhyme with 'Walde' and for its extra, lulling syllable. Bowring's 'then' makes the comfort sound like a logical conclusion from what precedes, where Goethe's 'nur' (only, just) is gentler and more intimate. Notice too that 'thou' rhymes with 'bough', a rhyme Bowring builds himself, while Goethe's last word, 'auch' (also), rhymes with 'Hauch' (breath): the human being will share the near-breathlessness of nature.

    Goethe, 'Ein Gleiches', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  8. Soon wilt find rest l. 8

    Goethe's 'Ruhest du auch' can mean the evening rest of a tired walker, a calm of the restless mind, or the final rest of death, and the poem does not choose. The context of the paired title favours spiritual peace: the 1776 'Wandrers Nachtlied' (goethe/68) is a prayer for 'sweet peace' (süßer Friede). The death reading gained force from the story of Goethe's return to the hut in August 1831, near the end of his life; a report by the mining official who accompanied him says Goethe read the lines and wept. Bowring's 'find rest' is slightly more active than the German present tense 'ruhest' (you rest), which presents the rest as a coming state rather than something sought.

Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem); Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  9. Afterlife: Lermontov and the song settings whole poem

    Lermontov's 'Gornye vershiny' ('Mountain peaks', 1840, headed 'Iz Gete', 'From Goethe') is itself a classic of Russian verse; it keeps Goethe's movement from summits to the waiting 'you' while changing the metre, and Russian schoolchildren learn it by heart. Schubert's setting 'Wandrers Nachtlied' (D 768, c. 1823) is one of his most compressed songs, and settings by many other composers followed. The poem has also been endlessly parodied in German. For students, comparing Bowring's English with Lermontov's Russian is a useful exercise: each translator keeps the descent through nature and the final address, but each has to give up some of the German's sound-patterning.
