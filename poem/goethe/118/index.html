The Erl-King

by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe 1749–1832

Translated by Edgar Alfred Bowring (1853) · From Ballads

Mythology & Folklore

WHO rides there so late through the night dark and drear?
The father it is, with his infant so dear;
He holdeth the boy tightly clasp'd in his arm,
He holdeth him safely, he keepeth him warm.

"My son, wherefore seek'st thou thy face thus to hide?"
"Look, father, the Erl-King is close by our side!
Dost see not the Erl-King, with crown and with train?"
"My son, 'tis the mist rising over the plain."

"Oh, come, thou dear infant! oh come thou with me!
Full many a game I will play there with thee;
On my strand, lovely flowers their blossoms unfold,
My mother shall grace thee with garments of gold."

"My father, my father, and dost thou not hear
The words that the Erl-King now breathes in mine ear?"
"Be calm, dearest child, 'tis thy fancy deceives;
'Tis the sad wind that sighs through the withering leaves."

"Wilt go, then, dear infant, wilt go with me there?
My daughters shall tend thee with sisterly care
My daughters by night their glad festival keep,
They'll dance thee, and rock thee, and sing thee to sleep."

"My father, my father, and dost thou not see,
How the Erl-King his daughters has brought here for me?"
"My darling, my darling, I see it aright,
'Tis the aged grey willows deceiving thy sight."

"I love thee, I'm charm'd by thy beauty, dear boy!
And if thou'rt unwilling, then force I'll employ."
"My father, my father, he seizes me fast,
Full sorely the Erl-King has hurt me at last."

The father now gallops, with terror half wild,
He grasps in his arms the poor shuddering child;
He reaches his courtyard with toil and with dread,--
The child in his arms finds he motionless, dead.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, setting and translator l. 1

    Goethe wrote 'Erlkönig' in 1782 for the opening of his Singspiel (a play with songs) Die Fischerin ('The Fisher-Girl'), where the heroine Dortchen sings it while at work, as a folk-ballad heard in passing. Detached from the play, it became one of the most famous German ballads, and it belongs to a run of Goethe ballads of the 1770s and 1780s ('Der König in Thule', 'Der Fischer', 'Der Sänger'). Goethe's first line asks 'Wer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind?' ('Who rides so late through night and wind?'); Bowring's 'through the night dark and drear' drops the wind, which matters because the father later explains the voice as wind in the leaves. Bowring's collection (1853) professes to keep Goethe's metres.

Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  2. Erl-King l. 6

    The figure comes from Danish ballad tradition, which Herder translated as 'Erlkönigs Tochter' ('The Erl-King's Daughter') in his Volkslieder (1778-79). The Danish 'ellerkonge' is a form of 'elverkonge', king of the elves; Herder rendered 'eller' as German 'Erle', alder. This has usually been called a mistranslation, though some scholars think Herder knew the double sense of Danish 'elle' and chose 'Erle' deliberately, to join elves with the damp alder groves of riverbanks. Either way, the name gave Goethe a creature of marsh, mist and trees, which suits the natural explanations the father offers. English 'Erl-King' (used by Walter Scott, 1797, and M. G. Lewis, Tales of Wonder, 1801) simply anglicizes the German and keeps its ambiguity.

    'Erlkönig (Ballade)', Wikipedia (German), on sources, Herder and the main interpretations; J. G. Herder, Volkslieder (1778-79), 'Erlkönigs Tochter' (from the Danish); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'erl-king'

  3. Metre and voices ll. 1-4

    Goethe's ballad is in quatrains of two rhymed couplets, each line having four stresses with one or two unstressed syllables between them, so the rhythm canters like the horse. Bowring regularizes this into anapaestic tetrameter ('he HOLD-eth the BOY tight-ly CLASP'D in his ARM'), a familiar English ballad measure that gallops more evenly than the German. The poem's design is dramatic: a narrator speaks the first and last stanzas, framing six stanzas of dialogue in which the father, the son and the Erl-King speak in turn. The Erl-King speaks three times, each time more insistently; the child's cries rise ('My father, my father'). Schubert's setting famously gives each voice its own vocal register.

  4. with crown and with train l. 7

    The child sees the Erl-King 'Den Erlenkönig mit Kron und Schweif' (the alder-king with crown and Schweif). 'Schweif' ordinarily means a tail (of an animal or a comet) and, by extension, a trailing robe. Bowring's 'train' takes the courtly sense; the other sense hints at something non-human. The father's reply, 'Mein Sohn, es ist ein Nebelstreif' ('a streak of mist'), matches the shape exactly: a long trailing streak of fog could look like a robe or a tail. The rhyme Schweif/Nebelstreif binds vision and explanation, so the poem lets both stand. Bowring's 'the mist rising over the plain' keeps the sense of mist but loses the trailing shape that makes the father's explanation plausible.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  5. the mist rising l. 8

    Each time the child reports the Erl-King, the father answers with a natural cause: a streak of mist, the wind rustling dry leaves, the old willows shining grey. The sequence makes the poem a contest between the father's rational, adult perception and the child's vision. Because the child dies, the ending seems to vindicate the child, yet Goethe never states that the Erl-King exists outside the child's mind; the narrator, who might settle the question, reports only the father's ride and the dead child. This careful balance is the heart of the ballad and the reason it supports both rationalist and supernatural readings.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; 'Erlkönig (Ballade)', Wikipedia (German), on sources, Herder and the main interpretations; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  6. The Erl-King's seduction ll. 9-12

    Goethe's Erl-King begins coaxingly: 'Du liebes Kind, komm, geh mit mir! / Gar schöne Spiele spiel ich mit dir' ('you dear child, come, go with me; very lovely games I'll play with you'). The promises appeal to a child's wishes: games, bright flowers on the shore, golden garments from his mother. Each later speech raises the pressure, from invitation to the offer of his daughters to the threat of force, so the three speeches form a climax. 'Full many a' is an archaic intensive in English, a ballad mannerism Bowring adds to give a folk tone. The inviting tone of the supernatural lover is traditional in ballads of elf-abduction, such as Herder's source.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; J. G. Herder, Volkslieder (1778-79), 'Erlkönigs Tochter' (from the Danish)

  7. strand l. 11

    'Strand' in English is now poetic or archaic for the edge of the sea, a lake or a river (OED); Bowring keeps Goethe's word exactly ('Manch bunte Blumen sind an dem Strand', 'many bright flowers are on the shore'). The detail fits the alder (Erle), a tree of damp ground and riverbanks, and recalls the watery spirits of Goethe's other ballads, such as 'Der Fischer' (1778), where a water-woman draws a fisherman down into the river. The Erl-King's realm is thus a place of beauty and damp, of flowers and drowning, rather than a heaven.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strand'; Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  8. the sad wind ... withering leaves l. 16

    The father's second explanation in German is brief and practical: 'Sei ruhig, bleibe ruhig, mein Kind; / In dürren Blättern säuselt der Wind' ('Be calm, stay calm, my child; the wind is rustling in dry leaves'). Bowring adds two things not in Goethe: 'tis thy fancy deceives', which states the rationalist diagnosis outright, and 'sad', which gives the wind a feeling. Both changes weaken the poem's reticence; in Goethe the father never tells the child he is imagining things, and the reader is left to infer it. 'Withering' keeps the autumnal sense of 'dürr' (dry, withered), a quiet sign of death in the landscape.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  9. My daughters ll. 18-20

    In 'Erlkönigs Tochter' (Herder, Volkslieder), Herr Oluf rides out on his wedding eve, meets the Erl-King's daughter dancing with her sisters, refuses to dance with her, and is struck and dies. Goethe keeps the dancing daughters ('Meine Töchter führen den nächtlichen Reihn, / Und wiegen und tanzen und singen dich ein': they lead the nightly round-dance, and will rock and dance and sing you to sleep) but makes the male king the seducer and a child the victim. Bowring's 'glad festival' for 'nächtlichen Reihn' (night-time round dance) misses the specific fairy ring-dance, but 'rock thee, and sing thee to sleep' keeps the lullaby, which here sounds like the sleep of death.

  10. the aged grey willows l. 24

    The father's third answer is the most exact: what the boy sees are old willows gleaming grey. Pollarded willows by streams, with their stumpy trunks and shocks of thin branches, do look like crouching figures at dusk, so the explanation is natural. Yet the word 'scheinen' (to shine, but also to seem) quietly concedes that appearances are in question. Bowring's 'deceiving thy sight' again spells out the rationalist moral. With 'My darling, my darling' Bowring imitates Goethe's doubled address, 'Mein Sohn, mein Sohn', which answers the child's doubled 'Mein Vater, mein Vater'.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; Goethe, Werke, Hamburger Ausgabe, vol. 1: Gedichte und Epen I, ed. Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem)

  11. force I'll employ ll. 25-26

    The Erl-King's final speech joins love and violence: 'Ich liebe dich, mich reizt deine schöne Gestalt; / Und bist du nicht willig, so brauch ich Gewalt' ('I love you, your beautiful form excites me; and if you're not willing, I'll use force'). The verb 'reizen' (to attract, provoke, stir) has an erotic edge, and the line has become a byword in German. Interpretations diverge. Many readers take the Erl-King as death itself, beautiful and then brutal; others read the whole as the hallucination of a feverish child. Some interpreters read it as a depiction of rape or sexual abuse (the sociologist Rüdiger Lautmann calls the Erl-King a rapist); others read it as the child's frightening awakening to sexuality. The poem's restraint allows each reading, and none has general agreement.

  12. has hurt me l. 28

    'Ein Leids tun' is an older German idiom for doing someone harm, with 'Leid' meaning both injury and sorrow; Goethe uses its archaic genitive form to give the child's cry a ballad tone. The verb 'faßt mich an' in the line before ('jetzt faßt er mich an', 'now he seizes me') makes the touch physical. This is the moment of contact between the two worlds, and the child's last words. Bowring's 'at last' is a filler for the rhyme with 'fast', and slightly suggests a long ordeal; Goethe's line is more sudden.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  13. The father now gallops ll. 29-31

    The last stanza returns to the narrator's voice of the first, but the scene has changed: the father who earlier held the boy 'safely' and 'warm' now rides hard in dread. Goethe's 'Dem Vater grauset's, er reitet geschwind' (horror seizes the father, he rides fast) does not tell us whether the father has seen anything, only that his calm has broken. 'Mit Mühe und Not' (with toil and trouble), Bowring's 'with toil and with dread', is a fixed German phrase meaning 'barely, with great difficulty'. The return to the opening stanza's narrative mode closes the frame and moves from present-tense dialogue towards the final past tense.

    Goethe, 'Erlkönig', German text, Wikisource; Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287; 'Erlkönig (Ballade)', Wikipedia (German), on sources, Herder and the main interpretations

  14. motionless, dead l. 32

    Goethe's final line breaks the poem's pattern twice. The ballad has been told largely in the present tense ('Wer reitet', 'Er hat den Knaben'), but the last clause shifts into the past, 'war tot' (was dead), as if time stopped. And the word order is inverted, so 'tot' comes last and lands as a single blunt stress. The German article on the poem notes that Herder's ballad makes a similar tense shift. Bowring adds 'motionless' and 'finds he', which make the moment one of the father's discovery and soften the abrupt final word, though he does keep 'dead' at the end.

  15. Supernatural or natural? ll. 15-16, 23-24

    The standard interpretive question is whether the Erl-King is real within the poem. The rationalist reading treats the child's fear or fever as what kills him, with the Erl-King a hallucination. A second line of criticism sees Goethe exposing the limits of Enlightenment rationalism: the father's explanations are sensible yet useless, and the forces of nature, figured in the alder-king, are real. A third reading simply sees Death in the mask of a seductive king, visible only to the dying. Bowring's added phrase 'tis thy fancy deceives' tilts the English towards the first reading by making the father's diagnosis explicit; Goethe's father only points to natural causes. Students should notice how carefully Goethe keeps the narrator neutral.

Erich Trunz (commentary to the poem); Goethe, The Poems of Goethe, Translated in the Original Metres, trans. Edgar Alfred Bowring (London, 1853), Project Gutenberg ebook 1287

  16. Schubert's setting whole poem

    The eighteen-year-old Schubert set the poem in 1815, with a relentless triplet accompaniment for the galloping horse and distinct vocal registers for narrator, father, son and Erl-King, whose music is seductively lilting until his last threat. The final line is delivered almost in recitative after the piano stops. Published in 1821 as his Opus 1, it remains one of the best-known Lieder. Schubert's circle sent it to Goethe, who did not respond; Goethe preferred simpler strophic settings like those of Reichardt and Zelter. Carl Loewe's setting (1818) is the other celebrated version. The music shaped how most later readers hear the poem's voices.
