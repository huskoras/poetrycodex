The Harp That Once thro' Tara's Halls

by Thomas Moore 1779–1852

From Irish Melodies

History & PoliticsTime & Brevity

The harp that once thro' Tara's halls
The soul of music shed,
Now hangs as mute on Tara's walls.
As if that soul were fled.--
So sleeps the pride of former days,
So glory's thrill is o'er,
And hearts, that once beat high for praise,
Now feel that pulse no more.

No more to chiefs and ladies bright
The harp of Tara swells;
The chord alone, that breaks at night,
Its tale of ruin tells.
Thus Freedom now so seldom wakes,
The only throbs she gives,
Is when some heart indignant breaks.
To show that still she lives.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Moore's Irish Melodies l. 1

    Thomas Moore (1779-1852) wrote new English words to old Irish tunes for the publisher James Power; the Melodies appeared in ten numbers from 1808 to 1834, with piano arrangements by Sir John Stevenson. 'The Harp that once' came out in the first number and was set to the air called 'Gramachree'. The Melodies were sung in drawing-rooms in Ireland and England and did much to create a romantic, elegiac image of Irish nationhood for a polite audience. The song is short, two eight-line stanzas, and meant to be sung, which explains its simplicity and its clear, balanced phrasing.

    'Irish Melodies', Wikipedia (publication 1808-34; Stevenson's arrangements); William & Mary Libraries, Special Collections, bound music vol. 20, listing 'The Harp that once, thro' Tara's Halls' to the air 'Gramachree'; Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  2. Tara l. 1

    Tara (Irish Teamhair) is a ridge with prehistoric earthworks near the River Boyne. Medieval Irish writers made it the seat of the High Kings, with Cormac mac Airt as the ideal king, and its feasts and assemblies became symbols of a lost national glory. The 'halls' are the legendary banqueting halls of the kings, of which nothing but mounds remains. Moore's audience would have understood Tara as the emblem of a vanished Gaelic sovereignty, and the song's whole movement is from that glory to present silence.

    'Hill of Tara', Wikipedia; Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  3. The harp l. 1

    The harp had been the emblem of Ireland on royal arms and coinage since the sixteenth century, and in the 1790s the United Irishmen adopted a harp with the motto 'It is new strung and shall be heard'. The Belfast Harp Festival of 1792, at which Edward Bunting transcribed the airs of the last traditional harpers, was linked with that political circle. Moore, a friend of the United Irishman Robert Emmet at Trinity College, inherits both meanings: the harp is national music and national freedom, and its silence is political.

    'Belfast Harp Festival', Wikipedia (1792 festival, Bunting, links with the United Irishmen); Helen Lawlor, Irish Harping, 1900-2010: 'It is New Strung' (Dublin: Four Courts Press, 2012), whose subtitle quotes the United Irishmen's harp motto; Mairead Ni Chathasaigh, 'The New-Strung Harp' (notes quoting the United Irishmen crest motto 'Equality: it is New Strung and Shall be Heard')

  4. The soul of music shed l. 2

    'Shed' means poured forth or diffused, as light is shed. The 'soul of music' is set up so that the next couplet can say the harp is mute 'As if that soul were fled': the instrument is pictured as a body whose soul has departed, so the song opens as an elegy for a dead thing. Moore's diction here is abstract and refined ('soul of music', 'pride of former days'), which suits the drawing-room song but was also what critics found too polished.

    Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shed'

  5. Now hangs as mute l. 3

    In Psalm 137 the Israelites in Babylon 'hanged our harps upon the willows' and refused to sing the Lord's song in a strange land. Moore's image of a harp hanging mute on the walls of a ruined royal hall evokes the same captivity and grief, comparing Ireland under English rule with Israel in exile. Note that the archive's text ends this line with a full stop; the 1821 Irish Melodies has no stop here, since the sentence runs on into 'As if that soul were fled'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 137:1-4; Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  6. So sleeps the pride of former days l. 5

    The double 'So ... So' turns the image into an analogy: as the harp is silent, so national pride sleeps and the 'thrill' of glory is over. 'Sleeps' rather than 'dies' leaves open the hope of reawakening, which the second stanza takes up with the word 'wakes'. The stanza ends with hearts that 'once beat high for praise' and now feel 'that pulse no more', linking the beating heart to the vibrating string.

    Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  7. glory's thrill l. 6

    'Thrill' meant a piercing or quivering sensation, and also a tremulous sound; the word fits both the emotion of glory and the vibration of a plucked string. Moore's imagery throughout connects musical and physiological pulses: the string's thrill, the heart's beat, the 'throb' of freedom. The song therefore works as one extended conceit of the nation as an instrument. The word is also proper to sensation, a shiver of feeling, so glory is felt in the body.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'thrill'; Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  8. chiefs and ladies bright l. 9

    The harp no longer 'swells' (sounds out with rising volume) to an aristocratic audience of chieftains and their ladies. The phrase belongs to the romantic picture of bardic Ireland that the Melodies helped to popularise, in which harpers played in the halls of chiefs. The loss of that patronage, completed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, was a real historical cause of the harp tradition's decline.

    Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription); 'Belfast Harp Festival', Wikipedia (1792 festival, Bunting, links with the United Irishmen)

  9. The chord ... that breaks at night l. 11

    'Chord' here means a string (the older spelling 'chord' and 'cord' were interchangeable for a harp-string). A string left unplayed in a cold hall may snap of its own accord, and its sudden sound at night is the harp's only 'tale of ruin'. The 1821 text sets 'alone' between commas ('The chord, alone, that breaks at night'), stressing that this is all that sounds. The image prepares the final conceit, in which a heart breaks like a string.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chord'; Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription)

  10. Freedom ... when some heart indignant breaks l. 13

    The closing quatrain applies the broken string to politics: Freedom, mostly asleep, gives her only 'throb' when some heart breaks in indignant protest, proving she still lives. Readers have often connected the line with the failed risings of 1798 and 1803 and with Moore's friend Robert Emmet, executed in 1803, to whom other Melodies allude. Note two textual points in the archive: '1821' reads 'The only throb she gives' (singular, agreeing with 'Is'), and 'breaks,' with a comma, not a full stop, since the sentence continues.

    Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription); 'Robert Emmet', Wikipedia

  11. Form l. 12

    The verse is in common measure, lines of eight and six syllables rhymed alternately, like a hymn or ballad, with the shorter lines indented. The regularity suits singing, and each stanza is built as an image followed by its application ('So ... So'; 'Thus'). The plainness that made the song popular is also what critics such as Hazlitt objected to.

    Thomas Moore, Irish Melodies (London: J. Power, 1821), 'The harp that once through Tara's halls' (Wikisource transcription); 'Irish Melodies', Wikipedia (publication 1808-34; Stevenson's arrangements)

  12. Patriotism or prettiness? Hazlitt's verdict l. 16

    In The Spirit of the Age (1825) Hazlitt attacked the Irish Melodies as vapid 'prettinesses' passed off as patriotism, saying that if they truly expressed Irish feeling 'the case of Ireland is hopeless', and that Moore 'converts the wild harp of Erin into a musical snuff-box'. Against this, the Melodies were sung widely in nineteenth-century Ireland, and the closing claim that freedom 'still ... lives' was a political statement in the years after the Act of Union (1800). Modern critics continue to debate whether Moore's elegiac nationalism consoled or roused his audience.

    William Hazlitt, The Spirit of the Age (1825), 'Mr. T. Moore--Mr. Leigh Hunt' (Project Gutenberg); 'Irish Melodies', Wikipedia (publication 1808-34; Stevenson's arrangements)
