Social Commentaries

Richard Cory

by Edwin Arlington Robinson 1869–1935

From The Children of the Night (1897)

Social Commentaries

Whenever Richard Cory went down town,
We people on the pavement looked at him:
He was a gentleman from sole to crown,
Clean favored, and imperially slim.

And he was always quietly arrayed,
And he was always human when he talked;
But still he fluttered pulses when he said,
"Good-morning," and he glittered when he walked.

And he was rich, -- yes, richer than a king, --
And admirably schooled in every grace:
In fine, we thought that he was everything
To make us wish that we were in his place.

So on we worked, and waited for the light,
And went without the meat, and cursed the bread;
And Richard Cory, one calm summer night,
Went home and put a bullet through his head.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Richard Cory and Tilbury Town l. 1

    Robinson grew up in Gardiner, Maine, which became the 'Tilbury Town' of his poems; 'Richard Cory', 'Miniver Cheevy' and 'Mr. Flood's Party' belong to this gallery of small-town lives. The Children of the Night appeared from a small Boston press and was little noticed until Theodore Roosevelt praised it and recommended it to Scribner's, which issued a new edition in 1904. The 1890s were years of economic depression after the Panic of 1893, which also damaged the Robinson family's fortunes: this is the background to the townspeople's hardship in stanza 4. Paul Simon adapted the poem as a song recorded by Simon and Garfunkel on Sounds of Silence (1966).

    'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Maine State Library, Maine Writers: Robinson, Edwin Arlington (1869-1935); Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007)

  2. The collective speaker l. 2

    The speaker is plural and anonymous; we know Cory only as the crowd sees him. 'On the pavement' puts them literally on a lower level than the gentleman, and the phrase anticipates 'we... went without the meat'. The irony depends on this limited view: the town admires surfaces and never sees inside him. W. R. Robinson reads Tilbury Town as a place of spiritual shallowness in which appearances pass for reality.

    'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; W. R. Robinson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poetry of the Act (Western Reserve UP, 1967)

  3. 'from sole to crown' l. 3

    The phrase echoes the biblical 'from the sole of the foot even unto the head' (Isaiah 1:6) and, closer in wording, Job 2:7 ('from the sole of his foot unto his crown'). Closer in spirit is 2 Samuel 14:25, where Absalom is praised for beauty 'from the sole of the foot even to the crown of his head', a handsome prince who comes to a bad end. 'Crown' means the top of the head but also starts the royal vocabulary of 'imperially', 'arrayed' and 'richer than a king'. Some readers also hear 'sole' as 'soul', which would make the line claim more than the crowd can know.

    Bible (KJV), Job 2:7; Bible (KJV), 2 Samuel 14:25; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 1:6; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com

  4. 'Clean favored' l. 4

    The OED records 'favour' as an archaic word for the face or features, and 'favoured' with a qualifier ('well-favoured', 'ill-favoured', as of Pharaoh's cattle in Genesis 41) for someone's looks. 'Clean favored' thus means well-featured, neat and fine. The slightly old-fashioned compound suits the gentlemanly portrait. Every detail in the first stanza is physical and external: we learn how Cory looks, never what he thinks, which is precisely the limitation of the watching crowd and the ground of the final surprise.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'favoured'; Bible (KJV), Genesis 41:2-4

  5. 'imperially slim' l. 4

    The adverb raises an ordinary physical trait to royal dignity, extending the 'crown' of line 3. Slimness also suggests a gentleman who does no heavy labour, set against the working townspeople. The phrase is among the most quoted in the poem because it captures the town's awe in two words, and the Gale commentary notes how it reinforces the speaker's view of Cory as a king.

    'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'imperially'

  6. 'quietly arrayed' l. 5

    'Arrayed' is the King James word for splendid dress: 'even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these' (Matthew 6:29). 'Quietly' tempers the splendour: Cory is tasteful, not showy. The Gale commentary reads the pairing as an oxymoron and notes that 'arrayed' can also mean drawn up in order, as for battle. Either way, clothing is part of the surface the town reads.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:29; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'array'

  7. 'human when he talked' l. 6

    To call a man 'human' when he talks implies that he might have been expected to be otherwise, a god or an aristocrat too grand to notice them. The townspeople are grateful for courtesy. The word is also ironic in retrospect: Cory's humanity, including his despair, is exactly what they never see. The adverb 'always', repeated in this stanza, stresses how unvarying the public performance was.

    'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007)

  8. 'he glittered' l. 8

    The image makes Cory a precious object and hints at a gap between surface and substance, as in the proverb used in The Merchant of Venice (Act 2, scene 7, 'All that glisters is not gold'). The rhymes of the stanza (arrayed/said, talked/walked) are simple and conversational, so the striking verbs 'fluttered' and 'glittered' stand out. The townspeople's pulses 'flutter' at a simple greeting, a phrase more often used of romantic excitement, which measures the strength of their fascination.

    Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice, Act 2 scene 7 (Folger Shakespeare); 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com

  9. Wealth and the American dream l. 9

    The dashes enclose an emphatic correction ('yes, richer than a king'), the voice of a speaker carried away by admiration. Richard P. Adams reads the poem as anti-materialist, and the Gale commentary describes it as an ironic comment on the American dream of wealth and success. Biographers note that Robinson watched his own family's fortune and his brothers' prospects collapse in the 1890s, which gave him a close view of what wealth could not protect.

    'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007)

  10. 'schooled in every grace' l. 10

    'Graces' here means polite accomplishments, the manners of a gentleman. But in a poem that ends in despair after the townspeople's wait for 'the light', the theological sense of grace, the free gift of God that saves, is ironically absent from Cory's life. 'Schooled' implies something learned, not felt. The word thus points to the inner life that the poem never shows, and that the town never imagines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grace'; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com

  11. 'In fine' l. 11

    The phrase was formal and slightly old-fashioned by the 1890s. Its point is sharp: the townspeople sum Cory up as 'everything', and their summary is about to be proved wrong. The third stanza closes the portrait with the confession of envy, 'To make us wish that we were in his place', the line the last stanza will turn inside out.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fine'; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com

  12. 'waited for the light' l. 13

    The phrase is deliberately open. It may mean that the workers rose before dawn, or that they waited for economic recovery after the depression of the 1890s, or that they waited for moral or religious illumination ('The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light', Isaiah 9:2). The title of the volume, The Children of the Night, frames human life as darkness awaiting light, and the Gale commentary surveys these readings.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 9:2; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com; Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007)

  13. Meat and bread l. 14

    Meat stands for the luxury the poor went without, bread for the bare staple they had and did not value. The line also turns biblical language: the Lord's Prayer asks for 'daily bread' (Matthew 6:11), and Jesus answers the tempter that 'man shall not live by bread alone' (Matthew 4:4). The townspeople curse the bread they are given; Cory, who has everything material, lacks whatever else one lives by.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 4:4; Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:11; 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com

  14. The ending l. 16

    The form (four quatrains of iambic pentameter rhymed ABAB) and the flat 'one calm summer night' prepare nothing for the violence, which is the point. Yvor Winters judged the poem a neat portrait with a cheap surprise ending. Defenders argue that the details (the crowd's envy, the 'light' they wait for) prepare the reversal, and that the ending makes us reread the whole poem. Simon's 1966 song keeps the suicide but moves the weight to a new refrain, in which the singer still wishes he could be Richard Cory.

    Yvor Winters, Edwin Arlington Robinson (New Directions, 1946); 'Richard Cory', Poetry for Students (Gale), encyclopedia.com
