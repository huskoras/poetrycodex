Miniver Cheevy

by Edwin Arlington Robinson 1869–1935

From Modern American Poetry (1921)

Living

Miniver Cheevy, child of scorn,
Grew lean while he assailed the seasons;
He wept that he was ever born,
And he had reasons.

Miniver loved the days of old
When swords were bright and steeds were prancing;
The vision of a warrior bold
Would set him dancing.

Miniver sighed for what was not,
And dreamed, and rested from his labors;
He dreamed of Thebes and Camelot,
And Priam’s neighbors.

Miniver mourned the ripe renown
That made so many a name so fragrant;
He mourned Romance, now on the town,
And Art, a vagrant.

Miniver loved the Medici,
Albeit he had never seen one;
He would have sinned incessantly
Could he have been one.

Miniver cursed the commonplace
And eyed a khaki suit with loathing;
He missed the mediæval grace
Of iron clothing.

Miniver scorned the gold he sought,
But sore annoyed was he without it;
Miniver thought, and thought, and thought,
And thought about it.

Miniver Cheevy, born too late,
Scratched his head and kept on thinking;
Miniver coughed, and called it fate,
And kept on drinking.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion l. 29

    The poem was collected in The Town Down the River (1910), which Robinson dedicated to Theodore Roosevelt. Roosevelt had admired Robinson's early poems, persuaded Scribner's to reissue The Children of the Night, and found him a clerkship at the New York Custom House in 1905. In these years Robinson was poor, unsuccessful and drinking heavily in New York, and critics and biographers have often seen something of the poet himself in Miniver, the romantic idler who despises the present. The portrait is comic and satirical, but not without sympathy: like Robinson's 'Richard Cory', it presents a small-town figure of failed aspiration.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699; Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Edwin Arlington Robinson'

  2. Miniver Cheevy l. 1

    Miniver (from Old French menu vair) was a white or spotted fur, often ermine, used to line and trim the ceremonial robes of medieval nobles and peers. The name thus dresses the man in the costume he dreams of, while 'Cheevy' is a homely surname with a possible echo of 'chivalry' or 'chevalier', a link some readers hear, though Robinson never confirmed it. The mock-heroic epithet 'child of scorn' imitates the formulas of romance and epic ('child of fortune'): he is born of scorn and lives by scorning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'miniver'; Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  3. child of scorn l. 1

    The phrase has the ring of epic or biblical formula. It is double-edged: Miniver scorns the world ('scorned the gold', 'cursed the commonplace'), and he is also a figure others might scorn. The first stanza sets the tone of the whole: grand gestures ('assailed the seasons', 'wept that he was ever born') undercut by the dry final line. The tone is set from the start: mock-solemn, affectionate and merciless at once.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  4. assailed the seasons l. 2

    To assail is to attack, usually with arms; Miniver's only weapon is complaint. 'The seasons' are the times, the age he has been born into. Growing 'lean' from discontent is a traditional sign of the malcontent or melancholic (compare Shakespeare's Cassius with his 'lean and hungry look', Julius Caesar 1.2). The heroic verb applied to grumbling is the poem's mock-heroic method in miniature.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assail'; William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar 1.2.194

  5. The stanza and its short last line l. 4

    Each stanza has three iambic tetrameter lines and a shortened fourth line of two stresses. Lines 2 and 4 rhyme on feminine (two-syllable) endings: seasons/reasons, prancing/dancing, labors/neighbors, fragrant/vagrant, seen one/been one, loathing/clothing, without it/about it, thinking/drinking. The pattern works like a joke's timing: three lines of grand sentiment, then a curt line that undercuts it. 'And he had reasons' is deadpan. It could mean his misery is justified, or mock the self-pitying reasons he gives.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Edwin Arlington Robinson'

  6. the days of old l. 5

    The past Miniver loves is a literary one, the world of romance and Victorian medievalism (bright swords, prancing steeds, the bold warrior). The phrase 'set him dancing' makes his enthusiasm childish. The poem satirises a sentimental medievalism that was widespread in late nineteenth-century culture, from Tennyson's Idylls of the King to popular historical romances. Miniver's past is costume, not history: he knows it only from books and pictures.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699; Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007)

  7. Thebes and Camelot l. 11

    Thebes was the Greek city of Cadmus, Oedipus and the Seven against Thebes; there was also a great Egyptian Thebes, and the poem does not say which. Camelot is the legendary court of King Arthur, made familiar to Robinson's readers by Malory and by Tennyson's Idylls of the King. Robinson himself later wrote Arthurian narrative poems (Merlin, 1917; Lancelot, 1920; Tristram, 1927). Miniver mixes classical and medieval legend indiscriminately: any heroic past will do, so long as it is not the present.

    Thomas Malory, Le Morte Darthur (1485); Alfred Tennyson, Idylls of the King (1859-85); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Edwin Arlington Robinson'

  8. Priam's neighbors l. 12

    Priam is the aged king of Troy in Homer's Iliad, father of Hektor, who goes to Akhilleus' tent to ransom his son's body in Book 24. Calling the Trojans 'Priam's neighbors' is a deliberately homely, slightly absurd periphrasis, as though the heroic age were a small town. The rhyme labors/neighbors also juxtaposes Miniver's idleness ('rested from his labors', itself an ironic echo of Revelation 14:13, 'they may rest from their labours') with his epic dreams.

    Homer, Iliad, Book 24; The Bible (KJV), Revelation 14:13

  9. Romance, now on the town ... Art, a vagrant l. 15

    Personified Romance and Art have fallen on hard times. 'On the town' was an idiom for being supported by parish or public relief, and also for living as a prostitute, both senses implying a fall from dignity. Art is 'a vagrant', homeless and wandering. Miniver sees commercial modern America as having no place for romance or art. The poem partly shares this view, since Robinson was himself a struggling poet, and partly mocks Miniver's self-pity. 'Fragrant' for renown is a comically perfumed epithet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'town, n.'; Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  10. the Medici l. 17

    The Medici ruled Florence for much of the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries. They were famous as patrons (Lorenzo il Magnifico, the Medici popes) and notorious for political violence and luxury. Miniver loves them without ever having met one, and imagines that he 'would have sinned incessantly' as one of them: his rebellion is purely imaginary. The rhyme 'Medici / incessantly' is comically strained, and the feminine rhyme 'seen one / been one' clinches the joke.

    Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Medici family'; Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  11. a khaki suit l. 22

    'Khaki' comes through Anglo-Indian usage from Urdu and Persian khak, 'dust', and named the dust-coloured cloth adopted by the British army in India and then generally. The United States Army adopted khaki field uniforms around the Spanish-American War of 1898. To Miniver, khaki is the modern soldier's dull uniform, opposed to the shining armour ('iron clothing') of the knight. The joke is that he loathes the real, modern form of the soldiering he romanticises.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'khaki'; Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  12. iron clothing l. 24

    Armour becomes 'iron clothing', a flat, faintly ridiculous periphrasis that undercuts the 'grace' of the line before. The spelling 'mediæval' (with the ligature) was the conventional form in 1910. The stanza sets the commonplace (khaki) against the picturesque (armour), and the plain diction deflates both. The rhyme loathing/clothing is a typical comic feminine rhyme, and the absurdity of the knight in iron 'clothing' undercuts the romance of chivalry that Miniver adores.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  13. scorned the gold he sought l. 25

    The stanza turns from medievalism to money. Miniver scorns materialism in theory but seeks it and is 'sore annoyed' to lack it. The inverted syntax ('sore annoyed was he') is mock-archaic, as if he spoke the language of his dream world. Robinson's own poverty, and his dependence on friends and patrons in these years, gives the satire an edge of self-knowledge.

    Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007); Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  14. thought, and thought, and thought, / And thought about it l. 27

    Four repetitions of 'thought' in two lines make thinking a futile circle, and the anticlimax 'And thought about it' deflates it completely. The joke continues in the last stanza with 'kept on thinking' and its rhyme partner 'kept on drinking'. Miniver's thought never becomes action. The chiming repetition makes the reader feel the tedium of his brooding, and it also sets up the poem's final rhyme, where 'thinking' and 'drinking' are revealed as the same activity.

    Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699

  15. coughed, and called it fate, / And kept on drinking l. 32

    The last line exposes what the dreaming has been hiding: Miniver is an alcoholic who calls his own weakness 'fate'. The cough may be a nervous gesture, or a sign of illness; either way 'called it fate' is self-excuse. 'Born too late' sums up his claim, and the poem's irony suggests he would have been a failure in any age. Biographers have noted that Robinson drank heavily in his New York years, and many readers take the poem as partly a self-satirising portrait. Others caution against too biographical a reading and stress its general satire on American romantic escapism.

    Scott Donaldson, Edwin Arlington Robinson: A Poet's Life (Columbia UP, 2007); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Edwin Arlington Robinson'; Edwin Arlington Robinson, The Town Down the River (New York: Scribner's, 1910), Project Gutenberg ebook 76699
