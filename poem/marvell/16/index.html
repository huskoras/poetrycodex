Love

The Definition of Love.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Love

MY Love is of a birth as rare
As 'tis for object strange and high:
It was begotten by despair
Upon Impossibility.

Magnanimous Despair alone
Could show me so divine a thing,
Where feeble Hope could ne'r have flown
But vainly flapt its Tinsel Wing.

And yet I quickly might arrive
Where my extended Soul is fixt,
But Fate does Iron wedges drive,
And alwaies crouds it self betwixt.

For Fate with jealous Eye does see
Two perfect Loves; nor lets them close:
Their union would her ruine be,
And her Tyrannick pow'r depose.

And therefore her Decrees of Steel
Us as the distant Poles have plac'd,
(Though Loves whole World on us doth wheel)
Not by themselves to be embrac'd.

Unless the giddy Heaven fall,
And Earth some new Convulsion tear:
And, us to joyn, the World should all
Be cramp'd into a Planisphere.

As Lines so Loves oblique may well
Themselves in every Angle greet:
But ours so truly Paralel,
Though infinite can never meet.

Therefore the Love which us doth bind,
But Fate so enviously debarrs,
Is the Conjunction of the Mind,
And Opposition of the Stars.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Definition of Love: title, date, form st. 1

    In scholastic logic a definition states a thing's genus and its distinguishing difference; Marvell's poem defines a love whose essence is its impossibility, ending with a two-term formula ('Conjunction of the Mind, / And Opposition of the Stars'). It was first printed in Miscellaneous Poems (1681), issued after Marvell's death, and cannot be dated securely; editors usually place it with the lyrics of the late 1640s or early 1650s. The form is eight quatrains of iambic tetrameter rhymed abab, compact and epigrammatic. The poem's argument by geometry and astronomy has made it a classic example of 'metaphysical' wit, often compared with Donne's 'Valediction: Forbidding Mourning'.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.; Elizabeth Story Donno, ed., Andrew Marvell: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1972), note ad loc.

  2. begotten by despair / Upon Impossibility ll. 3-4

    In Plato's Symposium (203b-c), Diotima tells how Eros was conceived by Poros (Resource) on Penia (Poverty) at Aphrodite's birthday feast, which explains love's needy yet resourceful nature. Marvell replaces the parents with Despair and Impossibility: the 'begetting upon' formula (the father begets upon the mother) gives a mock-scholastic precision. The love is 'rare' (unusual, precious) because its object is 'strange and high', too lofty to be attained.

    Plato, Symposium 203b-c; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  3. Magnanimous Despair l. 5

    Latin magnanimus, 'great-souled', translates Aristotle's megalopsychia, the virtue of the man who claims great things because he deserves them (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3). Hope, by contrast, is 'feeble' (l. 7): it calculates and so would never set out toward the impossible. The reversal of the usual moral values of hope and despair is the poem's first paradox. For a Christian reader despair was a sin, and the playful inversion is part of the poem's wit.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'magnanimous'; Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3

  4. Tinsel Wing l. 8

    'Tinsel' was a cloth interwoven with gold or silver thread, and by Marvell's time also meant anything showy but worthless (OED). Hope only 'flapt' its wings in vain, an undignified verb that makes it sound like a grounded bird. The image recalls Icarus and the Platonic winged soul (Phaedrus 246), but here flight is denied. Only despair, which expects nothing, can 'show' the divine object (l. 6); hope, which always expects some reward, is earthbound.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tinsel'; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.

  5. my extended Soul is fixt l. 10

    'Extended' means stretched out, reaching; there may be a glance at the philosophical term for spatial extension, which made the soul seem paradoxically bodily. 'Fixt' suggests both steadfastness and a star fixed in its sphere, preparing the astronomical imagery of stanzas 5-8. The lover 'might quickly arrive' were it not for Fate. The word 'alwaies' (l. 12) makes Fate's interference perpetual, the first statement of the permanent separation that the geometry of later stanzas will prove.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Elizabeth Story Donno, ed., Andrew Marvell: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1972), note ad loc.

  6. Fate's Iron wedges l. 11

    In Horace, Odes 1.35.17-20, Necessity goes before Fortune carrying nails, wedges, a clamp and molten lead, the tools of fixing and splitting. Editors cite the passage for Marvell's Fate. The wedge splits apart what would otherwise close, and 'crouds it self betwixt' makes Fate an intruder physically shouldering between the lovers. Marvell turns Horace's abstract Necessity into a jealous rival with a body and a will, and introduces the iron and steel ('Decrees of Steel', l. 17) that make Fate's rule unbreakable.

    Horace, Odes 1.35.17-20; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  7. Fate's jealousy and tyranny ll. 13-16

    Fate is pictured as a jealous tyrant who fears that two 'perfect Loves' joined would make her 'ruine' and 'depose' her 'Tyrannick pow'r'. Some critics have heard the political vocabulary of the 1640s and 1650s (tyranny, deposition, decrees) in these lines; others take it simply as the conventional rivalry of Love and Fortune. 'Close' means unite, and also join in combat.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Elizabeth Story Donno, ed., Andrew Marvell: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1972), note ad loc.

  8. the distant Poles ll. 17-20

    Fate's 'Decrees of Steel' fix the lovers like the earth's poles, the two ends of the axis around which the globe revolves. The parenthesis 'Though Loves whole World on us doth wheel' makes them the necessary centre of the universe of love while denying them contact. The image is a variant of the metaphysical cartographic conceit, compare Donne's 'Hymn to God my God, in my Sickness'.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  9. Planisphere l. 24

    A planisphere is a representation of the celestial or terrestrial sphere on a plane. Only cosmic catastrophe could unite the lovers: the 'giddy' (whirling) heaven would have to fall, the earth be torn by 'some new Convulsion', and the world 'cramp'd' (squeezed) flat, so that the poles touched. Editors explain the geometry differently according to the type of projection they suppose, but the hyperbole is clear: the union is literally world-ending.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'planisphere'; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  10. Lines oblique and parallel ll. 25-28

    Euclid's Elements, Book I, definition 23, defines parallels as straight lines in the same plane which, produced indefinitely in both directions, do not meet. Ordinary loves are 'oblique', and so can 'greet' at an angle (with a pun on corners, angles, and on angling for advantage). The lovers' love is 'so truly Paralel' (exactly matched, equal) that it can never converge. 'Though infinite' applies to both the lines and the love.

    Euclid, Elements I, def. 23 (D. E. Joyce online edition); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Elizabeth Story Donno, ed., Andrew Marvell: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1972), note ad loc.

  11. Conjunction of the Mind, / And Opposition of the Stars ll. 31-32

    In astrology two planets are in conjunction when they occupy the same position in the zodiac, and in opposition when they are 180 degrees apart, the most hostile aspect. The final definition splits the love into its two terms: union of minds, and separation decreed by the heavens. The lovers are like opposed planets, which see each other face to face across the whole sky. 'Enviously' (l. 30) recalls the jealous Fate of stanza 4, and 'debarrs' means bars, shuts out.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'opposition'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'conjunction'; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.
