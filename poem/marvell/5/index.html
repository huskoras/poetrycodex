Nature

Bermudas.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Nature

WHere the remote Bermudas ride
In th' Oceans bosome unespy'd,
From a small Boat, that row'd along,
The listning Winds receiv'd this Song.
What should we do but sing his Praise
That led us through the watry Maze,
Unto an Isle so long unknown,
And yet far kinder than our own?
Where he the huge Sea-Monsters wracks,
That lift the Deep upon their Backs.
He lands us on a grassy Stage;
Safe from the Storms, and Prelat's rage.
He gave us this eternal Spring,
Which here enamells every thing;
And sends the Fowl's to us in care,
On daily Visits through the Air.
He hangs in shades the Orange bright,
Like golden Lamps in a green Night.
And does in the Pomgranates close,
Jewels more rich than Ormus show's.
He makes the Figs our mouths to meet;
And throws the Melons at our feet.
But Apples plants of such a price,
No Tree could ever bear them twice.
With Cedars, chosen by his hand,
From Lebanon, he stores the Land.
And makes the hollow Seas, that roar,
Proclaime the Ambergris on shoar.
He cast (of which we rather boast)
The Gospels Pearl upon our Coast.
And in these Rocks for us did frame
A Temple, where to sound his Name.
Oh let our Voice his Praise exalt,
Till it arrive at Heavens Vault:
Which thence (perhaps) rebounding, may
Eccho beyond the Mexique Bay.
Thus sung they, in the English boat,
An holy and a chearful Note,
And all the way, to guide their Chime,
With falling Oars they kept the time.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Bermudas and the poem's occasion l. 1

    The poem was first printed in Marvell's posthumous Miscellaneous Poems (1681). In July 1653 Cromwell placed his ward William Dutton under Marvell's tutoring, and the two lodged at Eton with John Oxenbridge, a Fellow of Eton who had gone to Bermuda after losing his Oxford tutorship in the Laudian years. Oxenbridge had made voyages to Bermuda before returning to England in 1641, and he is said to have been made a commissioner for the islands in 1653. The poem is usually read as growing out of his talk and perhaps complimenting him. The islands (also called the Somers or Summer Islands) were settled from 1612 after Sir George Somers's ship was wrecked there in 1609, the wreck that lies behind Shakespeare's 'still-vexed Bermoothes' in The Tempest. 'Remote' and 'unespy'd' cast the islands as a secret refuge hidden in the ocean.

    Edward Wright, ed., The Poems and Some Satires of Andrew Marvell (Methuen, 1904), introduction and footnotes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B (The Sixteenth Century and the Early Seventeenth Century), headnote and notes to Marvell's 'Bermudas'; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  2. Frame and song ll. 1-4, 37-40

    Marvell sets the emigrants' psalm of thanks (ll. 5-36) inside a short narrative frame: an unseen boat, the 'listning Winds' as the only audience, and at the end the oars keeping time. The frame gives the poem distance, so that we overhear rather than join the song, and it lets the closing lines turn the singing into rowing. The metre throughout is the iambic tetrameter couplet Marvell also uses in 'The Garden' and 'To his Coy Mistress'. The song itself is close to a metrical psalm: its pattern of 'He led us ... He lands us ... He gave us' echoes the catalogue of God's providences in psalms such as 104 and 107.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'; Psalms 104 and 107 (KJV)

  3. 'far kinder than our own' l. 8

    The comparison carries the poem's politics in a phrase. England is the unkind country that drove the singers out; the island is unknown, small and generous. Readers divide over how far Marvell shares the emigrants' view. Many take the song as an unironic Puritan hymn of providence, while others hear a gentle irony in the singers' mix of piety and commercial delight (oranges, ambergris, jewels, the 'boast' of l. 29), and in the fact that they still sing in an 'English boat'. Recent criticism also reads the poem against English colonial projects in the Atlantic, of which Oxenbridge's commission was a part. The poem itself leaves these possibilities in balance.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B (The Sixteenth Century and the Early Seventeenth Century), headnote and notes to Marvell's 'Bermudas'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Bermudas', text from Miscellaneous Poems (1681)

  4. watry Maze l. 6

    A maze has a hidden way through it known only to its maker; the emigrants have been led along that way by Providence. The image draws on the Exodus pattern so important to Puritan settlers, in which God brings his people through the waters to a promised land (Exodus 14), and on Psalm 107:23-30, where those who 'go down to the sea in ships' are brought 'unto their desired haven'. 'Watry Maze' also fits the real hazards of Bermuda's reefs, notorious for wrecks.

    Exodus 14; Psalm 107:23-30 (KJV); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'

  5. Sea-Monsters wracks l. 9

    'Wracks' is an old form of 'wrecks', here a verb: God destroys or drives ashore the sea-beasts (OED, wrack/wreck). Whales were a familiar sight and a resource around the islands, and Edmund Waller's mock-heroic 'Battle of the Summer Islands' (1645) makes a fight between islanders and whales its subject. The biblical background is the Leviathan whom God has made 'to play' in the sea (Psalm 104:26) and whom he masters (Psalm 74:14; Isaiah 27:1). 'That lift the Deep upon their Backs' gives a vivid sense of their bulk.

    Edward Wright, ed., The Poems and Some Satires of Andrew Marvell (Methuen, 1904), introduction and footnotes to 'Bermudas'; OED, 'wrack, v.' and 'wreck, v.'; Psalm 104:26; Psalm 74:14 (KJV); Edmund Waller, 'The Battle of the Summer Islands' (Poems, 1645)

  6. Prelat's rage l. 12

    'Prelate' (a bishop or other high church dignitary) was a hostile word in Puritan mouths. Under Laud, Archbishop of Canterbury from 1633, nonconforming ministers were deprived and harassed, and the 1630s saw large migrations to New England and the Atlantic islands. Oxenbridge himself left for Bermuda in this period. The islands attracted settlers partly because they offered a refuge from High Church rule. By 1653, when the poem was probably written, bishops had been abolished, so the line looks back to the persecution that made the emigrants' journey necessary.

    Edward Wright, ed., The Poems and Some Satires of Andrew Marvell (Methuen, 1904), introduction and footnotes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B (The Sixteenth Century and the Early Seventeenth Century), headnote and notes to Marvell's 'Bermudas'

  7. eternal Spring l. 13

    Ovid says of the Golden Age that 'ver erat aeternum', spring was everlasting (Metamorphoses 1.107), and Virgil praises Italy for 'ver adsiduum', continual spring (Georgics 2.149). Milton too gives Eden 'eternal spring' (Paradise Lost 4.268). Early reports of Bermuda praised its temperate air and year-round greenness, so the emigrants find in it both the Golden Age of the poets and a recovered Eden. The present tense ('He gave us') presents this paradise as a gift already received.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.107; Virgil, Georgics 2.149; Milton, Paradise Lost 4.268; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  8. enamells l. 14

    To enamel is literally to coat metal with a vitreous glaze; by the seventeenth century it was common in the figurative sense of adorning a surface, especially with bright colours, as flowers 'enamel' a meadow (OED, enamel v.). The word begins a run of images in which the island's nature looks like a jeweller's work: lamps of gold, jewels in pomegranates, pearl. The effect is double: the place is precious, and God is the artist who has made it.

    OED, 'enamel, v.'; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  9. golden Lamps in a green Night l. 18

    Oranges hang in the shade of their leaves like golden lamps lit in a green darkness. The image turns the shade of the orange trees into a 'Night' that is green rather than black, and makes the fruit a source of light. It also recalls the golden apples of the Hesperides, the classical garden of the far West, which were often identified with oranges. Compare the 'green Thought in a green Shade' of Marvell's 'The Garden'. Critics often single out this line as an example of Marvell's combination of precise observation and wit.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B (The Sixteenth Century and the Early Seventeenth Century), headnote and notes to Marvell's 'Bermudas'

  10. Ormus l. 20

    Ormus (Hormuz) was the great trading emporium of the Persian Gulf, through which jewels and pearls reached Europe; held by the Portuguese, it was taken by Persian and English forces in 1622. It was a byword for oriental riches: Milton's Satan sits on a throne that outshines 'the wealth of Ormus and of Ind' (Paradise Lost 2.2). Marvell's point is that the seeds of a pomegranate, glistening red, are finer jewels than anything that port displays.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 2.1-4; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'

  11. Figs our mouths to meet ll. 21-2

    Figs rise to the mouth and melons are thrown at the feet. In classical Golden Age writing the earth gives its fruit 'of itself' without tilling (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.101-2), and in Genesis Adam eats without sweat before the Fall. Marvell uses the same fantasy, in a more voluptuous key, in 'The Garden', where grapes crush their wine upon the speaker's mouth and melons trip him up. Here the motif expresses gratitude rather than temptation, but readers who hear irony in the poem point to such passages of almost comic plenty.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.101-2; Marvell, 'The Garden', st. 5; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  12. Apples ... bear them twice ll. 23-4

    'Apple' was used loosely for any large unfamiliar fruit, and editors since the nineteenth century (Aitken's 1892 edition has the note 'Pineapples') have taken the 'apples' to be pineapples. The wit of 'No Tree could ever bear them twice' fits the pineapple, whose plant fruits once on each stem; the fruit was a rarity, prized in England as an exotic luxury. The syntax is compressed: God 'plants' apples of such value that no tree could bear them a second time. 'Price' means value, preciousness.

    Wikipedia, 'Pineapple' (botany and early English usage); George A. Aitken, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Lawrence & Bullen, 1892), note to 'Bermudas' l. 23

  13. Cedars ... From Lebanon ll. 25-6

    Bermuda's dominant tree was its native 'cedar' (in fact a juniper, Juniperus bermudiana), used for houses and ships. Marvell makes them cedars 'chosen' by God from Lebanon, the cedars Solomon ordered for the Temple at Jerusalem (1 Kings 5:6) and that the Psalmist calls the trees 'which he hath planted' (Psalm 104:16). The allusion prepares the 'Temple' of l. 32: God has supplied the timber of worship as well as fruit.

    1 Kings 5:6; Psalm 104:16 (KJV); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'

  14. Ambergris l. 28

    Ambergris (from French ambre gris, 'grey amber') forms in the gut of the sperm whale and is found floating or cast up on beaches; it was used in perfumes and medicines and fetched high prices. Early accounts of Bermuda, including John Smith's Generall Historie (1624), report finds of it, and Waller's 'Battle of the Summer Islands' plays on it. Marvell makes the roaring sea a herald that 'proclaims' the treasure on the shore. The line shows the emigrants' frank pleasure in the island's riches, which some readers find touching and others faintly ironic.

    OED, 'ambergris, n.'; Edmund Waller, 'The Battle of the Summer Islands' (1645); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  15. The Gospels Pearl l. 30

    In the parable a merchant sells all he has to buy one 'pearl of great price', a figure for the kingdom of heaven. The emigrants say God has 'cast' this pearl on their coast, and that it is what they 'rather boast' of: the free preaching of the gospel matters more to them than ambergris or fruit. There is a likely glance at Bermuda's real pearl-fishing hopes, which came to little, so that the spiritual pearl outvalues a material one the islands did not yield. 'Cast' also suggests the sea casting up treasure, linking the gospel to the ambergris of the previous couplet.

    Matthew 13:45-46 (KJV); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'

  16. 'of which we rather boast' l. 29

    The parenthesis is the hinge of the song's catalogue. After fruit, timber and ambergris, the singers correct themselves: the gift they would sooner boast of is the gospel. 'Boast' is a loaded word for Puritan singers, for whom boasting should be only 'in the Lord' (1 Corinthians 1:31; Psalm 34:2). Read straight, the phrase shows true priorities; read ironically, it shows how naturally material pride slips into the language of piety. Critics divide on which tone predominates.

    1 Corinthians 1:31; Psalm 34:2 (KJV); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B (The Sixteenth Century and the Early Seventeenth Century), headnote and notes to Marvell's 'Bermudas'

  17. A Temple l. 32

    God frames a temple 'in these Rocks' where the emigrants may praise him. For Puritans, worship did not depend on consecrated buildings or episcopal ceremony; a natural rock-church set against the 'Prelat's rage' makes that point. The image also connects with the cedar-built Temple of Solomon (ll. 25-6) and with the Psalms' figure of God as a rock. The practical freedom behind the image is the colonists' liberty to worship as they chose.

    Edward Wright, ed., The Poems and Some Satires of Andrew Marvell (Methuen, 1904), introduction and footnotes to 'Bermudas'; Psalm 18:2 (KJV); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'

  18. Eccho beyond the Mexique Bay l. 36

    The 'Mexique Bay' is the Gulf of Mexico, the heart of Spain's American empire. The singers hope their praise will rise to 'Heavens Vault' and rebound beyond it, a figure for the spread of reformed religion into lands English Protestants regarded as held by popery. Wright's edition recalls George Herbert's prophecy in 'The Church Militant' that 'Religion stands on tip-toe in our land, / Ready to pass to the American strand'. The 'perhaps' is a characteristic Marvellian touch of modesty or doubt. Cromwell's Western Design against Spanish America (1654-5) gives the line a topical edge if the poem dates from these years.

    Edward Wright, ed., The Poems and Some Satires of Andrew Marvell (Methuen, 1904), introduction and footnotes to 'Bermudas'; George Herbert, 'The Church Militant' (The Temple, 1633), ed. F. E. Hutchinson, The Works of George Herbert (Oxford, 1941); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'

  19. falling Oars kept the time ll. 39-40

    The closing couplet returns to the frame. 'Chime' is their singing in unison, and the oars falling together keep its beat, so that rowing becomes a kind of musical accompaniment and the song a working song. The image unites the active and contemplative sides of Puritan life and leaves the boat still moving: the poem ends without arrival. Some readers note that 'An holy and a chearful Note' frames the whole as simple and devout, which supports a non-ironic reading.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2003; rev. 2007), notes to 'Bermudas'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd ed. rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford, 1971), commentary on 'Bermudas'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Bermudas', text from Miscellaneous Poems (1681)
