Nature

The Garden.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Nature

HOW vainly men themselves amaze
To win the Palm, the Oke, or Bayes;
And their uncessant Labours see
Crown'd from some single Herb or Tree,
Whose short and narrow verged Shade
Does prudently their Toyles upbraid;
While all Flow'rs and all Trees do close
To weave the Garlands of repose.

Fair quiet, have I found thee here,
And Innocence thy Sister dear!
Mistaken long, I sought you then
In busie Companies of Men.
Your sacred Plants, if here below,
Only among the Plants will grow.
Society is all but rude,
To this delicious Solitude.

No white nor red was ever seen
So am'rous as this lovely green.
Fond Lovers, cruel as their Flame,
Cut in these Trees their Mistress name.
Little, Alas, they know, or heed,
How far these Beauties Hers exceed!
Fair Trees! where s'eer you barkes I wound,
No Name shall but your own be found.

When we have run our Passions heat,
Love hither makes his best retreat.
The Gods, that mortal Beauty chase,
Still in a Tree did end their race.
Apollo hunted Daphne so,
Only that She might Laurel grow.
And Pan did after Syrinx speed,
Not as a Nymph, but for a Reed.

What wond'rous Life in this I lead!
Ripe Apples drop about my head;
The Luscious Clusters of the Vine
Upon my Mouth do crush their Wine;
The Nectaren, and curious Peach,
Into my hands themselves do reach;
Stumbling on Melons, as I pass,
Insnar'd with Flow'rs, I fall on Grass.

Mean while the Mind, from pleasure less,
Withdraws into its happiness:
The Mind, that Ocean where each kind
Does streight its own resemblance find;
Yet it creates, transcending these,
Far other Worlds, and other Seas;
Annihilating all that's made
To a green Thought in a green Shade.

Here at the Fountains sliding foot,
Or at some Fruit-trees mossy root,
Casting the Bodies Vest aside,
My Soul into the boughs does glide:
There like a Bird it sits, and sings,
Then whets, and combs its silver Wings;
And, till prepar'd for longer flight,
Waves in its Plumes the various Light.

Such was that happy Garden-state,
While Man there walk'd without a Mate:
After a Place so pure, and sweet,
What other Help could yet be meet!
But 'twas beyond a Mortal's share
To wander solitary there:
Two Paradises 'twere in one
To live in Paradise alone.

How well the skilful Gardner drew
Of flow'rs and herbes this Dial new;
Where from above the milder Sun
Does through a fragrant Zodiack run;
And, as it works, th' industrious Bee
Computes its time as well as we.
How could such sweet and wholsome Hours
Be reckon'd but with herbs and flow'rs!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Garden: date and context title; st. 1

    First printed in Miscellaneous Poems (1681). Many critics associate it with Marvell's service as tutor to Mary Fairfax at Nun Appleton (c. 1650-52), though the date is uncertain. A Latin poem, 'Hortus', printed with it, treats similar material. The poem praises retirement over the active life, a theme with classical roots (Horace, Epode 2; Virgil, Georgics 2) and contemporary political resonance after the Civil War. 'Amaze' means bewilder, entangle as in a maze.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; Frank Kermode, 'The Argument of Marvell's "Garden"', Essays in Criticism 2 (1952)

  2. Palm, Oak, Bays l. 2

    In Roman custom the palm signified victory, the oak wreath (corona civica) was awarded for saving a citizen's life, and the bay or laurel crowned poets and triumphant generals. Men exhaust themselves to win a garland from a single plant, whose narrow shade rebukes their toil, while the garden offers all plants' garlands of 'repose'. The Latin 'Hortus' makes the same contrast between public crowns and the garden's peace. The 'single Herb or Tree' of line 4 makes a rhetorical point: those who strive gain one plant, while the contemplative gains all.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  3. verged l. 5

    'Verged' means bordered or limited. The shade cast by a wreath is tiny; the plant 'prudently' upbraids human striving. 'Close' in l. 7 means unite or join together. The stanza's argument is that the honours men pursue are small and grudging: the plant 'prudently' (wisely) upbraids the toil it rewards with so small a shade. In contrast, 'all Flow'rs and all Trees' freely weave garlands of repose for the retired man. The paradox of plenty in rest and scarcity in labour sets up the whole poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'verged'; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem

  4. Quiet and Innocence ll. 9-10

    Quiet (otium) and innocence are the classical and Christian goods of retirement. The paradox that their 'sacred Plants' grow only among plants plays on the double sense of 'plant'. 'Rude' (l. 15) means uncultivated or barbarous; society compared with solitude is uncivilised. The speaker confesses that he 'Mistaken long' sought these virtues in 'busie Companies of Men', perhaps glancing at public life in the Civil War years. The stanza closes with an epigram that inverts the usual valuation of society and solitude, a classical topos (compare Cicero's report, De Officiis 3.1, that Scipio was never less alone than when alone).

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; Frank Kermode, 'The Argument of Marvell's "Garden"', Essays in Criticism 2 (1952)

  5. delicious Solitude l. 16

    'Delicious' was a strong word, often used of sensual pleasure; applying it to solitude prepares for the garden's ripe fruits. The praise of solitude echoes a long tradition from Seneca to Montaigne's essay 'Of Solitude'. The word thus links the speaker's intellectual pleasure to physical delight. Critics differ on how far the poem's 'Solitude' is spiritual retirement and how far a witty, almost erotic, self-indulgence; Kermode reads it within the contemplative tradition, while others emphasise its sensuous play.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'delicious'; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  6. white and red vs green ll. 17-18

    Petrarchan lovers praised the beloved's white and red complexion; Marvell declares the green of plants more 'am'rous'. The joke is that the speaker's love is for the trees themselves. Green, an important colour throughout the poem, culminates in the 'green Thought' of stanza 6. 'Am'rous' keeps its older sense of 'lovely, inspiring love'. The stanza is a joke against conventional love poetry, replacing the beloved with the vegetable world.

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; William Empson, Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 4 on Marvell's 'Garden'

  7. carving names in bark ll. 19-24

    Carving the beloved's name in bark was a pastoral convention (Virgil, Eclogue 10.53-54; Orlando in As You Like It). The speaker parodies it: he will wound the trees only with their own names. 'Fond' means foolish. 'S'eer' is 'soe'er', i.e. wheresoever. 'Hers' in line 22 means 'the mistress's beauties', which the trees' beauty far exceeds. The speaker reverses the convention: he wounds the bark only with the tree's own name.

    Virgil, Eclogues 10.52-54; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  8. Apollo and Daphne; Pan and Syrinx ll. 29-32

    In Ovid, Metamorphoses 1, Daphne becomes a laurel to escape Apollo, and Syrinx becomes reeds to escape Pan, who makes his pipe from them. Marvell inverts the myths: the gods pursued the nymphs 'only' so they might become plants. The witty reinterpretation supports the stanza's claim that love's best retreat is the garden. 'Race' plays on the course of a pursuit and its end. The stanza converts erotic chase into a pursuit of vegetable peace: 'Love hither makes his best retreat' implies that desire, exhausted, finds rest among plants.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (Apollo and Daphne) and 1.689-712 (Pan and Syrinx); H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  9. the fruitful garden ll. 33-40

    The fruits press themselves upon the speaker, as in the golden age when the earth gave unbidden. The apple, the stumbling and the fall are a comic, innocent replay of Eden. 'Curious' means exquisite; 'Nectaren' is nectarine. Critics debate whether the stanza is innocent luxuriance or suggests temptation; Kermode reads it within a tradition of the garden as a place of contemplative pleasure.

    Frank Kermode, 'The Argument of Marvell's "Garden"', Essays in Criticism 2 (1952); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; Bible (KJV), Genesis 3

  10. from pleasure less l. 41

    The phrase allows several readings: (1) the mind, made less by pleasure, withdraws; (2) the mind withdraws from the lesser pleasure of the senses into its own greater happiness. Editors generally prefer the second, which marks the ascent from the senses to the intellect, a Neoplatonic movement. Empson discusses the ambiguity, seeing in such phrases the double meanings typical of Marvell's pastoral. 'Withdraws into its happiness' suggests both retreat and self-sufficiency.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; William Empson, Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 4 on Marvell's 'Garden'

  11. the mind as Ocean ll. 43-44

    Editors compare the 'vulgar error' that every land creature has its counterpart in the sea, which Sir Thomas Browne examined and doubted in Pseudodoxia Epidemica (1646). The mind contains ideas corresponding to everything in the world, a Platonic or Aristotelian notion. 'Streight' means straightaway. The mind then surpasses this mirroring by creating 'other Worlds, and other Seas'. The stanza's progression, from mirroring nature to creating new worlds, has been read as a Platonic ascent of the mind or as a celebration of poetic imagination.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Sir Thomas Browne, Pseudodoxia Epidemica (1646), book 3, ch. 24, 'That all Animals of the Land, are in their kind in the Sea'; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  12. a green Thought in a green Shade ll. 47-48

    'Annihilating' may mean reducing to nothing, or reducing to insignificance by comparison. 'Green' suggests fresh, innocent, immature, and the colour of the vegetable world. Empson discussed the line's balance between mind and nature; Kermode placed it in the tradition of contemplative ecstasy. Critics disagree whether the line shows mystical vision or witty paradox. The line's repetition of 'green' makes the thought and the shade almost identical, as if mind and nature merge. Smith surveys the range of readings, from Neoplatonic contemplation and Hermetic mysticism to witty parody of both.

    William Empson, Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 4 on Marvell's 'Garden'; Frank Kermode, 'The Argument of Marvell's "Garden"', Essays in Criticism 2 (1952); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  13. the soul as bird ll. 51-56

    The body as a 'Vest' (garment) cast aside is a Platonic image. The soul as a bird is an ancient emblem (cf. Plato's Phaedrus on the soul's wings). It 'whets' and 'combs' its wings and 'waves' the 'various Light' (many-coloured light), a Neoplatonic image of refracted divine light, before its 'longer flight' at death. 'Fountains sliding foot' means the base of a fountain where water slips away. The soul's preparation for 'longer flight' implies that this ecstasy is only a foretaste of death and heaven.

    Plato, Phaedrus 246a-249d (the winged soul); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; Frank Kermode, 'The Argument of Marvell's "Garden"', Essays in Criticism 2 (1952)

  14. help meet ll. 57-64

    In Genesis 2:18, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him'. Marvell's playful misogyny claims Eden was perfect before Eve, so a mate was superfluous. 'Meet' means fitting. 'Two Paradises 'twere in one' suggests that solitude in Paradise would be a double paradise, which was beyond mortal lot. The tone is witty rather than doctrinal, but the stanza also recalls the Christian and classical ideal of the contemplative solitary.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:18; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  15. the floral sundial ll. 65-72

    Floral sundials laid out in herbs were features of seventeenth-century gardens. The 'skilful Gardner' may be the human gardener, or God, the divine artist. The bee 'computes its time', possibly punning on thyme. Time, measured by flowers, becomes sweet and wholesome, in contrast to the toil of the first stanza. The final stanza returns from the inner life to the visible garden and closes the poem on the sweetness of time well spent, recalling the opening contrast with 'uncessant Labours'.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem
