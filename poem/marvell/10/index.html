Love

To his Coy Mistress.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Love

HAD we but World enough, and Time,
This coyness Lady were no crime.
We would sit down, and think which way
To walk, and pass our long Loves Day.
Thou by the Indian Ganges side
Should'st Rubies find: I by the Tide
Of Humber would complain. I would
Love you ten years before the Flood:
And you should if you please refuse
Till the Conversion of the Jews.
My vegetable Love should grow
Vaster then Empires, and more slow.
An hundred years should go to praise
Thine Eyes, and on thy Forehead Gaze.
Two hundred to adore each Breast:
But thirty thousand to the rest.
An Age at least to every part,
And the last Age should show your Heart.
For Lady you deserve this State;
Nor would I love at lower rate.

But at my back I alwaies hear
Times winged Charriot hurrying near:
And yonder all before us lye
Desarts of vast Eternity.
Thy Beauty shall no more be found;
Nor, in thy marble Vault, shall sound
My ecchoing Song: then Worms shall try
That long preserv'd Virginity:
And your quaint Honour turn to durst:
And into ashes all my Lust.
The Grave's a fine and private place,
But none I think do there embrace.

Now therefore, while the youthful hew
Sits on thy skin like morning grew,
And while thy willing Soul transpires
At every pore with instant Fires,
Now let us sport us while we may;
And now, like am'rous birds of prey,
Rather at once our Time dev out,
Than languish in his slow chapt pow'r.
Let us roll all our Strength, and all
Our sweetness, up into one Ball
And tear our Pleasures with rough strife,
Thorough the Iron gates of Life.
Thus, though we cannot make our Sun
Stand still, yet we will make him run.

Study notes by the editors

  1. title, date and argument title; ll. 1-2

    The poem was first printed in Miscellaneous Poems (1681), published after Marvell's death; its date of composition is unknown, though the early 1650s is often suggested. It belongs to the Latin carpe diem tradition (Horace, Odes 1.11; Catullus 5, 'Let us live, my Lesbia, and love'). Its three verse paragraphs follow a quasi-syllogism: if we had time (ll. 1-20), but we have not (ll. 21-32), therefore let us love now (ll. 33-46). Eliot, in his 1921 essay, defined Marvell's wit as 'a tough reasonableness' beneath lyric grace and quoted this poem repeatedly in support.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; T. S. Eliot, 'Andrew Marvell', Times Literary Supplement, 31 March 1921 (repr. Selected Essays, 1932); Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681)

  2. coyness l. 2

    From Old French coi (quiet, still), from Latin quietus. In the seventeenth century 'coy' meant modest or shy, often with a hint of coquettish reluctance. The 'crime' of l. 2 is a legal-ethical word that the speaker playfully applies to a courtship delay. The lady's coyness is presented as a social and rhetorical posture, which the speaker undertakes to argue away. The poem's opening concessive ('Had we but ...') grants her virtue full value, but only under impossible conditions.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coy'; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  3. Ganges and Humber ll. 5-7

    The Ganges evokes the wealth of India (rubies stand for the jewels of the East). The Humber is the estuary at Hull, where Marvell grew up and which he represented as MP from 1659. The comic disproportion places the lady among jewels and the lover by a muddy northern tide. 'Complain' means to compose love complaints, a lyric genre. The contrast of scale is part of the joke: the world is so large, and time so long, that the lovers could live on opposite sides of it. Hull's link with Marvell gives the line an autobiographical wink.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  4. complain l. 7

    The 'complaint' was an established genre in Renaissance poetry (e.g., Daniel's Complaint of Rosamond). The lover will sit by the Humber composing laments, a parody of Petrarchan patience that the poem's argument goes on to reject. Nigel Smith and other editors stress how the first paragraph parodies the patient lover of Petrarchan convention, who waits and laments for years. Placed beside the absurd scale of the courtship that follows, the word marks the lover's patience as a pose that the poem will drop.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'complain'; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  5. the Flood and the Conversion of the Jews ll. 8-10

    The Flood (Genesis 6-9) was dated in contemporary chronology to around 1656 years after Creation; the conversion of the Jews was expected just before the end of the world (based on Romans 11:25-26), a belief alive among millenarians of the 1650s. The lover would court for all of history and the lady would refuse until its close. The reference to the Jews' conversion had topical point in the 1650s, when millenarian expectation and debates about readmitting Jews to England were current. The hyperbole is precise: the lover would begin before the first great catastrophe and she could refuse until the last.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 7; Romans 11:25-26; Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  6. vegetable l. 11

    In Aristotelian psychology the soul had three faculties: vegetative (growth and nutrition, shared with plants), sensitive (animals) and rational (humans). A 'vegetable' love would grow imperceptibly but enormously, 'Vaster then Empires'. Readers have also found a comic, even phallic, undertone in the image of slow growth. 'Then' is the old spelling of 'than'. The adjective thus makes a philosophical joke: the lover pretends his passion could be as patient and unconscious as a growing tree, while the poem's later images reveal its animal energy.

    Aristotle, De Anima 2.2-4 (the nutritive soul); Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  7. the blazon ll. 13-18

    The blazon catalogues the lady's features (eyes, forehead, breasts, 'the rest') in conventional order, assigning absurd stretches of time to each. 'The rest' is a sly euphemism. 'The last Age should show your Heart': only at the end of time would she reveal her feelings. The lover claims she deserves this 'State' (dignity, ceremony) and he would not love at a 'lower rate' (price, valuation).

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem

  8. State; rate ll. 19-20

    These words close the first movement with mock-courtly compliment. 'State' (pomp, dignity) and 'rate' (cost, valuation) prepare for the shift to the economics of time in the second paragraph. The tone of extravagant compliment is sincere only in a conditional world. When the next paragraph begins 'But', these courtly measures are revealed as unaffordable: time, not the lady's merit, sets the true price. 'State' here carries the sense of dignity, ceremony or pomp suited to rank.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'state, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rate, n.'

  9. the turn l. 21

    'But' introduces the minor premise of the argument: we do not have world enough and time. The couplet is among the most quoted in English. The tetrameter couplets continue, but the tone darkens toward the grave. The phrase 'at my back' makes time a pursuer, felt rather than seen. The shift from the conditional mood of the first paragraph to the present indicative ('I alwaies hear') marks the move from fantasy to fact. Eliot quoted the passage in his 1921 essay to show how Marvell's wit can open suddenly onto large imaginative vistas.

    T. S. Eliot, 'Andrew Marvell', Times Literary Supplement, 31 March 1921 (repr. Selected Essays, 1932); H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem

  10. Time's winged chariot l. 22

    The image fuses the sun's chariot (Apollo or Phoebus driving across the sky) with Virgil's 'fugit inreparabile tempus' (Georgics 3.284) and emblematic images of winged Time. The lovers are figured as fugitives with the chariot always behind them. The present participle 'hurrying' creates a sense of continuous pursuit. The sound of the line is itself hurried, with 'hurrying' pressing on to the rhyme 'near'.

    Virgil, Georgics 3.284 ('sed fugit interea, fugit inreparabile tempus'); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  11. Deserts of vast Eternity l. 24

    'Desarts' is the period spelling of deserts, uninhabited wastes. The line reverses the first section: the time once imagined as abundant is now empty and barren. Commentators note the absence of any Christian heaven: eternity is a desolation, an effect that has drawn comment on the poem's secular or libertine stance. 'Yonder all before us' places eternity ahead of the lovers like a landscape stretching to the horizon; the spatial imagination of the first paragraph (Ganges, Humber, empires) returns, but emptied.

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem

  12. marble Vault l. 26

    The 'marble Vault' is a family tomb. The lover's 'ecchoing Song' will not sound there, and worms will 'try' (test, assay) her long-kept virginity, a grotesque image of violation by death. The macabre wit belongs to the memento mori tradition. The lady's chastity, imagined as long preserved, is mockingly given over to the worms. The lines rework a classical argument for love before death, found in classical epigrams that warn a reluctant girl that no lover will embrace her among the dead.

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem

  13. quaint Honour l. 29

    'Quaint' meant ingenious, elaborate, or fastidious; Chaucer's 'queynte' (Miller's Tale) also names the female sex, and most editors accept the pun. 'Honour' here is the lady's chastity. Note that the text in this archive reads 'turn to durst'; the 1681 Folio and standard editions read 'dust', and 'durst' looks like a transcription slip. 'Dust' and 'ashes' recall the burial service ('earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quaint'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  14. the grave ll. 31-32

    The couplet's laconic irony is often admired: 'fine' (elegant, exclusive, but also, perhaps, final, from Latin finis) and 'private' are the vocabulary of a lovers' assignation. 'I think' adds a dry, mock-tentative note. Eliot used the couplet to illustrate the alliance of levity and seriousness in Marvell's wit. The rhyme 'place' / 'embrace' pairs the private room of the lovers with the tomb, and the understatement is all the more chilling for its politeness.

    T. S. Eliot, 'Andrew Marvell', Times Literary Supplement, 31 March 1921 (repr. Selected Essays, 1932); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  15. conclusion l. 33

    The third paragraph draws the consequence, using the logician's 'therefore'. The repeated 'Now' (ll. 33, 37, 38) drives the rhetoric of urgency, in contrast to the leisured 'would' and 'should' of the opening. Critics have debated how strictly logical the poem is: some see a genuine syllogism, others a parody of logic used for seduction. Either way, 'therefore' marks the speaker's claim that his conclusion follows from the premises he has set out.

    H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  16. morning dew / glew l. 34

    This is the poem's best-known textual crux. The Folio prints 'glew'; an early annotated copy (Bodleian MS. Eng. poet. d. 49) corrects it to 'dew', and a manuscript variant (MS. Don b. 8) also reads 'dew'. The OED records 'glew' as an old spelling of 'glow' (and of 'glee'), and some editors defend the Folio reading on that basis; Elizabeth Story Donno's Penguin edition keeps 'glew'. 'Grew' in the archive text is probably a transcription error and has no early authority. Either 'dew' or 'glow' fits the warmth of the next couplet.

    Bodleian Libraries, The Conveyor blog, 'Poem, Amorous: Two Bodleian Marvell Manuscripts' (MS. Eng. poet. d. 49; MS. Don b. 8); Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Elizabeth Story Donno (ed.), Andrew Marvell: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1972)

  17. transpires l. 35

    'Transpire' is used in its literal sense, to pass out through the pores as vapour (Latin trans + spirare, to breathe). 'Instant' means pressing, urgent, or immediate. The image of the soul exhaling fire through the skin makes desire both physical and spiritual. The lady's body is imagined as a kind of furnace whose inner fire breathes out at every pore. The quasi-scientific vocabulary matches the precise, argumentative language of the rest of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'transpire'; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  18. amorous birds of prey l. 38

    The conventional doves of love poetry are replaced by birds of prey. 'Time dev out' in the archive text stands for 'devour' (Folio 'devour'). 'Slow chapt' means slow-jawed (chaps = jaws): Time is imagined as a devourer, the classical Cronos/Saturn eating his children (Ovid's 'tempus edax rerum', Metamorphoses 15.234). Editors note the striking violence of the image after the courtly decorum of the opening; the lover proposes to reverse the relation between lovers and time, making them the predators and time the prey.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To his Coy Mistress', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Miscellaneous Poems (1681); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.234 ('tempus edax rerum'); Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem

  19. one Ball ll. 41-42

    Editors offer several overlapping senses: a cannonball fired through iron gates (as in a siege), a pomander (a ball of sweet-smelling substances), and a perfect sphere symbolising union, perhaps recalling Plato's androgynes in the Symposium. The 'Iron gates of Life' have been read as the gates of a besieged city, of life itself, and, more bodily, of the womb or virginity. 'Thorough' is the older form of 'through'.

    Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem; Plato, Symposium 189c-193d (Aristophanes' speech)

  20. Sun stand still ll. 45-46

    In Joshua 10:12-13 the sun stood still at Gibeon; in Greek myth Zeus lengthened the night he spent with Alcmena. The lovers cannot command time like that, but their intensity will make the sun 'run', speeding up time instead of being its victims. Compare Donne's 'The Sun Rising', where the lover also defies the sun; the final couplet turns the poem's opening complaint of too little time into defiant energy.

    Bible (KJV), Joshua 10:12-13; Nigel Smith (ed.), The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes to this poem; H. M. Margoliouth (ed.), The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis with E. E. Duncan-Jones (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), commentary on this poem
