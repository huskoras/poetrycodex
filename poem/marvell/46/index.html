Nature

An Horation Ode upon Cromwel's Return from Ireland.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Nature

THE forward Youth that would appear
Must now forsake his Muses dear,
Nor in the Shadows sing
His Numbers languishing.
'Tis time to leave the Books in dust,
And oyl th' unused Armours rust:
Removing from the Wall
The Corslet of the Hall.
So restless Cromwel could not cease
In the inglorious Arts of Peace,
But through adventrous War
Urged his active Star.
And, like the three-fork'd Lightning, first
Breaking the Clouds where it was nurst,
Did through his own Side
His fiery way divide.
For 'tis all one to Courage high
The Emulous or Enemy;
And with such to inclose
Is more then to oppose.
Then burning through the Air he went,
And Pallaces and Temples rent:
And Caesars head at last
Did through his Laurels blast.
'Tis Madness to resist or blame
The force of angry Heavens flame:
And, if we would speak true,
Much to the Man is due.
Who, from his private Gardens, where
He liv'd reserved and austere,
As if his hightest plot
To plant the Bergamot,
Could by industrious Valour climbe
To ruine the great Work of Time,
And cast the Kingdome old
Into another Mold.
Though Justice against Fate complain,
And plead the antient Rights in vain:
But those do hold or break
As Men are strong or weak.
Nature that hateth emptiness,
Allows of penetration less:
And therefore must make room
Where greater Spirits come.
What Field of all the Civil Wars,
Where his were not the deepest Scars?
And Hampton shows what part
He had of wiser Art.
Where, twining subtile fears with hope,
He wove a Net of such a scope,
That Charles himself might chase
To Caresbrooks narrow case.
That thence the Royal Actor born
The Tragick Scaffold might adorn:
While round the armed Bands
Did clap their bloody hands.
He nothing common did or mean
Upon that memorable Scene:
But with his keener Eye
The Axes edge did try:
Nor call'd the Gods with vulgar spight
To vindicate his helpless Right,
But bow'd his comely Head,
Down as upon a Bed.
This was that memorable Hour
Which first assur'd the forced Pow'r.
So when they did design
The Capitols first Line,
A bleeding Head where they begun,
Did fright the Architects to run;
And yet in that the State
Foresaw it's happy Fate.
And now the Irish are asham'd
To see themselves in one Year tam'd:
So much one Man can do,
That does both act and know.
They can affirm his Praises best,
And have, though overcome, confest
How good he is, how just,
And fit for highest Trust:
Nor yet grown stiffer with Command,
But still in the Republick's hand:
How fit he is to sway
That can so well obey.
He to the Common Feet presents
A Kingdome, for his first years rents:
And, what he may, forbears
His Fame to make it theirs:
And has his Sword and Spoyls ungirt,
To lay them at the Publick's skirt.
So when the Falcon high
Falls heavy from the Sky,
She, having kill'd, no more does search,
But on the next green Bow to pearch;
Where, when he first does lure,
The Falckner has her sure.
What may not then our Isle presume
While Victory his Crest does plume!
What may not others fear
If thus he crown each Year!
A Caesar he ere long to Gaul,
To Italy an Hannibal,
And to all States not free
Shall Clymacterick be.
The Pict no shelter now shall find
Within his party-colour'd Mind;
But from this Valour sad
Shrink underneath the Plad:
Happy if in the tufted brake
The English Hunter him mistake;
Nor lay his Hounds in near
The Caledonian Deer.
But thou the Wars and Fortunes Son
March indefatigably on;
And for the last effect
Still keep thy Sword erect:
Besides the force it has to fright
The Spirits of the shady Night,
The same Arts that did gain
A Pow'r must it maintain.

Study notes by the editors

  1. An Horatian Ode: occasion, date, publication ll. 1-8

    Cromwell returned to London from his Irish campaign at the end of May 1650 and in July led the army into Scotland, where the Scots had proclaimed Charles II. The poem looks back to the regicide of January 1649 and forward to the Scottish war, so it is dated June-July 1650. It was printed in Marvell's Miscellaneous Poems (1681) but cancelled in all but one or two surviving copies, presumably because praise of Cromwell was dangerous after the Restoration; it reached most readers only in Thompson's edition (1776). The archive's title keeps the old spellings 'Horation' and 'Cromwel'.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.; Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts (Folger Shakespeare Library), author entry for Andrew Marvell, on the cancelled Cromwell poems in Miscellaneous Poems (1681); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'An Horatian Ode', notes

  2. The Horatian stanza ll. 1-4

    Each stanza pairs a couplet of four-stress lines with a shorter couplet of three-stress lines, producing a clipped, falling cadence that is an English stanza, not a Latin metre, but it aims at the compression of Horace's lyric stanzas. Horace's political odes, praising Augustus after the Roman civil wars (Odes 1.2, 1.37, 3.1-6, 4.4-5), supply the model of a poem that celebrates a strongman while remembering the defeated: Odes 1.37 honours Cleopatra's noble death as Marvell honours Charles's. The opening stanzas urge the ambitious young man to leave poetry for action.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Horace, Odes 1.37

  3. The forward Youth l. 1

    'Forward' means eager, ambitious, even presumptuous. 'Appear' means to make a public showing. Such a youth must leave 'the Shadows' (the shade of retirement, also the poet's pastoral shade) and his 'Numbers languishing' (love verses). The armour 'of the Hall' was the family armour hung up on the walls of a country house; 'Corslet' is body armour. Many critics see Marvell, then about twenty-nine and without employment, addressing his own situation; others think of young royalists or the generation called to arms.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'forward'; Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987)

  4. his active Star ll. 9-12

    The verb 'urged' makes Cromwell the master of his destiny rather than its servant, a notable ambiguity: is he driven by fate or driving it? 'Inglorious Arts of Peace' echoes Virgil's 'ignobilis oti' (Georgics 4.564), Virgil's description of his own leisure as a poet while Octavian fought abroad; Marvell reverses the Virgilian contrast, since the poet's leisure is now inglorious. The question of whether Cromwell is the instrument of providence or his own ambition recurs throughout the poem.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; John M. Wallace, Destiny His Choice: The Loyalism of Andrew Marvell (1968), ch. 2; Virgil, Georgics 4.559-66

  5. three-fork'd Lightning ll. 13-24

    Lucan, Pharsalia 1.151-57, compares Caesar to lightning forced out by the winds through the clouds, which strikes its own temples and scatters destruction where it falls and returns. Marvell's lightning likewise first breaks through 'his own Side', the cloud that nursed it, then burns through palaces and temples. Jove's three-forked bolt (trisulcum fulmen) is a common classical image. Lucan's poem, a lament for the Roman republic destroyed by Caesar, had been translated by Thomas May (1627), so the source lends the praise an unsettling resonance.

    Lucan, Pharsalia (De bello civili) 1.151-57; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.; R. H. Syfret, 'Marvell's "Horatian Ode"', Review of English Studies n.s. 12 (1961)

  6. through his own Side / The Emulous or Enemy ll. 15-20

    Cromwell displaced or outmanoeuvred Parliamentarian commanders such as the Earl of Manchester and the Earl of Essex (the Self-Denying Ordinance, 1645), and in 1649 crushed the Leveller mutiny. 'Emulous' means rival, ambitious competitor. For 'Courage high' an emulous ally and an enemy are the same thing, and 'to inclose', to confine such a man within limits, provokes more than outright opposition. The lightning image of the cloud split from within fits this exactly.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987)

  7. Caesar's head blasted through his Laurels ll. 23-24

    'Caesars head' is Charles I's, the royal 'Caesar'. Ancient lore held that laurel is never struck by lightning; Suetonius reports that Tiberius wore a laurel wreath in thunderstorms for that reason (Tiberius 69), and Pliny records the belief (Natural History 15.134-35). Marvell's lightning penetrates even the protective laurel, the emblem of victory and of royal majesty. The ambiguity is sharp: in Lucan Cromwell's model is Caesar, but here Charles is 'Caesar', so the poem gives the name to both.

    Suetonius, Tiberius 69; Pliny, Natural History 15.134-35; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  8. private Gardens ... the Bergamot ll. 29-32

    Cromwell was a modest country gentleman in Huntingdon, St Ives and Ely before entering national politics in the 1640s. The bergamot was a fine variety of pear; its name was popularly derived from Turkish beg-armudi, 'the lord's (prince's) pear', which allows a pun: his 'plot' (garden plot and scheme) was to plant a royal pear, that is, perhaps to grow into kingship. Critics differ on whether 'As if' exonerates him or hints at hidden ambition. The retired gardener who saves the state recalls the Roman Cincinnatus.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bergamot'; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947)

  9. the great Work of Time ll. 33-36

    'Ruine' is a verb: to destroy. The kingdom is a building (the 'great Work of Time') and then metal recast 'Into another Mold', a metaphor from casting or minting. The admiring and the appalled readings are both available: Brooks heard irony and regret here, while Bush insisted the praise of Cromwell is straightforward. The phrase 'Kingdome old' (l. 35) stresses antiquity: what is destroyed is not only Charles but centuries of constitutional history, which a single man undoes by labour and courage.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Douglas Bush, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', Sewanee Review 60 (1952); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.

  10. Justice against Fate; antient Rights ll. 37-40

    The lines state a de facto view of political authority: rights 'hold or break / As Men are strong or weak'. John Wallace related the poem to the Engagement controversy of 1649-50, in which writers argued that obedience was owed to whatever power actually governed. The stanza can be read as cold realism, as Hobbesian (Leviathan appeared in 1651), or as bitter irony; the legal language ('plead', 'antient Rights') remembers the constitutional case against Cromwell even as it is overruled.

    John M. Wallace, Destiny His Choice: The Loyalism of Andrew Marvell (1968), ch. 2; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947)

  11. Nature hates emptiness, allows less penetration ll. 41-44

    The Aristotelian doctrine that there can be no void (horror vacui; Physics 4.6-9) is paired with the impenetrability of bodies: nature 'allows of penetration less', permits two things in one space even less than it tolerates empty space. So when a 'greater Spirit' comes, something must give way. The political vacuum left by the old order will be filled, and Cromwell's spirit physically displaces the King's. The argument dresses political necessity as physical law.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.; Aristotle, Physics 4.6-9

  12. Hampton Court and Carisbrooke ll. 47-52

    Charles fled Hampton Court on 11 November 1647 and gave himself up on the Isle of Wight, where he was held in Carisbrooke Castle. Royalists and others alleged that Cromwell had frightened him into flight with rumours of assassination so as to get him into a closer prison; Marvell adopts the rumour, praising Cromwell's 'wiser Art' of 'twining subtile fears with hope'. 'Case' means enclosure, cage, with a hunting overtone ('chase'). Historians do not accept that Cromwell planned the flight.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  13. The Royal Actor and the Tragick Scaffold ll. 53-56

    Charles was beheaded on a scaffold built outside the Banqueting House, Whitehall. The theatrical metaphor is double-edged: 'Actor' means both performer and agent; 'adorn' makes the King an ornament to the stage; the soldiers' 'bloody hands' clapping make the audience brutal. The 'Scene' (l. 58) continues the metaphor. Royalist propaganda, especially Eikon Basilike (1649), presented Charles as a martyr, and Milton's Eikonoklastes attacked that theatre of sympathy; Marvell gives the King a dignified exit while keeping the stage frame.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987)

  14. He nothing common did or mean ll. 57-64

    Contemporary accounts of the execution record the King's composure and his concern with the block and the axe. 'Keener Eye' makes his glance sharper than the blade. He did not call the gods to 'vindicate' (avenge, defend) his 'helpless Right' with 'vulgar spight', and bowed his 'comely Head' as if to sleep. These eight lines, at the exact centre of the poem, are its emotional climax, and critics are divided over whether they undercut the praise of Cromwell or display Marvell's even-handed Horatian magnanimity toward the vanquished.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Douglas Bush, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', Sewanee Review 60 (1952); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.

  15. The bleeding Head on the Capitol ll. 67-72

    Livy (1.55) tells that while Tarquin was clearing the Capitoline hill for the temple of Jupiter, a human head with its face intact was uncovered, which seers read as a sign that the place would be the head (caput) of an empire; Pliny repeats the story. Marvell adds the blood. The King's severed head becomes the founding omen of the new republic, 'the forced Pow'r' assured by that 'memorable Hour'. The pun on caput/Capitol and the blood make the omen grimly ambiguous: the new state is founded on a killing.

    Livy, Ab urbe condita 1.55.5-6; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  16. The Irish 'tamed' ll. 73-80

    Cromwell landed at Dublin in August 1649, stormed Drogheda (September) and Wexford (October) with the killing of garrisons and many civilians, and returned in May 1650 leaving the conquest to others. The claim that the Irish themselves 'confest / How good he is, how just' has been read as straightforward English Protestant triumphalism, as conventional panegyric, or as savage irony. Critics including Brooks emphasised the irony; historians note that English opinion widely celebrated the campaign.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987)

  17. How fit he is to sway / That can so well obey ll. 81-84

    Aristotle's Politics (3.4, 1277b) says that a good citizen must know both how to rule and how to be ruled, and the maxim was a commonplace. In 1650 Cromwell was still a general serving Parliament ('still in the Republick's hand'). 'Sway' means rule. The stanza both praises his obedience and implies his fitness to rule, so that the compliment anticipates the future Protectorate (1653).

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Aristotle, Politics 3.4, 1277b; John M. Wallace, Destiny His Choice: The Loyalism of Andrew Marvell (1968), ch. 2

  18. the Common Feet ... the Publick's skirt ll. 85-90

    'The Common' plays on the Commons and the Commonwealth. Ireland is presented as 'his first years rents', the first year's yield paid by a tenant to the landlord. He 'forbears' (refrains from claiming) his fame, and lays sword and spoils at the 'Publick's skirt', the hem of the personified Republic's garment, a gesture of homage. The feudal language of rents and homage oddly reasserts hierarchy within a republic.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  19. the Falcon and the Falconer ll. 91-96

    In falconry the hawk stoops on its prey, kills, and is recalled by the falconer swinging the 'lure', a bunch of feathers on a cord. Marvell's falcon does not 'search' for more prey but perches on the next 'green Bow' (bough), where the falconer 'has her sure'. The simile praises Cromwell's obedience but also implies the danger: a falcon is a predator, controlled only so long as it consents.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lure'

  20. Caesar, Hannibal, Clymacterick ll. 101-104

    Julius Caesar conquered Gaul (58-50 BC), and Hannibal invaded Italy (218 BC); the prophecy imagines Cromwell carrying the Protestant republic's war to Catholic France and Rome. A 'climacteric' was a critical year in a person's life (every seventh or ninth year, the 63rd being the 'grand climacteric'), when great change or death was expected; Cromwell will be such a turning point for monarchies. The name 'Caesar', earlier applied to Charles, is now transferred to Cromwell.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'climacteric'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  21. The Pict ... party-colour'd Mind ... Plad ll. 105-112

    The Romans named the northern tribes Picti, 'painted men', from their body paint. Marvell puns: the Scots' minds are 'party-colour'd', variegated like tartan and divided by party, since the Covenanters had allied with Charles II while still opposing royalists. They will shrink under the 'Plad' (plaid) and hide in the bracken like deer, hoping the English hunter mistakes them for game. Cromwell's invasion began on 22 July 1650 and he defeated the Scots at Dunbar on 3 September.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Pict'

  22. the Wars and Fortunes Son l. 113

    Horace, Satires 2.6.49, describes the envious crowd calling a lucky man 'Fortunae filius', 'Fortune's son'. The phrase stresses luck rather than merit, and fortune is notoriously fickle. 'March indefatigably on' turns the stanza from description to exhortation, almost a soldier's command; the long Latinate adverb fills most of the line and enacts unwearied marching. Critics who read the Ode ironically point to this phrase as a reminder that Cromwell's power rests on chance and war rather than law.

    Horace, Satires 2.6.49; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.

  23. Still keep thy Sword erect ll. 115-118

    An upturned sword hilt forms a cross, believed to ward off evil spirits. Classical heroes draw swords against the shades: Odysseus keeps the dead off with his sword (Odyssey 11.48-50), and the Sibyl orders Aeneas to draw his blade on entering the underworld (Aeneid 6.260). The 'Spirits of the shady Night' may be the ghost of the King and the disaffected spirits of royalism. 'For the last effect' means to the final outcome.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.260; Homer, Odyssey 11.48-50; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell, 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones (1971), vol. 1, commentary ad loc.

  24. The same Arts that did gain / A Pow'r ll. 119-120

    Sallust, Catiline 2.4, says that power is easily kept by the arts by which it was first won ('imperium facile iis artibus retinetur, quibus initio partum est'). The maxim was also associated with Machiavelli's Prince. Applied to Cromwell, whose power was won by the sword, the closing couplet can be read as realistic advice, as approving, or as a warning that a regime founded on force must forever rely on force.

    Sallust, Bellum Catilinae 2.4; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, rev. edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), note ad loc.; Joseph A. Mazzeo, 'Cromwell as Machiavellian Prince in Marvell's "An Horatian Ode"', Journal of the History of Ideas 21 (1960)

  25. Praise or irony? The critical debate ll. 25-28

    'And, if we would speak true, / Much to the Man is due' sounds like a concession wrung from a reluctant speaker. Brooks's close reading (1946) found a poised irony, with admiration for Cromwell's force balanced by sympathy for Charles. Bush (1952) replied that such irony is a modern projection and that a 1650 reader would take the praise straight. Later historicist work (Wallace 1968; Worden 1987) set the poem in the debates over allegiance to the new regime. 'Tis Madness to resist or blame' (l. 25) makes resistance to Cromwell resistance to heaven's lightning.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', English Institute Essays 1946 (1947); Douglas Bush, 'Marvell's Horatian Ode', Sewanee Review 60 (1952); John M. Wallace, Destiny His Choice: The Loyalism of Andrew Marvell (1968), ch. 2; Blair Worden, 'Andrew Marvell, Oliver Cromwell, and the Horatian Ode', in Kevin Sharpe and Steven N. Zwicker, eds, Politics of Discourse (1987)
