Nature

The Mower against Gardens.

by Andrew Marvell 1621–1678

Nature

LUxurious Man, to bring his Vice in use,
Did after him the World seduce:
And from the fields the Flow'rs and Plants allure,
Where Nature was most plain and pure.
He first enclos'd within the Gardens square
A dead and standing pool of Air:
And a more luscious Earth for them did knead,
Which stupifi'd them while it fed.
The Pink grew then as double as his Mind;
The nutriment did change the kind.
With strange perfumes he did the Roses taint.
And Flow'rs themselves were taught to paint.
The Tulip, white, did for complexion seek;
And learn'd to interline its cheek:
Its Onion root they then so high did hold,
That one was for a Meadow sold.
Another World was search'd, through Oceans new.
To find the Marvel of Peru.
And yet these Rarities might be allow'd,
To Man, that sov'raign thing and proud;
Had he not dealt between the Bark and Tree,
Forbidden mixtures there to see.
No Plant now knew the Stock from which it came;
He grafts upon the Wild the Tame:
That the uncertain and adult rate fruit
Might put the Palate in dispute.
His green Seraglio has its Eunuchs too;
Lest any Tyrant him out-doe.
And in the Cherry he does Nature vex,
To procreate without a Sex.
'Tis all enforc'd; the Fountain and the Grot;
While the sweet Fields do lye forgot:
Where willing Nature does to all dispence
A wild and fragrant Innocence:
And Fauns and Faryes do the Meadows till,
More by their presence then their skill.
Their Statues polish'd by some ancient hand,
May to adorn the Gardens stand:
But howso'ere the Figures do excel,
The Gods themselves with us do dwell.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and the Mower sequence l. 1

    The poem appeared in Marvell's Miscellaneous Poems (1681), issued after his death; the capitals 'LU-' reproduce the printed drop-capital opening. It is the first of four Mower poems, followed by 'Damon the Mower', 'The Mower to the Glo-Worms' and 'The Mower's Song'. Here the Mower attacks the fashionable gardening of the mid-seventeenth century, with its exotic imports, breeding and grafting. Critics debate how far Marvell shares his view: the Mower's speech is itself highly artful, and he too cuts down the meadows he praises. Rosalie Colie and others read the poem as a deliberately partial voice in a debate about art and nature.

    Representative Poetry Online, Marvell, 'The Mower against Gardens', notes (text from Miscellaneous Poems, 1681); David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  2. Luxurious l. 1

    The primary early modern sense of 'luxurious' is lascivious, as in Macbeth's list of kingly vices ('luxurious, avaricious', 4.3); the sense of rich comfort was developing alongside. Marvell exploits both: man's vice is the pursuit of pleasure, and the following lines treat gardening in sexual terms (seduction, painting, adulterous mixtures, a seraglio). 'To bring his Vice in use' means to make his vice customary, to put it into practice.

    Representative Poetry Online, Marvell, 'The Mower against Gardens', notes (text from Miscellaneous Poems, 1681); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem

  3. seduce ... allure ll. 2-3

    'Seduce' (lead astray) and 'allure' (entice) cast man as a Satan figure who corrupts innocent nature, drawing flowers from the 'plain and pure' fields into the garden as the serpent drew Eve. The Fall story of Genesis 3 is reworked: originally a garden was the place of innocence and the fall came into it; here the garden is itself the fallen place and the open field is Eden. Barratt notes the Satan-seduction motif as a link with Paradise Lost (published later, 1667).

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3; David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  4. enclos'd within the Gardens square l. 5

    Seventeenth-century English gardens were typically square or rectangular plots, enclosed by walls or hedges and laid out in knots and beds. The word 'enclos'd' also carries social overtones: enclosure of common land for private use was a bitter grievance in rural England, and the Mower speaks for open, common fields. Behind the image lies the hortus conclusus, the 'garden enclosed' of the Song of Songs (4:12), traditionally a figure of purity; Marvell inverts it into a place of corruption.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 4:12; Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems

  5. A dead and standing pool of Air l. 6

    Gardeners valued walled gardens because they kept off wind and held warmth; the Mower sees the result as stagnant, unhealthy air, like the 'standing' (still, unmoving) water of a pond. The metaphor of air as a pool is characteristic of Marvell's way of making the invisible tangible. In seventeenth-century medical thinking stagnant air and water were thought to breed disease, which supports the sense of corruption.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem

  6. a more luscious Earth ... knead ll. 7-8

    Gardeners prepared beds with manure, compost and specially mixed soils. 'Knead' makes the gardener a baker working dough. 'Luscious' means over-rich, cloying. 'Stupifi'd' means drugged, made dull or senseless: the plants are overfed into stupor, as luxury dulls human beings. Coughlan's thesis on Marvell's classical themes argues for the pervasive influence of Pliny's Natural History on the poem; Pliny repeatedly attacks the luxury of Roman gardening and imported plants.

    Patricia Anne Coughlan, 'Classical Themes in the Non-Satiric Poetry of Andrew Marvell' (PhD thesis, Queen Mary College, University of London), on Pliny's Natural History in the poem; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  7. The Pink grew then as double as his Mind l. 9

    The pink (Dianthus) and its larger cousin the carnation or gillyflower were favourite florists' flowers, bred into 'double' forms with extra petals. Such forms are often sterile, which the poem later exploits. The pun is the point: the flower becomes 'double' as man's mind is double, false and two-faced. 'The nutriment did change the kind' (l. 10) means the rich feeding changed the species ('kind').

    Representative Poetry Online, Marvell, 'The Mower against Gardens', notes (text from Miscellaneous Poems, 1681); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  8. taught to paint ll. 11-12

    'Taint' means to tinge or to corrupt; the 'strange perfumes' may refer to attempts to scent flowers artificially. 'Paint' is the usual period word for applying make-up, condemned by moralists as deceit. Marvell aligns horticultural improvement with feminine vanity and sexual allure. The same analogy appears in Perdita's speech in The Winter's Tale (4.4), where she refuses to plant striped gillyflowers, saying she would no more do so than wish to be desired only because she was painted.

    Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale 4.4 (MIT Complete Works); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  9. The Tulip ... interline its cheek ll. 13-14

    The most prized tulips of the period were 'broken' ones, whose plain petals were feathered or flamed with streaks of another colour. The cause, a virus spread by aphids, was unknown; it was attributed to soils and treatments. Marvell's tulip, seeking 'complexion' (a coloured skin), learns to 'interline' (draw lines between, as in a manuscript, or paint blue veins on) its cheek, a cosmetic practice of the time. The image continues the analogy between the cultivated flower and the made-up woman.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem

  10. one was for a Meadow sold ll. 15-16

    In the Netherlands, speculation in tulip bulbs peaked in the winter of 1636-37, with rare varieties reportedly exchanged for houses, land and goods, before the market collapsed in February 1637. For the Mower, the absurdity is that an onion-like root ('Onion root') is valued above a meadow, the very field he works; the cultivated rarity is preferred to the useful common ground. Barratt reads the prices as evidence of the luxury the Mower condemns.

    Representative Poetry Online, Marvell, 'The Mower against Gardens', notes (text from Miscellaneous Poems, 1681); David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Anne Goldgar, Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age (Chicago, 2007)

  11. the Marvel of Peru l. 18

    Following Margoliouth, editors identify the plant as Mirabilis jalapa, called 'Mirabilia Peruviana' in John Parkinson's herbal of 1629 (Paradisi in Sole Paradisus Terrestris). It is the 'four o'clock' flower, whose blooms open in late afternoon and which can bear flowers of different colours on one plant. Its name allows Marvell a hidden signature: 'Marvel' is his own name. The couplet mocks the global search for exotic novelties: a whole New World ('Another World', 'Oceans new') has been ransacked for a garden flower.

    Representative Poetry Online, Marvell, 'The Mower against Gardens', notes (text from Miscellaneous Poems, 1681); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem; John Parkinson, Paradisi in Sole Paradisus Terrestris (London, 1629)

  12. Man, that sov'raign thing and proud l. 20

    The concession of ll. 19-20 grants that man, given 'dominion' over the earth in Genesis 1:28, may be allowed his exotic collections. The sarcasm is clear: 'sov'raign' and 'proud' are near synonyms of tyranny, preparing for the 'Tyrant' of l. 28. The poem turns at this point from luxury (exotics, prices) to what it presents as a graver offence, the tampering with generation itself through grafting.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:28; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  13. dealt between the Bark and Tree l. 21

    The proverb 'put not your hand between the bark and the tree' warned against interfering in intimate, especially marital, affairs; it is recorded in John Heywood's proverb collections. Marvell makes it literal: in grafting, the gardener slits the bark and inserts a scion beneath it, intruding into the tree's union with itself. The marital meaning colours the whole passage: grafting becomes an adulterous coupling of different kinds.

    John Heywood, Proverbs and Epigrams (1562); discussion at The Phrase Finder; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem

  14. Forbidden mixtures l. 22

    Leviticus 19:19 forbids breeding cattle 'with a diverse kind' and sowing a field 'with mingled seed', and Deuteronomy 22:9 forbids sowing a vineyard with 'divers seeds'. The Mower presents the gardener's hybrids as such forbidden unions. 'There to see' suggests voyeurism: man wants to watch the mixtures he has arranged. Barratt observes that the poem likens grafting to incest and miscegenation.

    Bible (KJV), Leviticus 19:19; Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 22:9; David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  15. He grafts upon the Wild the Tame l. 24

    In grafting, a shoot of a cultivated variety (the 'tame') is set on a hardy wild rootstock (the 'stock'). 'Stock' is also a family line, so 'No Plant now knew the Stock from which it came' means that plants no longer know their parentage, like children of uncertain fathers. The chiastic line places wild and tame side by side as the graft does.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale 4.4 (MIT Complete Works)

  16. Grafting and the art-nature debate ll. 23-24

    The poem joins a long Renaissance debate on whether art corrupts or perfects nature. In The Winter's Tale 4.4 Perdita refuses 'streak'd gillyvors', which some call 'nature's bastards', while Polixenes defends grafting: 'we marry / A gentler scion to the wildest stock', and the art that does so is itself nature. Marvell's Mower takes Perdita's side with far more vehemence. Whether Marvell had the scene in mind is not established, but the parallel shows the conventional terms of the argument, which Marvell's poem dramatises from one partisan point of view.

    Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale 4.4 (MIT Complete Works); Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems

  17. uncertain and adulterate fruit l. 25

    Early printings read 'adult'rate' (adulterate), a word contracted from 'adulterate'. The archive text divides it as 'adult rate'; the sense is 'adulterate'. The word means debased by mixture (like adulterated wine) and also born of adultery, hence 'uncertain' parentage. Such fruit 'puts the Palate in dispute': the taste cannot decide what it is eating, because the fruit is neither one kind nor another. The pun extends the marital imagery of 'between the Bark and Tree'.

    H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  18. His green Seraglio has its Eunuchs too ll. 27-28

    The seraglio was the palace or women's quarters of the Ottoman sultan, known to Europeans through travellers' accounts of the Topkapı Palace in Istanbul, where the harem was guarded by eunuchs. In the period the word often meant the harem itself. Marvell's gardener is a sultan with a 'green' harem of captive flowers, and the plants bred to sterility (double flowers that bear no seed, grafted trees that cannot breed true) are his eunuchs. 'Lest any Tyrant him out-doe': he rivals oriental despots in his sexual tyranny, recalling the 'sov'raign thing and proud' of l. 20.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  19. To procreate without a Sex ll. 29-30

    Two main readings exist. One takes the reference to a cherry without a stone, which gardening lore claimed could be produced by manipulation; since 'stones' was slang for testicles, the stoneless cherry is a eunuch fruit, continuing the seraglio image. The other reads it as propagation by grafting or budding, which reproduces the tree without seed, i.e. without sexual reproduction. Barratt records both explanations. 'Vex' means to harass or torment: the gardener persecutes Nature to force her out of her course.

    David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., The Poems and Letters of Andrew Marvell (Oxford, 1927; 3rd edn rev. Pierre Legouis and E. E. Duncan-Jones, 1971), commentary on the poem; Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  20. the Fountain and the Grot l. 31

    Grand gardens of the early seventeenth century imitated Italian models with waterworks, fountains and 'grots' or grottoes, artificial caves often decorated with shells and statues and fitted with water tricks. 'Tis all enforc'd' means everything is forced, artificial and constrained: the water is forced into jets, the cave forced from rock. The Mower opposes this to the 'sweet Fields' that lie neglected.

    Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  21. willing Nature ... wild and fragrant Innocence ll. 33-34

    The 'willing' Nature of the fields recalls the Golden Age of Ovid's Metamorphoses (1.101-102), when the earth gave everything of its own accord, untouched by hoe or ploughshare. 'To all' contrasts with the private, enclosed garden: nature's bounty is common. 'Innocence' sets the fields as the true Eden against the corrupt garden. The adjective 'wild' is here a term of praise, reversing its use in 'He grafts upon the Wild the Tame'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.89-112, Latin text, The Latin Library; Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems

  22. Fauns and Faryes l. 35

    Fauns are the Roman rustic deities associated with Faunus, half-goat spirits of the countryside; fairies belong to native English folklore. Marvell mixes the two traditions to suggest that the fields are still alive with divine presence. 'More by their presence then their skill' ('then' is the period spelling of 'than'): the meadows flourish because the spirits are there, not through any art. The line answers the gardener's skill with a kind of grace.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems

  23. Their Statues polish'd by some ancient hand l. 37

    Wealthy collectors of the period, following Italian fashion, placed antique or imitation-antique statues of gods and nymphs in their gardens. The Mower allows ('May to adorn the Gardens stand') that these statues may be excellent works of art. His point is that they are only images: the real gods are present in the meadows. Barratt reads the fauns and fairies as nature spirits reduced in the garden to ornament.

    David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes

  24. The Gods themselves with us do dwell l. 40

    The closing line sets presence against representation: gardens have figures, fields have gods. 'With us' places the Mower among the rustic company of divinities. The line may echo the language of the Incarnation (God dwelling with men, John 1:14), giving the field a sacred status. Critics note an irony that the poem does not resolve: the Mower, who denounces man's violence against nature, is himself the man with the scythe, and later Mower poems show him cutting down the grass and himself. Whether the poem endorses his primitivism or exposes it remains open.

    Bible (KJV), John 1:14; Rosalie L. Colie, 'My Ecchoing Song': Andrew Marvell's Poetry of Criticism (Princeton, 1970), chapter on the Mower poems; David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  25. Form: alternating couplets ll. 1-40

    Each couplet pairs a ten-syllable line with an eight-syllable line. The shorter line often lands like a sardonic punch ('Which stupifi'd them while it fed'). Although the poem is printed without breaks, it falls into grouped units: a description of man's luxury, the passage on grafting, and a contrasting picture of the fields, closed by a four-line conclusion. The steady form suits a speaker who is building a prosecution case.

    David Barratt, 'The Mower, Against Gardens' by Andrew Marvell, EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Nigel Smith, ed., The Poems of Andrew Marvell, Longman Annotated English Poets (2003; rev. 2007), headnote and notes
