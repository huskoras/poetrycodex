Living

Kitabe-i Seng-i Mezar

by Orhan Veli Kanık 1914–1950

Living

I

Hiçbir şeyden çekmedi dünyada
Nasırdan çektiği kadar;
Hattâ çirkin yaratıldığından bile
O kadar müteessir değildi.
Kundurası vurmadığı zamanlarda
Anmazdı ama Allahın adını,
Günahkâr da sayılmazdı.

Yazık oldu Süleyman Efendi’ye.

II

Mesele falan değildi öyle,
To be or not to be kendisi için;
Bir akşam uyudu;
Uyanmayıverdi.
Aldılar, götürdüler.
Yıkandı, namazı kılındı, gömüldü.
Duyarlarsa öldüğünü alacaklılar
Haklarını helâl ederler elbet.
Alacağına gelince...
Alacağı yoktu zaten rahmetlinin.

III

Tüfeğini deppoya koydular,
Esvabını başkasına verdiler.
Artık ne torbasında ekmek kırıntısı,
Ne matrasında dudaklarının izi;
Öyle bir rûzigâr ki,
Kendi gitti,
İsmi bile kalmadı yadigâr.
Yalnız şu beyit kaldı,
Kahve ocağında, el yazısıyla:
"Ölüm Allahın emri,
Ayrılık olmasaydı."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Kitâbe-i Seng-i Mezar ('Inscription on a Gravestone') title

    Kitâbe is Arabic kitāba, an inscription; seng is Persian sang, stone; mezar is Arabic mazār, a place visited, a tomb. Joined by the Persian izafet (-i), they form a learned phrase of the kind carved on Ottoman tombstones and used in divan poetry. Orhan Veli, whose Garip preface (1941) rejected poetic diction, metre and rhyme, uses the grand title ironically for epitaphs of a man whose great suffering was a corn on his foot. The epitaph is also an old literary genre, from Greek epigrams to Ottoman chronograms on tombs, which the poem parodies by giving it flat, conversational language.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  2. Three epitaphs sections I–III

    Orhan Veli wrote more than one poem under this title, and collected editions group them. Section I is the epitaph for Süleyman Efendi that caused a scandal on the appearance of Garip (1941); section II continues with his sudden death and funeral; section III is an epitaph for an anonymous soldier whose rifle and clothes are passed on. The parts close on short, plain lines, the last with a note of pathos. The sequence shows the Garip programme in action: free verse, no rhyme or regular metre, everyday vocabulary, and a hero who is no hero at all.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü); Habertürk, 'Orhan Veli Kanık kimdir?' (reception of 'Kitâbe-i Seng-i Mezar')

  3. nasır (a corn on the foot) l. 2

    Nasır is a corn or callus. The idea that a man's greatest suffering was a corn on his foot shocked many literary readers when Garip appeared in 1941, and the Süleyman Efendi lines were the most discussed in the book. The point is serious: the poem refuses heroic or tragic subject-matter and claims that a small, real pain is a fit subject for verse. The phrasing 'çekmek' (to suffer, to endure) belongs to everyday speech ('çok çektim').

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  4. müteessir (grieved, affected) l. 4

    Müteessir is the Arabic participle mutaʾaththir, 'affected, moved, grieved'. It belongs to official and educated speech, and its use in a sentence about ugliness and corns produces a small comic effect, like the Ottoman title. 'Çirkin yaratıldığından' (from having been created ugly) quietly brings God in as creator, preparing the ironic remarks on piety in the following lines. The understatement 'o kadar... değildi' (was not so much...) is typical of the poem's deadpan tone.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  5. kundura vurmak (a shoe pinching) l. 5

    Kundura is an everyday word for a shoe (especially a heavier leather shoe); a shoe that 'vurur' rubs and hurts the foot, the cause of the corn. The sentence structure is pointed: only when his shoes hurt did he utter the name of God, whether in complaint, in prayer or in oath. The line thus mocks a piety that appears only in discomfort, while staying affectionate towards the man.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  6. 'He did not mention the name of God' ll. 6–7

    Readers take 'anmak' (to mention, invoke) in two ways: either he never prayed except in pain, or he swore by God only when his corn hurt. The next line, 'Günahkâr da sayılmazdı' (nor was he considered a sinner), weighs his moral account as a gravestone might, and finds nothing either way. The irony lies in applying the language of religious reckoning, sin and the name of God, to a life whose only drama was a sore foot. The poem does not mock religion; it mocks the epitaph's habit of moral summary.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  7. 'Yazık oldu Süleyman Efendi'ye' ('What a pity about Süleyman Efendi') l. 8

    Efendi (from Byzantine Greek afentis, 'master', from authentēs) was by the twentieth century a polite title for an ordinary literate man, a clerk, shopkeeper or minor official, below 'Bey' and 'Paşa'. 'Yazık oldu' (it's a pity; what a waste) is everyday condolence. The line became one of the most discussed lines of Garip. The TDV article on Orhan Veli says it drew attention because it showed that an ordinary person can also be worth a poem; some critics at the time said it was borrowed. A recent Diken article discusses a poem of the same title by his friend Rıza Çavdarlı, which Orhan Veli quoted in 1947, but the order of composition is not established and the article treats the episode as friendship, not theft.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Habertürk, 'Orhan Veli Kanık kimdir?' (reception of 'Kitâbe-i Seng-i Mezar'); C. Hakkı Zariç, 'İki şiir, dostluk ve can sıkıntısı', Diken, 20 July 2025; Sevan Nişanyan, Nişanyan Sözlük: Çağdaş Türkçenin Etimolojisi (online)

  8. 'To be or not to be' sect. II, l. 2

    The English quotation is Hamlet's opening of his soliloquy in Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1.64 in the Folger text (3.1.56 in the Globe numbering), the most famous meditation on whether to live or die. Orhan Veli inserts it, untranslated, into colloquial Turkish ('Mesele falan değildi öyle': it was no big issue or anything for him) and so contrasts tragic philosophy with an anonymous death. The foreign phrase also mocks the intellectual pose of poetry. The joke is compressed into the next two lines, where death arrives without any question at all.

    Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1.64 (Folger Shakespeare Library text); Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  9. uyanmayıverdi ('just didn't wake up') sect. II, l. 4

    Turkish -(y)ıver- added to a verb expresses quickness, ease or suddenness ('yazıver', write it quickly). Attached to the negative 'uyanma-' (not wake), it produces an almost comic word: he just went and didn't wake up. The single-word line enacts the event's lack of drama and answers the Hamlet question with a shrug. It is one of Orhan Veli's best examples of making poetry out of grammar rather than ornament.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  10. The Muslim funeral in three verbs sect. II, l. 6

    Muslim burial follows set steps: the ritual washing of the corpse (gasl), wrapping in a shroud, the funeral prayer (cenaze namazı) said standing in the mosque courtyard, and burial, usually on the same day. The line lists them in impersonal passive verbs, as in the preceding 'Aldılar, götürdüler' (they took him, they carried him away). The flatness is the point: a whole life closes in routine.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  11. Hakkını helâl etmek (to forgive a claim) sect. II, l. 8

    Helâl (Arabic ḥalāl) means lawful or permitted; 'hakkını helâl etmek' is to renounce a claim on someone, to forgive. At Turkish funerals the imam traditionally asks those present whether they forgive the deceased their rights, and they answer 'helâl olsun'. The poem plays on this custom: his creditors, if they hear, will forgive him, of course. Then the pun on alacak (credit, what is owed to one) turns: as for his own claims on others, 'the late man had none anyway'. Rahmetli (the late, literally 'the one with mercy', from Arabic raḥma) is the polite term for a dead person.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  12. deppo, esvap (depot; clothes) sect. III, ll. 1–2

    Deppo is the colloquial, doubled-consonant pronunciation of depo (from French dépôt), the military store; the spelling imitates soldiers' speech. Esvap (from Arabic athwāb, plural of thawb) is clothing, here the uniform. The two lines show the soldier as replaceable equipment: rifle stored, clothes re-issued. Orhan Veli himself served in the army from 1942 to 1945, at Gelibolu, during the Second World War mobilisation, though the poem names no war.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  13. torba, matara (kitbag; water-flask) sect. III, ll. 3–4

    Torba is a cloth bag, here the soldier's ration or kit bag; matara is the metal water bottle of soldiers and travellers. The poem's grief is located in tiny traces, crumbs and lip-marks, rather than in heroic death. The repeated 'Ne... ne...' (neither... nor) creates a negative inventory, the opposite of the lists of virtues on a formal epitaph. The soldier is remembered only by objects of daily use, as Süleyman Efendi was by his corn.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  14. rûzigâr, yadigâr (time, fortune; keepsake) sect. III, ll. 5–7

    Rûzigâr is the Persian rūzgār, 'time, age, the turning of fortune' (the same word as modern rüzgâr, 'wind'); yadigâr is Persian yādgār, 'something left in memory, a keepsake'. The two rhyme, unusually for Garip-style verse, and both belong to the vocabulary of divan laments on the world's faithlessness. The effect is a brief echo of classical elegy inside the plain modern poem, before the final couplet takes over.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  15. The beyit on the coffee-house wall sect. III, ll. 8–11

    A beyit (Arabic bayt) is the two-line unit of classical and folk verse. The kahve ocağı is the coffee-making corner of a coffee-house or barracks canteen, where soldiers and workers gathered. The couplet quoted is in the style of folk song and popular wisdom: death is accepted as God's will, but the separation it brings is what hurts. The soldier's only memorial, therefore, is not his name but an anonymous line of popular verse in his handwriting, a true 'kitâbe' of the people. The ending moves the sequence from irony to a quiet pathos.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)
