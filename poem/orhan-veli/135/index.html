Living

İstanbul'u Dinliyorum

by Orhan Veli Kanık 1914–1950

Living

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Önce hafiften bir rüzgâr esiyor;
Yavaş yavaş sallanıyor
Yapraklar, ağaçlarda;
Uzaklarda, çok uzaklarda
Sucuların hiç durmayan çıngırakları;
İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı.

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Kuşlar geçiyor, derken;
Yükseklerden, sürü sürü, çığlık çığlık,
Ağlar çekiliyor dalyanlarda;
Bir kadının suya değiyor ayakları;
İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı.

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Serin serin Kapalı Çarşı;
Cıvıl cıvıl Mahmutpaşa;
Güvercin dolu avlular.
Çekiç sesleri geliyor doklardan,
Güzelim bahar rüzgârında ter kokuları;
İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı.

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Başımda eski âlemlerin sarhoşluğu,
Loş kayıkhaneleriyle bir yalı;
Dinmiş lodosların uğultusu içinde
İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı.

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Bir yosma geçiyor kaldırımdan;
Küfürler, şarkılar, türküler, lâf atmalar.
Bir şey düşüyor elinden yere;
Bir gül olmalı;
İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı.

İstanbul'u dinliyorum, gözlerim kapalı;
Bir kuş çırpınıyor eteklerinde;
Alnın sıcak mı değil mi, biliyorum;
Dudakların ıslak mı değil mi, biliyorum;
Beyaz bir ay doğuyor fıstıkların arkasından
Kalbinin vuruşundan anlıyorum;
İstanbul'u dinliyorum.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Refrain: 'I am listening to Istanbul, my eyes closed' l. 1 and every stanza

    The refrain is the poem's frame: the city is not seen but heard, and the closed eyes turn description into memory and imagination. Each stanza begins and ends with the line, except the last, which breaks the pattern. The device is a return to song-like repetition after the stark free verse of Garip (1941); by Vazgeçemediğim (1945) Orhan Veli had softened some Garip positions, objecting not to metre and rhyme as such but to treating them as essential to poetry. The poem became one of the best-known Turkish poems about Istanbul and gave its title to Talât Sait Halman's English selection of Orhan Veli (1971).

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Talât Sait Halman (trans. and intro.), I Am Listening to Istanbul: Selected Poems of Orhan Veli Kanık (New York: Corinth Books, 1971); Londra Edebiyat Kulübü, 'Orhan Veli ve Türk Şiirindeki Devrimi' (on Vazgeçemediğim, 1945, and the poet's later softening of Garip positions)

  2. Sucular (water-sellers) and their bells l. 6

    Before piped water reached all districts, sucular (water-sellers) carried water through the streets in containers on their backs or on carts and rang a bell (çıngırak) to call customers. The sound is placed 'far, very far away', as if from the past. Orhan Veli wrote a separate poem on the figure, 'Sucunun Türküsü', also held in this archive. The image places the poem in a city of trades and street life rather than of monuments.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Talât S. Halman, A Millennium of Turkish Literature, ed. Jayne L. Warner (Syracuse University Press, 2011)

  3. dalyan (fishing weir) l. 11

    A dalyan is a fixed fishery: a structure of poles and nets set in the water to catch migrating fish, with a lookout who signals when to haul. Bosphorus fishing weirs were a feature of the strait into the twentieth century. The line moves from the sky (flocks of birds 'crying, crying') to the water, and the Turkish doubled adverbs 'sürü sürü, çığlık çığlık' (in flocks, in cries) imitate the noise. Fishing, like the water-sellers, connects the listening speaker with working Istanbul.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  4. 'A woman's feet touch the water' l. 12

    The line is remarkable because it is not a sound at all, or only the faintest one; it is something the listening speaker imagines or remembers. It introduces the figure of a woman who returns in the fifth and sixth stanzas and finally merges with the city addressed as 'you'. The plain syntax (subject, object, verb) is typical of Orhan Veli's refusal of ornament, yet the image is sensuous and delicate.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Talât Sait Halman (trans. and intro.), I Am Listening to Istanbul: Selected Poems of Orhan Veli Kanık (New York: Corinth Books, 1971)

  5. Kapalı Çarşı (the Covered Bazaar) l. 15

    Kapalı Çarşı, the 'Covered Market' or Grand Bazaar, grew from Mehmed II's bedesten after the conquest of 1453 into a covered city of streets, shops and inns. Its stone vaults keep it cool: hence 'serin serin'. Orhan Veli also wrote a poem titled 'Kapalı Çarşı' (also held in this archive). The stanza lists sensations rather than sights: coolness, chirping, pigeons, hammers, the smell of sweat; the doubled adjectives (serin serin, cıvıl cıvıl) are a feature of spoken Turkish.

    Talât S. Halman, A Millennium of Turkish Literature, ed. Jayne L. Warner (Syracuse University Press, 2011); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  6. Mahmutpaşa l. 16

    Mahmutpaşa is the steep commercial district between the Grand Bazaar and the Golden Horn at Eminönü, named after Mahmud Pasha Angelović, grand vizier to Mehmed II, whose mosque and baths stand there. It is a quarter of crowded shops and cheap goods, and 'cıvıl cıvıl' (chirping, bustling, full of lively noise) catches its crowds. With the pigeon-filled courtyards of the next line, which suggest the mosque yards of the old city, the stanza moves through the historic peninsula.

    Talât S. Halman, A Millennium of Turkish Literature, ed. Jayne L. Warner (Syracuse University Press, 2011); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  7. Hammers from the docks l. 18

    The docks (doklar, from English 'dock' via French) of the Golden Horn and the shipyards at Kasımpaşa and Hasköy were places of shipbuilding and repair. The hammers and 'smells of sweat on the lovely spring wind' join work and pleasure, the ugly and the beautiful, in the same breath; this refusal to separate 'poetic' from 'unpoetic' material is central to Orhan Veli's practice and to the Garip preface's attack on special poetic diction.

    Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü); Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  8. âlem (world; a drinking party) l. 22

    Âlem is the Arabic ʿālam, 'world', but in everyday Istanbul Turkish it also means a party or night out, a spree, especially with drink and music ('âlem yapmak'). 'Eski âlemlerin sarhoşluğu' is therefore both 'the drunkenness of past worlds' and 'the hangover of old revels', and the ambiguity suits a stanza of nostalgia for a vanished city. Ahmet Haşim, earlier in this archive, uses âlem in the grand sense ('Âlemlerimizden'); Orhan Veli uses it in the tavern sense.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  9. yalı, kayıkhane (waterside mansion; boathouse) l. 23

    Yalı comes from Greek gialos, 'shore', and names the waterfront houses, often of wood, built by Ottoman officials and wealthy families along the Bosphorus. Kayıkhane joins Turkish kayık (rowing boat) and Persian khāna (house): the boathouse on the ground floor where caiques were kept. Orhan Veli himself was born in Beykoz on the Bosphorus. The 'dim' boathouses of a yalı are an image of old, fading elegance, set beside the memory of old revels.

    Sevan Nişanyan, Nişanyan Sözlük: Çağdaş Türkçenin Etimolojisi (online); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  10. lodos (the south-west wind) l. 24

    Lodos is the south-west wind of the Marmara region, notorious in Istanbul for high seas, rain and damaged boats and for a heavy, restless mood. The paradox 'the roar of lodos winds that have died down' is the sound of an absence, heard only in memory, like the 'old revels' above. The stanza is the shortest in the poem (five lines), and its sound-words (uğultu, the roaring hum) give way directly to the refrain.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  11. yosma (a pretty, flirtatious woman) l. 27

    Yosma is a colloquial word for a pretty, flirtatious woman, often with a hint of the street; it is common in folk songs. The line that follows is a list of crowd sounds: 'küfürler, şarkılar, türküler, lâf atmalar' (curses, songs, folk songs, catcalls), lâf atmak being the remarks men throw at a passing woman. The tone is unsentimental and urban. Then something falls from her hand, and the speaker, eyes still closed, guesses: 'it must be a rose'.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890)

  12. 'It must be a rose' l. 30

    Orhan Veli's Garip preface had attacked the stock apparatus of poetry; the rose, emblem of the beloved in divan verse, is the most traditional poetic object of all. Here it appears only as a guess by a man who cannot see: 'it must be a rose'. The line can be read as gentle irony, the poet catching himself in a poetic cliché, or as a sincere tenderness that the earlier list of curses and catcalls makes more touching. Either way, the rose leads into the final stanza's address to a beloved.

    Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  13. The shift to 'you' ll. 34–35

    Up to this point the poem has listed the city's sounds in the third person. In the last stanza a bird flutters 'in your skirts', and the speaker says he knows whether your forehead is hot and your lips wet, and understands from your heartbeat. The 'you' may be the woman of the previous stanzas, now held close, or it may be Istanbul personified as a beloved whose body he knows by touch and pulse. Most readers take the ambiguity to be deliberate: the city and the loved woman merge, so that listening to Istanbul becomes an act of love. The repeated 'biliyorum' (I know) replaces the earlier uncertainty of 'olmalı' (must be).

    Talât Sait Halman (trans. and intro.), I Am Listening to Istanbul: Selected Poems of Orhan Veli Kanık (New York: Corinth Books, 1971); Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  14. fıstık (stone pine) l. 36

    Fıstık normally means a nut (pistachio or pine nut), but 'fıstık çamı', the stone pine with its flat umbrella crown, is a characteristic tree of Istanbul's hills and old gardens, and 'fıstıklar' is a natural shortening. The rising white moon is the poem's only full visual image, yet it is still known through the heartbeat of the beloved in the next line. Night falls over the city as the poem closes.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  15. The broken refrain l. 38

    Every stanza until now has closed with the full refrain. The final line shortens it to 'İstanbul'u dinliyorum.' and ends with a full stop. The change is small but deliberate. It can suggest that the act of imagination is complete and the city now present, or that the beloved's heartbeat has replaced the blind listening. Such quiet variation of a fixed refrain is a device of song, and the poem's popularity owes much to this musical shape.

    Talât Sait Halman (trans. and intro.), I Am Listening to Istanbul: Selected Poems of Orhan Veli Kanık (New York: Corinth Books, 1971); Londra Edebiyat Kulübü, 'Orhan Veli ve Türk Şiirindeki Devrimi' (on Vazgeçemediğim, 1945, and the poet's later softening of Garip positions)
