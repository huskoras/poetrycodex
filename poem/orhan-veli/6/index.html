Living

Anlatamıyorum

by Orhan Veli Kanık 1914–1950

Living

Ağlasam sesimi duyar mısınız,
Mısralarımda;
Dokunabilir misiniz,
Gözyaşlarıma, ellerinizle?

Bilmezdim şarkıların bu kadar güzel,
Kelimelerinse kifayetsiz olduğunu
Bu derde düşmeden önce.

Bir yer var, biliyorum;
Her şeyi söylemek mümkün;
Epice yaklaşmışım, duyuyorum;

Anlatamıyorum.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Anlatamıyorum ('I Cannot Tell It') title; l. 11

    Anlatamıyorum is the negative potential of anlatmak, to tell or explain: 'I am unable to express it'. The poem is a love poem without a named beloved and a poem about poetry: the speaker asks whether his tears can reach readers through his lines. For a poet who, in the Garip preface, wanted poetry to rest on meaning and plain language rather than on metre, rhyme and ornament, the admission that 'words are insufficient' is a striking paradox. The same word closes the poem as its only, isolated line.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019); Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü)

  2. mısra (line of verse) l. 2

    Mısra (Arabic miṣrāʿ, originally one leaf of a double door) is a line of verse; in classical poetry two mısras make a beyit. Placing 'Mısralarımda' alone on a line, after 'Ağlasam sesimi duyar mısınız' (if I wept, would you hear my voice), turns the poem's own lines into the place where the speaker's voice is or is not heard. The question is addressed to 'you' in the plural, the readers.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  3. Touching the tears ll. 3–4

    The second question makes the problem bodily: a reader can see the word 'tears' but cannot touch them. The comma before 'ellerinizle' (with your hands) separates the hands and makes the gesture more deliberate. The two opening questions are unanswered, and their tone, at once shy and urgent, sets the poem apart from Orhan Veli's ironic pieces. The plural 'siz' addresses the readers collectively, perhaps also politely a beloved.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  4. Songs and words ll. 5–6

    The second stanza sets songs (şarkılar) against words (kelimeler). Love songs express what plain words cannot; the speaker has discovered this only in his own suffering. The contrast recalls the debate of the 1920s, when Ahmet Haşim argued that poetry's language lies near music rather than speech; Orhan Veli's Garip poetics had moved the other way, towards meaning and prose plainness. The poem does not cite Haşim, but it allows the student to see Orhan Veli conceding something to music.

    Ahmet Haşim, 'Şiir Hakkında Bazı Mülâhazalar', preface to Piyâle (1926); Orhan Veli, Oktay Rifat, Melih Cevdet, Garip (Istanbul, 1941), Orhan Veli's preface ('Garip' önsözü)

  5. kifayetsiz (insufficient) l. 6

    Kifâyet is Arabic kifāya, 'sufficiency, adequacy'; with the Turkish suffix -siz (without) it means inadequate. The educated loanword is apt: the speaker uses a slightly formal word for the failure of words. The syntax, with the particle -se ('as for words') attached to kelimeler, balances the two halves of the stanza: songs so beautiful, words so inadequate. The word occurs in everyday speech too, as in 'kelimeler kifayetsiz kalır', words fall short, a phrase used in condolences.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  6. dert (pain, trouble, love-sickness) l. 7

    Dert (Persian dard, pain) is both an illness and a sorrow, and in Turkish lyric and folk song it is the standard name for the pain of love ('derde düşmek', to fall into trouble or into love-sickness). The phrase is the only hint that the poem's occasion is love. It also echoes the long divan tradition in which the lover's dert is his distinction.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  7. 'There is a place' ll. 8–10

    The third stanza is the most open. 'Bir yer var' (there is a place) where 'it is possible to say everything' may be the ideal poem, a state of complete expression; it may be the beloved's presence, where nothing needs saying; or, for readers who know the poet's early death, a beyond. 'Epice yaklaşmışım, duyuyorum' (I have come quite near, I feel it) uses the evidential -mış, which conveys realisation: 'I find that I have come near'. The poem refuses to name the place, and the next line confirms the failure.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)

  8. The single-line ending l. 11

    After three stanzas of four, three and three lines, the last stanza is a single word. The white space before it is the gap between the 'place' where all can be said and the speaker's actual speech. Ending on a short, flat line is typical of Orhan Veli, who often closes a poem on a deflating or surprising phrase; here the effect is not ironic but plaintive. The poem's simplicity has made it one of his most widely known and quoted lyrics.

    Yılmaz Daşcıoğlu, 'Kanık, Orhan Veli', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, Ek-2 (2019)
