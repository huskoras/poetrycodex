Death & Dying

Remember

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

Death & Dying

Remember me when I am gone away,
Gone far away into the silent land;
When you can no more hold me by the hand,
Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.
Remember me when no more day by day
You tell me of our future that you planned:
Only remember me; you understand
It will be late to counsel then or pray.
Yet if you should forget me for a while
And afterwards remember, do not grieve:
For if the darkness and corruption leave
A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,
Better by far you should forget and smile
Than that you should remember and be sad.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Remember me when I am gone away l. 1

    The sonnet was composed in 1849 and held back for thirteen years until Rossetti's first trade volume, Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862), where it stands among the secular lyrics of the first half. The imperative 'Remember me' is a farewell formula with deep Christian resonance: the penitent thief asks Christ, 'Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom' (Luke 23:42), and the words of institution at the Eucharist ask believers to 'do this in remembrance of me'. Rossetti turns the formula toward a human beloved and then, in the sestet, questions it. 'Gone away' and 'gone far away' are euphemisms for death that the poem never names directly, a reticence characteristic of her early death lyrics.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Baton Rouge: Louisiana State University Press, 1979), textual notes to the poem; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 23:42

  2. the silent land l. 2

    The Psalms repeatedly picture death as silence: 'The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence' (Psalm 115:17); 'my soul had almost dwelt in silence' (Psalm 94:17). The phrase 'the silent land' had also become a popular nineteenth-century euphemism through Longfellow's 'Song of the Silent Land' (Voices of the Night, 1839), a translation of Johann Gaudenz von Salis-Seewis's 'Das stille Land', with its refrain 'Into the Silent Land!'. Rossetti's land is less a heaven than a place beyond communication: the speaker will be unable to hear the lover's plans or answer them. The silence links forward to the 'darkness and corruption' of the sestet and to the question of whether the dead keep any thought at all.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalms 115:17; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalms 94:17; H. W. Longfellow, 'Song of the Silent Land' (from Salis-Seewis), in Voices of the Night (1839); Kalliope edition contents

  3. half turn to go yet turning stay l. 4

    The line recalls the ordinary physical comedy of reluctant leave-taking: the speaker half turns to go, then, turning back, stays. The syntax enacts the gesture, moving 'turn ... go' and then 'turning stay', a near-chiasmus that halts the line's momentum just as the lover halts. The image is poignant because death will make this kind of reversible departure impossible: the 'going away' of line 1 is a turn that cannot be undone. Commentators often note how much of the octave consists of such remembered everyday intimacies (holding hands, talking of plans), which the speaker knows will end.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Crossref-it, Christina Rossetti: Selected Poems, 'Remember' - synopsis and commentary

  4. our future that you planned l. 6

    The octave imagines a courtship in progress: the beloved talks 'day by day' of a future he has planned. Rossetti was engaged in 1848-50 to the Pre-Raphaelite painter James Collinson, an engagement broken off when he returned to Roman Catholicism, and some biographers have read the 1849 sonnet against that background. Others caution against reading Rossetti's early lyrics as confession; she was a deliberate craftswoman working in the conventions of the love sonnet. Note also the small asymmetry of 'our future that you planned': the future is shared, but the planning is his, a detail some feminist readers take as a quiet comment on the speaker's limited agency.

    Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Wikipedia, 'James Collinson' (engagement to Christina Rossetti, 1848-50)

  5. late to counsel then or pray l. 8

    'Counsel' means to give or take advice, so the line closes the octave by stating that once the speaker is dead, nothing can be discussed or changed between them. 'Pray' is more ambiguous. It may mean pleading with each other (an older sense, 'I pray you'), or praying to God for the speaker's soul. Rossetti was a High Anglican shaped by the Oxford Movement, which revived interest in prayer for the dead, yet the line seems to say such prayer will come 'late', in keeping with the Protestant view that a soul's state is fixed at death. Editors leave the point open; the ambiguity sharpens the urgency of 'Only remember me' in line 7.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pray, v.'; Jerome J. McGann, 'The Religious Poetry of Christina Rossetti', Critical Inquiry 10.1 (1983): 127-44

  6. Repetition of 'remember' ll. 1, 5, 7, 10, 14

    The word 'remember' opens lines 1 and 5, recurs in line 7, and returns in lines 10 and 14, where its meaning is reversed: the sestet allows the beloved to forget and frames remembering as a cause of sadness. The anaphora of the octave ('Remember me when ...') builds an insistent, almost liturgical cadence, while 'Only remember me' in line 7 narrows the request to its bare minimum. Critics often treat the poem's movement as a lesson in self-renunciation: the speaker gives up the claim she has so carefully pressed. The rhyme on 'understand' (l. 7) with 'land', 'hand' and 'planned' ties this narrowed plea back to the images of parting.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Crossref-it, Christina Rossetti: Selected Poems, 'Remember' - synopsis and commentary

  7. Petrarchan sonnet and volta l. 9

    The octave rhymes abbaabba on just two sounds ('away/day', 'land/hand'), an Italian discipline Rossetti, half-Italian and a reader of Dante and Petrarch, handled easily. The sestet rhymes cddece, an unusual arrangement that delays the final rhyme of 'smile' with 'while' (l. 9) until line 13 and keeps the closing line, 'sad', echoing 'had' (l. 12). The turn comes, in the classical place, with 'Yet' at line 9: the speaker concedes that forgetting is permissible and even preferable. Unlike Shakespeare's sonnets, there is no closing couplet, but lines 13-14 work as an antithetical pair ('forget and smile' / 'remember and be sad').

    Crossref-it, Christina Rossetti: Selected Poems, 'Remember' - synopsis and commentary (Petrarchan structure); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem

  8. darkness and corruption l. 11

    'Corruption' is the standard Authorized Version word for bodily decay after death: 'neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption' (Psalm 16:10, quoted in Acts 2:27), and Paul's 'It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption' (1 Corinthians 15:42). Darkness and the grave are coupled in Job (e.g. 10:21-22, 'the land of darkness and the shadow of death'). Rossetti's phrase thus places the speaker's body firmly in the grave, yet the conditional 'if' (l. 11) leaves open whether any part of the mind survives there. For a Christian reader the Pauline context also implies a future resurrection beyond the decay the line describes.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalms 16:10; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:42; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 10:21

  9. vestige l. 12

    A 'vestige' is what remains of something that has otherwise disappeared, a trace or mark. The Latin vestigium literally means the sole of the foot or a footprint, so the word carries a faint image of a track left behind by someone who has gone away, quietly echoing 'gone away' in lines 1-2. The speaker imagines the possibility that a trace of her former thoughts might survive the grave's 'darkness and corruption', and specifically her thought of loving the beloved. The precise, almost scientific Latinate word contrasts with the plain monosyllables of the octave and marks the intellectual hesitation of the sestet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vestige, n.'; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary, s.v. 'vestigium' (Perseus)

  10. Do the dead remember? ll. 11-12

    The sestet doubts whether any 'vestige' of thought survives death. Jerome McGann argued that the idea of 'soul sleep' (psychopannychism), the belief that the soul rests unconscious or dreaming until the Last Judgement, is a central enabling principle of Rossetti's religious poetry, and many of her lyrics present the dead as sleeping and oblivious. Antony Harrison and later critics qualify this, noting that Rossetti treats the state as a brief interval and varies her imagery from poem to poem. Ecclesiastes offers a biblical basis for the doubt: 'the dead know not any thing ... for the memory of them is forgotten' (9:5). The uncertainty makes the speaker's final generosity more striking: she cannot know whether she will mind being forgotten.

    Jerome J. McGann, 'The Religious Poetry of Christina Rossetti', Critical Inquiry 10.1 (1983): 127-44; Antony H. Harrison, Christina Rossetti in Context (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1988), ch. 3, on 'soul sleep' (Victorian Web reprint); The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Ecclesiastes 9:5

  11. Better ... forget and smile ll. 13-14

    The final antithesis, 'forget and smile' against 'remember and be sad', resolves the sonnet in favour of the living. The renunciation has a famous precedent in Shakespeare's Sonnet 71 ('No longer mourn for me when I am dead'), where the speaker would rather be forgotten than cause grief; Rossetti's poem is often read alongside it. Readers differ on its tone. Some find selfless love; others hear a muted reproach, or the self-effacement expected of Victorian women; still others see Christian detachment from earthly attachment. The modest phrase 'Better by far' keeps the ending gentle rather than heroic, and the poem's last word, 'sad', lingers despite the consolation.

    William Shakespeare, Sonnet 71 (MIT Complete Works); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994)
