Love

Up-Hill

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

Love

Does the road wind up-hill all the way?
Yes, to the very end.
Will the day's journey take the whole long day?
From morn to night, my friend.

But is there for the night a resting-place?
A roof for when the slow dark hours begin.
May not the darkness hide it from my face?
You cannot miss that inn.

Shall I meet other wayfarers at night?
Those who have gone before.
Then must I knock, or call when just in sight?
They will not keep you standing at that door.

Shall I find comfort, travel-sore and weak?
Of labour you shall find the sum.
Will there be beds for me and all who seek?
Yea, beds for all who come.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Up-Hill: date and publication l. 1

    Crump dates the poem to 29 June 1858. Rossetti sent poems to David Masson, editor of Macmillan's Magazine, in January 1861; this one appeared in Macmillan's Magazine for February 1861, the first of her poems the magazine printed, and it helped prepare the success of Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862), where it ends the first, non-devotional half of the book. Its question-and-answer allegory of life as a journey and death as an inn made it one of the most anthologised Victorian religious lyrics. The plainness of the diction (almost all monosyllables) is deceptive: almost every phrase carries a scriptural or devotional echo.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Baton Rouge: Louisiana State University Press, 1979), textual notes to the poem; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Up-Hill', publication note (Macmillan's Magazine, February 1861); Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994)

  2. up-hill all the way l. 1

    The figure of life as a steep road is ancient, but for Victorian readers it would recall Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress, where Christian must climb the Hill Difficulty and, on its slope, finds rest at the Palace called Beautiful. Christ's words supply the moral: 'strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it' (Matthew 7:14). The reply 'to the very end' rules out any easy stretch: the Christian life is labour until death. Some readers also hear the Psalmist's question, 'Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD?' (Psalm 24:3).

    John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), Part I: the Hill Difficulty and the House Beautiful (Project Gutenberg); The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 7:14; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalms 24:3

  3. Dialogue form and metre st. 1

    Each stanza pairs an anxious traveller's questions (lines 1 and 3) with brief, assured answers (lines 2 and 4). The questions are mostly pentameter, the answers shorter, often trimeter, so the reassurance is visibly terser than the fear. In stanzas 2-4 the pattern blurs, with answers occasionally as long as questions (ll. 6, 12), but the alternating rhyme abab is kept throughout. The 1862 text gives no quotation marks or speaker names, so the reader must infer the turns. The catechetical exchange resembles Church catechism and the dialogue poems of George Herbert, whom Rossetti admired; the form makes doctrine sound like conversation.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Baton Rouge: Louisiana State University Press, 1979), textual notes to the poem

  4. From morn to night ll. 3-4

    The 'day's journey' compresses a lifetime into one day, an image familiar from Scripture: 'I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work' (John 9:4). The question 'Will the day's journey take the whole long day?' voices the weariness of the traveller who hopes for an early end; the answer refuses a shortcut. 'Morn' is a poetic form of 'morning'. The day-and-night metaphor governs the rest of the poem, so that 'night', 'darkness' and 'beds' all signify death.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 9:4; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'morn, n.'

  5. resting-place l. 5

    The traveller's first real worry is whether there will be any rest at the end. The answer draws on the New Testament promise of rest after toil: 'There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God' (Hebrews 4:9), and 'Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord ... that they may rest from their labours' (Revelation 14:13). 'Resting-place' was also a common Victorian euphemism for the grave, so the line holds both senses: the body's rest in the tomb and the soul's rest with God. Critics who stress Rossetti's belief in an intermediate 'sleep' of the dead read the rest as quite literally sleep before the resurrection.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Hebrews 4:9; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 14:13; Jerome J. McGann, 'The Religious Poetry of Christina Rossetti', Critical Inquiry 10.1 (1983): 127-44

  6. the slow dark hours ll. 6-8

    'The slow dark hours' are the onset of death, slow because dying, or the time after death, can seem long. The traveller's fear, 'May not the darkness hide it from my face?', is that in death one might miss the promised refuge; the reply, 'You cannot miss that inn', is both comfort and memento mori, since no one avoids death. The line answering line 5 begins with 'A roof', showing that the answer to the anxious question is something homely and solid. Darkness here carries no menace in the answers, only in the questions.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 9:4

  7. inn l. 8

    An inn is a lodging for travellers, so the metaphor fits the journey: death is a stopping place, not the end of being. The image recalls two Gospel inns: the inn where there was 'no room' for Mary and Joseph (Luke 2:7), whose exclusion the poem reverses with 'beds for all', and the inn to which the Good Samaritan brings the wounded traveller (Luke 10:34-35), a type of the Church's care. The older tradition of the world as an inn and heaven as home lies behind it too, though here it is death that offers shelter. Rossetti's spare noun makes the afterlife sound plain and hospitable rather than splendid.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 2:7; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 10:34; John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), Part I: the Hill Difficulty and the House Beautiful (Project Gutenberg)

  8. Those who have gone before ll. 9-10

    The 'other wayfarers' are the dead who have already finished the road. The phrase 'gone before' was a liturgical and epitaph commonplace for the faithful departed, and it echoes Paul on 'them which are asleep' (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14) and the 'great cloud of witnesses' surrounding the believer (Hebrews 12:1). The idea of the communion of saints, the bond between the living and the dead in Christ, was important to Rossetti's High Anglicanism. Note that the meeting is 'at night': in the poem's allegory one meets them only in death.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Thessalonians 4:13; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Hebrews 12:1

  9. knock, or call ll. 11-12

    The traveller wonders about the etiquette of arrival: must one knock, or shout from a distance? Two Gospel passages lie behind the exchange. Christ promises, 'knock, and it shall be opened unto you' (Matthew 7:7), while in the parable of the ten virgins the door is shut and latecomers knock in vain (Matthew 25:10-12; compare Luke 13:25). The answer, 'They will not keep you standing at that door', assures the traveller that the first passage applies, not the second. The homely image of being kept waiting on a doorstep turns the parable's threat into a social anxiety and dismisses it.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 7:7; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 25:10; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 13:25

  10. travel-sore l. 13

    'Travel-sore' is formed like 'footsore' or 'heartsore': worn out and aching from travelling. It turns the allegory back to the body, reminding us that the questions come from someone exhausted. The pairing with 'weak' prepares the poem's last promise of beds. Rossetti's plain English compounds keep the allegory concrete; the effect is close to the homely diction of Bunyan, whose pilgrims also arrive at shelters weary and footsore.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'travel-sore, adj. (under travel, n., compounds)'; John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), Part I: the Hill Difficulty and the House Beautiful (Project Gutenberg)

  11. Of labour you shall find the sum l. 14

    The answer is oddly indirect. The main reading takes 'sum' as total or outcome: the traveller will find comfort equal to, or consisting in, the sum of the labour done, as in 'their works do follow them' (Revelation 14:13). A bleaker reading hears that all one finds at the end is the sum of one's labour, that is, exhaustion and rest from it, with no further reward. The inversion of word order makes the line hard to parse at first, which some critics take as a deliberate check on easy consolation. Christ's 'Come unto me, all ye that labour' (Matthew 11:28) links the line to the closing stanza's 'all who come'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 14:13; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 11:28; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem

  12. beds for all who come ll. 15-16

    The closing line answers the traveller's last fear, that there may not be room. The language draws on several texts: Christ's invitation, 'Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest' (Matthew 11:28); Isaiah's promise that the righteous 'shall rest in their beds' (57:2), where the beds are graves; and 'In my Father's house are many mansions' (John 14:2). The bed is thus both grave and heavenly lodging. The universal 'all who come' reverses the 'no room in the inn' of Luke 2:7, though readers note the quiet condition: the beds are for those who 'seek' and 'come'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 11:28; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 57:2; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 14:2

  13. Yea l. 16

    'Yea' is the old affirmative particle, by Rossetti's time confined largely to the Bible and the liturgy (as in 'Let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay', Matthew 5:37). Its appearance in the last line, after the plain 'Yes' of line 2, shifts the answerer's voice into a more solemn, scriptural register, as if the respondent's authority is finally disclosed. It also has the force of 'indeed, and more than that': not only a bed for you, but beds for all.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'yea, adv.'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 5:37

  14. Who answers? l. 4

    The poem never identifies its speakers. Readers have taken the answerer to be a fellow pilgrim who knows the road, a guardian angel, Christ himself (who calls his disciples 'friends', John 15:15), or one of 'those who have gone before'. Others read the dialogue as internal, the anxious self questioned and calmed by the voice of faith, in the manner of Herbert's dialogue poems. The friendly address 'my friend' and the curtness of the replies have been heard both as tender and as slightly withholding, which keeps the poem's comfort from becoming sentimental. The rhyme 'end/friend' in stanza 1 links the companion to the journey's conclusion.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 15:15; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (London: Penguin Classics, 2001), note to the poem; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994)
