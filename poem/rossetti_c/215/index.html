Goblin Market

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

Mythology & Folklore

Morning and evening
Maids heard the goblins cry:
'Come buy our orchard fruits,
Come buy, come buy:
Apples and quinces,
Lemons and oranges,
Plump unpecked cherries,
Melons and raspberries,
Bloom-down-cheeked peaches,
Swart-headed mulberries,
Wild free-born cranberries,
Crab-apples, dewberries,
Pine-apples, blackberries,
Apricots, strawberries;--
All ripe together
In summer weather,--
Morns that pass by,
Fair eves that fly;
Come buy, come buy:
Our grapes fresh from the vine,
Pomegranates full and fine,
Dates and sharp bullaces,
Rare pears and greengages,
Damsons and bilberries,
Taste them and try:
Currants and gooseberries,
Bright-fire-like barberries,
Figs to fill your mouth,
Citrons from the South,
Sweet to tongue and sound to eye;
Come buy, come buy.'

Evening by evening
Among the brookside rushes,
Laura bowed her head to hear,
Lizzie veiled her blushes:
Crouching close together
In the cooling weather,
With clasping arms and cautioning lips,
With tingling cheeks and finger tips.
'Lie close,' Laura said,
Pricking up her golden head:
'We must not look at goblin men,
We must not buy their fruits:
Who knows upon what soil they fed
Their hungry thirsty roots?'
'Come buy,' call the goblins
Hobbling down the glen.
'Oh,' cried Lizzie, 'Laura, Laura,
You should not peep at goblin men.'
Lizzie covered up her eyes,
Covered close lest they should look;
Laura reared her glossy head,
And whispered like the restless brook:
'Look, Lizzie, look, Lizzie,
Down the glen tramp little men.
One hauls a basket,
One bears a plate,
One lugs a golden dish
Of many pounds weight.
How fair the vine must grow
Whose grapes are so luscious;
How warm the wind must blow
Through those fruit bushes.'
'No,' said Lizzie, 'No, no, no;
Their offers should not charm us,
Their evil gifts would harm us.'
She thrust a dimpled finger
In each ear, shut eyes and ran:
Curious Laura chose to linger
Wondering at each merchant man.
One had a cat's face,
One whisked a tail,
One tramped at a rat's pace,
One crawled like a snail,
One like a wombat prowled obtuse and furry,
One like a ratel tumbled hurry skurry.
She heard a voice like voice of doves
Cooing all together:
They sounded kind and full of loves
In the pleasant weather.

Laura stretched her gleaming neck
Like a rush-imbedded swan,
Like a lily from the beck,
Like a moonlit poplar branch,
Like a vessel at the launch
When its last restraint is gone.

Backwards up the mossy glen
Turned and trooped the goblin men,
With their shrill repeated cry,
'Come buy, come buy.'
When they reached where Laura was
They stood stock still upon the moss,
Leering at each other,
Brother with queer brother;
Signalling each other,
Brother with sly brother.
One set his basket down,
One reared his plate;
One began to weave a crown
Of tendrils, leaves, and rough nuts brown
(Men sell not such in any town);
One heaved the golden weight
Of dish and fruit to offer her:
'Come buy, come buy,' was still their cry.
Laura stared but did not stir,
Longed but had no money:
The whisk-tailed merchant bade her taste
In tones as smooth as honey,
The cat-faced purr'd,
The rat-faced spoke a word
Of welcome, and the snail-paced even was heard;
One parrot-voiced and jolly
Cried 'Pretty Goblin' still for 'Pretty Polly;'--
One whistled like a bird.

But sweet-tooth Laura spoke in haste:
'Good folk, I have no coin;
To take were to purloin:
I have no copper in my purse,
I have no silver either,
And all my gold is on the furze
That shakes in windy weather
Above the rusty heather.'
'You have much gold upon your head,'
They answered all together:
'Buy from us with a golden curl.'
She clipped a precious golden lock,
She dropped a tear more rare than pearl,
Then sucked their fruit globes fair or red:
Sweeter than honey from the rock,
Stronger than man-rejoicing wine,
Clearer than water flowed that juice;
She never tasted such before,
How should it cloy with length of use?
She sucked and sucked and sucked the more
Fruits which that unknown orchard bore;
She sucked until her lips were sore;
Then flung the emptied rinds away
But gathered up one kernel stone,
And knew not was it night or day
As she turned home alone.

Lizzie met her at the gate
Full of wise upbraidings:
'Dear, you should not stay so late,
Twilight is not good for maidens;
Should not loiter in the glen
In the haunts of goblin men.
Do you not remember Jeanie,
How she met them in the moonlight,
Took their gifts both choice and many,
Ate their fruits and wore their flowers
Plucked from bowers
Where summer ripens at all hours?
But ever in the noonlight
She pined and pined away;
Sought them by night and day,
Found them no more, but dwindled and grew grey;
Then fell with the first snow,
While to this day no grass will grow
Where she lies low:
I planted daisies there a year ago
That never blow.
You should not loiter so.'
'Nay, hush,' said Laura:
'Nay, hush, my sister:
I ate and ate my fill,
Yet my mouth waters still;
To-morrow night I will
Buy more:' and kissed her:
'Have done with sorrow;
I'll bring you plums to-morrow
Fresh on their mother twigs,
Cherries worth getting;
You cannot think what figs
My teeth have met in,
What melons icy-cold
Piled on a dish of gold
Too huge for me to hold,
What peaches with a velvet nap,
Pellucid grapes without one seed:
Odorous indeed must be the mead
Whereon they grow, and pure the wave they drink
With lilies at the brink,
And sugar-sweet their sap.'

Golden head by golden head,
Like two pigeons in one nest
Folded in each other's wings,
They lay down in their curtained bed:
Like two blossoms on one stem,
Like two flakes of new-fall'n snow,
Like two wands of ivory
Tipped with gold for awful kings.
Moon and stars gazed in at them,
Wind sang to them lullaby,
Lumbering owls forbore to fly,
Not a bat flapped to and fro
Round their rest:
Cheek to cheek and breast to breast
Locked together in one nest.

Early in the morning
When the first cock crowed his warning,
Neat like bees, as sweet and busy,
Laura rose with Lizzie:
Fetched in honey, milked the cows,
Aired and set to rights the house,
Kneaded cakes of whitest wheat,
Cakes for dainty mouths to eat,
Next churned butter, whipped up cream,
Fed their poultry, sat and sewed;
Talked as modest maidens should:
Lizzie with an open heart,
Laura in an absent dream,
One content, one sick in part;
One warbling for the mere bright day's delight,
One longing for the night.

At length slow evening came:
They went with pitchers to the reedy brook;
Lizzie most placid in her look,
Laura most like a leaping flame.
They drew the gurgling water from its deep;
Lizzie plucked purple and rich golden flags,
Then turning homeward said: 'The sunset flushes
Those furthest loftiest crags;
Come, Laura, not another maiden lags,
No wilful squirrel wags,
The beasts and birds are fast asleep.'
But Laura loitered still among the rushes
And said the bank was steep.

And said the hour was early still
The dew not fall'n, the wind not chill:
Listening ever, but not catching
The customary cry,
'Come buy, come buy,'
With its iterated jingle
Of sugar-baited words:
Not for all her watching
Once discerning even one goblin
Racing, whisking, tumbling, hobbling;
Let alone the herds
That used to tramp along the glen,
In groups or single,
Of brisk fruit-merchant men.

Till Lizzie urged, 'O Laura, come;
I hear the fruit-call but I dare not look:
You should not loiter longer at this brook:
Come with me home.
The stars rise, the moon bends her arc,
Each glowworm winks her spark,
Let us get home before the night grows dark:
For clouds may gather
Though this is summer weather,
Put out the lights and drench us through;
Then if we lost our way what should we do?'

Laura turned cold as stone
To find her sister heard that cry alone,
That goblin cry,
'Come buy our fruits, come buy.'
Must she then buy no more such dainty fruit?
Must she no more such succous pasture find,
Gone deaf and blind?
Her tree of life drooped from the root:
She said not one word in her heart's sore ache;
But peering thro' the dimness, nought discerning,
Trudged home, her pitcher dripping all the way;
So crept to bed, and lay
Silent till Lizzie slept;
Then sat up in a passionate yearning,
And gnashed her teeth for baulked desire, and wept
As if her heart would break.

Day after day, night after night,
Laura kept watch in vain
In sullen silence of exceeding pain.
She never caught again the goblin cry:
'Come buy, come buy;'--
She never spied the goblin men
Hawking their fruits along the glen:
But when the noon waxed bright
Her hair grew thin and grey;
She dwindled, as the fair full moon doth turn
To swift decay and burn
Her fire away.

One day remembering her kernel-stone
She set it by a wall that faced the south;
Dewed it with tears, hoped for a root,
Watched for a waxing shoot,
But there came none;
It never saw the sun,
It never felt the trickling moisture run:
While with sunk eyes and faded mouth
She dreamed of melons, as a traveller sees
False waves in desert drouth
With shade of leaf-crowned trees,
And burns the thirstier in the sandful breeze.

She no more swept the house,
Tended the fowls or cows,
Fetched honey, kneaded cakes of wheat,
Brought water from the brook:
But sat down listless in the chimney-nook
And would not eat.

Tender Lizzie could not bear
To watch her sister's cankerous care
Yet not to share.
She night and morning
Caught the goblins' cry:
'Come buy our orchard fruits,
Come buy, come buy:'--
Beside the brook, along the glen,
She heard the tramp of goblin men,
The voice and stir
Poor Laura could not hear;
Longed to buy fruit to comfort her,
But feared to pay too dear.
She thought of Jeanie in her grave,
Who should have been a bride;
But who for joys brides hope to have
Fell sick and died
In her gay prime,
In earliest Winter time
With the first glazing rime,
With the first snow-fall of crisp Winter time.

Till Laura dwindling
Seemed knocking at Death's door:
Then Lizzie weighed no more
Better and worse;
But put a silver penny in her purse,
Kissed Laura, crossed the heath with clumps of furze
At twilight, halted by the brook:
And for the first time in her life
Began to listen and look.

Laughed every goblin
When they spied her peeping:
Came towards her hobbling,
Flying, running, leaping,
Puffing and blowing,
Chuckling, clapping, crowing,
Clucking and gobbling,
Mopping and mowing,
Full of airs and graces,
Pulling wry faces,
Demure grimaces,
Cat-like and rat-like,
Ratel- and wombat-like,
Snail-paced in a hurry,
Parrot-voiced and whistler,
Helter skelter, hurry skurry,
Chattering like magpies,
Fluttering like pigeons,
Gliding like fishes,--
Hugged her and kissed her:
Squeezed and caressed her:
Stretched up their dishes,
Panniers, and plates:
'Look at our apples
Russet and dun,
Bob at our cherries,
Bite at our peaches,
Citrons and dates,
Grapes for the asking,
Pears red with basking
Out in the sun,
Plums on their twigs;
Pluck them and suck them,
Pomegranates, figs.'--

'Good folk,' said Lizzie,
Mindful of Jeanie:
'Give me much and many:'--
Held out her apron,
Tossed them her penny.
'Nay, take a seat with us,
Honour and eat with us,'
They answered grinning:
'Our feast is but beginning.
Night yet is early,
Warm and dew-pearly,
Wakeful and starry:
Such fruits as these
No man can carry;
Half their bloom would fly,
Half their dew would dry,
Half their flavour would pass by.
Sit down and feast with us,
Be welcome guest with us,
Cheer you and rest with us.'--
'Thank you,' said Lizzie: 'But one waits
At home alone for me:
So without further parleying,
If you will not sell me any
Of your fruits though much and many,
Give me back my silver penny
I tossed you for a fee.'--
They began to scratch their pates,
No longer wagging, purring,
But visibly demurring,
Grunting and snarling.
One called her proud,
Cross-grained, uncivil;
Their tones waxed loud,
Their looks were evil.
Lashing their tails
They trod and hustled her,
Elbowed and jostled her,
Clawed with their nails,
Barking, mewing, hissing, mocking,
Tore her gown and soiled her stocking,
Twitched her hair out by the roots,
Stamped upon her tender feet,
Held her hands and squeezed their fruits
Against her mouth to make her eat.

White and golden Lizzie stood,
Like a lily in a flood,--
Like a rock of blue-veined stone
Lashed by tides obstreperously,--
Like a beacon left alone
In a hoary roaring sea,
Sending up a golden fire,--
Like a fruit-crowned orange-tree
White with blossoms honey-sweet
Sore beset by wasp and bee,--
Like a royal virgin town
Topped with gilded dome and spire
Close beleaguered by a fleet
Mad to tug her standard down.

One may lead a horse to water,
Twenty cannot make him drink.
Though the goblins cuffed and caught her,
Coaxed and fought her,
Bullied and besought her,
Scratched her, pinched her black as ink,
Kicked and knocked her,
Mauled and mocked her,
Lizzie uttered not a word;
Would not open lip from lip
Lest they should cram a mouthful in:
But laughed in heart to feel the drip
Of juice that syrupped all her face,
And lodged in dimples of her chin,
And streaked her neck which quaked like curd.
At last the evil people,
Worn out by her resistance,
Flung back her penny, kicked their fruit
Along whichever road they took,
Not leaving root or stone or shoot;
Some writhed into the ground,
Some dived into the brook
With ring and ripple,
Some scudded on the gale without a sound,
Some vanished in the distance.

In a smart, ache, tingle,
Lizzie went her way;
Knew not was it night or day;
Sprang up the bank, tore thro' the furze,
Threaded copse and dingle,
And heard her penny jingle
Bouncing in her purse,--
Its bounce was music to her ear.
She ran and ran
As if she feared some goblin man
Dogged her with gibe or curse
Or something worse:
But not one goblin skurried after,
Nor was she pricked by fear;
The kind heart made her windy-paced
That urged her home quite out of breath with haste
And inward laughter.

She cried 'Laura,' up the garden,
'Did you miss me?
Come and kiss me.
Never mind my bruises,
Hug me, kiss me, suck my juices
Squeezed from goblin fruits for you,
Goblin pulp and goblin dew.
Eat me, drink me, love me;
Laura, make much of me:
For your sake I have braved the glen
And had to do with goblin merchant men.'

Laura started from her chair,
Flung her arms up in the air,
Clutched her hair:
'Lizzie, Lizzie, have you tasted
For my sake the fruit forbidden?
Must your light like mine be hidden,
Your young life like mine be wasted,
Undone in mine undoing,
And ruined in my ruin,
Thirsty, cankered, goblin-ridden?'--
She clung about her sister,
Kissed and kissed and kissed her:
Tears once again
Refreshed her shrunken eyes,
Dropping like rain
After long sultry drouth;
Shaking with aguish fear, and pain,
She kissed and kissed her with a hungry mouth.

Her lips began to scorch,
That juice was wormwood to her tongue,
She loathed the feast:
Writhing as one possessed she leaped and sung,
Rent all her robe, and wrung
Her hands in lamentable haste,
And beat her breast.
Her locks streamed like the torch
Borne by a racer at full speed,
Or like the mane of horses in their flight,
Or like an eagle when she stems the light
Straight toward the sun,
Or like a caged thing freed,
Or like a flying flag when armies run.

Swift fire spread through her veins, knocked at her heart,
Met the fire smouldering there
And overbore its lesser flame;
She gorged on bitterness without a name:
Ah! fool, to choose such part
Of soul-consuming care!
Sense failed in the mortal strife:
Like the watch-tower of a town
Which an earthquake shatters down,
Like a lightning-stricken mast,
Like a wind-uprooted tree
Spun about,
Like a foam-topped waterspout
Cast down headlong in the sea,
She fell at last;
Pleasure past and anguish past,
Is it death or is it life?

Life out of death.
That night long Lizzie watched by her,
Counted her pulse's flagging stir,
Felt for her breath,
Held water to her lips, and cooled her face
With tears and fanning leaves:
But when the first birds chirped about their eaves,
And early reapers plodded to the place
Of golden sheaves,
And dew-wet grass
Bowed in the morning winds so brisk to pass,
And new buds with new day
Opened of cup-like lilies on the stream,
Laura awoke as from a dream,
Laughed in the innocent old way,
Hugged Lizzie but not twice or thrice;
Her gleaming locks showed not one thread of grey,
Her breath was sweet as May
And light danced in her eyes.

Days, weeks, months, years
Afterwards, when both were wives
With children of their own;
Their mother-hearts beset with fears,
Their lives bound up in tender lives;
Laura would call the little ones
And tell them of her early prime,
Those pleasant days long gone
Of not-returning time:
Would talk about the haunted glen,
The wicked, quaint fruit-merchant men,
Their fruits like honey to the throat
But poison in the blood;
(Men sell not such in any town:)
Would tell them how her sister stood
In deadly peril to do her good,
And win the fiery antidote:
Then joining hands to little hands
Would bid them cling together,
'For there is no friend like a sister
In calm or stormy weather;
To cheer one on the tedious way,
To fetch one if one goes astray,
To lift one if one totters down,
To strengthen whilst one stands.'

Study notes by the editors

  1. Goblin Market: date, title and dedication l. 1

    Goblin Market opened Goblin Market and Other Poems (Macmillan, 1862), with frontispiece and title-page designed by Dante Gabriel Rossetti. William Michael Rossetti records that its original title was 'A Peep at the Goblins', dedicated 'To M. F. R.', their sister Maria Francesca Rossetti, and reports that Christina told him she meant nothing profound by this fairy tale, which is not a 'moral apologue'; he adds that its incidents are suggestive and that different readers will find different messages. That frankness has licensed a long tradition of readings: religious (temptation and redemption), sexual and social (the 'fallen woman'), economic (the market), feminist and psychoanalytic. The opening phrase 'Morning and evening' makes the goblins' cry habitual, part of every day.

    W. M. Rossetti, ed., The Poetical Works of Christina Georgina Rossetti (London: Macmillan, 1904), notes, pp. 459-60; R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994)

  2. 'Come buy our orchard fruits, / Come buy, come buy' l. 3

    The cry 'Come buy' recurs throughout the poem as a refrain. It sounds like the street-cries of Victorian costermongers and fruit-sellers, and critics such as Herbert Tucker have read the goblins' patter as a parody of advertising, the 'sugar-baited words' of an expanding consumer market. It also inverts a biblical invitation: 'Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters ... yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price' (Isaiah 55:1). The prophet offers free grace; the goblins sell, and the price turns out to be the buyer's body and life. Terrence Holt and Elizabeth Campbell read the poem as an analysis of exchange and of women's place in a market economy.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 55:1; Herbert F. Tucker, 'Rossetti's Goblin Marketing: Sweet to Tongue and Sound to Eye', Representations 82 (2003); Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990)

  3. The fruit catalogue: 'All ripe together' l. 15

    The opening list mixes English hedgerow fruit (crab-apples, dewberries, bilberries, damsons) with imported luxuries (oranges, lemons, pine-apples, citrons, dates, pomegranates), and fruit of spring, summer and autumn: 'All ripe together / In summer weather'. Such abundance belongs to Eden or to enchantment, and Lizzie later describes the goblin bowers 'Where summer ripens at all hours'. The incantatory list, with its tumbling rhymes and dactylic lines, is itself seductive: the reader is tempted along with Laura. The catalogue has also been read as an image of Victorian imperial commerce, which made exotic produce available to London buyers.

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market; Herbert F. Tucker, 'Rossetti's Goblin Marketing: Sweet to Tongue and Sound to Eye', Representations 82 (2003)

  4. bullaces ... greengages ... barberries l. 22

    A bullace is a wild plum (Prunus insititia), smaller and sourer than the damson, hence 'sharp'. The greengage, named after Sir William Gage who introduced it to England in the eighteenth century, is a sweet green plum. Barberries are bright red, acid berries of the Berberis shrub, so 'Bright-fire-like'. Dewberries are a low-growing blackberry; 'Swart-headed' means dark-topped ('swart', swarthy, black). Rossetti's precise country vocabulary makes the goblin catalogue sound like a real market stall even as its abundance becomes uncanny.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bullace'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'greengage'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'barberry'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swart'

  5. Laura and Lizzie l. 34

    The sisters are introduced in parallel ('Laura bowed her head to hear, / Lizzie veiled her blushes'), and the poem continues to set them side by side, one 'curious', the other cautious. 'Laura' is the name of Petrarch's beloved, the type of the woman praised by male sonneteers; Rossetti, of Italian family, later questioned that tradition in the preface to 'Monna Innominata'. Some critics read the name as making Laura the woman who is consumed, others as a figure of the poet-figure tempted by beauty. Many biographical readers have connected the pair with Christina and Maria Rossetti, to whom the poem was dedicated.

    W. M. Rossetti, ed., The Poetical Works of Christina Georgina Rossetti (London: Macmillan, 1904), notes, pp. 459-60; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994); Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti

  6. 'Who knows upon what soil they fed / Their hungry thirsty roots?' l. 44

    Ironically it is Laura who first voices the danger. The question of where the fruit comes from is central to economic readings: goblin goods have no known origin and no ordinary price, so they sit outside honest exchange; the refrain 'Men sell not such in any town' says the same. The 'hungry thirsty roots' also suggest that the fruit feeds on something, perhaps on the bodies of girls like Jeanie, over whose grave 'no grass will grow'. The image of consuming and being consumed runs through the poem.

    Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991)

  7. The goblins as animals l. 71

    The animal comparisons run through the goblins' introduction: a cat's face, a rat's face, a snail's gait, a wombat, a ratel, and a parrot's voice. Dante Gabriel Rossetti's frontispiece gave them animal heads and human bodies, fixing a reading that the poem itself leaves open. The comparisons suggest the goblins' mixed, unnatural nature and their appetite; they are also oddly comic. The poem never says what goblins are; their indeterminacy is part of their menace.

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  8. wombat ... ratel l. 75

    Both animals were exotic to English readers in 1859, known from natural history books and the London Zoological Gardens, and their foreignness adds to the goblins' strangeness. The wombat 'prowled obtuse and furry' (dull, blunt); the ratel, the honey-badger (Mellivora capensis), 'tumbled hurry skurry'. The ratel's taste for honey may be apt in a poem of honeyed temptation. The Rossetti family was fond of unusual animals, and Dante Gabriel later kept a wombat at Cheyne Walk (1869), a decade after the poem was written.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ratel'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wombat'

  9. a voice like voice of doves l. 77

    The dove is the biblical bird of innocence, love and the Holy Spirit ('be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves', Matthew 10:16), and the Song of Songs makes the voice of the turtle-dove a sign of spring and love (2:12). Here the goblins' cooing is a disguise: they 'sounded kind and full of loves'. The gap between sound and nature recurs: their tones are 'as smooth as honey', their words 'sugar-baited'. The poem keeps asking readers, like Laura, to judge between appearance and reality, a theme with strong roots in Rossetti's devotional writing.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 10:16; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:12; R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  10. The similes of Laura's neck l. 82

    Laura's gesture of longing is described in four similes: a swan among rushes, a lily in a beck (brook), a moonlit poplar branch, and a ship 'at the launch / When its last restraint is gone'. The sequence moves from stillness to irresistible motion: the last image makes her desire a vessel that cannot be recalled once released. Such clustered similes are a structural device of the poem, used again for Lizzie besieged ('Like a lily in a flood') and for Laura's collapse ('Like the watch-tower of a town'). Critics note how the parallel catalogues bind the sisters' stories together.

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market; Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983)

  11. Brother with queer brother l. 94

    The goblins exchange looks, 'Leering at each other, / Brother with queer brother; / Signalling each other, / Brother with sly brother'. The parallel with 'sly' shows that 'queer' has its common Victorian senses: strange, of questionable character (OED). The goblins are an all-male fraternity, a parody of the sisterhood the poem praises; critics reading the poem's gender politics stress that the goblins are 'men' throughout, merchants who trade with girls. Modern readers may hear later senses of the word, but they should be distinguished from Rossetti's.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'queer'; Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti

  12. 'To take were to purloin' l. 117

    Laura's scruple shows her to be a moral agent: she knows the rules of exchange. Her only gold is the gorse flower on 'the furze / That shakes in windy weather', natural wealth that cannot be spent. The goblins' answer, 'You have much gold upon your head', turns her body into currency. Holt and Campbell see the scene as exposing how a market economy makes women's bodies objects of exchange. 'Purloin' (to steal, from Anglo-French purloigner, to put far away) is an elevated word in her plain speech.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'purloin'; Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990)

  13. the golden curl l. 125

    A lock of hair was a familiar Victorian love-token and memento, kept in lockets; to give it was to give a part of oneself. Pope's Rape of the Lock had already made the stolen lock a mock-heroic emblem of violated chastity. Laura 'clipped a precious golden lock' and 'dropped a tear more rare than pearl', a phrase that recalls the 'pearl of great price' (Matthew 13:46). Readers concerned with the fallen-woman theme take the exchange as the loss of virginity; economic readers stress that the body has become currency.

    Alexander Pope, The Rape of the Lock (1714); Bible (KJV), Matthew 13:46; Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990)

  14. Sweeter than honey from the rock, / Stronger than man-rejoicing wine l. 129

    'Honey out of the rock' is God's gift to his people (Deuteronomy 32:13; Psalm 81:16, 'with honey out of the rock should I have satisfied thee'). 'Man-rejoicing wine' recalls Judges 9:13, 'wine, which cheereth God and man', and Psalm 104:15. Rossetti, a devout Anglican, would know these texts well. The triple comparison (honey, wine, water) makes the goblin fruit a counterfeit of divine nourishment: it seems to surpass the gifts of God but cannot satisfy. Hence the question 'How should it cloy with length of use?', which the plot answers: it never satisfies, and never comes again.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 81:16; Bible (KJV), Judges 9:13; R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  15. 'She sucked and sucked and sucked the more' l. 134

    Laura does not eat but sucks, 'until her lips were sore', and then forgets time ('knew not was it night or day'). The insistent repetition presents appetite as compulsion. Gilbert and Gubar read the scene as an image of forbidden female desire and of the woman artist's hunger; others have compared Laura's craving and withdrawal to addiction, and Mary Wilson Carpenter discusses the poem's frank treatment of the female body and its appetites. The compulsive structure, a craving that the first taste can never satisfy, drives the plot until Lizzie's intervention.

    Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti; Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991)

  16. one kernel stone l. 138

    Laura flings the rinds away but saves 'one kernel stone'. Later, in her decline, she plants it 'by a wall that faced the south', waters it with tears, and 'hoped for a root', but 'there came none'. The seed's failure shows that the goblin fruit cannot be reproduced or possessed: it is pure consumption, without increase. The episode has been read as an image of sterility, of desire that cannot be satisfied, and, in readings concerned with sexuality, of barrenness outside marriage, contrasted with the children of the coda.

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market; Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991)

  17. Jeanie l. 147

    Lizzie's warning tells of Jeanie, who took the goblins' gifts 'in the moonlight', 'pined and pined away', grew grey and died with the first snow; no grass grows on her grave. Later the poem adds that she 'should have been a bride', but 'for joys brides hope to have / Fell sick and died'. The language points to the Victorian figure of the fallen woman, who loses her chance of marriage through sexual experience. From about 1859 Rossetti worked as a volunteer at the St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary at Highgate, a refuge for such women, and critics including Jan Marsh and Mary Wilson Carpenter connect the poem with that work, though the dating of her involvement is uncertain.

    Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 1994); Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991); R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  18. 'Golden head by golden head ... Locked together in one nest' l. 184

    This tender passage presents sisterly unity through doubled similes: 'two pigeons in one nest', 'two blossoms on one stem', 'two flakes of new-fall'n snow', 'two wands of ivory / Tipped with gold for awful kings' (sceptres for kings who inspire awe). Moon, stars, wind and owls guard them. The physical closeness ('Cheek to cheek and breast to breast') has invited readings of homoerotic feeling, while others stress the Victorian conventions of sisters sharing a bed and the poem's celebration of female solidarity. The scene also marks the last moment of innocence before Laura's divided state, 'One content, one sick in part'.

    Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983); Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991); R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  19. The sisters' working day l. 199

    The domestic economy of the sisters' cottage, in which goods are produced by work, not bought, is the counter-image to the goblin market. Elizabeth Campbell reads this as a female economy set against the masculine marketplace. 'Neat like bees' links them with the industrious bee of moral tradition. Yet the passage divides at once: 'Lizzie with an open heart, / Laura in an absent dream'. When Laura sickens, the poem lists the same tasks undone ('She no more swept the house, / Tended the fowls or cows'), so that her decline is measured in household terms.

    Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990); R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  20. Laura can no longer hear the goblins l. 254

    The poem's cruellest rule is that the first taste brings a craving for a second, but the second taste is never granted. Laura listens in vain; Lizzie, who has refused, still hears 'Come buy'. The logic is that of temptation in Christian moral writing: the sinner, once fallen, is abandoned by the tempter, and desire becomes despair. Laura 'turned cold as stone', and her questions ('Must she then buy no more such dainty fruit? ... Gone deaf and blind?') show the senses that once revelled now shut.

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  21. Her tree of life drooped from the root l. 260

    The tree of life grows in Eden beside the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:9) and again in the New Jerusalem (Revelation 22:2). Proverbs 13:12 is especially close to Laura's state: 'Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life.' For Laura desire is perpetually deferred, so her tree of life droops. The phrase introduces a sequence of biblical language for her despair: she 'gnashed her teeth for baulked desire', recalling the 'weeping and gnashing of teeth' of the damned (Matthew 8:12).

    Bible (KJV), Proverbs 13:12; Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:9; Bible (KJV), Matthew 8:12

  22. a traveller sees / False waves in desert drouth l. 290

    The epic simile compares Laura's fantasy to a mirage: 'False waves in desert drouth / With shade of leaf-crowned trees', which only make the traveller 'burn the thirstier in the sandful breeze'. 'Drouth' is the older form of drought. The image extends the poem's opposition between true and false nourishment, and anticipates Laura's tears later 'Dropping like rain / After long sultry drouth', when the true remedy arrives. Fantasy feeds desire and starves the dreamer.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'drouth'; R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  23. Lizzie's silver penny l. 324

    Lizzie's coin turns the encounter into a test of exchange. She 'Tossed them her penny' and asks for fruit to take home; the goblins insist that she eat with them ('Honour and eat with us'), since their trade depends on consumption on the spot. When she asks for her penny back, their courtesy collapses into violence. Terrence Holt argues that the poem exposes a system in which the goblins never really sell; they take the buyer herself. The penny returned 'jingle / Bouncing in her purse' shows that Lizzie has gained without paying, an economy of gift.

    Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990); Herbert F. Tucker, 'Rossetti's Goblin Marketing: Sweet to Tongue and Sound to Eye', Representations 82 (2003)

  24. Mopping and mowing l. 336

    To 'mop' and 'mow' is to grimace, pull faces (OED). The pair occurs in the Quarto text of King Lear (4.1), where Edgar names the fiend 'Flibbertigibbet, of mopping and mowing', and Ariel's spirits come 'with mop and mow' in The Tempest (4.1.47). The Shakespearean echo associates the goblins with devils and mischievous spirits. The surrounding passage, a cascade of present participles ('Flying, running, leaping, / Puffing and blowing, / Chuckling, clapping, crowing'), recalls nursery rhyme and shows Rossetti's virtuoso handling of short, irregular rhyming lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mop'; Shakespeare, King Lear (Q1, 1608) 4.1; The Tempest 4.1.47

  25. The goblins' assault l. 406

    The scene moves from courtship to violence: they elbow and jostle her, claw, tear her gown, soil her stocking, pull her hair, stamp on her feet and squeeze fruit against her mouth. Many critics read it as a figure of sexual assault, which Lizzie survives without 'consenting'; others emphasise its spiritual sense, a martyr's ordeal. Lizzie's silence is her defence: 'Would not open lip from lip / Lest they should cram a mouthful in'. Her passive endurance is presented as heroism, which Dorothy Mermin calls a form of heroic sisterhood.

    Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983); Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991); Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti

  26. Lizzie's similes: 'a royal virgin town' l. 409

    This set of similes balances the earlier ones for Laura. Lizzie is 'White and golden' like a lily in floodwater, a blue-veined rock lashed by tides, a beacon sending up fire in a storm, an orange-tree (its blossom the traditional bridal flower) beset by wasps, and 'a royal virgin town / Topped with gilded dome and spire / Close beleaguered by a fleet / Mad to tug her standard down'. The last image draws on the old allegory of chastity as a besieged castle, as in the siege of Alma's castle in Spenser's Faerie Queene (2.11). 'Obstreperously' (noisily, unruly) is a deliberately heavy word in a rhythmic line.

    Spenser, The Faerie Queene 2.11; Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983); R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  27. 'One may lead a horse to water' l. 422

    'A man may well bring a horse to the water, but he cannot make him drink' is recorded in John Heywood's collection of proverbs (1546). Rossetti alters it ('Twenty cannot make him drink'), fitting the goblin crowd. The sudden drop from epic simile to homely proverb is typical of the poem's mixture of registers: nursery verse, fairy tale, biblical echo and moral fable. It also states the poem's ethic plainly: the will that refuses cannot be compelled.

    John Heywood, A Dialogue Conteinyng the Nomber in Effect of All the Proverbes in the Englishe Tongue (1546); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'horse'; R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market

  28. 'Eat me, drink me, love me' l. 471

    Lizzie returns smeared with juice and cries 'Hug me, kiss me, suck my juices ... Eat me, drink me, love me'. The words echo the Eucharist: 'Take, eat; this is my body ... Drink ye all of it' (Matthew 26:26-27) and 'Whoso eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, hath eternal life' (John 6:54). Lizzie, who has suffered for her sister, becomes a type of Christ; readers since Marian Shalkhauser (1956) have spoken of a 'feminine Christ', and Dorothy Mermin and Victoria Coulson discuss the poem's redemptive language. The bodily intensity of the scene has also invited erotic readings, and Mary Wilson Carpenter takes the phrase for the title of her essay. The two readings need not exclude each other.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 26:26-28; Bible (KJV), John 6:54; Mary Wilson Carpenter, "'Eat me, drink me, love me': The Consumable Female Body in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 29.4 (1991); Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983); Victoria Coulson, 'Redemption and Representation in Goblin Market: Christina Rossetti and the Salvific Signifier', Victorian Poetry 55.4 (Winter 2017), 423-50

  29. the fruit forbidden l. 479

    The phrase recalls Genesis 3 and the first line of Paradise Lost, 'Of Mans First Disobedience, and the Fruit / Of that Forbidden Tree'. Laura fears that Lizzie too has fallen ('Undone in mine undoing, / And ruined in my ruin'), the language of the fallen woman. The Eden pattern runs through the poem: the tempting fruit, the 'tree of life', the expulsion into a barren world. The redemption that follows is not by a male saviour but by a sister, which many feminist critics take as the poem's most daring revision of the story.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:1-6; Milton, Paradise Lost 1.1-2; Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti

  30. wormwood l. 494

    The same juice that was 'Sweeter than honey' now tastes of wormwood (Artemisia absinthium). In the KJV wormwood figures affliction: 'He hath filled me with bitterness, he hath made me drunken with wormwood' (Lamentations 3:15), and in Revelation 8:11 a star named Wormwood makes the waters bitter. The reversal is the cure: the antidote is the same fruit, now loathed. Laura 'gorged on bitterness without a name', and her frenzy ('Writhing as one possessed') recalls Gospel scenes of exorcism, where the demon tears its victim before leaving (Mark 9:26).

    Bible (KJV), Lamentations 3:15; Bible (KJV), Revelation 8:11; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wormwood'

  31. Laura's fall: the collapse similes l. 514

    The final cluster of similes mirrors Lizzie's: where Lizzie stood like a besieged town, Laura falls 'Like the watch-tower of a town / Which an earthquake shatters down', a lightning-struck mast, an uprooted tree, a waterspout 'Cast down headlong in the sea'. Before that, her hair streams like a racer's torch, a horse's mane, an eagle flying into the sun, a freed caged thing, a flag when armies run. The two sets of similes, for liberation and for ruin, make the crisis both a death and a release. The passage ends with a question: 'Is it death or is it life?'

    R. W. Crump, ed., Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to Goblin Market; Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983)

  32. 'Life out of death.' l. 524

    The short line, set apart, turns the poem. Its idea is the Pauline and Johannine paradox: 'Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit' (John 12:24); 'that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die' (1 Corinthians 15:36). Lizzie's vigil through the night leads to a dawn of reapers and 'golden sheaves', harvest imagery that contrasts with the barren goblin kernel. Laura wakes 'as from a dream', her grey hair gone golden again. Coulson reads the poem's language of salvation as central rather than ornamental.

    Bible (KJV), John 12:24; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:36; Victoria Coulson, 'Redemption and Representation in Goblin Market: Christina Rossetti and the Salvific Signifier', Victorian Poetry 55.4 (Winter 2017), 423-50

  33. The coda: wives and mothers l. 544

    The coda moves the tale into the past, as a story told to children. Critics have long noticed that the husbands are absent: the only men in the poem are goblins, and the household is still the sisters' world. Some readers find the ending a conventional retreat into marriage and motherhood; others, notably Gilbert and Gubar and later feminist critics, emphasise that the story's moral is about sisters, not spouses. Laura's words recall the poem's own refrain: the merchants' fruits were 'like honey to the throat / But poison in the blood', and Lizzie won 'the fiery antidote'.

    Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (Yale, 1979), ch. on Rossetti; Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983); Elizabeth Campbell, 'Of Mothers and Merchants: Female Economics in Christina Rossetti's Goblin Market', Victorian Studies 33.3 (1990)

  34. '(Men sell not such in any town)' l. 556

    The line appears twice in parentheses, first when a goblin weaves Laura a crown 'Of tendrils, leaves, and rough nuts brown', and again when Laura tells the children of the 'wicked, quaint fruit-merchant men'. Its point is that goblin wares are not part of the honest market: they cannot be bought in any town, and their price is not money. Terrence Holt took the line as the title of his influential study of exchange in the poem. The recurrence also binds the coda to the opening, as if the storyteller's voice were the narrator's.

    Terrence Holt, "'Men sell not such in any town': Exchange in Goblin Market", Victorian Poetry 28.1 (1990), 51-67; Herbert F. Tucker, 'Rossetti's Goblin Marketing: Sweet to Tongue and Sound to Eye', Representations 82 (2003)

  35. 'For there is no friend like a sister' l. 562

    The poem ends with a moral fit for children, set to a ballad-like rhythm: a sister will cheer, fetch, lift and strengthen. Proverbs 18:24 speaks of 'a friend that sticketh closer than a brother', and Rossetti feminises the idea. The original dedication 'To M. F. R.' (Maria Francesca Rossetti) gives it a personal resonance, and the story's central action is Lizzie's saving of Laura. Dorothy Mermin reads the poem as a myth of heroic sisterhood. The very simplicity of the last lines, after so much sensuous and violent writing, has been read both as resolution and as a deliberate understatement.

    Bible (KJV), Proverbs 18:24; Dorothy Mermin, 'Heroic Sisterhood in Goblin Market', Victorian Poetry 21.2 (1983)
