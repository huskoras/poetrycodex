Love

A Birthday

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

Love

My heart is like a singing bird
Whose nest is in a watered shoot;
My heart is like an apple-tree
Whose boughs are bent with thickset fruit;
My heart is like a rainbow shell
That paddles in a halcyon sea;
My heart is gladder than all these
Because my love is come to me.

Raise me a dais of silk and down;
Hang it with vair and purple dyes;
Carve it in doves, and pomegranates,
And peacocks with a hundred eyes;
Work it in gold and silver grapes,
In leaves, and silver fleurs-de-lys;
Because the birthday of my life
Is come, my love is come to me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A Birthday: date and placing l. 1

    Rossetti's manuscripts date the poem to 1857; it first appeared in Macmillan's Magazine in April 1861 and was collected in her first commercial volume, Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862). In a body of work dominated by renunciation, grief and waiting, this is an outburst of fulfilment, which has made its occasion a puzzle. No biographical lover has been identified with certainty. The poem's two stanzas move from nature (similes of bird, tree, shell) to art (commands to build a ceremonial dais), both expressing the inexpressible joy announced in the last line.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  2. Anaphora and similes ll. 1-8

    The first stanza is a series of parallel similes in loose iambic tetrameter, with few rhymes ('shoot' and 'fruit'; then 'tree', 'sea', 'these' and 'me', so that the closing lines gather into one rhyme), closing with 'My heart is gladder than all these / Because my love is come to me'. The structure echoes biblical parallelism, especially the Song of Songs, and the method of exceeding one's own comparisons is a traditional sign of overwhelming emotion. Each simile adds a dimension: song (bird), fruitfulness (tree), colour and calm (shell).

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:3

  3. a watered shoot l. 2

    A 'shoot' is a young branch. The phrase recalls Psalm 1:3, the blessed man 'like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season', and Isaiah 58:11, 'thou shalt be like a watered garden'. The bird is secure and the place fertile. The 'singing bird' also echoes the Song of Songs 2:12, 'the time of the singing of birds is come', in the passage where the beloved calls 'Rise up, my love'.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 1:3; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:12

  4. apple-tree with thickset fruit ll. 3-4

    'As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my beloved among the sons' (Song of Solomon 2:3). 'Thickset' means closely packed. The apple is also the fruit of Eden and of temptation, which Rossetti explored in 'Goblin Market' in the same volume, but here fruit means plenty and ripeness rather than danger. The bent boughs suggest a heart heavy with gladness.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:3; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  5. rainbow shell; halcyon sea ll. 5-6

    In Ovid's Metamorphoses (book 11) Alcyone and her drowned husband Ceyx are turned into kingfishers (Greek alkyon), and for seven winter days the sea is calm while they nest, the 'halcyon days'. 'Rainbow shell' suggests mother-of-pearl, or perhaps a nautilus, whose shell was believed to 'paddle' or sail on the surface. The word 'paddles' is playful, almost childlike. The rainbow also recalls the covenant of peace after the Flood (Genesis 9:13).

    Oxford English Dictionary; Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.410-748 (Ceyx and Alcyone); Bible (KJV), Genesis 9:13

  6. dais l. 9

    The second stanza turns from similes to imperatives addressed to unnamed craftsmen: 'Raise', 'Hang', 'Carve', 'Work'. The dais of 'silk and down' (soft feathers) is a throne, a bridal bed or a canopied seat, at once royal and soft. The scene evokes medieval romance and Pre-Raphaelite decoration; Rossetti's brother Dante Gabriel was painting such rich interiors in the 1850s. The imperatives make the speaker a queen or bride giving orders for her own festival.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  7. vair; purple dyes l. 10

    Vair (from Latin varius, variegated) was the fur of a squirrel with grey back and white belly, used for lining royal and noble garments, and in heraldry a pattern of alternating blue and white shapes. Purple, made from the murex shellfish of Tyre, was the imperial colour. The vocabulary of a heraldic, royal pageant is one of the poem's ways of treating the beloved's coming as a coronation or a wedding feast.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  8. doves and pomegranates l. 11

    In the Song of Songs the beloved is 'my dove' (2:14; 5:2) and her temples are 'like a piece of a pomegranate' (4:3); the lovers' garden has 'an orchard of pomegranates' (4:13). Pomegranates were embroidered on the hem of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:33-34) and carved on the pillars of Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:18). In Christian iconography, the pomegranate, with its many seeds, signifies resurrection and the church. The dove is the Holy Spirit, and in classical art the bird of Venus.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 4:3; Bible (KJV), Exodus 28:33; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  9. peacocks with a hundred eyes l. 12

    When Mercury killed the hundred-eyed giant Argus, Juno set his eyes in the tail of her bird, the peacock (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.720-723). In early Christian art the peacock, whose flesh was believed incorruptible, is a symbol of immortality and resurrection. Rossetti's details thus combine luxurious ornament with a possible sacred meaning, a doubleness that matters for the reading of the whole poem.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.720-723 (Argus' eyes and the peacock); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  10. fleurs-de-lys ll. 13-14

    The fleur-de-lis (lily-flower) was the emblem of the French monarchy; the lily is also the flower of the Annunciation and of the Virgin's purity, as in Dante Gabriel Rossetti's painting Ecce Ancilla Domini (1849-50), for which Christina modelled. 'Gold and silver grapes' recall the vine of Christ ('I am the true vine', John 15:1) and the Eucharist, but also simple abundance. The stanza is thick with images that can be read either as courtly splendour or as liturgical symbolism.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Bible (KJV), John 15:1; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  11. 'the birthday of my life' ll. 15-16

    The phrase means the day on which her true life begins. Readers who take the poem as secular see a joyful erotic lyric about a lover's coming. Others, noting Rossetti's devout Anglo-Catholicism and the poem's imagery from the Song of Songs (traditionally read as Christ's love for the soul or the church), hear a spiritual rebirth, or even death: in Christian tradition a saint's death-day is their dies natalis, birthday into heaven. The richness of the second stanza fits a heavenly marriage as well as an earthly one. The poem itself does not decide.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes; R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes; Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:10

  12. 'my love is come to me': the refrain ll. 8, 16

    'Is come' (instead of 'has come') is an archaic perfect, familiar from the KJV ('the time of the singing of birds is come', Song of Solomon 2:12) and from Advent and Christmas texts. The refrain gives the poem the shape of a song. In the second stanza 'Because' introduces the reason for all the ceremonial building. The poem has been set to music many times.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:12; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes
