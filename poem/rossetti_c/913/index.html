A Christmas Carol

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market, The Prince's Progress and Other Poems (1875)

Religion

In the bleak mid-winter
Frosty wind made moan,
Earth stood hard as iron,
Water like a stone;
Snow had fallen, snow on snow,
Snow on snow,
In the bleak mid-winter
Long ago.

Our God, Heaven cannot hold Him,
Nor earth sustain;
Heaven and earth shall flee away
When He comes to reign:
In the bleak mid-winter
A stable-place sufficed
The Lord God Almighty
Jesus Christ.

Enough for Him whom cherubim
Worship night and day,
A breastful of milk
And a mangerful of hay;
Enough for Him whom angels
Fall down before,
The ox and ass and camel
Which adore.

Angels and archangels
May have gathered there,
Cherubim and seraphim
Throng'd the air.
But only His mother
In her maiden bliss
Worshipped the Beloved
With a kiss.

What can I give Him,
Poor as I am?
If I were a shepherd
I would bring a lamb,
If I were a wise man
I would do my part,—
Yet what I can I give Him,
Give my heart.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'A Christmas Carol' (In the bleak mid-winter) l. 1

    Rossetti wrote the poem in response to a request for a Christmas poem from the American magazine Scribner's Monthly, where it appeared in January 1872; it was collected in Goblin Market, The Prince's Progress, and Other Poems (1875). Gustav Holst's congregational tune 'Cranham' was printed in The English Hymnal (1906), edited by Ralph Vaughan Williams; Harold Darke's more elaborate choral setting dates from 1909. Hymn and anthem versions often omit a stanza or alter words (Darke's anthem, for instance, drops the stanza on the angels and Mary), so the archive text, with five stanzas, is the poet's full poem.

    'In the Bleak Midwinter', Wikipedia (publication and musical settings); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem

  2. An English winter at Bethlehem st. 1

    The Gospels give no season for Christ's birth, and Bethlehem rarely sees snow; Rossetti deliberately sets the Incarnation in an English mid-winter, so that the cold, hard earth becomes an image of the world into which Christ comes. The personified wind that 'made moan' and the repetitions of 'Snow on snow' create the stillness of the scene. The past tense and 'Long ago' frame it as a story, before the second stanza moves into theological statement.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem; 'In the Bleak Midwinter', Wikipedia (publication and musical settings)

  3. 'hard as iron ... like a stone' ll. 3-4

    In Job 38:29-30 God asks Job who has made the frost, when 'The waters are hid as with a stone, and the face of the deep is frozen'. In Leviticus 26:19 the punishment of disobedience is a heaven 'as iron' and an earth 'as brass', and in Deuteronomy 28:23 the terms are reversed: heaven 'brass' and the earth 'iron', which is closest to Rossetti's line. The allusions suggest a fallen, hardened creation awaiting redemption, a reading strengthened when the next stanza speaks of heaven and earth fleeing away.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 38:29-30; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Leviticus 26:19; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Deuteronomy 28:23

  4. Repetition and short lines ll. 5-8

    Each stanza alternates longer and shorter lines, and the fifth to eighth lines of the first stanza contract from a full line to two words, 'Long ago'. The refrain-like 'In the bleak mid-winter' returns in the second stanza, binding the first two stanzas together. The simplicity of the language is a deliberate choice for a carol, a song for congregations and children.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem; 'In the Bleak Midwinter', Wikipedia (publication and musical settings)

  5. 'Heaven cannot hold Him' l. 9

    Solomon, dedicating the Temple, admits that no building can contain God, since even 'heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee' (1 Kings 8:27). Rossetti sets this immensity beside the humble sufficiency of 'A stable-place'. The paradox of the infinite God contained in an infant is the central theme of Christian Nativity poetry, from medieval carols to Milton's 'On the Morning of Christ's Nativity'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Kings 8:27; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem

  6. 'Heaven and earth shall flee away' l. 11

    The stanza places the first coming (in humility) against the second (in majesty), following Revelation 20:11, where 'the earth and the heaven fled away' before the one on the great white throne, and 21:1, 'a new heaven and a new earth'. Compare Christ's words, 'Heaven and earth shall pass away' (Matthew 24:35). The contrast is a staple of Advent preaching, the season of the two comings that precedes Christmas.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 20:11; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 24:35

  7. The sufficient stable ll. 13-16

    Luke 2:7 records the manger 'because there was no room for them in the inn'; tradition supplied the stable. The word 'sufficed', taken up as 'Enough' in the next stanza, becomes the poem's keyword: what is small and poor is enough for God, preparing the final claim that the speaker's poor gift of a heart is enough too. The stanza ends on the full title 'The Lord God Almighty / Jesus Christ', a credal affirmation of Christ's divinity.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 2:7; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem

  8. Cherubim and seraphim ll. 17-18

    Cherubim guard Eden and the Ark in the Hebrew Bible; seraphim sing 'Holy, holy, holy' in Isaiah 6:2-3. Christian tradition, systematised by Pseudo-Dionysius, placed seraphim and cherubim at the top of nine angelic orders, with 'Angels and archangels' (st. 4) below. The heavenly worship 'night and day' (Revelation 4:8) contrasts with the earthly sufficiency of milk and hay. The Hebrew plural ending -im is kept.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 6:2-3; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 4:8

  9. The ox, ass and camel ll. 23-24

    No animals are named in the Gospels' Nativity, but the ox and ass entered tradition from Isaiah 1:3 ('The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib'), read by early Christian writers as prophecy, and they appear in medieval Nativity art. The camel belongs to the Magi, whose gifts were linked with Isaiah 60:6 ('The multitude of camels ... they shall bring gold and incense'). Rossetti lets the beasts' simple adoration be 'Enough', like the milk and hay.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 1:3; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 60:6

  10. Mary's kiss ll. 31-32

    The fourth stanza is hedged ('May have gathered there') because the angels' presence at the manger is not recorded in Luke, where angels appear to the shepherds. The certain thing is Mary's human, bodily tenderness: 'In her maiden bliss' affirms her virginity, and 'the Beloved' borrows the language of the Song of Songs, read allegorically as the love between God and the soul. Darke's anthem omits this stanza, but in the full poem it prepares the speaker's own offering of love.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Song of Solomon 2:16; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem

  11. The shepherd and the wise man ll. 35-38

    The shepherds come from Luke 2:8-20, the wise men (Magi) from Matthew 2:1-11, who bring gold, frankincense and myrrh. A shepherd's lamb also anticipates Christ as the 'Lamb of God' (John 1:29). The conditional 'If I were' acknowledges the speaker's poverty and distance in time, and the dash after 'do my part' marks the pause before the answer. The two figures represent the poor and the learned, so that the speaker, who is neither, stands for every worshipper.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 2:8; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 2:11; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 1:29

  12. 'Give my heart' l. 40

    The final stanza turns from narrative to the first person, the move that has made the poem a congregational hymn. The offering of the heart answers the stanza's question 'What can I give Him, / Poor as I am?' and fulfils the poem's logic of sufficiency: as the stable and the manger were enough, so is a poor person's love. The biblical source is Proverbs 23:26, and the idea that God desires the heart rather than sacrifice runs through the Psalms (Psalm 51:16-17).

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Proverbs 23:26; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 51:17; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, ed. R. W. Crump, notes by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin, 2001), notes to the poem
