Love

Echo

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

Love

Come to me in the silence of the night;
Come in the speaking silence of a dream;
Come with soft rounded cheeks and eyes as bright
As sunlight on a stream;
Come back in tears,
O memory, hope, love of finished years.

Oh dream how sweet, too sweet, too bitter sweet,
Whose wakening should have been in Paradise,
Where souls brimfull of love abide and meet;
Where thirsting longing eyes
Watch the slow door
That opening, letting in, lets out no more.

Yet come to me in dreams, that I may live
My very life again though cold in death:
Come back to me in dreams, that I may give
Pulse for pulse, breath for breath:
Speak low, lean low,
As long ago, my love, how long ago!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Echo: title and date title; l. 1

    Rossetti composed the poem in 1854 and included it in Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862). In Ovid, Echo is a nymph who can only repeat others' last words; rejected by Narcissus, she pines until only her voice remains. The title thus suggests a voice repeating a past love, and a woman who has faded into memory. The poem itself is an invocation, repeated 'Come', to the beloved who survives only in dreams.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes; Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.339-510 (Echo and Narcissus); Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  2. speaking silence l. 2

    The 'speaking silence' of a dream is the poem's central paradox: the dead or lost beloved can speak only in the silence of sleep. Repetition of 'Come' (anaphora) in the first lines gives the effect of a voice calling into emptiness, perhaps answered only by echo. The beloved comes 'with soft rounded cheeks and eyes as bright / As sunlight on a stream', the youthful face preserved in memory.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes; R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes

  3. memory, hope, love of finished years l. 6

    The line is ambiguous: 'of finished years' may govern all three nouns (the memory, hope and love belonging to a past that is over). 'Finished' means ended, but may also suggest perfected or complete. Biographers have linked Rossetti's poems of lost love to her broken engagement to the painter James Collinson (ended 1850), though such readings are speculative and the poem may equally lament someone dead.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes; R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes

  4. 'too bitter sweet' l. 7

    Sappho called love 'glukupikron', sweet-bitter (fragment 130), and the idea of love's sweet bitterness became a commonplace of Petrarchan poetry. Rossetti's escalation, 'how sweet, too sweet, too bitter sweet', makes the dream's sweetness the cause of pain. The dream's 'wakening should have been in Paradise': that is, it ought to have ended not in ordinary waking but in heaven, where 'souls brimfull of love abide and meet'.

    Sappho, fragment 130 (Lobel-Page / Voigt): Eros glukupikron; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  5. Paradise ll. 8-9

    For a devout Anglican like Rossetti, reunion in heaven was a real hope, and many of her poems wait for it. 'Paradise' in Christian usage can mean the place of the blessed dead awaiting resurrection (Luke 23:43, 'To day shalt thou be with me in paradise'). 'Brimfull' (brim-full) suggests souls filled to the edge with love. The stanza contrasts the souls who 'meet' there with the 'thirsting longing eyes' that watch the door, which are also in Paradise, longing for those who have not yet arrived.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 23:43; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  6. the slow door ll. 11-12

    The 'slow door' is the gate of Paradise or of death, watched by 'thirsting longing eyes', which by the syntax belong to souls already within Paradise, waiting for those still outside. 'That opening, letting in, lets out no more' is a compressed line whose present participles mimic the slow swing of the door. The image recalls the parable of the wise and foolish virgins, 'and the door was shut' (Matthew 25:10). The irrevocability of the door explains why the speaker must rely on dreams.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:10; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  7. 'that I may live / My very life again though cold in death' ll. 13-14

    The syntax is ambiguous: 'though cold in death' may refer to the beloved, who is dead, or to the speaker, whose waking life is a kind of death. Either way the poem inverts life and dream: only dreaming is 'my very life'. 'Pulse for pulse, breath for breath' imagines a reciprocal, bodily exchange, recalling biblical formulas of exact exchange ('eye for eye', Exodus 21:24) transformed into love.

    Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes; R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes

  8. 'Speak low, lean low': the close ll. 17-18

    The poem has three six-line stanzas rhyming ababcc, with iambic pentameter lines and shortened fourth and fifth lines (a trimeter, then a dimeter: 'Come back in tears', 'Watch the slow door', 'Speak low, lean low'). The final line, 'As long ago, my love, how long ago!', repeats 'long ago' as an echo would, fulfilling the title. The beloved is asked to whisper and bend close, as in life, but the repeated phrase stresses the distance in time.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes; Christina Rossetti, The Complete Poems, text by R. W. Crump, notes and introduction by Betty S. Flowers (Penguin Classics, 2001), notes

  9. stanza form and refrain ll. 5, 11, 17

    The pattern of three pentameters, a trimeter, a dimeter and a final pentameter gives each stanza the shape of a breath drawn in and released. 'Come back in tears' asks the beloved to return weeping, perhaps as tears of reunion or as the speaker's own tears that bring the memory. The repeated verbs 'come', 'come back' structure the poem as a single prolonged appeal.

    R. W. Crump, ed., The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, 3 vols (Louisiana State UP, 1979-90), vol. 1, notes
