The Mind

My Secret

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862)

The Mind

I tell my secret? No indeed, not I:
Perhaps some day, who knows?
But not to-day; it froze, and blows, and snows,
And you're too curious: fie!
You want to hear it? well:
Only, my secret's mine, and I won't tell.

Or, after all, perhaps there's none:
Suppose there is no secret after all,
But only just my fun.
To-day's a nipping day, a biting day;
In which one wants a shawl,
A veil, a cloak, and other wraps:
I cannot ope to every one who taps,
And let the draughts come whistling through my hall;
Come bounding and surrounding me,
Come buffeting, astounding me,
Nipping and clipping through my wraps and all.
I wear my mask for warmth: who ever shows
His nose to Russian snows
To be pecked at by every wind that blows?
You would not peck? I thank you for good will,
Believe, but leave that truth untested still.

Spring's an expansive time: yet I don't trust
March with its peck of dust,
Nor April with its rainbow-crowned brief showers,
Nor even May, whose flowers
One frost may wither through the sunless hours.

Perhaps some languid summer day,
When drowsy birds sing less and less,
And golden fruit is ripening to excess,
If there's not too much sun nor too much cloud,
And the warm wind is neither still nor loud,
Perhaps my secret I may say,
Or you may guess.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and date l. 1

    The archive prints the poem as 'My Secret'. In Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862) it was called 'Winter: My Secret'. Rossetti's manuscript, from 1857, called it 'Nonsense', which marks the piece as light verse and teasing. The opening line starts in the middle of a conversation, as if answering a request to confess, and the poem spends its whole length refusing. Critics have offered very different accounts of the 'secret'. Biographical readings look for a hidden love. Others take the poem as a play on the reticence of a woman poet, or on the reader's wish to read her lyrics as confessions.

    R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Christina Rossetti's "Winter: My Secret"', Interesting Literature; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'

  2. fie l. 4

    'Fie' is an interjection that expresses reproach or (often playfully) disgust. By the nineteenth century it sounded old-fashioned or nursery-like. Its use sets the tone. The speaker scolds the questioner for being 'too curious', but lightly, flirting rather than taking offence. The short broken lines of the first stanza ('You want to hear it? well:') imitate a spoken exchange and make the reader the one who is being teased.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fie, int.'; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'

  3. Irregular form st. 1–4

    The poem has no fixed stanza. Lines run from two stresses to five, and rhymes come back unpredictably (shows / nose / snows / blows in the long second verse paragraph; me / me). In st. 2 there are bursts of internal and chiming rhyme, 'bounding and surrounding', 'buffeting, astounding', 'Nipping and clipping'. This freedom suits the tone of conversation and keeps the reader guessing, as the speaker does. Its structure follows the seasons. Winter (the long st. 2) justifies concealment, spring (st. 3) cannot be trusted, and summer (st. 4) is a conditional 'perhaps'. The title 'Winter: My Secret' links the secret to the cold season.

    R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'

  4. Perhaps there's none ll. 7–9

    Suddenly the speaker suggests there may be nothing to tell, so the secret becomes a pose. This turn is central to the main critical readings. If the secret is empty, the poem is about the reader's appetite for the poet's private life and about the power of keeping something back. If it is real, the denial is one more layer of disguise. Rossetti leaves both open. Biographical theories exist (critics have linked the poem to her relations with men), but they rest on thin evidence.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Christina Rossetti's "Winter: My Secret"', Interesting Literature; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994)

  5. nipping day l. 10

    To nip is to pinch or bite, and of cold it means sharp, frosty. Hamlet, waiting on the battlements, says 'The air bites shrewdly; it is very cold', and Horatio answers 'It is a nipping and an eager air' (Hamlet 1.4.1–2). The echo is faint but Rossetti would have known it, and Hamlet too is a play about a hidden truth. 'Nipping' comes back in line 17 ('Nipping and clipping'), and 'biting' picks up the idea of pecking that runs through the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nipping, adj.'; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.4.1–2 (Folger Shakespeare)

  6. Shawl, veil, cloak ll. 11–12

    In the list 'a shawl, / A veil, a cloak, and other wraps', literal protection against cold becomes a figure for reserve. The veil carries strong associations. It belongs to the religious woman (Rossetti's sister Maria later became an Anglican nun), to the bride, and to Victorian ideals of female modesty. The cloak is the traditional sign of concealment. The poem thus makes the speaker's privacy look like common sense, since nobody goes out into the cold uncovered.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994)

  7. ope to every one who taps l. 13

    'Ope' was already archaic and poetic by Rossetti's day. In this line the self is a house, and the visitors who 'tap' would let cold draughts 'whistling through my hall'. Readers who know Rossetti's devotional poetry may hear a reversal of Revelation 3:20 ('Behold, I stand at the door, and knock'), which Holman Hunt had famously painted in The Light of the World (1853–54), and of the lover knocking in Song of Solomon 5:2. Here the knocking is resisted, not welcomed. The echo is possible, not certain. Either way the house image makes reticence an act of self-protection.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ope, v.'; The Bible (KJV), Revelation 3:20; The Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 5:2

  8. Mask l. 18

    This is the poem's most quoted line. It admits disguise and at the same time makes it harmless, since the mask is 'for warmth'. Many critics see in it a statement of Rossetti's poetics of reserve. On this reading the lyric 'I' is a persona, not a confession, and the reader who demands self-revelation is being refused. The line also plays on the convention of the coquette's mask. The reader's protest ('You would not peck?') is answered politely, but the truth is left 'untested still'.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Christina Rossetti's "Winter: My Secret"', Interesting Literature; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994)

  9. Russian snows l. 19

    'Russian snows' is a proverbial figure for the harshest cold. It may also have been topical, since the poem was written in 1857, just after the Crimean War (1853–56), whose winters before Sevastopol had been widely reported in Britain. The rhetorical question ('who ever shows / His nose') makes a comic general case of it: no sensible person exposes his face 'To be pecked at by every wind'. The comic diction (nose, pecked) keeps the passage light.

    R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'

  10. peck of dust l. 24

    A peck is a measure of dry volume, a quarter of a bushel. The proverb 'A peck of March dust is worth a king's ransom' holds that a dry March is good for farming. The speaker distrusts even this lucky sign. The word also puns on the earlier 'peck' (to strike with the beak, ll. 20–21), so the threatening winds and the curious listener are carried into spring. In this stanza each spring month is undercut in turn: March's dust, April's 'brief showers', and May's flowers, which a frost may 'wither'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'peck, n.1'; F. P. Wilson (ed.), The Oxford Dictionary of English Proverbs, 3rd edn (1970), s.v. 'March dust'

  11. Summer day st. 4

    The last stanza imagines ideal conditions: drowsy birds, fruit 'ripening to excess', wind 'neither still nor loud'. The conditions are so finely balanced that they may never come. The phrase 'golden fruit is ripening to excess' carries a hint of sensual danger. It recalls the forbidden fruit of 'Goblin Market' in the same 1862 volume, and the Romantic language of ripeness in Keats's 'To Autumn'. The seasons have run from refusal to a deferred possibility, but the speaker still promises nothing.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 1 (Louisiana State UP, 1979), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'

  12. Or you may guess l. 34

    The short last line is a deliberate anticlimax. It may mean that the reader will someday guess the secret unaided, or that the poem has already given it away, or that guessing is all a reader of lyric ever does. Critics who read the poem as a statement about poetics stress this last move. The reader is shut out of the speaker's inner life and is left in the position of interpreter. The poem's rhyme and rhythm, as well as its sense, stay open to the end.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Christina Rossetti's "Winter: My Secret"', Interesting Literature; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on 'Winter: My Secret'
