In an Artist’s Studio

by Christina Rossetti 1830–1894

From New Poems (1896)

Arts & Sciences

One face looks out from all his canvases,
One selfsame figure sits or walks or leans:
We found her hidden just behind those screens,
That mirror gave back all her loveliness.
A queen in opal or in ruby dress,
A nameless girl in freshest summer-greens,
A saint, an angel — every canvas means
The same one meaning, neither more nor less.
He feeds upon her face by day and night,
And she with true kind eyes looks back on him,
Fair as the moon and joyful as the light:
Not wan with waiting, not with sorrow dim;
Not as she is, but was when hope shone bright;
Not as she is, but as she fills his dream.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Written 24 December 1856; first printed in New Poems (1896), ed. W. M. Rossetti.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date l. 1

    Christina Rossetti dated the sonnet 24 December 1856, but it did not appear in print in her lifetime. Her brother William Michael published it in New Poems (1896). In his note he connected it with their brother Dante Gabriel's studio, and with the way Gabriel kept painting and drawing one face, that of Elizabeth Siddal. Gabriel and Siddal had been engaged since the early 1850s and did not marry until 1860. The opening line sets out the poem's subject. The many canvases add up to one face, so the variety of the pictures hides an obsession. Most critics accept the biographical reference. Some add that Christina herself sat as a model for Gabriel (for example in Ecce Ancilla Domini, 1850), so she knew the muse's position from the inside.

    W. M. Rossetti (ed.), New Poems by Christina Rossetti, Hitherto Unpublished or Uncollected (Macmillan, 1896), poem and editor's note; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 3 (Louisiana State UP, 1990), note on the poem; Victorian Prose and Poetry (University of Saskatchewan open edition), 'In an Artist's Studio (1856/1896)'

  2. Petrarchan sonnet ll. 1–14

    The octave rhymes abba abba (canvases / leans / screens / loveliness ...) and lists the roles the woman plays on canvas. The volta comes at line 9, 'He feeds upon her face', where the poem moves from the pictures to the relation between painter and sitter. The sestet's alternating rhymes (night / him / light / dim / bright / dream) are close to monotonous, and that suits a poem about sameness. The form matters here. The Petrarchan sonnet is traditionally the male lover's form for praising an idealised and silent lady, so Rossetti uses it to examine that idealising habit itself. Critics often read the poem with her sonnet sequence 'Monna Innominata' (1881), whose preface complains that the ladies of Petrarch and Dante have no voice of their own.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 3 (Louisiana State UP, 1990), note on the poem; Christina Rossetti, 'Monna Innominata', prefatory note, in A Pageant and Other Poems (1881)

  3. Screens and mirror ll. 3–4

    The speaker suddenly becomes plural ('We found her'), as if visitors were looking round the studio. The woman is not met in person. She is found only in images, behind studio screens and in a mirror that 'gave back all her loveliness'. The mirror shows a reflection of a woman who is herself a picture, so the poem stresses mediation: she exists for the viewer only at one or two removes. Mirrors were also common props in Pre-Raphaelite painting. The verb 'hidden' anticipates the closing claim that the real woman lies behind the painted one.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; Victorian Prose and Poetry (University of Saskatchewan open edition), 'In an Artist's Studio (1856/1896)'

  4. Queen, girl, saint, angel ll. 5–7

    Queen, nameless country girl, saint and angel are the standard female types of Pre-Raphaelite painting, and Dante Gabriel Rossetti's Dantean and Arthurian subjects include all of them. The detail of 'opal or ... ruby dress' and 'freshest summer-greens' recalls the movement's jewel-like colour. However much the costume varies, the meaning stays the same. The list sets up the epigram that follows, which says every canvas 'means / The same one meaning'. Each role is something the painter projects, not a person.

    Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994); Victorian Prose and Poetry (University of Saskatchewan open edition), 'In an Artist's Studio (1856/1896)'

  5. One meaning ll. 7–8

    The phrase is deliberately redundant: 'same', 'one', and 'neither more nor less'. The lines empty the pictures of variety and make the painter's art a single fixed idea. They can be read neutrally, as devotion, or ironically, since an art that always means the same thing says nothing new about its subject. The run-on 'every canvas means / The same' delays the verdict across the line break and makes it land harder.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 3 (Louisiana State UP, 1990), note on the poem

  6. He feeds upon her face l. 9

    At the turn the verb shifts from looking to eating. 'Feeds upon' recalls the lover in poetry who feeds on his beloved's looks, but here the feeding seems to drain her, and the sestet goes on to set her past brightness against her present state. Many feminist readings see the image as predatory: the artist lives off the woman 'by day and night', and she is used up as material. Other critics are less severe and note that the poem stops short of condemning him outright. Rossetti's 'Goblin Market' (written 1859) uses similar language of feeding and being fed upon.

    Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994); Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; Victorian Prose and Poetry (University of Saskatchewan open edition), 'In an Artist's Studio (1856/1896)'

  7. Returned gaze l. 10

    Line 10 seems to give the sitter some agency, since she 'looks back on him' with 'true kind eyes'. But the pronoun is ambiguous. This 'she' is the woman on the canvas, so her steady kindness is the painter's fantasy of an unchanging return of love. The lines that follow show that the living woman no longer looks like this. The gaze is reciprocal only inside the picture.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 3 (Louisiana State UP, 1990), note on the poem

  8. Fair as the moon l. 11

    Song of Solomon 6:10 asks who 'looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun'. Christian tradition read the verse as a figure of the Church or the Virgin, and Rossetti knew the Bible intimately. Her line puts the painted woman into this language of sacred idealisation, which matches the 'saint' and 'angel' of line 7. The allusion also makes the ideal unreal: a woman made out of scriptural moonlight and light cannot age or suffer. The next line turns at once to what that ideal leaves out.

    The Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 6:10; Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem

  9. Not wan with waiting l. 12

    Line 12 describes the real woman through what the paintings leave out: she is 'wan with waiting', 'with sorrow dim'. Readers who take the biographical reference connect it with Siddal's long, uncertain engagement and her chronic illness. The repeated 'Not' at the start of lines 12–14 is anaphora, and it builds a picture of the actual woman out of what the paintings deny. The alliteration of 'wan with waiting' slows the line down.

    Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994); R. W. Crump (ed.), The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti: A Variorum Edition, vol. 3 (Louisiana State UP, 1990), note on the poem

  10. 'Not as she is ... but as she fills his dream' ll. 13–14

    The last two lines repeat 'Not as she is' and change the second half each time. The first says she is painted as she 'was when hope shone bright', so the art is nostalgic and fixed at an earlier moment. The second says she is painted 'as she fills his dream', so the art is the painter's own fantasy. The final word 'dream' is a half-rhyme with 'him' and 'dim', and the poem ends on a slightly jarring sound. Some readers find a sharp indictment of male aesthetic possession here. Others find sympathy for both figures, a painter held by a past image and a woman displaced by it. It has also been read as Rossetti's general comment on idealising art, her own included.

    Betty S. Flowers (ed.), Christina Rossetti: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 2001), notes on the poem; Jan Marsh, Christina Rossetti: A Literary Biography (Jonathan Cape, 1994); Victorian Prose and Poetry (University of Saskatchewan open edition), 'In an Artist's Studio (1856/1896)'
