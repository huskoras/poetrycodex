Nature

Locust-Like Down from the Sky the Snowflakes Wing

by Nejâtî d. 1508

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

Nature

Locust-like down from the sky the snowflakes wing their way;
From the green-plumaged bird, Delight, O heart! hope not for lay.

Like drunken camels, spatter now the clouds earth's winding-sheet;
Laded the caravan of mirth and glee, and passed away.

With lighted lamps in daytime seek the people for the sun;
Yet scarce, with trouble, a dim, fitful spark discover they.

* * *

The Moon in Sign of Bounteousness! the Shade of Allah's grace!
The King, star-armied! he in aspect fair as Hermes' ray —

The Khân Muhammed! at the portal of whose sphere of might
To wait as servants would Darius and Key-Khusrev pray!

E'en should the sun till the Last Day it measure with gold beam.
Nor shore nor depth could e'er it find to th' ocean of his sway!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

From his Winter Qasida.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: Necâtî's Winter Qasida for Mehmed II couplet 1

    Necâtî (İsa, born probably at Edirne in the 1440s, died 1509) is counted among the founders of classical Ottoman lyric poetry. According to the biographer Kınalızâde Hasan, he reached the capital in a severe winter and presented to the sultan a qasida describing the rigour of the season, which was approved; Gibb tells a separate story of how he won a secretaryship (see the note on Mehmed). He later wrote a Spring Qasida as well, which Gibb says was really a wedding poem for one of Mehmed's sons. Gibb's heading gives the opening in transliteration, with the Persian words for 'locust' (malakh) and 'snow' (barf) side by side. The archive prints Gibb's 1882 extract: three couplets of the winter description, a gap (* * *), and three couplets of the praise.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I, 'From his Winter Qasida', and Biographical Notices, p. 180; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), ch. on Necâtî, pp. 95-96 and the 'Winter Qasida' extract [92]; Bayram Ali Kaya, 'Necâtî Bey', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)

  2. Snowflakes like locusts l. 1

    The comparison is visual (a dense, whirling swarm) and moral (locusts destroy crops, as winter destroys the green of the garden). It sets up the line that follows, in which the 'green' bird of delight no longer sings. Gibb's 1902 version keeps the compound: 'the locust-snowflakes'. The opening of a qasida with a description of a season (here winter, in the Spring Qasida spring) is a regular type of exordium; such poems were named after their opening subject.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  3. The green bird of delight l. 2

    In 1882 Gibb renders the line as a bird (probably a parrot, the green bird of Ottoman poetry, or simply a garden bird) that will not sing in winter. In his History (1902) he translates instead: 'From the green fields of joy, O heart, hope for no harvest fair', making the image agricultural, the locusts having eaten the green crop. The difference suggests a word or construction in the original that can be read both ways, a common feature of divan wordplay; without the Turkish text before us, both readings should be kept in mind.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]

  4. Clouds as foaming camels l. 3

    A male camel in rut froths at the mouth, and 'drunken' (in 1902 'angry') camels is the poetic picture of that state. The snow is the foam they spatter, and it lies over the earth as a 'winding-sheet', the cloth in which the dead are wrapped (kefen), implying that nature has died. Camels belong to the caravan imagery of the next line. Gibb's 1902 version: 'The clouds have, like to angry camels, flecked the earth with foam'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'winding-sheet'

  5. The caravan of mirth departs l. 4

    A caravan loading its beasts and departing is a frequent figure for the passing of life or of happiness. Gibb's 1902 version reads: 'The litters of mirth's caravan are bounden, hence to fare'. The camels of the previous line make the picture continuous: the 'drunken camels' of the clouds belong to the caravan that has taken joy away. The departure of the caravan also anticipates the poem's turn, since it is the sultan's favour that will bring joy back.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]

  6. Seeking the sun with lamps at noon l. 5

    The paradox of hunting for the sun with a lamp is a vivid hyperbole of winter gloom. Turkish has the idiom 'mumla aramak', to search for something with a candle, meaning that it is very rare. English readers may recall the story of Diogenes the Cynic, who went about Athens with a lamp in daylight looking for an honest man; the parallel is not necessarily intended. Gibb's 1902 extract includes two further couplets of winter description omitted here, one in which the snowflakes are moths round the sun-taper, another in which the wind locks the stream in a fortress of ice.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]

  7. The gap: from description to praise after l. 6

    A qasida passes from its opening description to the praise of its patron at a transitional couplet, the girizgâh. Gibb's extract omits the intervening couplets, including the turn itself, and resumes with the sultan's titles. In a winter qasida the logic of the turn is usually that the sultan is the sun, or the spring, that will end the winter, which explains why the praise opens with astrological and solar imagery. Gibb's notes say that the occasions of the three qasidas to Mehmed and Bayezid from which his extracts come are described in the biographical notices.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 59 and Biographical Notices, p. 180; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  8. The sultan as a heavenly body in the sign of generosity l. 7

    Astrology gave each planet signs in which it was strongest. The compliment places the sultan, as a heavenly light, in the 'sign' or 'mansion' of bounty, so that generosity is his natural element. Gibb's two versions differ: 'The Moon in Sign of Bounteousness!' (1882) and 'Sun of the Sign Benevolence!' (1902). After a winter in which the sun could not be found, the 1902 reading makes the sultan the missing sun, which fits the transition well; the difference may reflect different manuscripts or a reconsidered reading.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 84, p. 240

  9. The Shadow of God l. 7

    The title 'Shadow of God on earth' (zıllullah fi'l-arz) was used by Abbasid caliphs and later by Ottoman sultans, drawing on a saying that the just ruler is God's shadow on earth, under whom the oppressed take refuge. A shade is also what one seeks in heat, so the image is one of protection. Here it follows the heavenly-body compliment: the sultan is both a light and a shade.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92]; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  10. 'Star-armied' and 'Hermes' l. 8

    'Star-armied' means having troops as countless as the stars. In Islamic tradition Hermes was identified with the prophet Idris (Enoch), master of writing, wisdom and astronomy, and the Greek god Hermes is the planet Mercury (Utârid in Arabic). Gibb's 1902 version makes the sultan 'a moon in the Mercurial sphere', and his footnote there says the 'sphere' is not meant technically: the sultan, surrounded by his hosts, is glorious like the moon among the stars and lofty like Mercury's sphere. So 'Hermes' ray' is best read as the light of Mercury, the scribe of heaven (compare Gibb's note 84 on the planets), and the original Turkish word is not given in Gibb's notes.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I; note 84, p. 240; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), extract [92], pp. 110-111, with footnotes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 148, n. 4 (Mercury as penman)

  11. Sultan Mehmed II l. 9

    Mehmed II took Constantinople in 1453 and made it his capital; he was also a patron of letters and wrote Turkish verse under the pen-name Avnî. Gibb tells the story of how Necâtî first came to his notice: he fixed a ghazel to the turban of one of the sultan's favourites who played chess with him, and Mehmed, finding and reading it, at once made the poet a secretary of the Divan. Shortly afterwards Necâtî dedicated the Winter Qasida to him. On Mehmed's death he wrote a qasida for Bayezid II.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Biographical Notices, p. 180; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. II (London: Luzac, 1902), pp. 95-96; Bayram Ali Kaya, 'Necâtî Bey', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)

  12. Darius and Kay Khusrev l. 10

    Gibb's note identifies Key-Khusrev with Cyrus and calls him one of the greatest monarchs of the legendary Kayanian (Keyânî) dynasty of Persia; in Firdawsī's Shāhnāma he is the ideal king who finally renounces the world. Darius (Dârâ) is the Persian king defeated by Alexander. To make famous kings servants at the patron's threshold is a standard panegyric move, which Bâkî's qasida for Süleyman (also in the archive) uses on a larger scale.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 60; TDV contributor, 'Hüsrev', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  13. The ocean of his sway ll. 11-12

    The closing hyperbole uses the sun's ray as a gold measuring line and the sultan's rule as a boundless sea. 'Till the Last Day' (the Day of Judgement) stretches the measuring to the end of time. The image returns, in the end, to the sun that the winter exordium could not find: in the praise the sun has reappeared, but even it cannot take the measure of the sultan.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nejâtî, extract I
