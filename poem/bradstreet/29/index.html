Relationships

To my Dear and loving Husband

by Anne Bradstreet c. 1612–1672

From Several Poems (1678)

Relationships

IF ever two were one, then surely we.
If ever man were lov'd by wife, then thee,
If ever wife was happy in a man,
Compare with me ye women if you can.
I prize thy love more then whole Mines of gold,
Or all the riches that the East doth hold.
My love is such that Rivers cannot quench,
Nor ought but love from thee, give recompence,
Thy love is such I can no way repay,
The heavens reward thee manifold I pray.
Then while we live, in love lets so persever,
That when we live no more, we may live ever.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its publication l. 1

    Anne Bradstreet (c. 1612-1672) married Simon Bradstreet in 1628 and emigrated with him to Massachusetts in 1630; he later became governor of the colony. Her first book, The Tenth Muse, was published in London in 1650 without her direct involvement; this poem appears in the expanded second edition, Several Poems, printed in Boston in 1678, six years after her death. The marriage poems are among the earliest love poems written in English in America. The poem is twelve lines of rhyming pentameter couplets.

    John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867); Peter Beal, "Anne Bradstreet", Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts 1450-1700 (Folger), introduction (Andover MS; fire of 1666); Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  2. two ... one l. 1

    The first line takes the biblical doctrine that husband and wife become one flesh and applies it with personal confidence: if any couple ever achieved this union, they did. Puritan writers on marriage stressed mutual love as a duty, and the poem's tone is at once private and exemplary. The repeated 'If ever' builds a series of hypotheses that are really boasts.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:24; Bible (KJV), Ephesians 5:31

  3. If ever ... (anaphora) ll. 1-3

    Anaphora is the repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses. The pattern moves from the couple ('two'), to the husband loved by the wife, to the wife happy in the husband, then turns outward: 'Compare with me ye women if you can'. The challenge is playful but also assertive, a woman claiming public pre-eminence in love. 'Then' in line 2 is the old spelling of 'than', and 'thee' takes the object form after it, which also supplies the rhyme with 'we'.

    John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867); Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  4. Mines of gold ... the East ll. 5-6

    The East (the Indies, Persia, Cathay) was proverbial for riches in the period, and Spanish American mines for gold. The comparison is a love-poetry commonplace, but the use of 'prize' (value) and 'recompence' (payment) makes the language of value and exchange run through the whole poem: love is a debt that cannot be repaid. The comparison prepares the biblical quotation of the next line, which also sets love above wealth.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "prize, v.1"; John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867)

  5. Rivers cannot quench l. 7

    The line echoes Song of Solomon 8:7, which also says that a man who offered all his wealth for love would be scorned, a link with the previous couplet's rejection of gold. Puritans read the Song both as an allegory of Christ and the Church and as a celebration of married love; Bradstreet's use of it here leans toward the human sense.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 8:7; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  6. Nor ought but love ... recompence l. 8

    'Ought' is 'aught', anything. The line's grammar is compressed: '[My love is such that] nothing but love from thee [can] give recompense'. 'Recompence' (requital, repayment) belongs to the language of debt and payment that runs through the poem, culminating in the next line's admission that she cannot repay his love, and in the prayer that the heavens will repay him.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "aught, n."; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "recompense, n."

  7. manifold l. 10

    Since she cannot repay his love, she asks the heavens to do it. 'Manifold' means in many ways or many times over; Christ promises those who leave family for the kingdom of God that they shall receive 'manifold more in this present time' (Luke 18:30). The prayer moves the poem from human exchange into the economy of grace. It also implies a modest theology: the wife prays for her husband, leaving reward to God.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 18:30; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "manifold, adv."

  8. persever l. 11

    'Persever' was the usual form in the seventeenth century (Shakespeare uses it), and its stress falls on the second syllable, which makes a full rhyme with 'ever'. Perseverance had a precise theological meaning for Puritans: the perseverance of the saints, the doctrine that the truly elect remain in grace to the end. Here it is applied to married love. The verb thus joins love and salvation in a single word.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "persever, v."; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  9. when we live no more, we may live ever l. 12

    The last line plays on living: when their mortal life ends, they may live eternally. The orthodox reading is that their faithful love will be part of a godly life rewarded by eternal life. Some critics find the line theologically daring, since it seems to make married love itself the means to immortality, which sits uneasily with Puritan teaching that salvation depends on grace alone; others hear a secular hope of lasting fame through the poem, or both at once. The couplet's balance leaves the question open.

    Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967); John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867)

  10. Form whole poem

    The poem's six couplets proceed by statement and balance, with many lines pivoting on a central caesura ('If ever two were one, | then surely we'). Some rhymes are slant to modern ears ('quench / recompence', 'persever / ever' only if mis-stressed), though pronunciation has changed. The plain form suits the poem's directness. Its length, twelve lines, is close to a sonnet, but Bradstreet does not use sonnet structure.

    John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867); Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)
