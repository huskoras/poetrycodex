Verses upon the Burning of our House, July 10th, 1666

by Anne Bradstreet c. 1612–1672

From Manuscript poems (first printed 1867)

ReligionLiving

In silent night, when rest I took.
For sorrow near I did not look.
I wakened was with thundering noise
And piteous shrieks of dreadful voice.
That fearful sound of "Fire!" and "Fire!"
Let no man know, is my desire.
I, starting up, the light did spy.
And to my God my heart did cry
To strengthen me in my distress.
And not to leave me succor less;
Then coming out, beheld apace
The flame consume my dwelling-place.
And when I could no longer look
I blest his name that gave and took.
That laid my goods now in the dust;
Yea, so it was, and so 't was just —
It was his own; it was not mine.
Far be it that I should repine.
He might of all justly bereft.
But yet sufficient for us left.
When by the ruins oft I passed
My sorrowing eyes aside did cast.
And here and there the places spy
Where oft I sat, and long did lie.
Here stood that trunk, and there that chest;
There lay that store I counted best;
My pleasant things in ashes lie.
And them behold no more shall I.
Under thy roof no guest shall sit.
Nor at thy table eat a bit;
No pleasant tale shall e'er be told.
Nor things recounted done of old;
No candle e'er shall shine in thee.
Nor bridegroom's voice e'er heard shall be.
In silence ever shalt thou lie.
Adieu, adieu; all's vanity.
Then straight I 'gan my heart to chide:
And did thy wealth on earth abide?
Didst fix thy hope on mouldering dust.
The arm of flesh didst make thy trust?
Raise up thy thoughts above the sky.
That dunghill mists away may fly.
Thou hast an house on high erect;
Framed by that mighty Architect,
With glory richly furnished.
Stands permanent though this be fled.
It's purchased, and paid for, too.
By Him who hath enough to do —
A price so vast as is unknown.
Yet, by his gift, is made thine own.
There's wealth enough; I need no more.
Farewell, my pelf; farewell, my store;
The world no longer let me love.
My hope and treasure lie above.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The fire and the text title; l. 1

    The fire destroyed the family home, papers and a library of some 800 books. The poem was not in the 1678 Several Poems; it survives in the Andover manuscript, a family copy made by her son Simon, where it is headed as copied 'out of a loose paper'. John Harvard Ellis first printed the manuscript poems in his 1867 edition. The poem is written in iambic tetrameter couplets, a plain measure suited to private meditation. It moves from narrative (the fire), to lament (the ruins), to self-rebuke and consolation (the heavenly house): the pattern of Puritan spiritual meditation on a providence.

    Peter Beal, "Anne Bradstreet", Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts 1450-1700 (Folger), introduction (Andover MS; fire of 1666); John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867); "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia

  2. Let no man know ll. 5-6

    'Let no man know, is my desire' means 'I wish that no one may ever hear that sound'. The inverted syntax of these lines ('I wakened was', 'the light did spy') is characteristic of the poem, and partly a result of the demands of rhyme and metre; some readers find it awkward, others hear in it the formality of a verse testimony.

    John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867); "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia

  3. succorless l. 10

    'Succor' (succour) means help or relief in need; 'succourless' is helpless, without aid. The prayer for strength in distress uses the language of the Psalms, where God is a 'very present help in trouble' (Psalm 46:1). Her first response to the fire is thus not to save goods but to cry to God, as a godly Puritan was expected to do.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "succourless, adj."; Bible (KJV), Psalm 46:1

  4. gave and took l. 14

    Job's words on hearing of the loss of his property and children were the classic text for Christian resignation under affliction. Bradstreet 'blest his name', exactly as Job does. Lines 15-18 continue the argument: the goods were God's own, not hers, and he was just in taking them ('so 't was just'). 'Repine' (l. 18) means to complain or fret discontentedly.

    Bible (KJV), Job 1:21; "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia

  5. Stewardship l. 17

    The Puritan doctrine of stewardship held that all property belongs to God and is lent to its holders. 'He might of all justly bereft' means 'He might justly have bereft us of everything', yet he left enough. This is the poem's first, formal resolution; the following section shows that the feeling has not yet caught up with the doctrine. The neat logic of these lines is that of a sermon applying doctrine to experience.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 24:1; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  6. The ruins ll. 21-28

    After the dutiful resignation of the first section, the poem admits a renewed sense of loss as she sees the places where she sat and lay, where the trunk and chest stood, and the 'store I counted best'. The concrete objects ('that trunk ... that chest') make this the most vivid passage in the poem. 'My pleasant things' echoes Lamentations 1:10 and Isaiah 64:11 ('all our pleasant things are laid waste'), passages lamenting the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 64:11; Bible (KJV), Lamentations 1:10

  7. Apostrophe to the house ll. 29-36

    The shift to 'thy' turns the poem into an elegy for the house. The negative catalogue (no guest, no tale, no candle, no bridegroom's voice) recalls the prophetic curses on cities where the sounds of daily life will cease. The section ends 'In silence ever shalt thou lie', returning to the silence of the opening line. The address to an abandoned dwelling is a classical and biblical topos as much as a personal outburst.

    "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia; John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867)

  8. bridegroom's voice l. 34

    Jeremiah repeatedly warns that God will silence 'the voice of mirth, and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride'. In Bradstreet's household this may also have a literal sense: weddings of her children might have been celebrated in the house. The allusion links domestic loss to the prophetic theme of judgement, suggesting the fire is a chastening providence.

    Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 7:34; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 16:9

  9. Adieu ... all's vanity l. 36

    'Vanity' in the biblical sense means emptiness, futility (Hebrew hebel, breath, vapour). The double 'Adieu' is a farewell to the house and to worldly attachment. The line closes the lament and prepares the turn of the next line. Ecclesiastes was traditionally ascribed to Solomon, the king who had possessed everything and found it empty, which makes it a fitting text for someone who has just lost a well-furnished house.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "vanity, n."

  10. The turn: self-rebuke l. 37

    'Straight' means immediately; 'gan' is 'began'. Self-address ('my heart') and self-questioning were standard in Puritan devotional practice, modelled on the Psalms ('Why art thou cast down, O my soul?', Psalm 42:5). The speaker now treats her grief as a spiritual fault: had she fixed her hope on earthly wealth? The questions are rhetorical: the expected answer to each is no, and the answer leads to the consolation that follows.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 42:5; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  11. the arm of flesh l. 40

    In 2 Chronicles 32:8 Hezekiah says of the Assyrian king, 'With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God'; Jeremiah 17:5 curses the man who 'maketh flesh his arm'. 'Mouldering dust' (l. 39) recalls Christ's warning against treasure that moth and rust corrupt. Together the phrases name the two false trusts: possessions and human power.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Chronicles 32:8; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 17:5

  12. dunghill mists l. 42

    The image contrasts the earthly with the heavenly: thoughts raised 'above the sky' will see past the mists that rise from the dung of earthly things. The dunghill was a common figure of worthless worldly goods (compare Paul counting all things 'but dung', Philippians 3:8). The line therefore asks for a clearing of spiritual vision: once the mist lifts, the heavenly house of the next lines becomes visible.

    Bible (KJV), Philippians 3:8; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  13. a house on high ll. 43-46

    Paul contrasts 'our earthly house of this tabernacle' with 'a building of God, an house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens' (2 Corinthians 5:1), and Christ promises 'In my Father's house are many mansions' (John 14:2). 'That mighty Architect' echoes Hebrews 11:10, of the city 'whose builder and maker is God'. The heavenly house is 'richly furnished' and 'permanent', answering point for point the lost house and its furniture.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 5:1; Bible (KJV), John 14:2; Bible (KJV), Hebrews 11:10

  14. purchased and paid for ll. 47-50

    The commercial language (purchased, paid, price) answers the earlier concern with goods and 'store'. 1 Corinthians 6:20 says 'ye are bought with a price'; Puritan theology stressed that salvation is purchased by Christ and given freely by grace. 'Him who hath enough to do' means 'Him who has means enough to pay'; 'A price so vast as is unknown' is the infinite cost of the atonement.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 6:20; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  15. pelf l. 52

    'Pelf' (from Old French, booty, spoil) was used by moralists and satirists for wealth viewed with contempt. Her farewell to 'pelf' and 'store' completes the ironic reversal of 'There lay that store I counted best' (l. 26). The word helps mark the poem's final distance from the possessions she earlier lamented. The repeated 'farewell' answers the 'Adieu, adieu' of line 36: the first farewell was to the house in grief, the second to wealth itself in resolve.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "pelf, n."; John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867)

  16. treasure ... above l. 54

    The final couplet resolves the poem with a direct echo of the Sermon on the Mount, and it inverts line 27 ('My pleasant things in ashes lie'): now it is hope and treasure that 'lie', but above. Critics differ on how complete this resolution is. Some read the poem as a successful exercise in Puritan submission; others see the vividness of the lament as evidence that attachment to the world is only overcome by effort, so that the ending is a willed act of faith rather than a feeling achieved.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:19-21; "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia; Jeannine Hensley, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1967)

  17. Metre and syntax whole poem

    The poem is in rhymed couplets of four stresses (aa bb). Many lines invert normal word order to place the rhyme word last ('For sorrow near I did not look', 'I wakened was with thundering noise'). Commentators note that the inversions can sound odd to modern ears; they also give the poem a deliberate, formal gravity. The plain style was a Puritan ideal: verse in the service of truth and devotion, not ornament.

    "Verses upon the Burning of Our House", Wikipedia; John Harvard Ellis, ed., The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse (Charlestown, 1867)
