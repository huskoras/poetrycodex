The Author to her Book

by Anne Bradstreet c. 1612–1672

From Several Poems (1678)

Arts & Sciences

THou ill-form'd offspring of my feeble brain,
Who after birth did'st by my side remain,
Till snatcht from thence by friends, less wise then true
Who thee abroad, expos'd to publick view,
Mae thee in raggs, halting to th' press to trudg,
Where errors were not lessened (all may judg)
At thy return my blushing was not small,
My rambling brat (in print) should mother call,
I cast thee by as one unfit for light,
Thy Visage was so irksome in my sight;
Yet being mine own, at length affection would
Thy blemishes amend, if so I could:
I wash'd thy face, but more defects I saw,
And rubbing off a spot, still made a flaw.
I stretcht thy joynts to make thee even feet,
Yet still thou run'st more hobling then is meet;
In better dress to trim thee was my mind,
But nought save home-spun Cloth, i'th' house I find
In this array, 'mongst Vulgars mayst thou roam
In Criticks hands, beware thou dost not come;
And take thy way where yet thou art not known,
If for thy Father askt, say, thou hadst none:
And for thy Mother she alas is poor,
Which caus'd her thus to send thee out of door.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion of the poem l. 1

    Bradstreet's brother-in-law, the Rev. John Woodbridge, took her manuscript poems to England, where they were printed in 1650 as The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America, the first book of poems by a woman resident in English America. The poem, written around 1666, when a second edition was under consideration, was printed in the posthumous second edition, Several Poems (Boston, 1678). The extended conceit of the book as an unruly child, which the author tries to clean and dress, runs throughout. The self-deprecation is a long-standing humility convention, but it also has personal force for a woman writing for print in Puritan New England.

    'The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America', Wikipedia; The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse, ed. John Harvard Ellis (1867), archive.org

  2. The book as child ll. 1-2

    Books as children of their authors' minds is an old figure (Plato's Symposium speaks of poems as children of the soul; Sidney calls Arcadia his 'child'). Bradstreet makes it maternal and domestic: the infant 'after birth' stays by its mother, is washed, dressed, and finally sent out 'of door'. The 'feeble brain' is a conventional apology, but as a woman's apology it engages the period's assumptions about female intellect, which Bradstreet elsewhere challenges in her 'Prologue'.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; The Norton Anthology of American Literature, vol. A, headnote and notes to Bradstreet

  3. The unauthorised publication l. 3

    The line pays the friends a backhanded compliment: they meant well but showed poor judgement. 'Then' was a common seventeenth-century spelling of 'than', used again in l. 16 ('more hobling then is meet'). Woodbridge's preface to The Tenth Muse indeed claimed he printed the poems without the author's knowledge. Bradstreet later wrote to her publisher in terms that suggest she knew of the plan, so whether she was truly surprised has been questioned; the claim of unwilling publication was a familiar modesty device, especially for women authors, so the poem may be partly conventional.

    'The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America', Wikipedia; The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'then'

  4. 'in raggs, halting to th' press' l. 5

    The archive's 'Mae' reproduces a typographical slip for 'Made'; standard texts read 'Made thee in rags'. The rags are the faults of the first edition (errors in transcription and printing that Bradstreet regrets in the next line), and 'halting' (limping) anticipates the metrical joke on feet in l. 15. 'Trudg' means to walk wearily, and 'all may judg' is ironic: anyone can see the errors were not corrected in the press.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'halt'

  5. The 'rambling brat' l. 8

    'Brat' could mean simply a child, often with a contemptuous edge, and 'rambling' suggests both a wandering child and diffuse, disordered writing. The parenthesis '(in print)' points to the public exposure: what was private is now displayed. Her 'blushing was not small' (l. 7) suggests both a mother's shame at a child's behaviour and a woman's modesty at public attention.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brat'; The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'

  6. visage, irksome l. 10

    'Visage' means face, and 'irksome' means tiresome or disagreeable. The mother's first impulse is to reject the child 'as one unfit for light' (that is, for public view), but natural affection ('being mine own') makes her try to improve it instead. The lines dramatise an author's ambivalence towards her published work. The phrase 'unfit for light' plays on the idea of a deformed infant hidden from the world, and on the publication of a book, which brings it to light.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'irksome'; The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'

  7. Washing the child's face ll. 13-14

    The domestic image describes Bradstreet's own revisions for the second edition, which were substantial. The witty observation that correcting one fault introduces another is familiar to any reviser. Critics often single out these lines as evidence of Bradstreet's humour and her confident use of domestic experience as poetic material. 'Still' means 'always' or 'each time', a common seventeenth-century sense, so the failure is presented as inevitable rather than occasional.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; The Norton Anthology of American Literature, vol. A, headnote and notes to Bradstreet

  8. Metrical feet l. 15

    The pun on 'feet' (body and metre) is the poem's sharpest wit. The book's verses still 'run ... more hobling then is meet', that is, limp more than is fitting; 'meet' means proper. The poem itself is written in regular heroic couplets (rhymed iambic pentameter), so the complaint of hobbling verse is partly a modest disclaimer. Stretching joints may also recall the rack, giving the image of revision a touch of violence.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'meet'

  9. 'home-spun Cloth' l. 18

    Homespun cloth was woven at home rather than bought, and in New England it suggested frugality and self-reliance. The image claims a plain style for her verse, consistent with Puritan suspicion of ornament, and implies the material modesty of a colony far from London's fashions. It also echoes the classical convention of the book sent out in plain dress, found in Ovid's Tristia 1.1, where the exiled poet's book goes to Rome unadorned.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; Ovid, Tristia 1.1 (the poet sends his book to Rome), The Latin Library

  10. 'Vulgars' and 'Criticks' ll. 19-20

    'Vulgars' means the common people (Latin vulgus, crowd), here unpretentious readers. The warning against critics reflects the risks for a woman poet whose work might be judged by learned men; in her 'Prologue' Bradstreet had anticipated readers who would say her hand was better suited to a needle. The address to one's book as it is sent into the world has classical precedents in Horace (Epistles 1.20) and Ovid (Tristia 1.1).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vulgar'; Horace, Epistles 1.20 (address to his book), The Latin Library; Ovid, Tristia 1.1 (the poet sends his book to Rome), The Latin Library

  11. 'If for thy Father askt' l. 22

    The ending extends the conceit to its limit. A child without a father was a bastard, and the line plays on the scandal of a book without a male author or sponsor. Some critics read 'Father' as her husband or her own father, Thomas Dudley, both public men in Massachusetts, whom she will not implicate; others as a male literary model (Bradstreet had admired Du Bartas and Sidney), whose paternity she disclaims. Feminist readers find here an assertion of the book as wholly hers.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; The Norton Anthology of American Literature, vol. A, headnote and notes to Bradstreet

  12. Sending the child out ll. 23-24

    Poor families in the period often sent children out to service or apprenticeship; the mother's poverty 'caus'd her thus to send thee out of door'. The closing couplet combines humility (she is 'poor' in talent) and resignation, and it makes publication an act of letting go. The poem frames Bradstreet's second edition with the ambivalence of an author who corrects, disowns and finally releases her work.

    The Works of Anne Bradstreet, ed. Jeannine Hensley (Harvard University Press, 1967), notes to 'The Author to Her Book'; The Works of Anne Bradstreet in Prose and Verse, ed. John Harvard Ellis (1867), archive.org
