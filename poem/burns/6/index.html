A Red, Red Rose

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

Love

O my Luve’s like a red, red rose,
That’s newly sprung in June:
O my Luve’s like the melodie,
That’s sweetly play’d in tune.

As fair art thou, my bonie lass,
So deep in luve am I;
And I will luve thee still, my dear,
Till a’ the seas gang dry.

Till a’ the seas gang dry, my dear,
And the rocks melt wi’ the sun;
And I will luve thee still, my dear,
While the sands o’ life shall run.

And fare-thee-weel, my only Luve!
And fare-thee-weel, a while!
And I will come again, my Luve,
Tho’ ’twere ten thousand mile!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Burns as song-maker: sources and publication l. 1

    In his last decade Burns collected, mended and rewrote Scottish songs for James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum and George Thomson's Select Collection. This lyric is a characteristic piece of that work: several of its images and phrases are found in earlier chapbook and broadside songs, including 'The Turtle Dove' and a farewell song printed in garlands, while Burns's achievement is the selection and compression. The song was first printed in Pietro Urbani's Selection of Scots Songs (1794), and then in the fifth volume of the Museum (1797) as no. 402, with an 'Old Set' as no. 403. Burns did not publish it as a 'poem' at all, which is why editors place it among the songs.

    'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); James Johnson, ed., The Scots Musical Museum, vol. 5, song no. 402 ('O my Luve's like a red, red rose'), with an 'Old Set' as no. 403; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song

  2. a red, red rose l. 1

    Repetition such as 'red, red' is a mark of oral song rather than literary poetry, and it gives the line its emphatic, unsophisticated warmth. Very similar opening lines are found in older popular songs; one late-seventeenth-century broadside has a closely parallel first stanza. The rose as an emblem of beauty and love is ancient, and its usual companion idea, that roses fade, is quietly present: the rose is 'newly sprung', and the song's later vows set permanence against time.

    'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song

  3. newly sprung in June l. 2

    'Sprung' is the past participle of 'spring', to grow or come up. June is the season of roses in Scotland. The detail is simple, but the word 'newly' matters: it makes the comparison about a beginning, which the rest of the song then tries to extend to the end of time. Compare the double sense in the next simile, where love is like a melody that exists only while it is being played.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spring'

  4. like the melodie ... sweetly play'd in tune ll. 3-4

    The second simile turns from sight to sound. The song was written to be sung, and its tune history is complicated. Burns asked Johnson to set it to Niel Gow's strathspey 'Major Graham', and the Museum printed it so; Thomson later used a different air. The tune now usual, 'Low Down in the Broom', was attached to it in R. A. Smith's Scotish Minstrel (1821) and made the song world-famous. Kirsteen McCue has argued that the choice of melody materially changes how the words are heard.

    The Traditional Tune Archive, annotation 'My Love is Like a Red Red Rose (2)' (Gow's 'Major Graham'; Johnson's Museum no. 402); 'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); Kirsteen McCue, 'O My Luve's Like a Red, Red Rose: Does Burns's Melody Really Matter', in Editing Robert Burns for the 21st Century (2014); James Johnson, ed., The Scots Musical Museum, vol. 5, song no. 402 ('O my Luve's like a red, red rose'), with an 'Old Set' as no. 403

  5. Stanza and metre st. 2

    The song uses the commonest metre of British popular song and hymn, the ballad quatrain (8.6.8.6 syllables, with variations). Only the even lines rhyme ('June'/'tune', 'I'/'dry'). The switch from third person ('my Luve's like') to direct address ('As fair art thou') in this stanza turns the song into a vow spoken to the girl. The comparisons 'As fair ... / So deep' balance her beauty and his love.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song

  6. bonie l. 5

    Burns's own preferred spelling is 'bonie' with one n. The word probably comes from French bon, bonne, 'good', and became a key word of Scots love-song. It is the only Scots word in this line, and the song as a whole is in a light Scots that English singers could manage easily, which helped its wide circulation. In this song it is one of a handful of Scots markers ('a'', 'gang', 'weel', 'o'') that keep the lyric recognisably Scottish without making it hard for non-Scots singers. Burns's other songs use the word constantly ('Ye banks and braes o' bonie Doon').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bonny'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  7. Luve / luve ll. 1, 6

    The spelling reflects Scots pronunciation, closer to 'lyuv'. Many printings capitalise 'Luve' when it means the beloved and not when it means the passion, as this text does. Readers have noticed that the opening similes could therefore apply either to the girl or to the speaker's love itself; the second stanza resolves it by addressing 'thou', but the ambiguity gives the opening its generality.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song

  8. Till a' the seas gang dry l. 8

    'A'' is 'all' and 'gang' is 'go'. The figure is adynaton, the rhetorical naming of an impossibility to express forever, which is common in popular love songs and farewell songs. Similar vows appear in the older broadside material Burns drew on. The line is then repeated at the start of stanza 3. The vow's grandeur is deliberately out of proportion to the simple speaker, a familiar effect of folk song, where ordinary lovers swear by the whole world. It also introduces the theme of time and change that runs through the rest of the song.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; 'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'adynaton'

  9. Repetition across stanzas l. 9

    This device, repeating the end of one unit at the beginning of the next (anadiplosis), gives the song its incantatory force and also suits singing, where the repeated words can carry the melody. 'I will luve thee still, my dear' recurs as well (ll. 7, 11). The refrain-like repetitions make the vow sound as if it is being sworn again.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song

  10. the rocks melt wi' the sun l. 10

    The image heightens the first vow by adding a cosmic heat to the drying seas. The older broadside 'The Turtle Dove' and related songs have similar lines about seas going dry and rocks melting. Burns keeps the folk idiom but makes the stanza more compact. 'Wi'' is 'with'. The cumulative structure (seas, rocks, sands) moves from water to stone to the grains of time, each image a little more intimate, until the vow reaches the length of a human life. Groups of three impossibilities are easy to remember and common in popular verse.

    'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  11. the sands o' life l. 12

    After two images of the end of the world, the third vow is about the length of the lover's own life. The hourglass emblem of mortality was a commonplace in eighteenth-century writing and church art. 'While the sands o' life shall run' therefore admits a limit that the hyperboles deny, giving the vows a human measure. The image may also recall the sands of the seashore, linking it with the 'seas' of the preceding lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sand'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  12. fare-thee-weel ... a while ll. 13-14

    'Weel' is 'well'. The last stanza reveals the situation: the vows are a leave-taking. 'A while' insists that the separation is temporary. Parting songs of soldiers and sailors were a staple of the broadsides Burns knew, and the hyperbolic last line belongs to that tradition. The repetition of 'fare-thee-weel' and of 'my Luve' gives the stanza the sound of a refrain. Some readers find the sudden revelation of departure a little abrupt, a sign of the song's patchwork origins; others feel that it explains, after the event, the extravagance of the vows that precede it.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; 'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934)

  13. ten thousand mile l. 16

    The phrase is found in the older song 'The Turtle Dove' and related broadside farewells, which Burns knew. Using 'mile' without -s after a number is standard in Scots and in many English dialects. The exaggerated distance mirrors the exaggerated time of the earlier vows and ends the song on the promise of return. The song thus closes on a borrowed phrase, a sign of its place in a shared tradition rather than a lapse in originality.

    'A Red, Red Rose', Wikipedia (publication history, older sources, tunes; cites McCue 2014, Davidson Cook 1934); James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Donald A. Low, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London: Routledge, 1993), note on the song
