Robert Bruce’s March To Bannockburn

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

From Poems and Songs, 1793

History & Politics

Scots, wha hae wi’ Wallace bled,
Scots, wham Bruce has aften led,
Welcome to your gory bed,
Or to Victorie!

Now’s the day, and now’s the hour;
See the front o’ battle lour;
See approach proud Edward’s power—
Chains and Slaverie!

Wha will be a traitor knave?
Wha can fill a coward’s grave?
Wha sae base as be a Slave?
Let him turn and flee!

Wha, for Scotland’s King and Law,
Freedom’s sword will strongly draw,
Free-man stand, or Free-man fa’,
Let him on wi’ me!

By Oppression’s woes and pains!
By your Sons in servile chains!
We will drain our dearest veins,
But they shall be free!

Lay the proud Usurpers low!
Tyrants fall in every foe!
Liberty’s in every blow!—
Let us Do or Die!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion st. 1

    Burns wrote to Thomson that the old tune 'Hey tuttie taitie' was said by tradition to have been Robert Bruce's march at Bannockburn, and that the thought, in his solitary wanderings, roused him to an enthusiasm 'on the theme of liberty and independence', which he threw into a Scots ode imagined as the king's address to his men. The Morgan Library holds an autograph headed 'Robert Bruce's March to Bannockburn', catalogued as 30 August 1793, which explains the archive's title. John Syme gave a rival account, placing its composition on a stormy tour of Galloway in July 1793; Henley and Henderson find the two stories irreconcilable.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; Morgan Library & Museum, autograph manuscript 'Robert Bruce's March to Bannockburn', catalogued as [30 Aug. 1793]

  2. Wallace st. 1

    Wallace led the rising against English occupation in 1297 and defeated an English army at Stirling Bridge, but was beaten at Falkirk in 1298 and later betrayed, taken to London and executed in 1305. The 'Scots, wha hae wi' Wallace bled' are those who fought and bled under him, now under Bruce. For Burns and his contemporaries Wallace was the type of the patriot martyr.

    'William Wallace', Wikipedia; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  3. Bruce st. 1

    Bruce seized the throne in 1306 and fought a long guerrilla war before the decisive victory at Bannockburn near Stirling on 23-24 June 1314. The English king he faced was Edward II, son of Edward I. Bruce is the speaker of the whole song.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; 'Battle of Bannockburn', Wikipedia

  4. gory bed ... Or to Victorie! st. 1

    The 'gory bed' is the bloody grave of the battlefield. Bruce greets his men with the choice of death or victory, which the last line ('Let us Do or Die!') repeats as a slogan. Spellings such as 'Victorie' and 'Slaverie' give a Scots flavour (and a stressed final syllable for the tune) to verse that is otherwise largely English. The bleak welcome is a rhetorical device: by naming death first, the speaker makes it a thing to be faced, not feared.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  5. lour st. 2

    'Lour' (or 'lower') means to scowl or to look dark and menacing, used especially of weather and faces. The 'front o' battle' is the advancing English line, which darkens like a storm. The urgent repetitions ('Now's the day, and now's the hour'; 'See ... See') give the stanza the pace of an address in the moment of crisis. The word links the moral climate of the battle with weather, a common figure in heroic poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lour'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  6. proud Edward st. 2

    Edward II led a large English army to relieve Stirling Castle in 1314. 'Power' here means armed force, host. The line ends in the cry 'Chains and Slaverie!', what defeat would bring.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  7. 'Struggles not quite so ancient' st. 2

    Henley and Henderson quote Burns's admission that the song was prompted by the glowing ideas of 'some other struggles of the same nature not quite so ancient', and conclude that it is partly an effect of the French Revolution. In 1793 Britain was at war with France and the government was prosecuting Scottish radicals: the trial of the reformer Thomas Muir began on 30 August 1793. Read in that light, the rhetoric of chains, tyrants and usurpers addresses the present as much as 1314, which is why Burns, a government exciseman, published it anonymously.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; 'Scots Wha Hae' annotation, The Traditional Tune Archive (on Burns's letter and the Muir trial)

  8. The rhetorical questions st. 3

    The third stanza is built on anaphora (repeated 'Wha ...'), a device of classical oratory. Each question names a disgrace: treachery, a 'coward's grave', and slavery. The fourth stanza answers with the positive counterpart ('Wha, for Scotland's King and Law ...'), so the song moves in paired stanzas from shame to resolve. Rhetorically, the questions exclude the unworthy before the battle, as heroic speakers in epic and history often do; the man who would rather be a slave is told to turn and run.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  9. Free-man stand, or Free-man fa' st. 4

    The chiasmic balance of 'stand ... fa'' (fall) makes freedom independent of the outcome: the free man is free whether he survives or not. 'Scotland's King and Law' joins loyalty to lawful government with national independence, a careful formulation in the 1790s, when 'Liberty' was a dangerous word. The capitalised 'Free-man' recurs as a title of dignity, opposed to the 'Slave' of the previous stanza. The line's compression, with the subject repeated and the verbs in balance, gives it the force of a motto.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  10. By Oppression's woes ... servile chains st. 5

    The fifth stanza takes the form of an oath ('By ... By ...'), common in heroic speeches. 'Servile' (from Latin servus, slave) chains belong to slaves. 'We will drain our dearest veins' is a vow to shed their own blood so that their sons may be free. The stanza widens the stakes from the soldiers to their descendants, so that the battle becomes a fight for future generations, a standard theme of patriotic oratory. 'They shall be free!' is a prophecy as well as a vow.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'servile'

  11. Usurpers ... Tyrants st. 6

    A usurper seizes power to which he has no right. Burns, writing to Lord Buchan, called the English king 'a cruel, but able usurper' leading the finest army in Europe. 'Tyrants fall in every foe' means that each enemy killed is a tyrant overthrown. These words, with 'Liberty', were the vocabulary of the revolutionary decade. The exclamations of the final stanza are almost entirely abstract nouns and imperatives, the language of the political ode rather than of the ballad.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  12. Liberty's in every blow! st. 6

    Henley and Henderson quote Burns's letter to the Earl of Buchan sending him a copy, in which he says that nothing in history moves him more than Bannockburn, and that Liberty is a prize that 'never canst thou be too dearly bought'. The line distils that sentiment into four words of action. The shift from 'Freedom' to 'Liberty' in the last stanza gives the ode the vocabulary of the age of revolutions, in which 'Liberty' was the watchword of France and of British reformers.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  13. Do or Die st. 6

    'Do or die' was already an English phrase for a desperate resolve, and Burns's use of it as the climax of the song made it famous as a Scottish rallying cry. The close returns to the opening alternative of 'gory bed' or 'Victorie'. The song became an unofficial national anthem of Scotland. The capital letters, as printed in the archive's text, emphasise the slogan; in performance the line is often sung with special force.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'do'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  14. Stanza and tune st. 2

    Each stanza has three rhyming four-stress lines and a short refrain-like fourth line. Thomson disliked 'Hey tuttie taitie' and persuaded Burns to add words to each fourth line so that it would fit 'Lewie Gordon'. After Burns's death opinion favoured the original, and Thomson printed the ode as first written, to its own air, in 1802; Henley and Henderson follow that text.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; National Trust for Scotland collections, 'Tune, Lewis Gordon' (Burns's revision for Thomson)

  15. Anonymous first printing st. 3

    Burns allowed James Perry of the Morning Chronicle, a Whig paper sympathetic to reform, to print the ode provided it appeared as something 'met with by accident and unknown to me'. The paper prefaced it with praise for a faithful imitation of the old Scottish bards and hinted that only one living poet could have written it. As a government excise officer suspected of radical sympathies, Burns had reason to be careful.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  16. Scots or English? st. 4

    Words like 'wha', 'wham', 'aften', 'sae' and 'fa'' are Scots spellings, but the grammar, diction and rhetoric (abstract nouns such as Oppression, Liberty, Usurpers) are those of the English ode. This sits oddly with the Morning Chronicle's claim that it imitated the old bards. The blend suits a song meant for national and political use beyond Scotland.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes
