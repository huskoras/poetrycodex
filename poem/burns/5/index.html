Tam O’ Shanter: A Tale

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

Mythology & Folklore

“Of Brownyis and of Bogillis full is this Buke.”

Gawin Douglas.

When chapman billies leave the street,
And drouthy neibors, neibors, meet;
As market days are wearing late,
And folk begin to tak the gate,
While we sit bousing at the nappy,
An’ getting fou and unco happy,
We think na on the lang Scots miles,
The mosses, waters, slaps and stiles,
That lie between us and our hame,
Where sits our sulky, sullen dame,
Gathering her brows like gathering storm,
Nursing her wrath to keep it warm.

This truth fand honest Tam o’ Shanter,
As he frae Ayr ae night did canter:
(Auld Ayr, wham ne’er a town surpasses,
For honest men and bonie lasses).

O Tam! had’st thou but been sae wise,
As taen thy ain wife Kate’s advice!
She tauld thee weel thou was a skellum,
A blethering, blustering, drunken blellum;
That frae November till October,
Ae market-day thou was na sober;
That ilka melder wi’ the Miller,
Thou sat as lang as thou had siller;
That ev’ry naig was ca’d a shoe on
The Smith and thee gat roarin’ fou on;
That at the Lord’s house, ev’n on Sunday,
Thou drank wi’ Kirkton Jean till Monday,
She prophesied that late or soon,
Thou wad be found, deep drown’d in Doon,
Or catch’d wi’ warlocks in the mirk,
By Alloway’s auld, haunted kirk.

Ah, gentle dames! it gars me greet,
To think how mony counsels sweet,
How mony lengthen’d, sage advices,
The husband frae the wife despises!

But to our tale: Ae market night,
Tam had got planted unco right,
Fast by an ingle, bleezing finely,
Wi reaming saats, that drank divinely;
And at his elbow, Souter Johnie,
His ancient, trusty, drougthy crony:
Tam lo’ed him like a very brither;
They had been fou for weeks thegither.
The night drave on wi’ sangs an’ clatter;
And aye the ale was growing better:
The Landlady and Tam grew gracious,
Wi’ favours secret, sweet, and precious:
The Souter tauld his queerest stories;
The Landlord’s laugh was ready chorus:
The storm without might rair and rustle,
Tam did na mind the storm a whistle.

Care, mad to see a man sae happy,
E’en drown’d himsel amang the nappy.
As bees flee hame wi’ lades o’ treasure,
The minutes wing’d their way wi’ pleasure:
Kings may be blest, but Tam was glorious,
O’er a’ the ills o’ life victorious!

But pleasures are like poppies spread,
You seize the flow’r, its bloom is shed;
Or like the snow falls in the river,
A moment white—then melts for ever;
Or like the Borealis race,
That flit ere you can point their place;
Or like the Rainbow’s lovely form
Evanishing amid the storm.—
Nae man can tether Time nor Tide,
The hour approaches Tam maun ride;
That hour, o’ night’s black arch the key-stane,
That dreary hour he mounts his beast in;
And sic a night he taks the road in,
As ne’er poor sinner was abroad in.

The wind blew as ’twad blawn its last;
The rattling showers rose on the blast;
The speedy gleams the darkness swallow’d;
Loud, deep, and lang, the thunder bellow’d:
That night, a child might understand,
The deil had business on his hand.

Weel-mounted on his grey mare, Meg,
A better never lifted leg,
Tam skelpit on thro’ dub and mire,
Despising wind, and rain, and fire;
Whiles holding fast his gude blue bonnet,
Whiles crooning o’er some auld Scots sonnet,
Whiles glow’rin round wi’ prudent cares,
Lest bogles catch him unawares;
Kirk-Alloway was drawing nigh,
Where ghaists and houlets nightly cry.

By this time he was cross the ford,
Where in the snaw the chapman smoor’d;
And past the birks and meikle stane,
Where drunken Charlie brak’s neck-bane;
And thro’ the whins, and by the cairn,
Where hunters fand the murder’d bairn;
And near the thorn, aboon the well,
Where Mungo’s mither hang’d hersel’.
Before him Doon pours all his floods,
The doubling storm roars thro’ the woods,
The lightnings flash from pole to pole,
Near and more near the thunders roll,
When, glimmering thro’ the groaning trees,
Kirk-Alloway seem’d in a bleeze,
Thro’ ilka bore the beams were glancing,
And loud resounded mirth and dancing.

Inspiring bold John Barleycorn!
What dangers thou canst make us scorn!
Wi’ tippenny, we fear nae evil;
Wi’ usquabae, we’ll face the devil!
The swats sae ream’d in Tammie’s noddle,
Fair play, he car’d na deils a boddle,
But Maggie stood, right sair astonish’d,
Till, by the heel and hand admonish’d,
She ventur’d forward on the light;
And, wow! Tam saw an unco sight!

Warlocks and witches in a dance:
Nae cotillon, brent new frae France,
But hornpipes, jigs, strathspeys, and reels,
Put life and mettle in their heels.
A winnock-bunker in the east,
There sat auld Nick, in shape o’ beast;
A towzie tyke, black, grim, and large,
To gie them music was his charge:
He screw’d the pipes and gart them skirl,
Till roof and rafters a’ did dirl.—
Coffins stood round, like open presses,
That shaw’d the Dead in their last dresses;
And (by some devilish cantraip sleight)
Each in its cauld hand held a light.
By which heroic Tam was able
To note upon the haly table,
A murderer’s banes, in gibbet-airns;
Twa span-lang, wee, unchristened bairns;
A thief, new-cutted frae a rape,
Wi’ his last gasp his gabudid gape;
Five tomahawks, wi’ blude red-rusted:
Five scimitars, wi’ murder crusted;
A garter which a babe had strangled:
A knife, a father’s throat had mangled.
Whom his ain son of life bereft,
The grey-hairs yet stack to the heft;
Wi’ mair of horrible and awfu’,
Which even to name wad be unlawfu’.

As Tammie glowr’d, amaz’d, and curious,
The mirth and fun grew fast and furious;
The Piper loud and louder blew,
The dancers quick and quicker flew,
The reel’d, they set, they cross’d, they cleekit,
Till ilka carlin swat and reekit,
And coost her duddies to the wark,
And linkit at it in her sark!

Now Tam, O Tam! had they been queans,
A’ plump and strapping in their teens!
Their sarks, instead o’ creeshie flainen,
Been snaw-white seventeen hunder linen!—
Thir breeks o’ mine, my only pair,
That ance were plush o’ guid blue hair,
I wad hae gien them off my hurdies,
For ae blink o’ the bonie burdies!
But wither’d beldams, auld and droll,
Rigwoodie hags wad spean a foal,
Louping an’ flinging on a crummock.
I wonder did na turn thy stomach.

But Tam kent what was what fu’ brawlie:
There was ae winsome wench and waulie
That night enlisted in the core,
Lang after ken’d on Carrick shore;
(For mony a beast to dead she shot,
And perish’d mony a bonie boat,
And shook baith meikle corn and bear,
And kept the country-side in fear);
Her cutty sark, o’ Paisley harn,
That while a lassie she had worn,
In longitude tho’ sorely scanty,
It was her best, and she was vauntie.
Ah! little ken’d thy reverend grannie,
That sark she coft for her wee Nannie,
Wi twa pund Scots (’twas a’ her riches),
Wad ever grac’d a dance of witches!

But here my Muse her wing maun cour,
Sic flights are far beyond her power;
To sing how Nannie lap and flang,
(A souple jade she was and strang),
And how Tam stood, like ane bewithc’d,
And thought his very een enrich’d:
Even Satan glowr’d, and fidg’d fu’ fain,
And hotch’d and blew wi’ might and main:
Till first ae caper, syne anither,
Tam tint his reason a thegither,
And roars out, “Weel done, Cutty-sark!”
And in an instant all was dark:
And scarcely had he Maggie rallied.
When out the hellish legion sallied.

As bees bizz out wi’ angry fyke,
When plundering herds assail their byke;
As open pussie’s mortal foes,
When, pop! she starts before their nose;
As eager runs the market-crowd,
When “Catch the thief!” resounds aloud;
So Maggie runs, the witches follow,
Wi’ mony an eldritch skreich and hollow.

Ah, Tam! Ah, Tam! thou’ll get thy fairin!
In hell, they’ll roast thee like a herrin!
In vain thy Kate awaits thy comin!
Kate soon will be a woefu’ woman!
Now, do thy speedy-utmost, Meg,
And win the key-stone o’ the brig;
There, at them thou thy tail may toss,
A running stream they dare na cross.
But ere the keystane she could make,
The fient a tail she had to shake!
For Nannie, far before the rest,
Hard upon noble Maggie prest,
And flew at Tam wi’ furious ettle;
But little wist she Maggie’s mettle!
Ae spring brought off her master hale,
But left behind her ain grey tail:
The carlin claught her by the rump,
And left poor Maggie scarce a stump.

Now, wha this tale o’ truth shall read,
Ilk man and mother’s son, take heed:
Whene’er to Drink you are inclin’d,
Or Cutty-sarks rin in your mind,
Think ye may buy the joys o’er dear;
Remember Tam o’ Shanter’s mare.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The epigraph from Gavin Douglas epigraph

    Gavin Douglas (c. 1474-1522), Bishop of Dunkeld, translated Virgil's Aeneid into Scots; each book has an original prologue. In the Prologue to Book VI, which introduces Aeneas's descent to the underworld, he remarks that the book is full of brownies (household spirits) and bogles (goblins). Henley and Henderson note that the line should read 'Of browneis and of bogillis full this buke'. Prefixing it to a tale of a drunk farmer and a witches' dance is a mock-heroic joke: Tam's ride past Alloway Kirk is set beside Aeneas's journey to the dead.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Gavin Douglas, Eneados (1513), Prologue to Book VI (overview, 'Eneados', Wikipedia)

  2. Occasion: Grose's Antiquities and Alloway Kirk l. 1

    Burns met the antiquary Francis Grose in 1789 and asked him to include Alloway Kirk, near his birthplace, in his survey; Grose agreed if Burns supplied a witch story. Burns first sent prose versions of three local legends, then, in December 1790, the poem. It was printed in Grose's volume in April 1791, and also appeared in the Edinburgh Herald and Edinburgh Magazine in March 1791; Bill Dawson argues that Grose's printing nonetheless has priority. Burns told Mrs Dunlop that it showed 'a finishing polish' he despaired of excelling. The opening paragraph, a general scene of market-day drinking, sets the comic, confiding voice of the narrator.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Bill Dawson, 'The First Publication of Burns's "Tam o' Shanter"', Studies in Scottish Literature 40 (2014), 105-115; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  3. chapman billies ... drouthy neibors ... tak the gate l. 2

    A chapman is a travelling pedlar and 'billies' are fellows or comrades; 'drouthy' means thirsty, that is, fond of drink; 'to tak the gate' is to set off on the road ('gate' is the Scots word for way, from Old Norse gata). In the next lines 'bousing at the nappy' means drinking strong ale (nappy is ale with a head on it), 'fou' is drunk, and 'unco' is very. The narrator includes himself ('we sit bousing'), so the tale opens as fellow-feeling, not sermon.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'gate'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41

  4. Nursing her wrath to keep it warm l. 12

    The wife at home is 'Gathering her brows like gathering storm', a simile that looks ahead to the real storm of the ride. The second line turns anger into something cherished and fed, like the fire the drinkers are sitting by. The couplet is comic and conventional (the shrewish wife of popular tales) but its precision has made it proverbial. Kate is not mocked for long, since the narrator later concedes she was right.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  5. Tam o' Shanter l. 13

    Henley and Henderson report the tradition that Burns drew his hero from Douglas Graham, tenant of the farm of Shanter near Kirkoswald, a convivial man with a scolding wife, whose grey mare's tail was plucked by pranksters as she waited at a tavern door; Graham, so the story goes, blamed the witches of Alloway. 'Tam o' Shanter' means 'Tom of Shanter', the farmer being called by his farm's name in Scots fashion. 'Honest' is affectionate irony. The broad bonnet called a tam o' shanter takes its name from the poem.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  6. Kate's warnings: skellum, blellum, melder l. 18

    Kate's catalogue of charges is given in Scots: a 'skellum' is a rascal, a 'blellum' an idle, babbling fellow; 'ilka melder wi' the Miller' means every time he took corn to be ground (a 'melder' is one load of grain ground at the mill), he sat drinking as long as he had 'siller' (money); 'ev'ry naig was ca'd a shoe on' means every time a nag was shod, he and the smith got 'roarin' fou'. She also accused him of drinking from Sunday to Monday with 'Kirkton Jean', probably Jean Kennedy, who kept a tavern at Kirkoswald with her sister (Henley and Henderson); 'the Lord's house' puns on church and inn. Her prophecy that he will drown in Doon or be caught by warlocks at Alloway Kirk sets up the plot. The passage is comic, but the narrator's next aside admits she was right.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'skellum'; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'melder'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41

  7. Alloway's auld, haunted kirk l. 32

    The parish of Alloway was joined to Ayr in 1690, after which its church was left to fall into ruin; it stands a short way north of the Auld Brig o' Doon. Burns's father is buried in its kirkyard, and Burns was born in a cottage close by, so the place and its legends were familiar to him from childhood. He supplied Grose with witch stories attached to the kirk, and the poem makes it the scene of the Devil's dance.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Undiscovered Scotland, 'Tam o' Shanter' (Alloway Kirk and Brig o' Doon)

  8. ingle ... reaming saats ... Souter Johnie l. 39

    'Ingle' is a fire on the hearth; 'bleezing' is blazing. 'Reaming saats' (usually spelled 'swats') is foaming new ale, which 'drank divinely'. 'Souter' means shoemaker. Henley and Henderson note that a shoemaker named John Davidson (1728-1806) lived near Shanter and moved to Kirkoswald, and that he may lie behind the character, though they call the identification doubtful. The tavern scene is one of warmth, music and flirtation, the comic paradise before the storm.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'swats'

  9. But pleasures are like poppies spread l. 59

    The passage changes register from Scots narrative to the elegant English of Augustan moral verse. Pleasure is like a poppy whose bloom falls when it is picked, snow that falls on a river and melts at once, the aurora borealis that vanishes before one can point to it (the 'Borealis race', the darting northern lights), and a rainbow fading in a storm. The shift is partly parody of poetic moralising and partly genuine lyricism, and critics continue to debate how far the reader should take the lines seriously. Henley and Henderson defend the grammar of 'the snow falls in the river' against editors who emended it.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  10. night's black arch the key-stane l. 69

    The keystone is the central stone at the top of an arch. Midnight is the high point of the night's arch. The image is not idle: the ride will end at another keystone, that of the Brig o' Doon, where Tam reaches safety. The proverb 'Nae man can tether Time nor Tide' (time and tide wait for no man) marks the end of indulgence.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41

  11. The haunted landmarks l. 90

    The paragraph is a grim catalogue: the ford where a chapman was 'smoor'd' (smothered) in snow, the birch trees ('birks') and big stone ('meikle stane') where drunken Charlie broke his neck, the gorse ('whins') and cairn where hunters found a murdered child ('bairn'), and the thorn tree above the well where Mungo's mother hanged herself. Each is a reminder of the fates Tam might share. The run of 'Where ... Where ... Where' builds suspense, and the Doon in flood and the lightning bring the scene to Kirk-Alloway 'in a bleeze' (ablaze).

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'smoor'

  12. John Barleycorn, tippenny, usquabae l. 105

    John Barleycorn is the hero of a traditional ballad (which Burns himself reworked) in which barley is 'killed', buried and crushed, and rises again as ale and spirits. 'Tippenny' is cheap ale sold at twopence the Scots pint; 'usquabae' is whisky, from Gaelic uisge beatha, 'water of life'. 'The swats sae ream'd in Tammie's noddle' (the ale so foamed in his head) that he did not care a 'boddle' (a small copper coin) for devils. The mock invocation treats drink as Tam's Muse and source of courage.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Robert Burns, 'John Barleycorn: A Ballad', Wikipedia ('John Barleycorn'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'usquebaugh'

  13. The witches' dance and Auld Nick's piping l. 116

    The witches dance native Scottish dances, not the 'brent new' (brand-new) French cotillion of polite assemblies, a patriotic joke. In a 'winnock-bunker' (window-seat) sits Auld Nick, the Devil, 'in shape o' beast', a 'towzie tyke' (shaggy dog), who plays the bagpipes until roof and rafters 'dirl' (vibrate). Burns's prose letter to Grose also describes witches dancing round their 'old sooty blackguard master' and his bagpipe. Henley and Henderson record the first drafts of the coffin lines that follow.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'dirl'

  14. The horrors on the holy table l. 130

    The 'haly table' is the communion table, desecrated with relics of crime: bones in 'gibbet-airns' (the iron cage in which a hanged body was displayed), two tiny unbaptised babies, a thief just cut down from a 'rape' (rope), bloody tomahawks and scimitars, a garter used to strangle a baby, and a knife a son used on his father. The objects are lit by the corpses in open coffins 'like open presses' (cupboards), each holding a light by 'devilish cantraip sleight' (witchcraft). The list is gruesome and also absurd (the exotic tomahawks, the counting in fives), mixing gothic horror with comedy. Note that the archive's text reads 'his gabudid gape', a run-together misprint for 'his gab did gape' (his mouth gaped).

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41

  15. The four lines cut on Tytler's advice l. 142

    The omitted passage had three lawyers' tongues turned inside out, seamed with lies like a beggar's rag, and three priests' hearts, rotten and stinking, lying in every corner. A note on the manuscript by Alexander Fraser Tytler says Burns removed them at his request, as out of keeping with the pure horror of the scene. Some readers regret the cut, since the satire on professional hypocrisy sharpened the joke that the Devil's altar is little worse than the world's; others think the poem gains by not straying from Tam.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  16. cleekit ... carlin ... swat and reekit l. 147

    'Cleekit' means hooked or linked arms (from 'cleek', a hook); 'carlin' is an old woman; she 'swat and reekit' (sweated and smoked with heat), 'coost her duddies' (cast off her rags) and 'linkit at it in her sark' (danced briskly in her shift). The archive's line reads 'The reel'd'; the authorised texts have 'They reel'd', which the sense needs. The rising tempo of the verbs imitates the accelerating dance.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'cleek'

  17. Rigwoodie hags wad spean a foal l. 160

    'Spean' means to wean, so the hags are hideous enough to wean a foal by fright. 'Rigwoodie' is a crux. Henley and Henderson explain that a rigwiddie is the rope or chain crossing a horse's saddle; some editors gloss the word as 'gallows-worthy' (from 'widdie', the gallows-rope); they themselves think it most probably means ancient or lean, since the simile concerns a mare. The narrator has just contrasted them with plump young 'queans' in 'snaw-white seventeen hunder linen', very fine linen woven, Henley and Henderson explain, in a reed of 1,700 divisions, for whose sight he would give his only breeches. 'Louping an' flinging on a crummock' means leaping and kicking on a crooked staff.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'rigwiddie'

  18. Nannie's cutty sark l. 171

    'Cutty' means short and 'sark' a shift or chemise; 'harn' is coarse linen, and Paisley was a centre of weaving. The garment, 'In longitude tho' sorely scanty', is the one her grandmother 'coft' (bought) for two pounds Scots, all her riches, never imagining it would grace a witches' dance. Nannie is 'waulie' (jolly, buxom) and 'vauntie' (proud of it). Burns's prose source has a farmer laughing at a witch whose smock was too short. The clipper ship Cutty Sark (1869) takes its name and figurehead from her.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'cutty'

  19. my Muse her wing maun cour l. 179

    'Cour' means to lower or fold. The narrator pretends that the theme is too sublime for his powers, a standard epic topos (the poet's inadequacy before his subject) applied to a witch in a short shift. The device follows the Douglas epigraph and the Augustan similes in giving a local tale epic dress. Even Satan 'fidg'd fu' fain' (fidgeted with delight) and 'hotch'd' (jerked about) at the sight.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'cour'

  20. Weel done, Cutty-sark! l. 189

    'Tint his reason' means lost his reason (the archive's 'a thegither' should read 'a' thegither', altogether). The shout is the turning point: by speaking, Tam reveals himself, and the scene collapses into darkness. Burns's prose version has the farmer laugh 'Weel luppen, Maggy wi' the short sark!' (well leapt). The poem's climax thus comes from Tam's own unguarded delight, the same appetite that kept him at the tavern.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  21. The chase similes l. 193

    The witches pour out like bees ('bizz out wi' angry fyke', buzz out in a fuss) when boy-herds rob their 'byke' (nest), like the enemies of 'pussie' (the hare) when she starts up before them, like a market crowd at the cry 'Catch the thief!'. Extended similes are a feature of epic, and their use here continues the mock-heroic. Henley and Henderson explain the 'plundering herds' as herd-boys who robbed bumble-bees' nests. The witches' cry is 'eldritch' (unearthly).

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'byke'

  22. the key-stone o' the brig l. 208

    In the editions of 1793 and 1794 Burns noted that witches or evil spirits have no power to follow a poor wight any farther than the middle of the next running stream, and that turning back from bogles is more dangerous than going on. His prose letter to Grose calls this a 'universally known fact'. The keystone of the Brig o' Doon marks the midpoint of the river, so Meg must reach it to save Tam. The image fulfils the earlier description of midnight as 'night's black arch the key-stane', the keystone at the top of the arch of night.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  23. Maggie's tail l. 216

    'Ettle' means intent or aim, 'claught' clutched, and 'the fient a tail she had to shake' means she had no tail at all left. The detail comes from local legend: in Burns's prose story the pursuing witch catches only the horse's tail, which gives way 'as if blasted by a stroke of lightning', and the tradition about Douglas Graham's plucked mare follows the same pattern. The hero escapes intact; his horse pays.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  24. The moral l. 224

    The closing paragraph adopts the form of a cautionary ballad ('take heed') but its moral is comically inadequate: Tam has suffered nothing, his mare has lost her tail, and the warning lumps drink with lustful thoughts of 'Cutty-sarks'. Most readers see the ending as a final irony, in keeping with the narrator's earlier mock-solemnity; the poem celebrates the very pleasures it pretends to condemn.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia

  25. Form and language l. 67

    The poem is in four-stress (octosyllabic) rhyming couplets, a metre associated in the eighteenth century with comic narrative, notably Samuel Butler's Hudibras. The language moves between broad Scots for action and dialogue and polished English for moralising and description, and the shifts are part of the comedy.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh: T. C. & E. C. Jack, 1896), vol. I, text pp. 278-87 and notes pp. 433-41; 'Tam o' Shanter (poem)', Wikipedia
