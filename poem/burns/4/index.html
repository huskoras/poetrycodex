Auld Lang Syne

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

Relationships

Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
And never brought to mind?
Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
And auld lang syne!

Chorus.—For auld lang syne, my dear,
For auld lang syne.
We’ll tak a cup o’ kindness yet,
For auld lang syne.

And surely ye’ll be your pint stowp!
And surely I’ll be mine!
And we’ll tak a cup o’kindness yet,
For auld lang syne.
For auld, &c.

We twa hae run about the braes,
And pou’d the gowans fine;
But we’ve wander’d mony a weary fit,
Sin’ auld lang syne.
For auld, &c.

We twa hae paidl’d in the burn,
Frae morning sun till dine;
But seas between us braid hae roar’d
Sin’ auld lang syne.
For auld, &c.

And there’s a hand, my trusty fere!
And gie’s a hand o’ thine!
And we’ll tak a right gude-willie waught,
For auld lang syne.
For auld, &c.

Study notes by the editors

  1. auld lang syne st. 1

    The phrase combines 'auld' (old), 'lang' (long) and 'syne' (since, ago), so it means literally 'old long since', the good old days. 'For auld lang syne' means 'for the sake of old times'. Burns wrote to Mrs Dunlop in December 1788 asking whether the Scots phrase was not 'exceedingly expressive'. In the song the phrase is a refrain and an object: the friends are asked not to forget either old acquaintance or old times.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'syne'

  2. Origin and printing st. 1

    Burns told Thomson that this 'old song of the olden times' had never been printed or written down until he took it down from an old man's singing. Henley and Henderson suspect the claim was partly a modest device, since Burns had certainly reworked older material, though they allow that he may have heard a traditional fragment. The song was printed in James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum, vol. V (issued after Burns's death, 1796-97), no. 413, and in Thomson's Select Collection (1799), where it was joined to the tune now sung everywhere. How much is Burns's own remains disputed; critics often credit him especially with the childhood stanzas.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; University of Glasgow, Centre for Robert Burns Studies, news item on the first appearance of 'Auld Lang Syne' (2021)

  3. Earlier 'Old Long Syne' songs st. 1

    Watson's 'Old Long Syne', a love-song attributed variously to Sir Robert Ayton and to Francis Sempill, opens 'Should old acquaintance be forgot / And never thought upon'; Allan Ramsay wrote another love-song on the same opening; and anti-Union and Jacobite parodies used the tune. Henley and Henderson reprint these. Burns kept the opening question but turned the song from love to friendship and memory, and from formal English to intimate Scots.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  4. a cup o' kindness chorus

    The 'cup o' kindness' is a cup drunk to good will. In Scots the chorus is often sung 'my jo' (sweetheart, dear) in place of 'my dear'. 'Yet' means still, even now, after all the years. The chorus, sung after every stanza, is the part of the song nearly everyone knows; it turns the opening rhetorical question into an answer: old acquaintance will not be forgotten, because we will drink to it now.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  5. be your pint stowp st. 2

    A 'stowp' or stoup is a drinking vessel or tankard; to 'be' one's pint-stoup means to pay for it. The stanza is jovial and practical, grounding the sentiment in the shared drink. In the Thomson manuscript this stanza came last; in Johnson's printing and in Henley and Henderson's text it is second, as in the archive. The tone is that of a tavern reunion between old friends, each insisting on paying his share, and it keeps the song from sentimentality.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'stoup'

  6. braes ... gowans st. 3

    'Braes' are hillsides or banks, 'pou'd' pulled or picked, and 'gowans' daisies (Bellis perennis). The childhood memory is the emotional heart of the song: two children together in the fields, set against the 'weary fit' of adult wandering. The plain verbs ('run about', 'pou'd') and the small, exact flower make the memory concrete. Gathering daisies is a child's pastime, and the stanza's pairing of past play with present weariness gives the song much of its emotional force. Many readers think this and the next stanza are most fully Burns's own.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'gowan'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  7. mony a weary fit st. 3

    'Fit' is the Scots form of 'foot', so the line means they have trudged many weary miles since. The stanza contrasts the free running of childhood with the burdened wandering of adulthood, separated by 'Sin' auld lang syne'. The word 'Sin'' (since) answers the 'syne' of the refrain, so the last line of each memory stanza measures the distance between then and now.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'fit'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  8. paidl'd in the burn ... till dine st. 4

    A 'burn' is a stream or brook; 'dine' is dinner-time, the midday meal in rural Scotland. The image of children paddling all morning pairs with the next line's 'seas between us braid hae roar'd', broad seas that have roared between them since, a powerful contrast of the tiny stream with the oceans of emigration and separation. Henley and Henderson print the song with this stanza fourth, after the stanza on the braes, so the memories move from hillside to stream.

    Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'burn'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  9. seas between us braid st. 4

    'Braid' means broad. The line is often read in the context of Scottish emigration, to the colonies and the Americas, which was common in Burns's day; the song's later popularity among Scots abroad owes something to it. The verb 'roar'd' makes the separation loud and violent beside the quiet 'paidl'd'. Whether or not Burns had a particular separation in mind, the line generalises the experience of friends divided by time and distance, which is why the song has served for leave-takings of every kind.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; University of Glasgow, Centre for Robert Burns Studies, news item on the first appearance of 'Auld Lang Syne' (2021)

  10. fere st. 5

    'Fere' (also spelled 'fiere') is an old word for a companion or mate, from Old English gefera, 'one who travels with'. It is often mis-sung as 'my trusty friend'. The handshake that follows ('gie's a hand o' thine') gives the custom of linking hands while singing the last stanza. The word survives mainly in Scots verse and ballads, which adds to the song's antique, traditional feel. Its sense of a comrade on the road fits the stanzas about wandering and separation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fere'; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'fere'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes

  11. a right gude-willie waught st. 5

    A 'waught' is a deep drink, a draught; 'gude-willie' means full of good will, so the phrase repeats the 'cup o' kindness' of the chorus. Henley and Henderson note that the accent of the popular tune encouraged singers to divide it wrongly as 'a richt guid willie-waught', inventing a non-existent 'willie-waught'. The final stanza thus ends the song in a shared, ceremonial drink.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; Dictionaries of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'waucht'

  12. The tune chorus

    Burns told Thomson that the old air was 'but mediocre'. The melody now universal is first documented with the words in Thomson's Select Collection of 1799 (it resembles an air in William Shield's overture to Rosina, 1782, though borrowing either way is thought unlikely); it has since become the song of farewells and of New Year's Eve across the English-speaking world.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes; University of Glasgow, Centre for Robert Burns Studies, news item on the first appearance of 'Auld Lang Syne' (2021)

  13. Form and stanza order st. 2

    The verses alternate lines of four and three stresses, the common metre of Scottish and English ballads and hymns, which makes the song easy to sing in company. In the Thomson manuscript the 'pint stowp' stanza comes last; Johnson's order, followed here, places it second, so the song moves from greeting and drink, through childhood memory and separation, to the handshake and the final draught.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. III, text and notes
