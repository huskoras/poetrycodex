Holy Willie’s Prayer

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

Social Commentaries

O Thou, who in the heavens does dwell,
Who, as it pleases best Thysel’,
Sends ane to heaven an’ ten to hell,
A’ for Thy glory,
And no for ony gude or ill
They’ve done afore Thee!

I bless and praise Thy matchless might,
When thousands Thou hast left in night,
That I am here afore Thy sight,
For gifts an’ grace
A burning and a shining light
To a’ this place.

What was I, or my generation,
That I should get sic exaltation,
I wha deserve most just damnation
For broken laws,
Five thousand years ere my creation,
Thro’ Adam’s cause?

When frae my mither’s womb I fell,
Thou might hae plunged me in hell,
To gnash my gums, to weep and wail,
In burnin lakes,
Where damned devils roar and yell,
Chain’d to their stakes.

Yet I am here a chosen sample,
To show thy grace is great and ample;
I’m here a pillar o’ Thy temple,
Strong as a rock,
A guide, a buckler, and example,
To a’ Thy flock.

O Lord, Thou kens what zeal I bear,
When drinkers drink, an’ swearers swear,
An’ singin there, an’ dancin here,
Wi’ great and sma’;
For I am keepit by Thy fear
Free frae them a’.

But yet, O Lord! confess I must,
At times I’m fash’d wi’ fleshly lust:
An’ sometimes, too, in wardly trust,
Vile self gets in:
But Thou remembers we are dust,
Defil’d wi’ sin.

O Lord! yestreen, Thou kens, wi’ Meg—
Thy pardon I sincerely beg,
O! may’t ne’er be a livin plague
To my dishonour,
An’ I’ll ne’er lift a lawless leg
Again upon her.

Besides, I farther maun allow,
Wi’ Leezie’s lass, three times I trow—
But Lord, that Friday I was fou,
When I cam near her;
Or else, Thou kens, Thy servant true
Wad never steer her.

Maybe Thou lets this fleshly thorn
Buffet Thy servant e’en and morn,
Lest he owre proud and high shou’d turn,
That he’s sae gifted:
If sae, Thy han’ maun e’en be borne,
Until Thou lift it.

Lord, bless Thy chosen in this place,
For here Thou hast a chosen race:
But God confound their stubborn face,
An’ blast their name,
Wha bring Thy elders to disgrace
An’ public shame.

Lord, mind Gaw’n Hamilton’s deserts;
He drinks, an’ swears, an’ plays at cartes,
Yet has sae mony takin arts,
Wi’ great and sma’,
Frae God’s ain priest the people’s hearts
He steals awa.

An’ when we chasten’d him therefor,
Thou kens how he bred sic a splore,
An’ set the warld in a roar
O’ laughing at us;—
Curse Thou his basket and his store,
Kail an’ potatoes.

Lord, hear my earnest cry and pray’r,
Against that Presbyt’ry o’ Ayr;
Thy strong right hand, Lord, make it bare
Upo’ their heads;
Lord visit them, an’ dinna spare,
For their misdeeds.

O Lord, my God! that glib-tongu’d Aiken,
My vera heart and flesh are quakin,
To think how we stood sweatin’, shakin,
An’ p-’d wi’ dread,
While he, wi’ hingin lip an’ snakin,
Held up his head.

Lord, in Thy day o’ vengeance try him,
Lord, visit them wha did employ him,
And pass not in Thy mercy by ’em,
Nor hear their pray’r,
But for Thy people’s sake, destroy ’em,
An’ dinna spare.

But, Lord, remember me an’ mine
Wi’ mercies temp’ral an’ divine,
That I for grace an’ gear may shine,
Excell’d by nane,
And a’ the glory shall be thine,
Amen, Amen!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Willie Fisher and Gavin Hamilton l. 1

    Burns's prefatory 'Argument' (Glenriddell manuscript), quoted by Henley and Henderson, explains that 'Holy Willie', an oldish bachelor elder of Mauchline, and his minister 'Father Auld' lost a case before the Presbytery of Ayr against Gavin Hamilton, a Mauchline lawyer, thanks partly to the pleading of Robert Aiken; 'the muse overheard him at his devotions'. William Fisher (1737-1809), an elder of Mauchline kirk, was the chief accuser of Hamilton in the sessional case over neglect of ordinances and Sabbath-breaking. The Presbytery found for Hamilton in 1785. The poem was too dangerous for the Kilmarnock volume (1786); it circulated in manuscript and was first printed as an anonymous chapbook in 1789 (Patrick Scott argues, at Kilmarnock), and in book form only after Burns's death.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, pp. 320-1, notes (quoting Burns's 'Argument'); Patrick Scott, 'The First Publication of "Holy Willie's Prayer"', Scottish Literary Review 7.1 (2015), 1-18; 'Holy Willie's Prayer', Wikipedia (summary of dates)

  2. predestination: 'ane to heaven an' ten to hell' ll. 1-6

    The Westminster Confession, the standard of the Church of Scotland, teaches that by God's decree, 'for the manifestation of his glory', some are predestined to everlasting life and others foreordained to everlasting death (III.3), and that the elect are chosen without any foresight of faith or good works (III.5). Willie states the doctrine with comic bluntness and arithmetic ('ane ... ten'), and the phrase 'as it pleases best Thysel'' makes God's choice look arbitrary. The opening stanza is accurate theology in a mouth that will go on to show its moral consequences: if salvation has nothing to do with conduct, the elect can sin with confidence.

    Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), ch. III.3-5 (Wikisource); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 321 (Burns's 'Argument' on 'tippling orthodoxy')

  3. Standard Habbie and the parody of prayer ll. 4-6

    Burns uses the stanza named after Robert Sempill's elegy on Habbie Simson, the piper of Kilbarchan (c. 1640), revived by Ramsay and Fergusson and so associated with Burns that it is also called the Burns stanza: three tetrameters on one rhyme, a dimeter, a fourth tetrameter on the same rhyme, and a closing dimeter on the rhyme of line four. The short lines act as punchlines ('A' for Thy glory', 'Thro' Adam's cause'). Henley and Henderson treat it as a parody of Calvinist intercession. Its vocabulary is the language of Presbyterian prayer and the Bible, mixed with Ayrshire Scots, so that Willie exposes himself as he speaks; critics often see it as anticipating the dramatic monologue of Browning.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 320, note (on the poem as parody); 'Burns stanza', Wikipedia (Standard Habbie)

  4. a burning and a shining light l. 11

    In John 5:35 Jesus says of John the Baptist, 'He was a burning and a shining light'. Willie claims the phrase for himself as the beacon of his parish, 'To a' this place', having just thanked God that 'thousands' are 'left in night'. The self-application is the first of many biblical texts he bends to his own glory. Henley and Henderson's stage-by-stage collation shows that this stanza is stable across the manuscripts, so the boast belongs to the poem from its earliest form.

    John 5:35 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 322, textual notes on stanza ii

  5. original sin: five thousand years ere my creation ll. 13-18

    The doctrine of original sin (Romans 5:12; Westminster Confession VI) holds that Adam's guilt is imputed to all his descendants. Popular chronologies, such as Archbishop Ussher's, dated the Creation to about 4000 BC, so 'Five thousand years' roughly measures the time from Adam to Willie. The humility is false: Willie admits a 'damnation' he owes to Adam's 'broken laws' only to make his own election more wonderful ('That I should get sic exaltation'). Stewart's text reads ''fore my creation'.

    Romans 5:12 (King James Version); Westminster Confession of Faith, ch. VI (Wikisource); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 322, textual notes on stanza iii

  6. to gnash my gums, burning lakes ll. 19-24

    Willie imagines that God might have plunged him into hell at birth ('When frae my mither's womb I fell'). The phrase recalls the 'weeping and gnashing of teeth' of the damned in Matthew 8:12 and 13:42, but a baby has no teeth, so 'gums' makes the vision grotesque. The 'burnin lakes' are the 'lake which burneth with fire and brimstone' (Revelation 21:8), and the devils 'Chain'd to their stakes' recall bear-baiting or the fires of martyrs. The Calvinist teaching that unregenerate infants might be damned was a frequent target of Burns's New Light friends.

    Matthew 8:12; Revelation 21:8 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 322, textual notes on stanza iv

  7. a pillar o' Thy temple ... a buckler ll. 25-30

    In Revelation 3:12 the promise is 'Him that overcometh will I make a pillar in the temple of my God'. Willie claims the reward as already his: 'a chosen sample', 'Strong as a rock'. A 'buckler' is a small shield; the Psalms call God 'my buckler' (Psalm 18:2), so Willie assigns himself a divine attribute. Henley and Henderson note that the manuscripts read 'A guide, a ruler, and example', which stresses his office as an elder; the archive's 'buckler' is the reading of Stewart's early printed text.

    Revelation 3:12; Psalm 18:2 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 322, textual notes on stanza v

  8. the 'zeal' stanza ll. 31-36

    Henley and Henderson report that after stanza v Stewart's early printed text (1801-2) has a stanza found in no later manuscript, probably from an earlier copy; they thought it inferior and that it broke the run of thought, and so left it out. Some modern texts print it in square brackets, and the archive prints it. In it Willie boasts of his zeal against drinkers, swearers, singing and dancing, from which God's 'fear' keeps him free. The stanza does set up the hypocrisy that follows, since he is soon confessing drunkenness and lust, and some editors keep it for that reason. 'Kens' means knows; 'keepit' means kept.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 322, textual note after stanza v; Dictionary of the Scots Language, s.v. 'ken, v.'

  9. fash'd; wardly; we are dust ll. 37-42

    The Centenary glossary gives 'fash', to trouble; Willie is 'fash'd wi' fleshly lust' as if the lust were an outside nuisance. 'Wardly trust' (Stewart reads 'warldly') is trust in worldly goods, and 'Vile self gets in' acknowledges pride. He then quotes Psalm 103:14, 'he remembereth that we are dust', turning a text of God's pity into an excuse. This is the turn of the poem from boasting to confession, and the confession is managed like a legal plea.

    Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Fash'; Psalm 103:14 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, textual note on stanza vi

  10. yestreen ... wi' Meg ll. 43-48

    'Yestreen' means yesterday evening, last night (Centenary glossary). The dash after 'Meg' is a comic aposiopesis: God already 'kens', so Willie need not say what happened. His prayer is not for forgiveness of the sin but that it may never be 'a livin plague / To my dishonour', that is, a child whose birth would expose him to kirk discipline. Elders like Fisher policed exactly such cases; Burns himself was rebuked for fornication by the Mauchline session. 'Lift a lawless leg / Again upon her' is coarse and comic, the language of the farmyard set in a prayer.

    Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Yestreen'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, pp. 317-18, note on William Auld (to The Twa Herds), on his rebuke of Burns and Jean Armour in 1786

  11. Leezie's lass; fou; steer ll. 49-54

    'Maun allow' means must admit; 'trow' means believe, think. 'Fou' (full) means drunk, and 'steer' here means to touch or meddle with, a euphemism for sexual intercourse (Centenary glossary: 'to touch, meddle with'). Stewart's text reads 'Wad ne'er hae steer'd her'. Drunkenness, a sin Willie condemned in the 'zeal' stanza, becomes his excuse for another sin. The shift from 'I' to 'Thy servant true' as he pleads is the rhetoric of a defendant addressing a judge.

    Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Fou' and 'Steer'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, textual notes on stanza viii; Dictionary of the Scots Language, s.v. 'fou, adj.' and 'steer, v.'

  12. the fleshly thorn ll. 55-60

    Paul writes that 'there was given to me a thorn in the flesh, the messenger of Satan to buffet me, lest I should be exalted above measure' (2 Corinthians 12:7). Willie takes the verse literally and sexually: his lust is a humbling trial sent by God because he is 'sae gifted', and so he must bear it 'Until Thou lift it'. The reasoning turns sin into evidence of election, the antinomian danger that Burns's satire attributes to hyper-Calvinism. Stewart reads 'Beset Thy servant' and ''Cause he's sae gifted'.

    2 Corinthians 12:7 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, textual notes on stanza ix

  13. Thy chosen in this place ... a chosen race ll. 61-66

    The language echoes Scripture's 'chosen generation' (1 Peter 2:9) and the election of Israel (Deuteronomy 7:6). The turn from confession to cursing is abrupt: 'But God confound their stubborn face, / An' blast their name'. Henley and Henderson note variant readings; in the later manuscripts the last couplet reads 'Wha bring thy rulers to disgrace', where the archive has 'Thy elders'. The 'elders' are the lay members of the kirk session who, with the minister, exercised parish discipline.

    1 Peter 2:9; Deuteronomy 7:6 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, textual notes on stanza x

  14. Gavin Hamilton ll. 67-70

    Hamilton, a writer (solicitor) in Mauchline and Burns's landlord at Mossgiel, was popular and generous, which is exactly Willie's complaint: he 'drinks, an' swears, an' plays at cartes' (cards), yet 'Wi' great and sma'' his 'takin arts' (engaging manners) steal the people's hearts from 'God's ain priest'. Burns's 'Argument' calls Hamilton one of the most irreproachable characters in the country. The session's charges included neglect of public worship and of family worship and Sabbath-breaking. 'Mind ... deserts' means remember what he deserves: Willie asks God to take note, as if God kept accounts like a kirk session.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, pp. 320-1, notes on Fisher and Hamilton (quoting the 'Argument'); Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Cartes'

  15. God's ain priest: William Auld l. 71

    Henley and Henderson identify 'God's ain priest' as William Auld (1709-91), minister of Mauchline from 1742, whom they describe as a bitter Calvinist and rigid disciplinarian; he rebuked Burns and Jean Armour in 1786. Calling a Presbyterian minister 'priest' is itself an irony: the Reformed church rejected priesthood, and the word smells of the Catholicism Willie would abhor. Auld appears again, in the later manuscripts, at ll. 89-90, where 'Auld wi' hingin lip gaed sneakin / And hid his head'. Burns satirised him as 'Daddy Auld' in other kirk satires such as The Kirk's Alarm.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, note on stanza xi, l. 5, and pp. 317-18, note on William Auld (to The Twa Herds); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, The Kirk's Alarm, text and notes

  16. splore; curse thou his basket ... Kail an' potatoes ll. 73-78

    'Chasten'd' means disciplined; 'splore' means an uproar or row (the Centenary glossary cites this line). The curse is from the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28:17, 'Cursed shall be thy basket and thy store'. The bathetic last line has a precise point: Henley and Henderson note that one charge against Hamilton was that he sent his servants to dig potatoes on a Sunday. 'Kail' is colewort, cabbage, the staple of the Scots kitchen garden. The anticlimax from biblical malediction to vegetables is one of the poem's best jokes.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, note on stanza xii, l. 6; Deuteronomy 28:17 (King James Version); Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Splore' and 'Kail'

  17. the Presbytery of Ayr ll. 79-84

    In the Church of Scotland the kirk session (minister and elders) is subject to the presbytery, a court of ministers and elders from a group of parishes; above that stand the synod and the General Assembly. The Presbytery of Ayr vindicated Hamilton against the Mauchline session in 1785; the session carried the matter to the synod, but in the end had to give Hamilton the certificate of good standing he sought (sources differ on whether this was 1785 or 1786). 'Make it bare' recalls Isaiah 52:10, 'The LORD hath made bare his holy arm', and 'visit' means punish, as in the biblical 'visiting the iniquity' (Exodus 20:5).

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 321 (the Presbytery's decision and the appeal); Isaiah 52:10; Exodus 20:5 (King James Version)

  18. Robert Aiken ll. 85-90

    Aiken, a writer in Ayr and early patron of Burns, successfully defended Hamilton before the presbytery; Burns's 'Argument' credits his 'oratorical powers'. Willie remembers the session's members standing 'sweatin', shakin, / An' p-'d wi' dread' (the dash spares the reader 'pish'd'), while Aiken held up his head. In Stewart's early text Aiken, 'wi' hingin lip an' snakin' (sneering, according to the Centenary glossary), held up his head; in the later manuscripts the lines describe Auld, who 'wi' hingin lip gaed sneakin / And hid his head'. The archive follows Stewart's early reading, and editors differ over which version is the original.

    W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 323, note on stanza xiv (variants and identification); Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Snakin'

  19. Thy day o' vengeance ll. 91-96

    The phrase is prophetic (Isaiah 61:2; 63:4). 'Try' means bring to trial, judge. In this text 'him' is Aiken, the advocate, and 'them wha did employ him' are Hamilton and his supporters, so the curse falls on those who had most humbled the session. 'Pass not in Thy mercy by 'em' inverts the language of mercy: God is asked not to pass over them, as the angel passed over the Israelites' houses (Exodus 12:23), but to strike. The repeated 'dinna spare' completes the vindictive litany.

    Isaiah 61:2; Exodus 12:23 (King James Version); W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, p. 324, note on stanza xv

  20. grace an' gear ... Amen ll. 97-102

    'Gear' means goods, money, wealth (Centenary glossary). 'Mercies temp'ral an' divine' joins worldly prosperity to grace, as if election were a commercial arrangement. The final lines echo the doxology of the Lord's Prayer ('thine is ... the glory') and return to the opening 'A' for Thy glory', closing the prayer's circle: the glory is God's, the gear is Willie's. Burns's 'Argument' describes Willie's religion as 'spiritualized bawdry which refines to liquorish devotion'; the ending shows its self-interest. Critics have praised the poem as Burns's finest satire, whose target is less one elder than a theology that makes hypocrisy easy.

    Henley and Henderson, Centenary Burns, vol. IV (1896), Glossary, s.v. 'Gear'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, Edinburgh, 1896), vol. II, pp. 320-1 (the 'Argument' and editors' comment); Matthew 6:13 (King James Version)
