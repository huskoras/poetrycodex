Love

John Anderson, My Jo

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

From Poems and Songs, 1789

Love

John Anderson, my jo, John,
When we were first acquent;
Your locks were like the raven,
Your bonie brow was brent;
But now your brow is beld, John,
Your locks are like the snaw;
But blessings on your frosty pow,
John Anderson, my jo.

John Anderson, my jo, John,
We clamb the hill thegither;
And mony a cantie day, John,
We’ve had wi’ ane anither:
Now we maun totter down, John,
And hand in hand we’ll go,
And sleep thegither at the foot,
John Anderson, my jo.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Song and source title; st. 1

    Burns contributed hundreds of songs to James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum, often reworking old songs to traditional tunes. Several older 'John Anderson' songs existed: a bawdy one, of which a version later appeared in The Merry Muses of Caledonia, and a short text printed in Percy's Reliques (1765); the claim, found in Brewer's Dictionary of Phrase and Fable, that an old version satirised the Catholic clergy is not firmly established. Burns kept the refrain and tune and made a love song of long married life. Editors such as Kinsley and Dick discuss the older texts; further stanzas sometimes printed with the song in the nineteenth century are not by Burns.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'; James C. Dick, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London, 1903), notes to 'John Anderson, my jo'; The Traditional Ballad Index, 'John Anderson, My Jo (I)' (the older text in Percy's Reliques, 1765); The Traditional Ballad Index, 'John Anderson, My Jo, John' (Burns's song, Scots Musical Museum, 1790)

  2. jo l. 8

    The Scottish National Dictionary records 'jo' (also 'joe') as a sweetheart or darling, used as a term of endearment to either sex, and derives it from 'joy'. The phrase 'my jo' is common in Scots song (for example in the traditional song 'My Jo Janet'). Repeated as a refrain, it frames each stanza as affectionate address. Here a courting word is used by a wife to her husband after a long marriage, and the warmth of the term outlasts the physical changes the stanza records.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'jo'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  3. acquent l. 2

    'Acquent' is the Scots form of 'acquainted' (past participle). The DSL records 'acquent' in this sense. The line places the first stanza in a contrast of then and now: then black hair and smooth brow, now baldness and white hair. Burns uses the past tense of courtship only for two lines before turning to the present; the brevity of the memory suits the song's acceptance of age rather than longing for youth.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'acquent'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  4. locks like the raven l. 3

    The Song of Solomon 5:11 describes the beloved: 'his locks are bushy, and black as a raven'. Whether or not Burns intended the biblical echo, the simile is traditional in love song. The black of the raven is set against the white of 'snaw' in l. 6, so that the stanza turns on a colour contrast that marks the passing of years.

    Bible, King James Version, Song of Solomon 5:11; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  5. brent l. 4

    The DSL gives 'brent' of the forehead as high, smooth and unwrinkled, a usage common in Scots verse; the root sense is steep or upright. 'Bonie' is Burns's usual spelling of 'bonny', handsome. The pair 'brent ... beld' in ll. 4-5 makes the change from youth to age audible in the near-rhyme of the words. The rhyme 'acquent / brent' (ll. 2, 4) depends on Scots pronunciation and shows that the song is meant to be sung in Scots, not anglicised.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'brent'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  6. beld l. 5

    The DSL records 'beld' (also 'bell', 'bauld') as bald. 'Your brow is beld' means his hair has receded from the forehead. 'Snaw' is snow. The plainness of the description, without lament, is characteristic of the song's tone: age is stated affectionately, not mourned. Burns's alliterative pairing 'brow ... beld' follows 'bonie brow ... brent' in the previous line, so that the repeated sounds hold together the two halves of life, youth and age, in one description of the same face.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'beld'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  7. pow l. 7

    The DSL records 'pow' as the head or scalp. 'But blessings on your frosty pow' is the turn of the stanza: 'But' rejects regret, and the blessing transforms the signs of age into objects of love. The winter imagery (snaw, frosty) is the natural metaphor of old age. 'Blessings on' is a common Scots formula of affection. The stanza ends on the refrain, so the blessing and the endearment together answer the losses listed earlier; the effect is of a wife who loves her husband's white head more, not less.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'pow'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  8. Stanza and refrain st. 2

    The metre is close to the common ballad measure doubled: lines of four and three stresses, rhyming on the second and fourth lines of each quatrain (acquent/brent, snaw/jo; thegither/anither, go/jo). The name 'John' recurs inside the lines too, a repeated tender address suited to singing. The first stanza contrasts past and present; the second follows the whole of married life as a journey, up the hill and down to its foot. The song's brevity is part of its effect.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'; James Johnson, ed., The Scots Musical Museum, vol. III (Edinburgh, 1790), 'John Anderson my jo'; James C. Dick, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London, 1903), notes to 'John Anderson, my jo'

  9. The hill of life l. 10

    'Clamb' is a Scots past tense of 'climb', and 'thegither' is 'together'. The hill of life, climbed in youth and descended in age, is a traditional emblem (popular prints showed the 'steps of life' rising and falling). Here the emblem is made personal and shared: 'thegither' recurs in l. 15. The 'foot' of the hill is the grave. The two halves of the stanza mirror the two halves of life: the climb and its 'cantie' days in ll. 10-12, the descent in ll. 13-16. The plain, physical verbs (clamb, totter, go, sleep) make the allegory concrete.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'clim, climb'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  10. cantie l. 11

    The DSL records 'canty' (also 'cantie') as lively, cheerful, pleasant. The line 'And mony a cantie day, John, / We've had wi' ane anither' summarises a lifetime of happiness in the plainest terms, and 'ane anither' (one another) stresses mutuality. 'We've had' gives the sense of a shared stock of happiness looked back on from old age. The adjective belongs to the vocabulary of convivial and domestic contentment that runs through Burns's songs.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'canty'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  11. maun l. 13

    'Maun' is the regular Scots form of 'must'. 'Totter' gives the physical feebleness of age, but the next line 'hand in hand' turns weakness into companionship. The acceptance in 'maun' is not resignation but calm recognition. Burns uses the plain present 'Now' to mark the old couple's actual situation, and the verbs that follow are all in the future ('we'll go', 'sleep'), so the last four lines look calmly forward to death. The DSL records 'maun' as the ordinary Scots word for 'must'.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary / DOST), s.v. 'maun'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'

  12. Sleeping together at the foot ll. 15-16

    The image of death as sleep is biblical and traditional, and burial side by side was the conventional hope of married couples. The song ends not with lament but with the refrain, so the last word is the endearment 'my jo'. Critics have often praised the song as one of the finest expressions of married love in the language, a tenderness the more striking given its origins in a bawdy older song.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'; W. E. Henley and T. F. Henderson, eds., The Poetry of Robert Burns (Centenary Edition, 4 vols, Edinburgh, 1896-97), vol. III, notes to the song

  13. The wife's voice l. 14

    Nothing in the text names the speaker, but the address to John and the tradition of the song indicate the wife. Burns composed many songs in women's voices, often for tunes associated with older women's songs. 'Hand in hand' may recall the end of Paradise Lost, where Adam and Eve leave Eden 'hand in hand', though this is a suggestion rather than an established allusion; either way it pictures equal companionship.

    James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), text and commentary on 'John Anderson my Jo'; James C. Dick, ed., The Songs of Robert Burns (London, 1903), notes to 'John Anderson, my jo'; John Milton, Paradise Lost, xii.648
