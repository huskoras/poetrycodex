Nature

To a Mouse, on Turning Her Up in Her Nest with the Plough, November, 1785

by Robert Burns 1759–1796

Nature

Wee, sleekit, cow’rin, tim’rous beastie,
O, what a panic’s in thy breastie!
Thou need na start awa sae hasty,
Wi’ bickering brattle!
I wad be laith to rin an’ chase thee,
Wi’ murd’ring pattle!

I’m truly sorry man’s dominion,
Has broken nature’s social union,
An’ justifies that ill opinion,
Which makes thee startle
At me, thy poor, earth-born companion,
An’ fellow-mortal!

I doubt na, whiles, but thou may thieve;
What then? poor beastie, thou maun live!
A daimen icker in a thrave
’S a sma’ request;
I’ll get a blessin wi’ the lave,
An’ never miss’t!

Thy wee bit housie, too, in ruin!
It’s silly wa’s the win’s are strewin!
An’ naething, now, to big a new ane,
O’ foggage green!
An’ bleak December’s winds ensuin,
Baith snell an’ keen!

Thou saw the fields laid bare an’ waste,
An’ weary winter comin fast,
An’ cozie here, beneath the blast,
Thou thought to dwell—
Till crash! the cruel coulter past
Out thro’ thy cell.

That wee bit heap o’ leaves an’ stibble,
Has cost thee mony a weary nibble!
Now thou’s turn’d out, for a’ thy trouble,
But house or hald,
To thole the winter’s sleety dribble,
An’ cranreuch cauld!

But, Mousie, thou art no thy lane,
In proving foresight may be vain;
The best-laid schemes o’ mice an ’men
Gang aft agley,
An’lea’e us nought but grief an’ pain,
For promis’d joy!

Still thou art blest, compar’d wi’ me
The present only toucheth thee:
But, Och! I backward cast my e’e.
On prospects drear!
An’ forward, tho’ I canna see,
I guess an’ fear!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion: Mossgiel, November 1785 title; l. 1

    The full title fixes the poem to a working moment: 'on turning her up in her nest with the plough, November, 1785'. Burns's brother Gilbert, who farmed Mossgiel with him, later reported that the poem was composed while Robert was still holding the plough. A further tradition, told by early editors on the recollection of the farm-hand John Blane, says that the boy chased the mouse to kill it and was called back by his master. Whether or not the poem was literally made in the furrow, the anecdote shaped its reception as the work of a 'ploughman poet'. It appeared in Poems, Chiefly in the Scottish Dialect (Kilmarnock, 1786), the volume that made Burns famous. The address to a small creature, mixing comic diminutives with serious moral reflection, belongs with 'To a Louse' and 'To a Mountain Daisy' in the same book.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; 'To a Mouse', Wikipedia (summary of Gilbert Burns's account and later reception)

  2. sleekit l. 1

    The adjective describes the mouse's glossy fur, and the line's run of diminutive epithets ('wee', 'cow'rin', 'tim'rous', 'beastie') establishes a tone of affectionate pity. Modern Scots speakers often hear 'sleekit' in its extended sense of 'sly, smooth-mannered, untrustworthy', and some readers enjoy the hint that the speaker half expects the mouse to be a thief (as stanza 3 admits). But the primary sense here is physical, as editors gloss it; the poem's movement is towards sympathy, not suspicion. Note the elisions marked by apostrophes ('cow'rin', 'tim'rous'): they record spoken Ayrshire pronunciation and keep the four-stress line quick.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sleek'

  3. The 'Standard Habbie' (Burns stanza) st. 1

    Burns writes in the stanza named 'Standard Habbie' after Robert Sempill of Beltrees's mock-elegy on the Kilbarchan piper Habbie Simson (c. 1640); Allan Ramsay gave it the name, and Ramsay and Robert Fergusson made it the chief vehicle of eighteenth-century Scots vernacular verse before Burns used it so often that it is also called the 'Burns stanza'. The short fourth and sixth lines act like a bob or tag: here 'Wi' bickering brattle!' and 'Wi' murd'ring pattle!' Its usual associations are comic and satirical, so Burns's use of it for compassion and, at the end, near-despair is part of the poem's surprise. The rhyme scheme in RPO's description is aaabab.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language, entry on 'Habbie' (stanza aaabab named by Allan Ramsay; mock-elegy ascribed to Sempill of Beltrees, c. 1640); 'Burns stanza', Wikipedia; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note

  4. bickering brattle l. 4

    'Brattle' in Scots is a clattering noise or a short, sudden rush; the Scottish National Dictionary also records the verb 'to move quickly with a clatter of feet'. 'Bickering' (Kilmarnock 'bickerin') is the Scots verb 'bicker', to move quickly with a pattering sound, used of running water and of quick feet. Together the alliterating words make a comically loud noise for so small an animal, seen from the mouse's panic. The line is the stanza's first short 'tag' line.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'brattle'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  5. pattle l. 6

    The pattle (or pettle) was a short staff with a flat blade, used to scrape clay and soil off the mould-board or ploughshare. The Scottish National Dictionary cites this very line under the word. 'Murd'ring' turns a farm tool into a weapon, and the speaker refuses to use it. The detail fits the farm-hand tradition that the boy ran after the mouse to kill it and was stopped. The hyperbole is half comic, but it prepares for the poem's serious account of human power over creatures.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'pattle'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  6. man's dominion l. 7

    In Genesis 1:26-28 God gives man 'dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing'. Burns, raised on Scripture in Calvinist Ayrshire, takes the word but tilts it: dominion has become domination, and it has 'broken' a prior 'social union'. The apology ('I'm truly sorry') is mock-formal and also sincere. Critics commonly read the stanza against eighteenth-century ideas of sympathy and of a lost harmony between creatures, ideas that Burns knew from Pope and the moral philosophers.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 1:26-28; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Robert Crawford, The Bard: Robert Burns, a Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 2009)

  7. nature's social union l. 8

    The phrase sounds like moral philosophy rather than farm talk, and the shift into polished English diction is deliberate. Pope's Essay on Man (1733-34), Epistle III, imagines an original 'state of Nature' in which 'Man walk'd with beast, joint tenant of the shade', a harmony later destroyed by human tyranny over animals; Burns knew Pope well. The idea also chimes with the Scottish Enlightenment's stress on sympathy and fellow-feeling. The 'ill opinion' of the next line is the mouse's justified fear of man. Critics note how Burns makes the abstract phrase concrete by setting it beside a ruined nest.

    Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man (1733-34), Epistle III ('the state of Nature'), Project Gutenberg text; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Robert Crawford, The Bard: Robert Burns, a Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 2009)

  8. earth-born companion / fellow-mortal ll. 11-12

    In Genesis man is formed 'of the dust of the ground' (2:7) and told 'dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (3:19). Burns uses this shared origin to level the hierarchy that 'dominion' set up: the ploughman and the mouse are companions in being 'earth-born' and 'fellow-mortal'. 'Fellow-mortal' is the stanza's climactic tag-line, and its seriousness is unusual for the stanza form. The idea anticipates the last stanzas, where the comparison will turn against the man.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 3:19; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  9. thou may thieve ... thou maun live ll. 13-14

    'Whiles' means 'sometimes' and 'maun' means 'must'. The mouse is a pest, and the farmer knows it; the stanza's generosity lies in his calculation that its theft is trivial against the need to live. The colloquial shrug 'What then?' keeps the tone conversational. Readers have compared the argument with the moral sense that need excuses small theft, but Burns's emphasis is practical and neighbourly rather than legal.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  10. A daimen icker in a thrave l. 15

    'Daimen' means rare, occasional; the Scottish National Dictionary quotes this line under the phrase 'daimen-icker'. An 'icker' is a single ear of grain. A 'thrave' was a measure of cut corn, reckoned at twenty-four sheaves (two stooks of twelve). The farmer's arithmetic shows how little the mouse takes. The gesture also recalls the biblical law that the harvester should leave the gleanings and the corners of his field 'for the poor and stranger' (Leviticus 19:9-10), which is perhaps why he expects 'a blessin' for it.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'daimen'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Leviticus 19:9-10

  11. the lave l. 17

    The Scots noun 'lave' (Old English laf, 'remainder') is common in Burns; RPO glosses it 'rest'. The line half-seriously claims that sparing the mouse's portion will bring God's blessing on what remains, a proverbial piety that gives the generosity a comic edge. 'An' never miss't!' closes the stanza with a shrug. The idiom is that of farm proverb: a little given away is a blessing on the whole.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lave'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  12. silly wa's l. 20

    The older English and Scots sense of 'silly' is 'helpless, deserving pity, weak' (compare 'silly sheep'), not 'foolish'; RPO glosses it 'feeble'. 'Wa's' is 'walls' and 'win's' is 'winds'. The comic diminutive 'wee bit housie' and the domestic word 'walls' turn the nest into a little cottage, so that its ruin anticipates the human theme of eviction in stanza 6. Many of Burns's tenant-farming neighbours knew that fear.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'silly'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  13. big ... foggage green ll. 21-22

    The Scottish National Dictionary defines foggage as regrowth of grass after haymaking, or rough grass left uneaten among the stubble, and quotes these lines. RPO glosses 'big' as 'build' and 'foggage' as 'coarse grass'. In November there is nothing green to build with, so the loss is final. The stanza moves from the ruin itself to the season, with December's winds in the tag-lines.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'foggage'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note

  14. snell l. 24

    'Snell' is an old word shared by Scots and northern English (Old English snell, 'quick, active'), which in Scots came to mean bitterly cold and cutting, of weather. Paired with the English 'keen', it doubles the sense, a common Burns habit of setting a Scots word beside its English equivalent. The tag line ends the stanza on the threat of winter, which the next stanza dramatises.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'snell'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  15. coulter / cell ll. 29-30

    The coulter (Latin culter, 'knife') slices the soil before the share turns it. 'Cruel' gives the tool the guilt its user feels. The dash and the exclamation 'Till crash!' break the stanza's syntax as the blade breaks the nest. 'Cell' suggests a hermit's or monk's small room, and so gives the mouse a kind of dignity; the word also anticipates 'house or hald' in the next stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coulter'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  16. But house or hald l. 34

    'But' here is the Scots preposition 'without'. 'Hald' is 'hold', a holding or place of shelter; RPO glosses the phrase 'house or habitation' and compares 'Address to the Deil'. The alliterating legal-sounding pair suits a tenancy: the mouse is evicted from the home it laboured over, a fate many Scottish tenant farmers feared in the 1780s, including Burns's own family at Lochlea and Mossgiel.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Robert Crawford, The Bard: Robert Burns, a Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 2009)

  17. thole l. 35

    The Scottish National Dictionary cites this very line under the sense 'to suffer, endure'. The word was once general English (Old English þolian, Old Norse þola) but survived chiefly in Scots and northern dialects. The mouse must endure the 'sleety dribble' (the sleet dripping through) and the frost of the following tag-line. Burns uses it again in 'The Twa Dogs' of tenants who must 'thole a factor's snash' (put up with a land-agent's abuse), a reminder that the mouse's endurance mirrors that of poor farmers.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'thole'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note

  18. cranreuch l. 36

    The Scottish National Dictionary defines 'cranreuch' as hoar-frost and treats it as a Scots word of probably Gaelic origin, the second part perhaps reòthadh, 'frost'. It closes the stanza with a strong, rough-sounding Scots word, typical of how Burns saves his densest dialect for physical hardship.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'cranreuch'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note

  19. no thy lane l. 37

    The Scots idiom 'my lane', 'thy lane' means 'by myself, by yourself, alone'; RPO glosses 'no thy lane' as 'not alone'. The phrase opens the turn of the poem from the mouse to the human condition. The mouse 'proved' (tested and found) that foresight may be useless; so do men. The construction is idiomatic in Scots ('his lane', 'their lane') and has no exact English equivalent. Here it introduces a fellowship of failure between the speaker and the mouse, the closest the two come in the poem.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem

  20. The best-laid schemes ... Gang aft agley ll. 39-40

    'Gang aft' is 'go often'. The lines generalise the mouse's loss into a statement about all planning, and their currency as a proverb is largely Burns's doing; John Steinbeck took the title Of Mice and Men (1937) from them. The archive's text prints 'o' mice an 'men' with a misplaced apostrophe; the Kilmarnock text reads 'o' Mice an' Men'. In context the lines are not consoling: they lead to 'grief an' pain' instead of 'promis'd joy'. Biographers relate the gloom to Burns's own position in 1785-86, with the Mossgiel farm failing and plans to emigrate to Jamaica.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'agley'; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Robert Crawford, The Bard: Robert Burns, a Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 2009)

  21. agley l. 40

    The Scottish National Dictionary explains 'agley' as 'a-' plus 'gley', a squint, so that the literal sense is 'squinting, askew' or 'off target', and the figurative 'wrong, awry'. Its earliest dated quotation is this line from 'To a Mouse'. RPO glosses it 'amiss'. The image is of an aim that goes astray, a plan that misses its mark. Because the lines became proverbial, they are often quoted in anglicised form ('go oft awry'), which loses the Scots word's visual image of a squint.

    Dictionary of the Scots Language (Scottish National Dictionary), s.v. 'agley'; Representative Poetry Online, 'To a Mouse', ed. G. G. Falle, text from the Kilmarnock edition with glosses and note

  22. The present only toucheth thee ll. 43-48

    The diction moves into formal English ('toucheth', 'prospects drear') for the moral, then back to Scots in 'Och!' and 'canna'. The thought, that animals are spared knowledge of the future, has a long history; Pope's Essay on Man, Epistle I, says that Heaven 'from all creatures hides the book of Fate' and that the lamb, if it had human reason, could not play as it does. Burns turns the commonplace personal: the man looks back on 'prospects drear' and forward with guessing fear. Biographers connect the mood with Burns's troubles of 1785-86. The ending reverses the poem's starting hierarchy, in which the man pitied the frightened beast.

    Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man (1733-34), Epistle I, Project Gutenberg text; James Kinsley, ed., The Poems and Songs of Robert Burns, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968), commentary on the poem; Robert Crawford, The Bard: Robert Burns, a Biography (London: Jonathan Cape, 2009)
