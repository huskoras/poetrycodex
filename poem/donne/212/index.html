Religion

Hymne to God my God, in my sicknesse

by John Donne 1572–1631

From Poems (Grierson, 1912)

Religion

Since I am comming to that Holy roome,
Where, with thy Quire of Saints for evermore,
I shall be made thy Musique; As I come
I tune the Instrument here at the dore,
And what I must doe then, thinke here before.

Whilst my Physitians by their love are growne
Cosmographers, and I their Mapp, who lie
Flat on this bed, that by them may be showne
That this is my South-west discoverie
Per fretum febris, by these streights to die,

I joy, that in these straits, I see my West;
For, though theire currants yeeld returne to none,
What shall my West hurt me? As West and East
In all flatt Maps (and I am one) are one,
So death doth touch the Resurrection.

Is the Pacifique Sea my home? Or are
The Easterne riches? Is Ierusalem?
Anyan, and Magellan, and Gibraltare,
All streights, and none but streights, are wayes to them,
Whether where Iaphet dwelt, or Cham, or Sem.

We thinke that Paradise and Calvarie,
Christs Crosse, and Adams tree, stood in one place;
Looke Lord, and finde both Adams met in me;
As the first Adams sweat surrounds my face,
May the last Adams blood my soule embrace.

So, in his purple wrapp'd receive mee Lord,
By these his thornes give me his other Crowne;
And as to others soules I preach'd thy word,
Be this my Text, my Sermon to mine owne,
Therfore that he may raise the Lord throws down.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and date title, l. 1

    Izaak Walton, Donne's first biographer, dated the hymn 23 March 1630 (1631 in modern reckoning), eight days before Donne's death. A copy among the papers of Sir Julius Caesar says it was written in Donne's 'great sickness in December 1623', the illness that produced the Devotions upon Emergent Occasions. Grierson weighed both and inclined to Walton; G. C. Moore Smith, Gardner and most later scholars favour 1623. The poem was first printed in 1635. The title's doubled 'God my God' recalls the Psalms (e.g. Psalm 22:1, 'My God, my God').

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, notes on the Hymne (and appendix note); N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary

  2. that Holy roome l. 1

    The speaker is at the door of heaven. The 'Holy roome' contrasts with the room in which he lies sick, and the whole first stanza turns on this threshold. Walton, who dates the poem to Donne's last illness, tells how Donne posed in his shroud for the drawing from which his monument in St Paul's was carved; Grierson adds that Donne designed the monument himself and that its epitaph is the natural supplement to the hymn.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, quoting Walton; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  3. Quire of Saints ... I shall be made thy Musique ll. 2-3

    Endicott glosses 'thy Musique' as God's company of musicians: the speaker will not only sing but become music. The image draws on Revelation's heavenly choirs (Revelation 5:8-9, 14:2-3) and on the old idea of the soul as a harmony. 'Quire' is the usual seventeenth-century spelling of 'choir'. The future tense ('I shall be made') makes heaven a transformation rather than a destination: the singer becomes the song. Donne often wrote of the soul's harmony with God, and the image prepares for the tuning of the next line.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 3; Bible (KJV), Revelation 14:2-3

  4. I tune the Instrument here at the dore l. 4

    Musicians tuned their instruments outside the chamber before playing. The instrument is the speaker himself, body and soul, and the poem is his tuning. The last line of the stanza makes the poem a meditation in advance: 'what I must doe then, thinke here before'. The musical image also recalls Herbert's habit of figuring devotion as tuning, as in 'The Temper'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  5. my Physitians ... Cosmographers, and I their Mapp ll. 6-7

    Cosmographers described and mapped the world. The physicians poring over the patient are pictured as geographers studying a chart. Grierson adds that Sir Julius Caesar's manuscript reads 'by their Loer' (lore, learning) instead of 'by their love', which he thought probably the true reading; most editors keep 'love'. The map conceit governs the next three stanzas. The love of the physicians makes them attentive readers of his body.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368 and Addendum, note to l. 6; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  6. my South-west discoverie l. 9

    On the map of his body, the fever's heat is the hot south, and death is the west, where the sun sets. Voyages of discovery in Donne's lifetime sought routes to the Pacific through the south-west, by the Straits of Magellan. The doctors' examination therefore shows that he is about to make the final voyage. The pun on 'discovery' (revelation, uncovering) adds a medical sense.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  7. Per fretum febris l. 10

    Endicott translates the phrase as 'through the straits of fever' and notes the pun on 'straits', meaning both narrow sea passages and hardships. Ancient etymologists connected fretum with fervere, to boil, so the word may also suggest the burning of fever. The Latin also gives the line a ceremonial, inscription-like quality, as if it were a legend on a map.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 10; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  8. straits l. 11

    The ordinary sense of 'in great straits' (in hardship) meets the geographical one. The sick man is in straits; but straits, as navigators knew, lead to oceans. Grierson quotes sermons in which Donne uses the same figure: a narrower way, but to a better land, through straits to the Pacific Sea. The pain of sickness is thus the passage to his 'home'.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on ll. 18-20 (p. 368), quoting Sermons on straits and the Pacific; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strait'

  9. I see my West l. 11

    The west, where the sun sets, is the conventional region of death. The surprise is 'I joy': the sight of death is not fearful because, as the next lines argue, west and east meet. The phrase may also glance at the sea-currents of the Straits of Magellan, which ran westward so strongly that ships could not return ('theire currants yeeld returne to none').

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  10. theire currants yeeld returne to none l. 12

    Contemporary accounts described the Straits of Magellan as hard to pass eastward because of currents and winds. The image fuses this sailor's knowledge with the proverbial 'undiscovered country, from whose bourn no traveller returns' (Hamlet). The fact that there is no return is accepted with equanimity, since the voyage has a happy end. Grierson cites Hakluyt's Principal Navigations for the geography of the straits.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on ll. 18-20 (p. 368)

  11. As West and East / In all flatt Maps ... are one ll. 13-15

    On a flat map the extreme east and west edges represent the same meridian. Donne used the figure in a sermon on Psalm 6, quoted by Grierson: paste a flat map on a round body and west and east are all one. In the same sermon he recalls that one of Christ's names is Oriens, 'the East' (Zechariah 6:12 in the Vulgate), so that death's west becomes Christ's east. His epitaph in St Paul's says he looks toward him whose name is the Rising. The parenthesis '(and I am one)' makes the speaker himself a flat map, lying on his bed.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, quoting Sermons on Psalm 6:8-10; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 13

  12. So death doth touch the Resurrection l. 15

    The stanza's conclusion is a theological claim made through geography. 'Touch' is precise: on the globe the two edges are contiguous. The line also echoes the Christian tradition of burying the dead facing east, toward the rising sun and the expected return of Christ, which Grierson illustrates from Walton's account of Donne's monument, turned to the east. The conclusion is stated as calmly as a geometrical proof.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, quoting Walton on the monument; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  13. the Pacifique Sea ... The Easterne riches ... Ierusalem ll. 16-17

    Each destination is also a figure for heaven: the Pacific is the sea of peace (Latin pacificus); the 'Easterne riches' are the spices and treasures of the Moluccas and the Far East; Jerusalem is the earthly city and the New Jerusalem of Revelation 21. Grierson explains that, on the maps of the period, each could be reached only through a strait.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, note on ll. 18-20; Bible (KJV), Revelation 21:2

  14. Anyan, Magellan, Gibraltare l. 18

    Grierson explains the condensed syntax: whether his home is the Pacific, the East or Jerusalem, it is reached through a strait. The Strait of Anian, a supposed passage between America and Asia, is often identified with the Bering Strait (Endicott glosses it so); maps in Hakluyt show it leading to Japan and the Moluccas. Magellan's strait, at the tip of South America, leads to the Pacific; Gibraltar leads to the Mediterranean and Jerusalem. Grierson rejects Grosart's and Chambers's emendations of the line and notes that Donne uses the geography of his youth.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, note on ll. 18-20; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 18

  15. Japhet, Ham, Shem l. 20

    Genesis 10 traces the nations of the earth to Shem, Ham and Japheth; by tradition Japheth's descendants peopled Europe, Ham's Africa and Shem's Asia. The line thus maps the three straits and three destinations onto the three continents of the old world, so that all the known world is gathered into the speaker's voyage. The early modern division of the world among Noah's sons was a commonplace of maps and universal histories. Each continent, like each strait, becomes a way to the one destination, so that the whole of human geography points toward heaven.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 10:1; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  16. Paradise and Calvarie, / Christs Crosse, and Adams tree ll. 21-22

    Patristic and medieval legend placed Golgotha ('the place of a skull') over Adam's grave, and some traditions said the wood of the cross came from the tree of Eden. Endicott notes that no source gives a precise identification of Paradise with Calvary, though such linking of biblical places was common in early commentary, and that 'place' may mean region. The tradition supports the typology of the next lines.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Hymn to God, My God, in my Sickness', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 21; Bible (KJV), John 19:17

  17. both Adams ll. 23-25

    Paul calls Christ 'the last Adam' (1 Corinthians 15:45) and says 'as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive' (15:22; compare Romans 5:12-19). The first Adam's curse was to eat bread 'in the sweat of thy face' (Genesis 3:19); the sick man's fever-sweat is that curse. The last Adam's blood, shed on the cross, will 'embrace' his soul. Christ's own sweat 'as it were great drops of blood' in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44) links the two.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:45; Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Bible (KJV), Luke 22:44

  18. in his purple wrapp'd l. 26

    At the Passion the soldiers put on Jesus 'a purple robe' and a crown of thorns (John 19:2). Purple is royal, and it is also the colour of blood, so the speaker asks to be wrapped in Christ's blood and kingship. 'Wrapp'd' suggests the shroud as well, recalling Walton's account of Donne posing in his winding-sheet. The colour thus unites mockery, kingship, blood and burial in a single word.

    Bible (KJV), John 19:2; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, quoting Walton

  19. By these his thornes give me his other Crowne l. 27

    The crown of thorns (John 19:2) is set against 'a crown of righteousness' (2 Timothy 4:8) and 'the crown of life' promised to those who endure temptation (James 1:12). Suffering is the condition of glory: the sickness that makes him share the thorns is the way to the other crown. The two crowns sum up the poem's logic of opposites meeting: thorns and glory, west and east, the first and the last Adam. 'These his thornes' identifies the speaker's present pains with Christ's.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Timothy 4:8; Bible (KJV), James 1:12

  20. Be this my Text, my Sermon to mine owne l. 29

    Donne had been Dean of St Paul's since 1621 and was the most celebrated preacher of his age. A sermon begins with a text from Scripture; here the 'text' is the sentence that follows, the poem's last line, which reads like a biblical maxim. The turn inward ('mine owne' soul) gathers the poem's public images into private application. The speaker becomes both preacher and congregation.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, epitaph quoted with the Hymne

  21. Therfore that he may raise the Lord throws down l. 30

    The idea is biblical: 'The LORD killeth, and maketh alive: he bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up' (1 Samuel 2:6; compare Psalm 146:8, 'the LORD raiseth them that are bowed down'). Grierson compares a phrase from the Devotions: cast down so as not to be cast away. The inverted syntax places 'raise' before 'throws down', so that the sentence reverses the order of events and ends on the fall, which the reader knows is temporary. The stanza form, five pentameter lines rhyming ababb, closes the poem on its rhyme with 'Crowne'.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Samuel 2:6; Bible (KJV), Psalm 146:8; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 368, appendix note quoting the Devotions
