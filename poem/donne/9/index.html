Love

(untitled)

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

At the round earths imagin'd corners, blow
Your trumpets, Angells, and arise, arise
From death, you numberlesse infinities
Of soules, and to your scattred bodies goe,
All whom the flood did, and fire shall o'erthrow,
All whom warre, death, age, agues, tyrannies,
Despaire, law, chance, hath slaine, and you whose eyes,
Shall behold God, and never tast deaths woe,
But let them sleepe, Lord, and mee mourne a space,
For, if above all these, my sinnes abound,
'Tis late to aske abundance of thy grace,
When wee are there; here on this lowly ground,
Teach mee how to repent; for that's as good
As if thou'hadst seal'd my pardon, with thy blood.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Holy Sonnets and this sonnet's place l. 1

    The Holy Sonnets were first printed in 1633 and 1635 and survive in different orders in the manuscripts; Grierson, following the editions of 1635 to 1669, numbers this one VII, while in the 1633 set of twelve it comes fourth. Helen Gardner argued that most were written around 1609 to 1610, before Donne's ordination, and grouped this sonnet with others on the Last Things. Louis Martz showed how the sonnets follow the stages of Ignatian meditation: a vivid 'composition of place' (here the Judgement), followed by analysis and a closing prayer. This one opens with a cosmic scene and closes on private repentance.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 322, headnote to Holy Sonnets; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Holy Sonnets: At the round earth's imagin'd corners, blow', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1, general note; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary; Louis L. Martz, The Poetry of Meditation (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1954), on the Holy Sonnets

  2. the round earths imagin'd corners l. 1

    Revelation 7:1 describes four angels standing at 'the four corners of the earth'. Donne, aware that the earth is a globe, calls the corners 'imagin'd': the visionary geography of Scripture is held together with modern cosmology in one paradox. Grierson, quoting the line in his introduction among Donne's 'magnificent openings', prints 'quarters' there, though the text of his edition reads 'corners', as here.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'Holy Sonnets: At the round earth's imagin'd corners, blow', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1; Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:1; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, quoting the opening

  3. blow / Your trumpets, Angells ll. 1-2

    Paul writes that 'the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible' (1 Corinthians 15:52), and that the Lord will descend 'with the trump of God' (1 Thessalonians 4:16); in Matthew 24:31 the angels are sent 'with a great sound of a trumpet' to gather the elect from the four winds. Donne commands the angels as if he were directing the Judgement himself, an audacity the sestet will retract. The doubled imperative 'arise, arise' enacts the summons.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; Bible (KJV), 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Bible (KJV), Matthew 24:31

  4. you numberlesse infinities / Of soules ll. 3-4

    The phrase multiplies infinity, a characteristic Donne hyperbole. The line break after 'infinities' leaves the word hanging before it is defined as 'soules'. Compare Revelation 7:9, which follows the four angels of 7:1 with 'a great multitude, which no man could number'. The resurrection is pictured as a vast gathering of every soul since Adam, which makes the later turn to one sinner all the more striking.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:9; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  5. to your scattred bodies goe l. 4

    Christian doctrine taught the resurrection of the body, not merely the survival of the soul: the soul, separated at death, would be reunited with its own flesh. Theologians debated how God would reassemble bodies whose dust had been scattered, eaten or burned, a question Donne returns to in his sermons. The imperative 'goe' sends the souls down, reversing the usual image of ascent.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:42-44; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  6. the flood did, and fire shall o'erthrow l. 5

    The line spans the whole of history in two catastrophes. The Flood is Genesis 6 to 8; the final burning comes from 2 Peter 3:6-7, where the world that 'perished' by water is 'reserved unto fire against the day of judgment', and 2 Peter 3:10, where 'the elements shall melt with fervent heat'. The tenses, past and future ('did ... shall'), place the speaker between them.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:6-7; Bible (KJV), Genesis 7:21-23

  7. The catalogue of deaths ll. 6-7

    The lines pile up nouns in a breathless list: 'warre, death, age, agues, tyrannies, / Despaire, law, chance'. 'Agues' are fevers; 'tyrannies' are deaths at the hands of tyrants; 'Despaire' is suicide; 'law' is judicial execution. The enumeration, crossing the line-ending, gives the octave its sweep before the sestet narrows to one sinner. Grammatically the singular 'hath slaine' follows a long plural subject, an irregularity of the period.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ague'

  8. death / dearth l. 6

    Grierson argued that 'dearth' must be right: the poet is listing the modes in which death comes, and death itself cannot be one of them. He thought the 'death' of l. 8 ('deaths woe') helped cause the error. Most modern editions, including Gardner's, read 'dearth', meaning famine, which completes a traditional set of scourges with war. This text keeps the 1633 reading 'death'.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 325, note to VII. 6; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary

  9. you whose eyes, / Shall behold God, and never tast deaths woe ll. 7-8

    Paul says 'We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed' (1 Corinthians 15:51), and those who are 'alive and remain' will be caught up to meet the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17). The phrase 'taste of death' is from the Gospels (Luke 9:27, Matthew 16:28), where Jesus says some standing there 'shall not taste of death' till they see the kingdom of God. The octave thus embraces the dead of all ages and the living at the end.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:51; Bible (KJV), Luke 9:27

  10. The volta l. 9

    The sonnet is Petrarchan in its octave (abbaabba) but closes, as Donne's Holy Sonnets usually do, with a couplet (cdcdee). The turn at l. 9 is sudden: having summoned the trumpets, the speaker now asks that the dead 'sleepe' a while longer, the usual Christian euphemism for death. The cosmic scene shrinks to one man's need: 'and mee mourne a space'. In Martz's terms the move can be read as the shift from composition of place to self-examination in meditation.

    Louis L. Martz, The Poetry of Meditation (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1954), on the Holy Sonnets; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  11. mourne a space l. 9

    'Space' in the sense of an interval of time was common in the period. Mourning here is penitential sorrow for sin, the first stage of repentance in the theology of the time. The petition for time is ironic, since it asks God to postpone the event the octave had so grandly commanded.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'space'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  12. my sinnes abound ... abundance of thy grace ll. 10-11

    Donne plays on 'abound' and 'abundance': if his sins exceed all the deaths just listed, he will need more grace than it is safe to ask for at the last moment. Paul's assurance in Romans 5:20 that grace abounds more than sin lies behind the pun, giving a hopeful undertone to the fear. 'Tis late to aske ... When wee are there': once at the Judgement, the time for repentance is past.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 5:20; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  13. here on this lowly ground l. 12

    The antithesis of 'there' and 'here' splits the line at its centre. 'Lowly' carries both physical and moral senses: the low earth and the humility proper to penitence. The opening's vast globe has contracted to the ground the speaker stands on. The phrase also recalls that Adam was formed 'of the dust of the ground' (Genesis 2:7), so that 'lowly ground' is both the place and the material of fallen humanity, where alone repentance can be learned before the trumpets sound.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  14. Teach mee how to repent l. 13

    In the Reformed theology of Donne's Church, a sinner cannot repent by his own power; contrition is itself God's work. The plea therefore asks for grace at its root. The final couplet claims that true repentance is 'as good' as a sealed pardon: being taught to repent is the evidence that one is already forgiven. Critics differ on how far the Holy Sonnets reflect Calvinist anxiety about election; the couplet here is comparatively confident.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  15. seal'd my pardon, with thy blood l. 14

    A royal pardon was valid only when sealed. Donne makes Christ's blood the sealing wax, drawing on the Epistle to the Hebrews, where Christ's blood ratifies the new covenant (Hebrews 9:12-22: 'without shedding of blood is no remission'). The sonnet ends with a legal image of assurance after the terror of the Judgement. The paradox is that the blood which seals the pardon has already been shed; what the speaker needs is to be taught to repent, which is 'as good' as the seal.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 9:22; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem
