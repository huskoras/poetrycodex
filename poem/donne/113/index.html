Love

Aire and Angels.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

Twice or thrice had I loved thee,
Before I knew thy face or name;
So in a voice, so in a shapelesse flame,
Angells affect us oft, and worship'd bee,
Still when, to where thou wert, I came
Some lovely glorious nothing I did see,
But since, my soule, whose child love is,
Takes limmes of flesh, and else could nothing doe,
More subtile then the parent is,
Love must not be, but take a body too,
And therefore what thou wert, and who
I bid Love aske, and now
That it assume thy body, I allow,
And fixe it selfe in thy lip, eye, and brow.

Whilst thus to ballast love, I thought,
And so more steddily to have gone,
With wares which would sinke admiration,
I saw, I had loves pinnace overfraught,
Ev'ry thy haire for love to worke upon
Is much too much, some fitter must be sought;
For, nor in nothing, nor in things
Extreme, and scattring bright, can love inhere;
Then as an Angell, face, and wings
Of aire, not pure as it, yet pure doth weare,
So thy love may be my loves spheare;
Just such disparitie
As is twixt Aire and Angells puritie,
'Twixt womens love, and mens will ever bee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Aire and Angels': title and argument title; ll. 1-2

    The title names the poem's controlling conceit: angels, being pure spirit, assume bodies of air to appear to men. Stanza 1 argues that the speaker's love, at first bodiless, must take the beloved's body; stanza 2 finds her body too much and settles instead on her love as the airy 'body' for his. The poem was first printed in 1633. John R. Roberts observed that no other Donne poem has received so many contradictory readings, and a whole number of the John Donne Journal (1990) was given to it. The opening echoes Neoplatonic ideas of love before sight, as the soul recognises a beauty it already knows.

    Achsah Guibbory, ed., 'Interpreting "Aire and Angels"', special issue, John Donne Journal 9.1 (1990), essays by Schoenfeldt, Herz, Roberts, Stein and others; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  2. a voice ... a shapelesse flame ll. 3-4

    The speaker compares his earlier, unseen love to the way angels 'affect' (act upon, move) people without visible form: as a voice, or a shapeless flame. Biblical models include the angel in the burning bush (Exodus 3:2) and the voice from heaven; the shapeless fire recalls Pentecost (Acts 2:3). 'Worship'd bee' hints at the idolatry of worshipping angels (Colossians 2:18), and at the lover's worship of his mistress.

    Bible (King James Version), Exodus 3:2; Acts 2:3; Colossians 2:18; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  3. Some lovely glorious nothing l. 6

    The oxymoron sums up the first phase of love: brilliant, adorable, and empty, because love has not yet fixed on a body. It anticipates the second stanza, where 'nothing' recurs ('nor in nothing, nor in things / Extreme'): love can rest neither in nothing nor in excess. Some readers hear wit at the beloved's expense, as if she were a beautiful nothing; most take the phrase as describing the speaker's undirected passion.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Achsah Guibbory, ed., 'Interpreting "Aire and Angels"', special issue, John Donne Journal 9.1 (1990), essays by Schoenfeldt, Herz, Roberts, Stein and others

  4. my soule, whose child love is ll. 7-10

    Scholastic psychology held that the soul cannot act in this life without bodily organs: it 'takes limmes of flesh, and else could nothing doe'. Love, being the soul's child and 'more subtile' (finer, more rarefied) than its parent, needs a body still more. The argument is a parody-in-earnest of philosophical reasoning, a hallmark of Donne's wit; the same doctrine of body and soul shapes 'The Extasie' ('Else a great Prince in prison lies').

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I, q. 76, a. 5

  5. assume ll. 11-14

    'Assume' was the theologians' word for an angel's taking of a temporary body (Aquinas, Summa I q. 51 'Whether angels assume bodies'), and for the Incarnation. The speaker bids love inquire 'what' and 'who' she is, then allows it to settle in her 'lip, eye, and brow', a brief Petrarchan blazon. The triple rhyme 'now / allow / brow' closes the stanza firmly, as if the problem were solved; stanza 2 shows it is not.

    Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I, q. 51, a. 2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assume'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  6. ballast ... pinnace overfraught ll. 15-18

    Ballast is weight stowed in a ship to steady it; a pinnace is a small, light vessel used as a tender. Giving love her body as ballast so as to sail 'more steddily', the speaker finds he has 'overfraught' (overloaded) the boat with 'wares' that would sink 'admiration' (wonder). The mercantile vocabulary belongs to the poem's language of exploration. Thomas Docherty's reading of the pinnace as sexually suggestive was challenged by Bernard Richards; most editors take the image as a compliment: her beauty is too much for love to carry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pinnace'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Achsah Guibbory, ed., 'Interpreting "Aire and Angels"', special issue, John Donne Journal 9.1 (1990), essays by Schoenfeldt, Herz, Roberts, Stein and others

  7. Ev'ry thy haire l. 19

    The 1633 reading 'Ev'ry thy haire' is unusual, and the 1650-69 editions changed it to 'Thy every hair'. Grierson kept 1633, arguing that the inverted order adds the sense 'even the least of your hairs'. The hyperbole caps the ballast conceit: every single hair is 'much too much' weight for love, so 'some fitter' object must be found. The idea that a single hair could overwhelm love also parodies Petrarchan hyperbole.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'Aire and Angels'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  8. inhere ll. 21-22

    In scholastic philosophy, accidents (qualities) 'inhere' in a substance. Love, like a quality, needs a fitting subject: neither 'nothing' (the bodiless phase of stanza 1) nor 'things / Extreme, and scattring bright' (the beloved's overwhelming beauty, which disperses radiance). The lines echo the Aristotelian mean between extremes and set up the closing solution. 'Scattring bright' suggests dispersing rays of light, too brilliant to rest upon.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'inhere'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  9. an Angell, face, and wings / Of aire ll. 23-24

    Grierson glosses the lines from Aquinas, who holds that angels assume bodies not of earth or water but of air, condensed so that it takes shape and colour. Air is 'not pure as it' (the angel) but still pure. Grierson cites parallels in Tasso and Fairfax's translation, and in Milton's corporeal angels (Paradise Lost), as well as Neoplatonic ideas of daemons with airy bodies. The point for the poem: the beloved's love is to be the airy 'body' of the speaker's love, a medium fine enough not to swamp it.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'Aire and Angels'; Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I, q. 51, a. 2; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  10. my loves spheare l. 25

    Medieval cosmology assigned to each crystalline sphere an 'intelligence', often identified with an angel, which moves it. The speaker's love is thus the angel and her love the sphere in which it dwells and which it governs. The same image appears in 'The Extasie' ('Wee are / The intelligences, they the spheares'). The analogy is hierarchical: the mover is superior to the moved, which prepares for the final lines' claim of 'disparitie'.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981)

  11. Just such disparitie ... will ever bee ll. 26-28

    The close applies the angel conceit to the sexes: women's love, like air, is the body for men's angelic love, and must always be somewhat less pure. Many readers find the ending a sudden, deflating barb; Judith Scherer Herz calls it a resistance to the mutuality the poem seemed to build. Others read it as a joke at the speaker's own expense, or note the possible reversal: some take the lines to mean that women's love is the purer, airy element and men's the angelic but grosser one, though the grammar resists this. Michael Schoenfeldt places the ending between patriarchal assumption and egalitarian design. The 1990 John Donne Journal symposium shows that no consensus has emerged. The final triple rhyme echoes the first stanza's close.

    Achsah Guibbory, ed., 'Interpreting "Aire and Angels"', special issue, John Donne Journal 9.1 (1990), essays by Schoenfeldt, Herz, Roberts, Stein and others; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem
