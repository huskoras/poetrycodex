The Sunne Rising.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

BUsie old foole, unruly Sunne,
Why dost thou thus,
Through windowes, and through curtaines call on us?
Must to thy motions lovers seasons run?
Sawcy pedantique wretch, goe chide
Late schoole boyes, and sowre prentices,
Goe tell Court-huntsmen, that the King will ride,
Call countrey ants to harvest offices,
Love, all alike, no season knowes, nor clyme,
Nor houres, dayes, moneths, which are the rags of time.

Thy beames, so reverend, and strong
Why shouldst thou thinke?
I could eclipse and cloud them with a winke,
But that I would not lose her sight so long:
If her eyes have not blinded thine,
Looke, and to morrow late, tell mee,
Whether both the'India's of spice and Myne
Be where thou leftst them, or lie here with mee.
Aske for those Kings whom thou saw'st yesterday,
And thou shalt heare, All here in one bed lay.

She'is all States, and all Princes, I,
Nothing else is.
Princes doe but play us, compar'd to this,
All honor's mimique; All wealth alchimie;
Thou sunne art halfe as happy'as wee,
In that the world's contracted thus.
Thine age askes ease, and since thy duties bee
To warme the world, that's done in warming us.
Shine here to us, and thou art every where;
This bed thy center is, these walls, thy spheare.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Sunne Rising: aubade title; l. 1

    The aubade (Provencal alba) laments the dawn that parts lovers. Grierson compares Ovid, Amores 1.13, contrasting Ovid's natural images with Donne's ingenious hyperboles; Ovid begs Aurora to linger and complains that she drives boys to their teachers, and Donne's scolding of the sun picks up that complaint. But where Ovid pleads, Donne scolds: the opening insult ('Busie old foole') makes the sun a meddling old man. The poem's argument then inflates the lovers' bed until it becomes the whole world, so that the sun need shine nowhere else.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'The Sunne Rising' (p. 11); Ovid, Amores 1.13 (Latin text); Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  2. unruly l. 1

    The sun's course was the standard of order and regularity, and it governed the reckoning of time. Calling it 'unruly' is an inversion: from the lovers' point of view the sun is the disorderly intruder. The word also prepares for the speaker's claim in l. 4 that lovers' 'seasons' need not obey the sun's 'motions'. The insult reverses the usual praise of the sun as the eye and lamp of heaven.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unruly, adj.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 1

  3. lovers seasons l. 4

    'Seasons' means both the times of year and the fitting occasions for something. The rhetorical question expects the answer no, and stanza one ends by asserting it: love 'no season knowes, nor clyme'. The idea that true love transcends time is ancient, but Donne puts it in the language of astronomy ('motions'), which the poem keeps returning to through spheres and centre.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'season, n.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  4. Sawcy pedantique wretch l. 5

    'Saucy' means insolent; 'pedantic' means schoolmasterly, from 'pedant', a schoolmaster or tutor. The sun, which tells the time, is cast as a fussy tutor, and the speaker turns its own role against it by sending it to 'chide' (scold) those who really need scolding. Donne's string of insults belongs to the comic tradition of abusing personified forces. Here the sun, honoured in emblem and royal iconography, is reduced to a classroom tyrant.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pedantic, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'saucy, adj.1'

  5. schoole boyes ... prentices l. 6

    Ovid's Amores 1.13 complains that Aurora cheats boys of sleep and hands them over to masters' whips. Donne's 'Late schoole boyes' are those running late; 'sowre prentices' are sulky apprentices who must open shop at dawn. Apprentices bound to London masters were a recognisable social group. The list that follows (huntsmen, farmworkers) surveys the working, daylit world that the lovers have left behind.

    Ovid, Amores 1.13 (Latin text); H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'The Sunne Rising', citing Amores 1.13; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 6

  6. Court-huntsmen ... the King will ride l. 7

    James I was notorious for hunting, often at the cost of government business, and courtiers had to rise early to follow him. Most editors take the line as a topical allusion to James, which would place the poem after March 1603. 'Court-huntsmen' are courtiers who hunt, or who 'hunt' for favour, a satirical double edge that suits Donne's sense of the court as a place of ambition.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary, on dating; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote and note to l. 7; R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on James I's court

  7. countrey ants l. 8

    The ant was a proverbial image of busy labour and provision for the future (Proverbs 6:6, 'Go to the ant, thou sluggard'). Donne's 'countrey ants' are rural labourers, viewed condescendingly from the great height of the lovers' self-importance. 'Offices' means duties or tasks. The line completes a social survey: school, trade, court and field. The ant image also anticipates the shrinking of the outside world as the poem enlarges the bedroom.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Proverbs 6:6-8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'office, n.'

  8. the rags of time l. 10

    'Clyme' (clime) is region or zone of the earth, so love is free of both time and place. Hours, days and months are 'rags', torn shreds of a whole cloth, worthless and divided. The image belittles the sun's work of measuring time, and implies that love participates in something whole and timeless, a notion close to the theological idea of eternity as an undivided present.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clime, n.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 10

  9. eclipse ... with a winke l. 13

    The sun's beams were 'reverend' (worthy of awe). The speaker deflates them: a mere blink would 'eclipse' them for him. Yet he declines, because that would mean not seeing her for even that long. The argument is a typical Donne move: a boast that turns into a compliment to the beloved, whose eyes may in turn be bright enough to blind the sun (l. 15).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eclipse, v.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  10. both the Indias of spice and Myne l. 17

    The East Indies were valued for spices, the West Indies (the Spanish Americas) for gold and silver mines. Grierson cites a sermon of Donne's that sets the two Indias side by side, and notes, citing Coleridge as quoted by Norton, that 'mine' used specifically for a mine of gold, silver or gems seems peculiar to Donne. The speaker challenges the sun to see whether these riches are still where it left them, or lie here in the bed: the woman is all the wealth of both Indies. Elision ('the'India's') marks a slurred syllable to keep the metre.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'The Sunne Rising', l. 17, quoting Coleridge; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 17

  11. All here in one bed lay ll. 19-20

    The stanza ends on an extravagant hyperbole that the next stanza explains: since he is all princes and she is all states, the sun will hear that every king lies in this one bed. The lovers' bed has become a microcosm, containing the political and economic world in little. Readers note the imperial and colonial vocabulary of possession throughout the poem, which some recent critics read as revealing the period's acquisitive fantasies.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981), ch. 3, 'Ambition'

  12. all States ... all Princes l. 21

    The line divides the political world into territories ('States') and rulers ('Princes'), assigning the first to her and the second to him: a gendered division typical of the period's language of love as conquest and possession. The short second line, 'Nothing else is', gives the hyperbole the force of a metaphysical statement. John Carey reads such images in light of Donne's own frustrated ambition for court office after his marriage.

    John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981), ch. 3, 'Ambition'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  13. Princes doe but play us l. 23

    'Play' means to act a part in a play. Rulers merely perform the roles that the lovers truly hold. The theatrical metaphor leads straight into 'mimique' (mimicry) in l. 24: all outward honour is imitation. The inversion of reality and appearance is a favourite Donne device, setting private love against the public world of the court. The lovers are the originals; monarchs merely copy them.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'play, v.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 23

  14. mimique ... alchimie l. 24

    Alchemy sought to turn base metal into gold, and the word came to mean imposture or showy counterfeit metal. Grierson glosses it as 'imposture' or glittering dross and cites Harington's Orlando Furioso (1591). Compared with the lovers, worldly wealth is fake gold and honour is a mimic show. Donne returns to alchemy in 'Loves Alchymie' and often in the sermons.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'The Sunne Rising', l. 24; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alchemy, n.'

  15. the world's contracted l. 26

    The microcosm idea, that man is a little world, is here applied to the pair of lovers, who contain the whole world in concentrated form. 'Contracted' also hints at a marriage contract. The sun is therefore 'halfe as happy' as they are: with the world shrunk to one room it can do its duty of warming the world with half the effort (ll. 27-28), but the lovers hold the world itself. The idea of the world contracted into lovers recurs in 'The Canonization' ('the whole worlds soule contract').

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 26

  16. Thine age askes ease l. 27

    Picking up 'old foole' from l. 1, the sun is now an aged servant who deserves to take things easy. The tone shifts from contempt to condescending kindness. It also reflects the period's widespread notion that the world and the heavens were decaying with age, a theme Donne treats at length in the Anniversaries (1611-12). The sun, once a schoolmaster, has become a pensioner.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 27; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  17. center ... spheare l. 30

    In the Ptolemaic system the earth is the fixed centre of the universe and the sun moves in its own sphere around it. Donne redraws the cosmos with the lovers' bed as centre and the bedroom walls as the sun's sphere. Though the Copernican model was known (Donne mentions the 'new Philosophy' in the First Anniversary), the poem uses the older picture because it allows this domestic universe. The closing couplet balances the opening insult: the sun, dismissed in stanza one, is now invited in.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 29-30; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; John Donne, An Anatomy of the World: The First Anniversary (1611), l. 205 ('And new Philosophy cals all in doubt')

  18. stanza form st. 1-3

    Each stanza opens with a tetrameter followed by a two-stress line ('Why dost thou thus', 'Why shouldst thou thinke?', 'Nothing else is.'), then expands to pentameters ending in a couplet. The clipped second line acts like an interjection in a speaking voice, while the couplets give each stanza a rounded epigrammatic close. As in most of the Songs and Sonets, the stanza is Donne's own invention.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction, on stanza forms; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote
