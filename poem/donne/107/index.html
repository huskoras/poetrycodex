Love

The Canonization.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

FOr Godsake hold your tongue, and let me love,
Or chide my palsie, or my gout,
My five gray haires, or ruin'd fortune flout,
With wealth your state, your minde with Arts improve
Take you a course, get you a place,
Observe his honour, or his grace,
Or the Kings reall, or his stamped face
Contemplate, what you will, approve,
So you will let me love.

Alas, alas, who's injur'd by my love?
What merchants ships have my sighs drown'd?
Who saies my teares have overflow'd his ground?
When did my colds a forward spring remove?
When did the heats which my veines fill
Adde one more, to the plaguie Bill?
Soldiers finde warres, and Lawyers finde out still
Litigious men, which quarrels move,
Though she and I do love.

Call us what you will, wee are made such by love;
Call her one, mee another flye,
We'are Tapers too, and at our owne cost die,
And wee in us finde the'Eagle and the dove,
The Phoenix ridle hath more wit
By us, we two being one, are it.
So, to one neutrall thing both sexes fit.
Wee dye and rise the same, and prove
Mysterious by this love.

Wee can dye by it, if not live by love,
And if unfit for tombes and hearse
Our legends bee, it will be fit for verse;
And if no peece of Chronicle wee prove,
We'll build in sonnets pretty roomes;
As well a well wrought urne becomes
The greatest ashes, as halfe-acre tombes,
And by these hymnes, all shall approve
Us Canoniz'd for Love.

And thus invoke us; You whom reverend love
Made one anothers hermitage;
You, to whom love was peace, that now is rage,
Who did the whole worlds soule contract, & drove
Into the glasses of your eyes
So made such mirrors, and such spies,
That they did all to you epitomize,
Countries, Townes, Courts: Beg frow above
A patterne of our love.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Canonization: title and opening title; l. 1

    Canonization is the process by which the Roman Catholic Church enrols a person in its 'canon' or list of saints. Donne, raised a Catholic, turns the process into the poem's structure: the lovers renounce the world, suffer and die (in love), have their 'legends' written, and are finally invoked by later lovers as saints. The abrupt opening addresses an unseen friend who has been urging the speaker to give up love for a career. Readers often link the poem's 'ruin'd fortune' to Donne's secret marriage in 1601, which cost him his post with Sir Thomas Egerton.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the marriage of 1601 and its consequences

  2. palsie ... gout ... five gray haires ll. 2-3

    'Palsy' is paralysis or uncontrollable trembling; gout a painful disease of the joints associated with age and rich living. The speaker offers his critic any other target: mock his ailments, his age ('five gray haires', comically precise), or his 'ruin'd fortune' ('flout' = scoff at), so long as he lets him love. The comic exaggeration of age and decay also anticipates the poem's later claim that lovers are above worldly ruin.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'palsy, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flout, v.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 2-3

  3. Take you a course ... his honour, or his grace ll. 5-6

    A 'course' is a career or line of action; a 'place' is a post or office. 'His honour' and 'his grace' are forms of address for nobles (and, for 'grace', for dukes and archbishops). The lines satirise the courtier's life of observing and flattering great men, exactly the ambition Donne himself pursued in the 1590s and lost by his marriage.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 5-6; Arthur F. Marotti, John Donne, Coterie Poet (Madison: University of Wisconsin Press, 1986)

  4. the Kings reall, or his stamped face l. 7

    The critic can gaze on the King's actual face (as a courtier) or on his image stamped on money (as a man of business). 'Reall' sets the actual face against the stamped one, with a play on 'royal' (some also hear the Spanish silver coin, the real). Grierson quotes at length a sermon of Donne's on Caesar's face on coins, which recalls Christ's question about Caesar's image on the tribute money (Matthew 22:20-21).

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to l. 7, quoting the Sermons; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 22:20-21; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'real, n.1'

  5. stanza and refrain on 'love' st. 1-5

    The first line of every stanza ends on 'love', and so does the last, a short line that varies a formula: 'So you will let me love', 'Though she and I do love', 'Mysterious by this love', 'Us Canoniz'd for Love', 'A patterne of our love'. The progression traces the poem's argument from defence, through mystery, to canonization. The stanza, rhymed abbacccaa with the last line shortened, is again Donne's own design.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction, on stanza forms; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote

  6. merchants ships ... sighs drown'd ll. 11-14

    Petrarchan lovers routinely claimed that their sighs were tempests, their tears floods, and their alternating fever and chill (the 'icy fire' of love) affected the weather. Donne takes these conventions literally to show that his love harms nobody's trade, land or harvest. 'Forward spring' is an early spring; 'remove' means put back. The joke turns a defence of private love into a satire on the inflated language of love poetry.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 11-14

  7. the plaguie Bill l. 15

    London parishes published weekly 'bills of mortality' recording burials and causes of death, issued regularly during epidemics, and continuously from the great plague of 1603. The 'heats' in his veins (the fever of love) have not added a single plague death to the list. The detail gives the poem a sharp topical texture: real deaths in the city are set against the lover's metaphorical sickness.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bill of mortality, n.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 15; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  8. Soldiers ... Lawyers ... Litigious men ll. 16-17

    The stanza's argument concludes that the lovers harm no one: soldiers still find wars, lawyers still find quarrelsome clients ('Litigious men' who 'move' or start quarrels). Donne, a former student at Lincoln's Inn and soldier on the Cadiz and Islands expeditions (1596-7), knew both worlds. The satire implies the public world is itself driven by conflict, against which private love is innocent.

    R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on Lincoln's Inn and the expeditions of 1596-7; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 16-17

  9. flye ... Tapers ... die ll. 20-21

    The lovers accept any name. A 'flye' may be a moth that flies into a candle and is destroyed; they are also 'Tapers' (candles) consuming themselves. 'Die' was a common Elizabethan pun on sexual climax, and a popular belief held that each act of love shortened life, hence 'at our owne cost'. Brooks takes this cluster as an example of how Donne's paradoxes fuse the sacred and the sexual.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'die, v.1'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 20-21

  10. the Eagle and the dove l. 22

    The eagle stood for strength, masculinity and royalty, the dove for meekness, femininity and peace (and the Holy Spirit). The lovers contain both. Some critics also read both as alchemical symbols for stages in the transmutation of matter, which leads into the alchemical-sounding 'neutrall thing' and the Phoenix. Critics differ over how far to press the alchemical reading; Brooks emphasises the paradox of opposites united.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 22; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1

  11. The Phoenix ridle ll. 23-24

    The phoenix of classical legend (described by Pliny, Natural History 10.2, and in Lactantius' poem De ave phoenice) is unique, without a mate, and periodically dies in flames to be reborn from its ashes. Its 'riddle' is how one bird could reproduce itself. The lovers, 'two being one', make the riddle meaningful: they are the phoenix, dying and rising 'the same'. The phoenix was also a Christian emblem of resurrection.

    Pliny the Elder, Natural History 10.2; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 23-6

  12. one neutrall thing l. 25

    'Neutral' means of neither sex (as in grammar), suggesting an androgynous union like Plato's original double humans in the Symposium, or the alchemical hermaphrodite. Grierson argued from manuscript punctuation that the line must go with what follows: both sexes fit so entirely into one neutral thing 'that' we die and rise the same. Chambers and others link it with the preceding Phoenix lines. The archive's comma after 'So' follows the 1633 edition.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to ll. 24-5; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 25; Plato, Symposium 189c-193d (speech of Aristophanes)

  13. Wee dye and rise the same ... Mysterious ll. 26-27

    The lovers 'die' in sexual consummation and 'rise' unchanged, like the phoenix and, blasphemously, like Christ or the faithful at the resurrection. 'Mysterious' carries the religious sense of a 'mystery', a truth beyond reason revealed only to initiates, as in the sacraments. Brooks made this stanza central to his argument that the language of poetry is the language of paradox.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mystery, n.1'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  14. tombes and hearse ... legends ... Chronicle ll. 29-31

    A 'legend' was originally the life of a saint, read aloud (Latin legenda), as in the medieval Golden Legend. A 'hearse' was the frame over a coffin on which verses and epitaphs were pinned. 'Chronicle' is official history. The lovers will not be commemorated in public monuments or histories, but verse will serve as their legend, preparing their canonization. The poem itself becomes the saints' legend, the written evidence of their holy life.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'legend, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hearse, n.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 29-31

  15. sonnets pretty roomes l. 32

    In Donne's time 'sonnet' could mean any short love lyric, not only the fourteen-line form (the collection of his lyrics printed in 1633 is called Songs and Sonets, though it contains few sonnets). 'Pretty roomes' plays on the Italian stanza ('room, stopping-place'), so the poem is itself the chapel or tomb in which the lovers are enshrined. The idea that verse outlasts monuments goes back to Horace (Odes 3.30, 'exegi monumentum').

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stanza, n.'; Horace, Odes 3.30

  16. a well wrought urne ll. 33-34

    A cinerary urn, holding the ashes of the dead, is set against 'halfe-acre tombes', the enormous monuments of the great. The small, carefully made object honours its contents as well as any grand tomb, as the lyric honours the lovers. Cleanth Brooks took the phrase as the title of The Well Wrought Urn (1947), the classic New Critical study, where he reads the poem itself as such an urn, a self-contained structure of paradox.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  17. hymnes ... Canoniz'd ll. 35-36

    The 'sonnets' of l. 32 have become 'hymnes', songs of praise for saints. 'Approve' means to confirm or attest, as in a formal process of canonization, where evidence of sanctity is approved. The stanza thus completes the analogy: renunciation, death, legend, and now official recognition of the lovers as saints of love. All who read the hymns become witnesses in the cause.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'approve, v.1'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 35-6

  18. And thus invoke us l. 37

    Catholic practice invokes saints, asking them to pray to God for the petitioner. Grierson observes that Donne is 'pedantically accurate' here: later lovers ask the canonized pair to 'beg from above' a pattern of love for them. The Protestant Church of England rejected the invocation of saints, so the conceit has a daring, even provocative edge given Donne's Catholic background.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to ll. 37-45; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981), on Donne's apostasy

  19. one anothers hermitage l. 38

    A hermitage is the solitary dwelling of a religious recluse. The lovers withdrew from the world into each other, as hermits withdraw to the desert. The line 'You, to whom love was peace, that now is rage' is not easy to read; it seems to set the calm of the canonized lovers against the turbulence ('rage') of love for those who now invoke them. The monastic image continues the poem's sacred vocabulary.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hermitage, n.'; Cleanth Brooks, 'The Language of Paradox', in The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 1

  20. contract ... glasses of your eyes ll. 40-44

    Grierson defends 'contract' against the manuscript variant 'extract', and explains that the object of 'drove' is 'Countries, Townes, Courts': the lovers compressed the world's essence and drove it into each other's eyes, which reflect it in miniature like mirrors or optical 'perspective' glasses. 'Epitomize' means to sum up in brief. The image is the same microcosm as 'The Sunne Rising', where the world is 'contracted' into the lovers.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to ll. 37-45 and l. 40; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'epitomize, v.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 40-44

  21. Beg frow above / A patterne of our love ll. 44-45

    Grierson prints 'your love', arguing that 'Beg' is an imperative addressed by future lovers to the canonized pair ('You whom reverend love ...'): they ask the saints to obtain from heaven a pattern of 'your' love, the saints' love, as a model for themselves. The 1633 reading 'our love' would then be a copyist's error, though it can be read as 'a pattern for our love'. Modern editors are divided. 'Frow' in the archive text is a misprint for 'from'. 'Patterne' means exemplar, model to copy.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to l. 45; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), textual notes; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 45
