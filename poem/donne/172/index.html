Love

A Valediction: forbidding mourning

by John Donne 1572–1631

From Poems (Grierson, 1912)

Love

As virtuous men passe mildly away,
And whisper to their soules, to goe,
Whilst some of their sad friends doe say,
The breath goes now, and some say, no:

So let us melt, and make no noise,
No teare-floods, nor sigh-tempests move,
T'were prophanation of our joyes
To tell the layetie our love.

Moving of th'earth brings harmes and feares,
Men reckon what it did and meant,
But trepidation of the spheares,
Though greater farre, is innocent.

Dull sublunary lovers love
(Whose soule is sense) cannot admit
Absence, because it doth remove
Those things which elemented it.

But we by a love, so much refin'd,
That our selves know not what it is,
Inter-assured of the mind,
Care lesse, eyes, lips, and hands to misse.

Our two soules therefore, which are one,
Though I must goe, endure not yet
A breach, but an expansion,
Like gold to ayery thinnesse beate.

If they be two, they are two so
As stiffe twin compasses are two,
Thy soule the fixt foot, makes no show
To move, but doth, if the'other doe.

And though it in the center sit,
Yet when the other far doth rome,
It leanes, and hearkens after it,
And growes erect, as that comes home.

Such wilt thou be to mee, who must
Like th'other foot, obliquely runne;
Thy firmnes makes my circle just,
And makes me end, where I begunne.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion title; l. 1

    A valediction is a farewell; the speaker forbids his beloved to mourn his going. In the 1675 edition of his Life of Donne, Izaak Walton quotes the poem after recounting Donne's vision of his wife during his journey to France with Sir Robert Drury (1611-12), saying he gave the verses to her when he parted from her. Grierson's note quotes Walton's account; later scholars note that the story appears only in Walton's late revision and lacks documentary support, so the poem cannot be dated securely. Donne wrote several 'Valedictions', and the poem was first printed in 1633.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'A Valediction: forbidding mourning' (p. 49), quoting Walton; R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the Drury journey; Izaak Walton, The Life of Dr. John Donne (1640; revised 1675)

  2. virtuous men passe mildly away ll. 1-4

    The ars moriendi (art of dying) tradition held that a good man dies peacefully, his soul leaving the body gently. Donne's simile makes parting like such a death: so quiet that friends at the bedside dispute whether the breath has gone. The comparison sets the tone of calm restraint and also anticipates the theme of soul and body: the lovers' parting will be a separation of bodies but not of souls. The tenor of the simile (our parting) is withheld until 'So let us melt' in stanza two.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 1-4; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  3. melt l. 5

    The verb continues the image of a peaceful death (the soul 'melting' from the body) and suggests a soft dissolving rather than a violent tearing apart. It also introduces the language of metals and refining that returns in 'refin'd' and in the gold 'to ayery thinnesse beate'. 'Make no noise' rejects the stage-show of lamentation. The gentleness of the verb contrasts with the violent 'teare-floods' and 'sigh-tempests' of the next line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'melt, v.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 5

  4. teare-floods ... sigh-tempests l. 6

    The compounds parody the stock exaggerations of Petrarchan love poetry, where lovers' tears are floods and their sighs storms. Donne uses the same hyperboles elsewhere (e.g. 'A Valediction: of weeping'), and mocks them in 'The Canonization' ('What merchants ships have my sighs drown'd?'). Here their rejection marks a love that is above outward display. Their outward show would also be a kind of public advertisement, which the next lines treat as profanation of a sacred secret.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 6

  5. prophanation ... layetie ll. 7-8

    The lovers are imagined as priests of a religion of love, and everyone else as the 'laity', ordinary people not admitted to the sacred mysteries. 'Prophanation' (profanation) means treating sacred things as common. The religious metaphor is typical of Donne's love poetry (compare 'The Canonization') and suggests a love that is secret, exclusive and holy. It also suits a poem that forbids public mourning: private grief is reserved for the initiated.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'laity, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'profanation, n.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  6. Moving of th'earth ll. 9-10

    'Moving of th'earth' means an earthquake, an event of terror widely read as a portent; men 'reckon' both its damage and its 'meaning'. The phrase is not a reference to Copernican motion of the earth: the contrast is between violent sublunary disturbances (like the noisy grief of ordinary lovers) and the vast but harmless movement of the heavens. The analogy is completed in ll. 11-12.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 9-12; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  7. trepidation of the spheares ll. 11-12

    Grierson glosses 'trepidation' as the precession of the equinoxes, ascribed in Ptolemaic astronomy to the ninth or crystalline sphere, and cites Chambers. More exactly, medieval astronomers proposed a 'trembling' or oscillation of the spheres to account for irregularities in precession. Though 'greater farre' than an earthquake, this celestial movement is 'innocent', unfelt and harmless. So the lovers' parting, a movement in the higher sphere of souls, should cause no disturbance. Grierson also cites Sir John Davies's Orchestra for a parallel passage.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to ll. 9-12, quoting Chambers and Davies's Orchestra; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trepidation, n.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 11

  8. sublunary l. 13

    In Aristotelian-Ptolemaic cosmology the region below the moon is the realm of the four elements, of change and corruption, while the heavens above are perfect and unchanging. 'Sublunary lovers' are therefore earthly ones, whose love is made of physical presence. 'Whose soule is sense' means that sense perception is the very essence of their love, so they cannot endure absence, which removes its ingredients. The cosmology continues from the spheres of the previous stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sublunary, adj.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  9. elemented l. 16

    Grierson glosses 'elemented' simply as 'composed', defending the reading 'Those things' against the 1669 variant 'The thing', and pointing forward to the list 'eyes, lips, and hands' in l. 20. The word keeps the sublunary vocabulary of the four elements: earthly love is literally made of bodily stuff and so falls apart when the body is absent. Grierson cites Katherine Philips's later echo 'Did element our love'.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to l. 16; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'element, v.'

  10. refin'd l. 17

    'Refined' has the metallurgical sense of purified of dross, anticipating the gold of stanza six, and the alchemical sense of raising matter to a higher state. The lovers do not fully know 'what it is', a humble or mystical touch: refined love exceeds understanding. Yet the stanza does not reject the body entirely: they care 'lesse' to miss eyes, lips and hands, not that they do not care at all.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'refine, v.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 17

  11. Inter-assured l. 19

    The compound stresses reciprocity: each is pledged to and sure of the other. 'Assured' also had the sense of betrothed or contracted in marriage, so the lovers are, as it were, mutually engaged at the level of the mind. The mind's certainty replaces the sublunary lovers' dependence on sense. The hyphenated compound enacts the mutual bond it describes, joining two words as the lovers are joined.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assured, adj.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 19

  12. gold to ayery thinnesse beate ll. 21-24

    Gold is the most malleable of metals and can be hammered into leaf so thin it is almost translucent, without breaking. The two souls, which are one, will 'endure' not 'A breach, but an expansion': distance thins the union but does not divide it. Gold was also the incorruptible metal, the symbol of perfection, and 'ayery thinnesse' suggests a spiritualisation of matter, linking to the earlier 'refin'd'. Johnson quotes these lines in the Life of Cowley, as part of his compasses example.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 21-4; Samuel Johnson, 'Cowley', in Lives of the English Poets (1779-81); Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  13. twin compasses ll. 25-28

    If their souls are two, they are two as the legs of a compass are: separate but joined at the hinge. Her soul is the 'fixt foot' at the centre, which seems not to move but does lean as the other foot travels. Samuel Johnson, in the 'Life of Cowley', cited the passage as the extreme example of metaphysical wit, saying it may be doubted 'whether absurdity or ingenuity has the better claim'. Grierson notes a parallel figure in Persian verse attributed to Omar Khayyam. The compass was also a familiar emblem of constancy, for example in the device of the Plantin press.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Cowley', in Lives of the English Poets (1779-81); H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to ll. 25-36; John Freccero, 'Donne's "Valediction: Forbidding Mourning"', ELH 30.4 (1963), 335-76

  14. the fixt foot ll. 27-32

    The image is precise: when the moving leg goes out, the central leg tilts towards it; when it closes in, the centre stands upright again. The woman 'hearkens after' (listens for, longs for) the absent man. Many readers note a sexual undertone in 'growes erect' and 'comes home'. Feminist critics have also questioned the gender roles: the woman stays put and the man roams. Others stress that the fixed foot is the source of the circle's perfection.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 29-32; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  15. obliquely l. 34

    Literally, the travelling compass leg moves at an angle to the fixed leg as it draws the circle. 'Oblique' also meant indirect or deviating, so the line may hint at the wandering, imperfect course of the man as against her steadfastness. Astronomy also used 'oblique' of the ecliptic and of the sun's slanting path, keeping the cosmic vocabulary in play.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'obliquely, adv.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 34

  16. my circle just ... end, where I begunne ll. 35-36

    'Just' means exact, true. The circle was the perfect figure, a symbol of eternity and of God, and the circle with a dot at its centre was also the alchemical and astronomical sign for gold and the sun, which some critics connect with the earlier gold image. The final line closes the poem on return: departure becomes a circular journey home. John Freccero's essay reads the compasses against the Neoplatonic and Renaissance symbolism of the circle and its centre.

    John Freccero, 'Donne's "Valediction: Forbidding Mourning"', ELH 30.4 (1963), 335-76; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 35-6; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'just, adj.'

  17. metre and stanza st. 1-9

    Unlike most of Donne's lyrics, with their elaborate invented stanzas, this poem uses the simple four-stress alternately rhymed quatrain. Its quiet regularity enacts the calm parting it recommends. The argument proceeds through a chain of analogies (death, earthquake and spheres, metals, compasses), each stanza adding a new comparison, which is itself characteristic of metaphysical method. Johnson's complaint that heterogeneous ideas are 'yoked by violence together' was written with such chains in mind.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote
