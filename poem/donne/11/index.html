Love

(untitled)

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

Death be not proud, though some have called thee
Mighty and dreadfull, for, thou art not soe,
For, those, whom thou think'st, thou dost overthrow,
Die not, poore death, nor yet canst thou kill mee;
From rest and sleepe, which but thy pictures bee,
Much pleasure, then from thee, much more must flow,
And soonest our best men with thee doe goe,
Rest of their bones, and soules deliverie
Thou art slave to Fate, chance, kings, and desperate men,
And doth with poyson, warre, and sicknesse dwell.
And poppie, or charmes can make us sleepe as well,
And better then thy stroake; why swell'st thou then?
One short sleepe past, wee wake eternally,
And death shall be no more, death thou shalt die.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Holy Sonnet X: context and date l. 1

    The poem belongs to the 'Holy Sonnets' (also called 'Divine Meditations'), first printed in 1633. Numbering differs between the early editions and manuscripts; it is commonly called Holy Sonnet X after Grierson, whose first sixteen sonnets follow the order of the 1635 edition (the Westmoreland manuscript, discovered later, has its own sequence). Helen Gardner dated the main group to 1609-10, a period of illness, poverty and religious crisis before Donne's ordination in 1615. Many of the Holy Sonnets meditate on death and judgment with fear; this one instead taunts death with confident faith, though some readers hear the bravado as an attempt to overcome fear.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and commentary, on dating; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), textual introduction; H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, commentary on the Holy Sonnets

  2. apostrophe to Death ll. 1-2

    The sonnet speaks directly to Death, as one might rebuke a boastful man. Personified Death was familiar from the Dance of Death and from Scripture ('O death, where is thy sting?', 1 Corinthians 15:55, itself echoing Hosea 13:14). 'Some have called thee Mighty and dreadfull' concedes the common opinion before refuting it. The metre of the opening line is notably irregular, giving a strong stress to 'Death' and to 'proud', a roughness Ben Jonson famously criticised in Donne's verse generally.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:55; Hosea 13:14; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary

  3. Die not, poore death ll. 3-4

    The argument rests on the Christian doctrine of the immortality of the soul and the resurrection of the body. 'Poore death' reverses the usual roles: the speaker condescends to Death. 'Nor yet canst thou kill mee' is personal: the speaker's own salvation is the confident ground of the taunt, though some critics note how this confidence contrasts with the anxiety of other Holy Sonnets.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary

  4. rest and sleepe ... thy pictures ll. 5-6

    Sleep as the image or brother of death is a classical commonplace: in Hesiod's Theogony and Homer's Iliad (16.672), Sleep and Death are twin brothers, and the Latin tag 'somnus est imago mortis' was familiar. Donne turns the comparison into an argument a fortiori: since sleep, a mere 'picture', gives pleasure, the original must give 'much more'. 'Pictures' means likenesses or images.

    Hesiod, Theogony 756-66; Homer, Iliad 16.672 (Sleep and Death as twin brothers); Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary

  5. soonest our best men l. 7

    The thought recalls the classical proverb 'whom the gods love dies young', found in Menander and in Plautus, Bacchides 816 ('quem di diligunt adulescens moritur'). In Christian terms the good are taken early into rest. Donne uses it to argue that Death cannot be dreadful if the best people go with it willingly and soonest. The phrase 'with thee doe goe' presents Death as a travelling companion rather than an executioner.

    Plautus, Bacchides 816; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary

  6. Rest of their bones, and soules deliverie l. 8

    The line is in apposition: going with Death is rest for the bones and 'deliverie' (release, setting free) for the soul. The idea of the body as the soul's prison is Platonic (Phaedo) and Pauline ('who shall deliver me from the body of this death?', Romans 7:24). 'Deliverie' may also carry the sense of childbirth, death as birth into new life, an image Donne uses in his last sermon, Deaths Duell (1631). The archive text has no punctuation at the end of the octave.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Romans 7:24; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary

  7. slave to Fate, chance, kings, and desperate men l. 9

    The volta at l. 9 inverts Death's status: far from 'Mighty', Death is the servant of other powers: fate (or Providence), chance accidents, kings who order executions and wars, and 'desperate men', probably men driven to murder or to suicide. Despair was itself a sin, and Donne had examined suicide in Biathanatos (c. 1608). The rhythm of the line, crowded with stresses, is part of its contemptuous force.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary; R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on Biathanatos; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'desperate, adj.'

  8. doth with poyson ... dwell l. 10

    Death keeps degrading company, which further lowers its dignity. The verb in this archive text is 'doth'; Grierson and most modern editions read 'dost', agreeing with 'Thou' in l. 9. The three companions correspond loosely to the agents of the previous line: poison (desperate men), war (kings), sickness (chance or fate). The line therefore extends the list of Death's masters into a list of its low associates, completing the reversal of l. 9.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, Holy Sonnet X, l. 10 (reads 'dost'); Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), textual apparatus

  9. poppie, or charmes l. 11

    The poppy was the source of opium, used as a sleeping draught; 'charmes' are incantations or spells believed to induce sleep. Death's power to bring sleep is therefore not unique: drugs and magic do as much, and 'better then thy stroake' (stroke, blow), so Death has no reason to 'swell' with pride. The argument returns to the sleep analogy of ll. 5-6: if sleep can be bought from an apothecary, Death's gift is nothing special.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'poppy, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'charm, n.1'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary

  10. swell'st l. 12

    'Swell' means to be puffed up with pride or arrogance, a sense common in the period. The rhetorical question closes the logical case against Death and brings the poem back to its opening command. In some readings the swelling also suggests the bloating of a corpse. The question is the last of the poem's arguments before the couplet's statement of faith, so the rhetoric shifts from disputation to proclamation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swell, v.'; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary

  11. One short sleepe past l. 13

    The New Testament speaks of the dead as 'them which are asleep' who will be raised (1 Thessalonians 4:13-17; 1 Corinthians 15:51-52, 'we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed'). Death is a short sleep between this life and the general resurrection, after which the faithful 'wake eternally'. The line turns the pagan commonplace of sleep as Death's image into the Christian promise of waking.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:51-52; 1 Thessalonians 4:13-17; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary

  12. death shall be no more, death thou shalt die l. 14

    The final line combines two biblical promises: 'The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death' (1 Corinthians 15:26) and 'there shall be no more death' (Revelation 21:4). The paradox that Death itself will die is ancient in Christian writing (Hosea 13:14, 'O death, I will be thy plagues'). The sonnet thus ends by turning Death's own weapon against it, in the repeated 'death ... death ... die', where Death is killed with its own name.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:26; Revelation 21:4; Hosea 13:14; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), commentary; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary

  13. sonnet form ll. 1-14

    The octave rhymes thee / soe / overthrow / mee / bee / flow / goe / deliverie (abbaabba); the sestet men / dwell / well / then / eternally / die (cddcee). The combination of an Italian octave with a closing couplet is characteristic of Donne's Holy Sonnets. The turn at l. 9 shifts from arguing that Death is not dreadful to arguing that it is base, and the couplet delivers the theological conclusion.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction, on the form of the Holy Sonnets; Gary A. Stringer et al. (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005), commentary
