Love

The Extasie.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

WHere, like a pillow on a bed,
A Pregnant banke swel'd up, to rest
The violets reclining head,
Sat we two, one anothers best;
Our hands were firmely cimented
With a fast balme, which thence did spring,
Our eye-beames twisted, and did thred
Our eyes, upon one double string,
So to'entergraft our hands, as yet
Was all the meanes to make us one,
And pictures in our eyes to get
Was all our propagation.
As 'twixt two equall Armies, Fate
Suspends uncertaine victorie,
Our soules, (which to advance their state,
Were gone out,) hung 'twixt her, and mee.
And whil'st our soules negotiate there,
Wee like sepulchrall statues lay,
All day, the same our postures were,
And wee said nothing, all the day.
If any, so by love refin'd,
That he soules language understood,
And by good love were growen all minde,
Within convenient distance stood,
He (though he knowes not which soule spake,
Because both meant, both spake the same)
Might thence a new concoction take,
And part farre purer then he came.
This Extasie doth unperplex
(We said) and tell us what we love,
Wee see by this, it was not sexe
Wee see, we saw not what did move:
But as all severall soules containe
Mixture of things, they know not what,
Love, these mixt soules, doth mixe againe,
And makes both one, each this and that.
A single violet transplant,
The strength, the colour, and the size,
(All which before was poore, and scant,)
Redoubles still, and multiplies.
When love, with one another so
Interanimates two soules,
That abler soule, which thence doth flow,
Defects of lonelinesse controules.
Wee then, who are this new soule, know,
Of what we are compos'd, and made,
For, th'Atomies of which we grow,
Are soules, whom no change can invade.
But O alas, so long, so farre
Our bodies why doe wee forbeare?
They are ours, though not wee, Wee are
The intelligences, they the spheares.
We owe them thankes, because they thus,
Did us, to us, at first convay,
Yeelded their senses force to us,
Nor are drosse to us, but allay.
On man heavens influence workes not so,
But that it first imprints the ayre,
For soule into the soule may flow,
Though it to body first repaire.
As our blood labours to beget
Spirits, as like soules as it can,
Because such fingers need to knit
That subtile knot, which makes us man:
So must pure lovers soules descend
T'affections, and to faculties,
Which sense may reach and apprehend,
Else a great Prince in prison lies.
To'our bodies turne wee then, that so
Weake men on love reveal'd may looke;
Loves mysteries in soules doe grow,
But yet the body is his booke.
And if some lover, such as wee,
Have heard this dialogue of one,
Let him still marke us, he shall see
Small change, when we'are to bodies gone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'The Extasie': title and tradition title; l. 29

    'Ecstasy' (Greek ekstasis) meant the departure of the soul from the body in trance or rapture, as in St Paul's being caught up 'whether in the body, or out of the body, I cannot tell' (2 Corinthians 12:2-3), in Plotinus, and in Aquinas. Grierson called the poem one of Donne's most important statements of the interdependence of body and soul, and noted that Donne in a letter compares serious letter-writing to ecstasy. Lord Herbert of Cherbury's 'Ode upon a Question moved, Whether Love should continue for ever' appears to imitate it. First printed 1633.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Bible (King James Version), 2 Corinthians 12:2-4; Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959)

  2. A Pregnant banke ... violets ll. 1-4

    The opening places the lovers in a pastoral landscape with erotic overtones: the 'pregnant' bank swells like a pillow on a bed, and the violet reclines its head. The violet was an emblem of love and faithfulness. Readers who take the poem as seduction see the setting as preparing for the bodily conclusion; others read it as the natural fertility that the poem's argument eventually sanctifies. The violet returns in ll. 37-40.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Pierre Legouis, Donne the Craftsman (Paris: Henri Didier, 1928), chapter on 'The Extasie'

  3. cimented / With a fast balme ll. 5-6

    'Balm' was an aromatic resin and, in Paracelsian medicine, a vital preservative essence in the body. Here it is the moisture of the joined palms, 'fast' because it binds firmly. In Elizabethan love-lore a moist palm signified amorousness (compare Othello III.iv). The hands are 'cimented' (cemented): the first of a series of images of joining (thread, graft, knot) that culminate in the 'subtile knot' of l. 64.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'balm'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  4. eye-beames twisted ... one double string ll. 7-8

    Many ancient and Renaissance writers held that sight works by rays sent out from the eye. Donne literalises the idea: the lovers' eye-beams twist together and thread their eyes on a single double string, like beads. The image is both precise and slightly grotesque, typical of what Samuel Johnson called the metaphysical poets' yoking of heterogeneous ideas. The 'double string' prepares for the idea of two souls made one.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981)

  5. entergraft l. 9

    Grafting joins a shoot of one plant to another so that they grow as one. Grierson notes that 'entergraft' (inter-graft) has the best manuscript support and alone conveys the mutual sense: each hand grafted into the other. The horticultural image anticipates the violet transplanted in l. 37. Grafting was also a familiar figure for marriage and for the union of two families, so the word hints at a lasting bond. The elision mark in 'to'entergraft' shows that the two syllables are run together to keep the four-stress line.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'intergraft'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  6. pictures in our eyes ... propagation ll. 11-12

    To 'get' meant to beget. The reflections of each lover in the other's pupils, popularly 'babies in the eyes', are wittily called their only 'propagation'. The joke implies a sexual union not yet made, and so raises the question the poem must answer: whether their union is to remain purely spiritual. Compare 'The Good-morrow', l. 15. 'All the meanes' and 'all our propagation' stress how little, so far, the bodies have done.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'get'

  7. Our soules ... were gone out ll. 13-16

    This is the ecstasy proper: the souls leave their bodies 'to advance their state' (to improve their condition, and in diplomatic language to promote their interests) and hang between the lovers like Fate suspending victory over two evenly matched armies. The military simile gives way in l. 17 to diplomatic 'negotiation', a characteristic Donne mixture of public and private vocabulary.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959)

  8. sepulchrall statues ll. 18-20

    Recumbent effigies on Tudor and Jacobean tombs, often of husband and wife side by side, are the model. The comparison stresses that the bodies are now empty, as if dead. Grierson cites Pascal on the eloquence of lovers' silence for l. 20. The repetition 'All day ... all the day' slows the verse to match the stillness. The tomb image also hints at death, which the poem later answers with the bodies' return to life.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  9. concoction ll. 21-28

    The imagined bystander, 'by love refin'd' and grown 'all minde', could understand the souls' wordless language. He would take 'a new concoction': in alchemy and physiology, concoction was the process of maturing or refining by heat, as food is 'concocted' into blood. He would leave purer than he came. The figure introduces the idea, completed at the end, of the lovers as an example to others.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'concoction'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  10. it was not sexe ll. 29-32

    The ecstasy 'unperplexes' (clarifies) their love: they now see that its source was the soul, not bodily desire. Grierson explains that the lovers perceive their attraction to have been spiritual in origin. 'Sexe' here may mean sexual difference as well as desire. The phrase bears on the larger debate: the poem first denies that sex was the motive and then argues for a return to the body.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  11. Mixture of things ... each this and that ll. 33-36

    Renaissance theorists debated whether souls were simple or compounded. Donne takes them as mixtures, then makes love an alchemist that mixes two souls again to produce one compound in which 'each [is] this and that'. Helen Gardner related the poem's argument to Leone Ebreo's Dialoghi d'amore (1535), where lovers' souls are united yet need the body; others point to Ficino and the wider Florentine Neoplatonic tradition.

    Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959); Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  12. A single violet transplant ll. 37-40

    Gardeners believed that transplanting improved a plant. The violet, picked up from the opening landscape, now becomes an emblem of the new, double soul; 'redoubles' and 'multiplies' also hint at propagation. The image extends the grafting of l. 9. 'Still' means continually: the transplanted flower goes on increasing. The point of the analogy is that the soul, like the plant, gains from being moved out of its single state and set in a new soil, that is, joined with another soul. The 'scant' violet becomes abundant.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  13. Interanimates ll. 41-44

    This archive's text reads 'Interanimates', the form of the early editions; Grierson printed 'Interinanimates', with manuscript support, and defended related forms from Donne's sermons. The word means to animate mutually. The 'abler soule' that results is stronger than either alone; 'controules' means overpowers, corrects (compare 'The Good-morrow', l. 10). The 'abler soule' is the speaker's name for the compound soul of the two lovers.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'interanimate'

  14. Atomies ll. 45-48

    'Atomy' (atom) was used of the tiny indivisible particles of Democritean and Epicurean physics, and loosely for any minute particle. The lovers' new soul is built not of physical atoms, which decay as compounds change, but of souls, which are immune to change. The argument parallels the scholastic premise of 'The Good-morrow', l. 19. The lovers thus claim for their joint soul the immortality that philosophy granted to the simple soul.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'atomy'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  15. Wee are / The intelligences, they the spheares ll. 49-52

    The turn at 'But O alas' asks why they keep apart from their bodies. The answer adapts Ptolemaic cosmology, in which each sphere is moved by an intelligence: the bodies are 'ours, though not wee', instruments that the soul governs. Grierson notes that all the manuscripts read the singular 'spheare' (the early editions print the plural), and he kept it. The same image appears in 'Aire and Angels'.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  16. drosse ... allay ll. 53-56

    Metallurgy: dross is the scum thrown off in smelting; alloy ('allay') is the base metal mixed with gold or silver to make it hard enough for coinage. The body, then, is a necessary admixture, not refuse. The bodies first 'convay' (conveyed, introduced) the lovers to each other and yielded their senses' force. This archive reads 'senses force' with 1633; Grierson emended to 'forces, sense', which he thought the harder, more characteristic reading.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alloy'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants)

  17. heavens influence ... imprints the ayre ll. 57-60

    Astrology held that the stars act on man through the medium of the air; Grierson illustrates from Du Bartas. The analogy: just as heaven's influence must first 'imprint' the air, so one soul must pass through the body to reach another. This text reads 'For soule' (1633); Grierson emended to 'Soe soule', citing Aquinas on the heavenly intelligences, and later editors are divided. With 'For', the lines read as a reason; with 'Soe', as the second term of an analogy.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  18. Spirits ... that subtile knot ll. 61-64

    Physicians taught that the blood produced 'spirits' (natural, vital and animal), very fine substances that served as the link between the immaterial soul and the body. Grierson quotes Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy and a Donne sermon on the spirits as the knot binding soul and body. The 'subtile knot' (fine, delicate) 'makes us man': a human being is neither soul nor body alone but their union.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'; Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621), Part 1, sect. 1, memb. 2, subs. 2 (of spirits); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  19. a great Prince in prison ll. 65-68

    Pure souls must 'descend' to the affections (emotions) and faculties that the senses can reach; otherwise the soul is a prince imprisoned. The image reverses the Platonic commonplace of the body as the soul's prison (Phaedo; compare Marvell's 'Dialogue between the Soul and Body'): here the soul is imprisoned if it is cut off from the body. Gardner took this as the heart of the poem's serious argument about the incarnate nature of human love.

    Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Plato, Phaedo 82d-83a (the body as prison)

  20. the body is his booke ll. 69-72

    'Mysteries' carries a religious sense (the sacraments, revealed truths), and 'love reveal'd' parallels revealed religion. As Scripture is the book in which weak men read God, so the body is the book in which love can be read. The lines justify the return to the bodies as both natural and exemplary. Critics who read the poem as seduction see the theology as specious; others take it as Donne's serious incarnational view of love.

    Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; 'The Erotology of Donne's "Extasie" and the Secret History of Voluptuous Rationalism', SEL: Studies in English Literature 1500-1900 44.1 (2004), 121-47

  21. this dialogue of one ll. 73-76

    The poem ends by recalling the observer of ll. 21-28: if some lover like them has 'heard' this 'dialogue of one' (two souls speaking as one), he will see 'small change' when they are 'to bodies gone'. Whether 'small change' means that bodily love will preserve the spiritual union, or archly understates what is about to happen, is one of the cruxes on which the reading of the whole poem depends.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Pierre Legouis, Donne the Craftsman (Paris: Henri Didier, 1928), chapter on 'The Extasie'; 'The Erotology of Donne's "Extasie" and the Secret History of Voluptuous Rationalism', SEL: Studies in English Literature 1500-1900 44.1 (2004), 121-47

  22. Philosophy or seduction? ll. 69-76

    Grierson read the poem as a serious statement of the interdependence of soul and body. Pierre Legouis (Donne the Craftsman, 1928) argued that it is a dramatised seduction in which Neoplatonic language serves physical ends. Helen Gardner's 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"' (1959) answered Legouis, linking the poem to Leone Ebreo and stressing that its 'we' is shared by both lovers, so there is no one to seduce. A 2004 essay in SEL argues that the seduction reading, later dominant, misses a tradition of 'voluptuous rationalism' behind the poem. Students should notice that the speaker reports what 'we said' together, which supports Gardner, while the setting and witty logic support Legouis.

    Pierre Legouis, Donne the Craftsman (Paris: Henri Didier, 1928), chapter on 'The Extasie'; Helen Gardner, 'The Argument about "The Ecstasy"', in Elizabethan and Jacobean Studies Presented to Frank Percy Wilson (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959); 'The Erotology of Donne's "Extasie" and the Secret History of Voluptuous Rationalism', SEL: Studies in English Literature 1500-1900 44.1 (2004), 121-47; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Extasie'

  23. Form ll. 1-4

    The short four-stress line and alternate rhymes give the poem the movement of a steady argument; its length is unusual for the Songs and Sonets. The quatrain units are clear even though the text is printed as one block: each quatrain usually completes a step in the reasoning, with longer syntactic periods across ll. 21-28 and 61-68 where the argument is most intricate. Ll. 1-4 complete the opening sentence: the lovers sat on the bank, 'one anothers best'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem
