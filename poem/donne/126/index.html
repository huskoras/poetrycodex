Love

The Flea.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

MArke but this flea, and marke in this,
How little that which thou deny'st me is;
It suck'd me first, and now sucks thee,
And in this flea, our two bloods mingled bee;
Thou know'st that this cannot be said
A sinne, nor shame nor losse of maidenhead,
Yet this enjoyes before it wooe,
And pamper'd swells with one blood made of two,
And this, alas, is more then wee would doe.

Oh stay, three lives in one flea spare,
Where wee almost, yea more then maryed are.
This flea is you and I, and this
Our mariage bed, and mariage temple is;
Though parents grudge, and you, w'are met,
And cloysterd in these living walls of Jet.
Though use make you apt to kill mee,
Let not to that, selfe murder added bee,
And sacrilege, three sinnes in killing three.

Cruell and sodaine, hast thou since
Purpled thy naile, in blood of innocence?
Wherein could this flea guilty bee,
Except in that drop which it suckt from thee?
Yet thou triumph'st, and saist that thou
Find'st not thy selfe, nor mee the weaker now;
'Tis true, then learne how false, feares bee;
Just so much honor, when thou yeeld'st to mee,
Will wast, as this flea's death tooke life from thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Flea: genre and date title; l. 1

    Poems in which a lover envies the flea that may touch his mistress's body were a fashionable continental game. A medieval Latin 'Carmen de pulice' circulated under Ovid's name, and in 1579 at Poitiers Etienne Pasquier's notice of a flea on Catherine des Roches's breast set off a contest whose results were printed as La Puce de Madame des Roches (1582-3). Donne changes the convention: his flea is not an object of envy but a piece of evidence in an argument, pressed through three stanzas as the woman reacts between them. The poem was first printed in 1633; it stood first among the Songs and Sonets in 1633; the 1635 edition moved it, and Grierson restored it to the 1633 position, noting that it was admired as a master-piece of wit. It is usually dated to the 1590s, though there is no firm evidence.

    H. J. C. Grierson (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note on 'The Flea' (p. 40); La Puce de Madame Des-Roches (Paris, 1582-3), University of Virginia Library exhibit; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on 'The Flea'

  2. Marke ... marke l. 1

    The doubled imperative ('Marke but this flea, and marke in this') sets the speaker up as a demonstrator: 'but' means 'only', implying the lesson is easy. 'Marke in this' asks her to read a meaning inside the flea, the habit of the emblem book and of the sermon, where a small visible thing is 'marked' for its moral. Readers have often heard a parody of the preacher's 'mark' and of scholastic argument: the poem proceeds by premise, objection and conclusion, with the woman's silent actions supplying the objections between stanzas.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mark, v.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on 'The Flea'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote to 'The Flea'

  3. two bloods mingled l. 4

    The conceit depends on the old belief, derived from Aristotle and Galen, that semen is a refined form of blood, so that intercourse is literally a mingling of bloods. If the flea mixes their blood in its body, the speaker argues, it has achieved the physical union she withholds, and nothing terrible has happened. The phrase also echoes the biblical idea of man and wife becoming 'one flesh' (Genesis 2:24), which prepares for the marriage language of stanza two.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on 'The Flea'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 4; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:24

  4. maidenhead l. 6

    'Maidenhead' is virginity, and also (in the period) the hymen. The line itemises the three costs of yielding in the moral, social and bodily registers: sin before God, shame before the world, and physical loss. The speaker claims the flea's mingling involves none of them. The argument is sophistical, since it ignores the obvious difference between a flea bite and intercourse; part of the pleasure is in watching that gap being talked over.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'maidenhead, n.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 6

  5. enjoyes before it wooe l. 7

    'Enjoy' in early modern English often means to have sexual possession of someone. The flea reverses the order of courtship: it enjoys first and never woos at all. The line turns the speaker's frustration into a joke about his own role as wooer, which is what the whole poem is. The subjunctive 'wooe' (for 'woos') is normal in a 'before' clause in Donne's English.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'enjoy, v.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on 'The Flea'

  6. pamper'd swells l. 8

    'Pampered' means overfed, glutted. The flea's body, swollen 'with one blood made of two', has commonly been read as a comic image of pregnancy, the outcome a seduced woman of the period most had to fear. The speaker slips the image in as though it were innocent. 'One blood made of two' also restates the mingling of l. 4 as a kind of union or child.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pampered, adj.'

  7. one ... of two l. 8

    Donne returns again and again to the paradox of two lovers becoming one (compare 'The Canonization', 'we two being one', and the 'two soules ... which are one' of 'A Valediction: forbidding mourning'). In 'The Flea' the same idea is lowered into comedy: the union of souls becomes the mixing of blood in an insect's gut. Readers differ on whether this is simple parody of Donne's more serious poems or a reminder that his idealism is always tied to the body.

    John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981); Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction

  8. more then wee would doe l. 9

    'Then' is the regular early spelling of 'than'. The line is mock-pathetic ('alas'): the flea has achieved a fuller union than the lovers would. The line can be heard as mock-modest, since the speaker plainly would do more; 'would' also glances at her unwillingness. The triplet rhyme (wooe / two / doe) gives the line the weight of a conclusion to stanza one's syllogism.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 9

  9. Oh stay l. 10

    The break between stanzas marks an action we never see narrated: she moves to crush the flea. 'Stay' means 'stop, hold back'. The poem thus works like a small play, with her gestures supplying the turns of the argument: at stanza three she has killed it. This silent but active listener is characteristic of Donne's Songs and Sonets, where poems often begin in the middle of an exchange.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  10. three lives in one l. 10

    The flea contains three lives, his, hers and its own, in one body. The phrasing recalls the doctrine of the Trinity (three persons in one God), which the stanza continues with sacred language: 'mariage temple', 'cloysterd', 'sacrilege'. Critics disagree about the tone: some find the religious imagery merely playful, a young man's show of wit; others find its manipulation of sacred language, and of the woman, more troubling. The line also anticipates the 'three sinnes in killing three' of l. 18.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 10; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  11. more then maryed l. 11

    The argument moves from sex to marriage: if mingled blood is what marriage consummates, the flea has already married them. 'Yea more then' is a typical Donne self-correction, raising the stakes mid-line. The claim parodies the sacramental idea that marriage makes two into 'one flesh', and it sets up the flea as both marriage bed and church in the next lines.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:24; Matthew 19:5-6; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  12. mariage bed, and mariage temple l. 13

    St Paul calls the body 'the temple of the Holy Ghost' (1 Corinthians 6:19). Donne makes the flea's tiny body the temple where the lovers are wed and the bed where they are united, so that killing it becomes an act of desecration. Bed and temple are named together, so that consummation and sacrament collapse into one tiny space, a joke in keeping with 'enjoyes before it wooe'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 6:19; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 13

  13. cloysterd ... walls of Jet l. 15

    'Cloistered' continues the religious imagery: the lovers are shut up together as in a convent, hidden from the 'parents' who 'grudge' (disapprove). Jet, a black lignite polished for jewellery and mourning ornaments, describes the flea's glossy dark shell; the 'living walls' make the insect a little sacred building. The image wittily joins seclusion, chastity (the cloister) and secret union. Comparing the insect to a polished ornamental stone is mock-heroic, giving a flea the dignity of a shrine.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'jet, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cloister, v.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  14. use make you apt l. 16

    'Use' means custom or habit; 'apt' means inclined. The lover as 'killed' by his mistress's disdain is a Petrarchan cliche, which Donne literalises: she may be used to killing him with refusals, but she should not add the murder of herself and the sacrilege of killing the flea. 'Use' may also carry a sexual sense ('use' of a person), adding a teasing double meaning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'use, n.'; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 16

  15. selfe murder ... sacrilege, three sinnes ll. 17-18

    The speaker solemnly reckons the killing of the flea as three sins against three lives: murder of the lover, 'selfe murder' (since her blood is in it too) and 'sacrilege' (violating a temple). Suicide was a grave sin in Christian teaching, a subject Donne treated seriously in Biathanatos (written c. 1608), a defence of the view that suicide is not always sinful. The mock-theology is the climax of the second stanza and is immediately punctured by her action.

    Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 17-18; R. C. Bald, John Donne: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on Biathanatos

  16. Cruell and sodaine l. 19

    Between stanzas two and three she has crushed the flea with her fingernail, ignoring his argument. 'Sodaine' (sudden) here means impetuous or rash, as in Shakespeare's 'sudden and quick in quarrel'. The speaker's exaggerated outrage keeps up the comedy, but her act also seems to refute him, and he must now change direction. His exclamations ('Cruell', 'sodaine') are the language of tragedy applied to an insect, a mock-heroic effect.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sudden, adj.'; Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary

  17. Purpled thy naile, in blood of innocence l. 20

    'Purple' as a verb means to dye crimson, a high poetic word set comically beside 'naile'. 'Blood of innocence' casts the flea as an innocent martyr; some readers hear a glance at the Massacre of the Innocents (Matthew 2:16) or at 'innocent blood' in Scripture. The next lines ask what the flea's crime was, except taking a drop of her blood, ironically echoing the speaker's own wish.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'purple, v.'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 2:16; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to l. 20

  18. Just so much honor ... will wast ll. 26-27

    She has answered that neither of them is weaker for the flea's death. He seizes on her argument: then learn how false such fears are, since losing her honour by yielding ('yeeld'st') will 'wast' (waste, be used up) only as much life as the flea's death took from her, a trivial amount. The reversal is the poem's real wit: having argued that the flea was sacred, he now argues it was negligible. Critics debate whether she wins the exchange (her action exposes his sophistry) or he does (he turns her logic). 'Wast' and 'yeeld' carry military and sexual senses of surrender.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), note to ll. 25-7; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, notes to Donne

  19. stanza form st. 1-3

    Each stanza alternates lines of four and five stresses in couplets and ends with a rhyming triplet, usually all pentameter. The structure suits a disputation: each stanza builds through couplets to a summarising triplet, like the conclusion of a syllogism, and the white space between stanzas holds the woman's response. Donne invented most of the stanza forms of the Songs and Sonets for individual poems; this one exists only here.

    Helen Gardner (ed.), John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), introduction, on Donne's stanza forms; Robin Robbins (ed.), The Poems of John Donne, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2 vols (Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2008), headnote
