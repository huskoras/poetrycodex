Love

The Relique.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

WHen my grave is broke up againe
Some second ghest to entertaine,
(For graves have learn'd that woman-head
To be to more then one a Bed)
And he that digs it, spies
A bracelet of bright haire about the bone,
Will he not let'us alone,
And thinke that there a loving couple lies,
Who thought that this device might be some way
To make their soules, at the last busie day,
Meet at this grave, and make a little stay?

If this fall in a time, or land,
Where mis-devotion doth command,
Then, he that digges us up, will bring
Us, to the Bishop, and the King,
To make us Reliques; then
Thou shalt be a Mary Magdalen, and I
A something else thereby;
All women shall adore us, and some men;
And since at such time, miracles are sought,
I would have that age by this paper taught
What miracles wee harmelesse lovers wrought.

First, we lov'd well and faithfully,
Yet knew not what wee lov'd, nor why,
Difference of sex no more wee new,
Then our Guardian Angells doe,
Comming and going, wee,
Perchance might kisse, but not between those meales
Our hands ne'r toucht the seales,
Which nature, injur'd by late law, sets free,
These miracles wee did; but now alas,
All measure, and all language, I should passe,
Should I tell what a miracle shee was.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'The Relique': relics and the Reformation title; l. 16

    The poem imagines the lovers' bones dug up and venerated as relics in some future age of 'mis-devotion'. In Donne's England relic-cults had been suppressed and shrines destroyed since the 1530s, but Donne, raised a Catholic, knew the practices well. The poem plays with Catholic devotion while seeming to scorn it. First printed in 1633. Grierson thought it Platonic rather than Petrarchan in spirit and suggested it was addressed to Magdalen Herbert, mother of George Herbert and Lord Herbert of Cherbury, whom Donne knew well from about 1607.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Relique'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981)

  2. grave ... broke up againe ll. 1-2

    In crowded churchyards graves were reopened for new burials and old bones removed to the charnel house (compare the gravediggers in Hamlet V.i). Donne's sermons dwell often on the scattering and mingling of the dead in the earth. The opening is abrupt and macabre, and the poem moves from this grim future to the lovers' hoped-for meeting at the Resurrection. 'Ghest' (guest) treats the second corpse as a visitor to be 'entertained', preparing the joke of the next lines.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; William Shakespeare, Hamlet V.i

  3. woman-head ll. 3-4

    'Woman-head' is an old form of 'womanhood' (OED). The cynical parenthesis, typical of the Songs and Sonets, imputes promiscuity to women in general, even as the poem goes on to praise one woman's chaste love. The tension between misogynist wit and idealising devotion is characteristic of Donne and has been much discussed by feminist critics, among them Ilona Bell in her Cambridge Companion chapter, 'Gender matters: the women in Donne's poems'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'womanhood'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Ilona Bell, 'Gender matters: the women in Donne's poems', in Achsah Guibbory, ed., The Cambridge Companion to John Donne (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006)

  4. A bracelet of bright haire about the bone l. 6

    Gifts of hair were common love tokens. Grierson, discussing 'The Funerall', links this poem with that one, where 'That subtile wreath of haire, which crowns my arme' guards the speaker's body. In 'The Relique' the striking line juxtaposes the bright hair with the bare bone, the living token with mortality. The image also connects with the Magdalen, traditionally depicted with long hair.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Funerall'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  5. the last busie day ll. 9-11

    At the general resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:51-52; Revelation 20:12-13) souls were expected to rejoin their bodies; theologians debated how scattered particles would be gathered. The day is 'busie' because of all this collecting. The lovers' device is that the bracelet will make the lady's soul come to his grave in search of her hair, so that their souls 'meet' and 'make a little stay'. Donne treats the resurrection of the body at length in his sermons.

    Bible (King James Version), 1 Corinthians 15:51-52; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  6. mis-devotion l. 13

    Grierson keeps 'mis-devotion', the reading of the earlier editions and the manuscripts, and rejects 1669's 'Mass-devotion' as an ingenious editorial guess; he shows Donne using 'mis-devotion' elsewhere. The word places the poem in a Protestant frame: only in a time or land of false devotion would bones be made relics. The speaker's tone is ambiguous, since he plainly relishes the prospect.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Relique'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'misdevotion'

  7. the Bishop, and the King ll. 14-15

    In a Catholic setting, the bishop would authenticate relics and the king might sponsor a shrine. The pairing glances at the union of church and state that controlled religion in Donne's own England, where the monarch was Supreme Governor of the Church and bishops enforced conformity. The ironic picture of solemn authorities venerating two lovers' bones is part of the poem's comedy.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  8. a Mary Magdalen l. 17

    Western tradition merged Mary Magdalene (Mark 16:9; John 20) with the sinful woman who wiped Christ's feet with her hair (Luke 7:37-38), hence her iconography of long hair, which the bracelet recalls. Grierson notes that some manuscripts read 'You shalbe', explains Donne's use of 'thou' as the pronoun of feeling and intimacy, and suggests the poem was addressed to Mrs Magdalen Herbert. The comparison is double-edged: a famous saint, but one with a sinful past.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Relique'; Bible (King James Version), Luke 7:37-38; Mark 16:9; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  9. A something else ll. 17-18

    If she is Mary Magdalen, the obvious partner is Christ, and many readers find the vagueness a deliberate evasion of blasphemy. Others note medieval legends that made the Magdalen the bride of St John, or simply hear a mock-modest shrug. The next line, 'All women shall adore us, and some men', adds a further joke: women would worship the Magdalen, but only 'some men' the speaker.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981)

  10. this paper ll. 20-22

    Since such an age will seek miracles to authenticate its saints, the speaker leaves 'this paper' to set the record straight, as relics were accompanied by written authentication. The 'miracles' of 'harmelesse lovers' (innocent, doing no harm) turn out to be the paradoxes of a chaste love, listed in stanza 3. 'Taught' carries a teasing note: the speaker means to correct the future Church's errors about his own sainthood. The paper is also the speaker's claim that poetry, not relics, preserves the lovers' story.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  11. Difference of sex ... Guardian Angells ll. 25-26

    Angels were held to be without sex (compare Matthew 22:30: in the resurrection they 'neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God'). Each person was believed to have a guardian angel (Matthew 18:10). The lovers' love is thus angelic. Note that this archive prints 'wee new'; Grierson's text reads 'wee knew', and the sense requires 'knew'.

    Bible (King James Version), Matthew 22:30; 18:10; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  12. Comming and going ... kisse ... meales ll. 27-28

    Grierson explains the kisses as those of salutation and parting, quotes a Donne sermon on the kiss, and cites Erasmus's letter of 1499 describing the English custom of kissing on arrival and departure. 'Between those meales' treats kisses as nourishment: they took their kisses only at the proper 'mealtimes' of meeting and leaving. The kisses are thus within social propriety, a sign of the love's chastity.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Relique'; Desiderius Erasmus, letter to Fausto Andrelini (1499), on English kissing customs; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  13. the seales ll. 29-30

    A seal confirms a legal document, and 'seals of love' was a common figure for kisses or for the sexual act that consummates a bond. The lovers never 'touched the seals', that is, never consummated their love. The image belongs with the legal language of 'late law' in the following line. Some editors take the seals more concretely as the private parts, which nature 'sets free'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seal'

  14. Which nature, injur'd by late law, sets free l. 30

    The line appeals to a 'state of nature' before custom and positive law, when love was free. Editors compare Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.329-31, where Myrrha complains that what nature allows, envious laws forbid; the idea also appears in Tasso's Aminta and in Donne's own 'Elegy: Variety'. The lovers did not take the liberty nature grants, which is their 'miracle'. The line's sympathy with nature against law sits uneasily with the poem's celebration of chastity, an irony critics have noted.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.329-31; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  15. what a miracle shee was ll. 31-33

    The final lines move from 'wee' to 'shee': the real miracle is the beloved, whom no measure (metre, and moderation) or language could describe. The modesty topos (inexpressibility) closes a poem that has mocked relic-worship by itself canonising her. The ending contrasts with the cynical 'woman-head' of l. 3. Each stanza has eleven lines of varied length, rhyming aabbcddceee, and ends on a triplet, here 'alas / passe / was'.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem
