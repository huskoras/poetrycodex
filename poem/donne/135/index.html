Death & Dying

The Funerall.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Death & Dying

WHo ever comes to shroud me, do not harme
Nor question much
That subtile wreath of haire, which crowne, my arme;
The mystery, the signe you must not touch,
For'tis my outward Soule
Viceroy to that, which unto heaven being gone,
Will leave this to controule,
And keepe these limbes, her Provinces, from dissolution.

For if the sinewie thread my braine lets fall
Through every part,
Can tye those parts, and make mee one of all;
Those haires which upward grew, and strength and art
Have from a better braine,
Can better do'it; except she meant that I
By this should know my pain,
As prisoners then are manacled, when they'are condem'nd to die.

What ere shee meant by'it, bury it by me,
For since I am
Loves martyr, it might breed idolatrie,
If into others hands these reliques came;
As 'twas humility
To afford to it all that a Soule can doe,
So, 'tis some bravery,
That since you would have none of mee, I bury some of you.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening and occasion l. 1, title

    The poem is spoken from beyond death: 'shroud' means to wrap a corpse in a winding-sheet. It was printed in 1633 and belongs to a group of poems (with 'The Blossome', 'The Primrose' and 'The Relique') that recur together in the manuscripts. Grierson thought it probable that this sequence was addressed to Magdalen Herbert, mother of George Herbert, in the earlier days of Donne's acquaintance with her; there is no firm evidence. The lady of this poem is the conventional Petrarchan mistress whose cruelty has killed her lover, whereas in 'The Relique' Grierson hears a Platonic tone.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 58, note on The Funerall; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, on The Funerall and The Relique; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  2. That subtile wreath of haire l. 3

    'Subtile' means fine, delicate, finely woven, with a hint of 'cunning' that the poem will develop. Bracelets of a beloved's hair were real tokens of the period. Grierson illustrates the custom from Sir Kenelm Digby's Private Memoirs, in which Theagenes (Digby himself) wears a bracelet of Stelliana's hair about his arm, later throwing it into the fire when he heard that she was to marry another. The same token returns in Donne's 'The Relique': 'A bracelet of bright haire about the bone'.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 58, note to l. 3; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'subtle'

  3. crowne, my arme l. 3

    Grierson's text reads 'which crowns my arme', and modern editions agree; the comma here is probably a punctuation slip, and the sense is 'which crowns my arm'. The verb is a deliberate hyperbole: a crown belongs on the head, the seat of the soul and of government, and this wreath will indeed claim to govern the body as the soul's 'Viceroy'. The word thus opens the political conceit of the first stanza.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 58, note to l. 3 (lemma reads 'crowns'); H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  4. The mystery, the signe l. 4

    'Mystery' was the term for a sacrament (Latin sacramentum translates Greek mysterion), and the Book of Common Prayer's catechism defines a sacrament as an 'outward and visible sign' of an inward grace. The bracelet is such a sign of an inward reality, the lady's soul or love. The vocabulary begins the poem's play with sacred objects, which ends in 'martyr', 'idolatrie' and 'reliques'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mystery'

  5. my outward Soule l. 5

    In scholastic thought the soul is the form that holds the body together; when it departs, the body dissolves. The speaker's real soul has gone 'unto heaven', so the hair, an 'outward Soule', is left to rule in its place. Donne's wit lies in taking a love-token literally as a metaphysical principle. The second stanza supplies a physiological reason for the claim.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  6. Viceroy ... her Provinces ll. 6-8

    A viceroy governs a province in the name of an absent sovereign, as Spanish viceroys ruled Naples or Peru. The soul, gone to heaven, leaves the hair to 'controule' the body; the limbs are 'her Provinces' (the soul's, grammatically feminine, as in Latin anima). The task is to keep them 'from dissolution', from both decay and political break-up. The political conceit of the body as a state was a commonplace that Donne sharpens by placing it in a corpse.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'viceroy'

  7. the sinewie thread ll. 9-11

    Endicott glosses the 'sinewie thread' as the nervous system. Galenic anatomy described the nerves as issuing from the brain and spinal marrow and spreading through all parts, carrying the animal spirits that give sense and motion; in this sense they 'tye those parts' into one person. The lady's hairs grew 'upward' from 'a better braine' (hers), so they should be stronger ties still. The pseudo-scientific argument is typical of the Songs and Sonnets.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'The Funeral', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 9; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  8. strength and art / Have from a better braine ll. 12-13

    The compliment rests on the idea that hair grows from the brain, nourished by it. Hairs that 'upward grew' (on her head) are nearer heaven, and their 'art' may also hint at craft or cunning, recalling 'subtile' in l. 3. The stanza then swerves into suspicion: perhaps she meant the bracelet not as a soul but as a fetter. The hairs are thus a superior nervous system.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  9. As prisoners then are manacled l. 16

    The 'except' clause reverses the stanza's flattering argument: the wreath might be a sign of his doom, like the irons placed on a prisoner condemned to execution. The long last line, with its stumbling elisions ('when they'are condem'nd'), imitates the weight of the chain. The ambiguity of the lady's intention is left open, and the next stanza begins by brushing it aside: 'What ere shee meant by'it'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  10. Loves martyr l. 19

    The lover who dies for love as a martyr dies for faith is a Petrarchan commonplace, which Donne develops through Catholic devotional practice: martyrs' remains were venerated as relics. The same religious wit structures 'The Canonization' and 'The Relique'. The pathos of the dead lover is undercut by the playful precision with which he follows the logic of his metaphor.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, on The Funerall and The Relique

  11. it might breed idolatrie l. 19

    Reformed critics of Rome attacked the veneration of saints' relics as idolatry, and the English Church had suppressed shrines and relic-cults. The dead speaker, prudently Protestant, asks for the hair to be buried with him lest others worship it. The joke depends on the reader's awareness of the controversy, in which Donne, born a Catholic and later an Anglican controversialist, was personally involved. 'The Relique' imagines exactly what this poem forbids: the bones and bracelet dug up and taken for relics.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981)

  12. reliques l. 20

    From Latin reliquiae, remains. The word links the poem with 'The Relique', where the grave-digger finds 'A bracelet of bright haire about the bone' and the lovers become objects of misguided worship. Grierson notes that the two poems travel together in the manuscripts but differ in tone: here the lady is cruel and the love Petrarchan, there the relation has become Platonic.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, on The Funerall and The Relique; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'relic'

  13. humility ... bravery ll. 21-23

    Endicott glosses 'bravery' as 'a splendid gesture'; the word also carried the senses of bravado and of showy finery. The antithesis balances two attitudes in the lover: humility in honouring her token as if it could do 'all that a Soule can doe', and pride, or revenge, in taking part of her into the grave. The final stanza thus turns the poem from devotion to a sharp retort.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'The Funeral', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 23; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bravery'

  14. since you would have none of mee l. 24

    'Would have none of' means 'would not accept', a common idiom (Grierson cites Twelfth Night, 'she'll none of me'). Grierson found 'save' in the manuscript H49, and possibly in D, and adopted it, arguing that it was hard to see how it could arise as a corruption, that it gives a sharper antithesis (she would not save him, keep him alive, so he buries part of her), and that being saved is the lover's usual prayer. Many editors keep the 1633 reading, as this text does. Either way the final line's paradox stands: rejected whole, he takes a part of her to the grave.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 58, note to l. 24; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  15. Stanza form ll. 1-8, 9-16, 17-24

    Each stanza rhymes ababcdcd, with lines ranging from two or three stresses ('Nor question much', 'Through every part', 'For since I am') to long final lines of twelve syllables or more ('And keepe these limbes, her Provinces, from dissolution'). The short lines act as hesitations within the argument, while the drawn-out last line of each stanza gives a weight like a sentence passed. The pattern is repeated exactly in each stanza.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem
