Love

The good-morrow.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

I Wonder by my troth, what thou, and I
Did, till we lov'd, were we not wean'd till then?
But suck'd on countrey pleasures; childishly?
Or snorted we in the seaven sleepers den?
T'was so; But this, all pleasures fancies bee.
If ever any beauty I did see,
Which I desir'd, and got, t'was but a dreame of thee.

And now good morrow to our waking soules,
Which watch not one another out of feare;
For love, all love of other sights controules,
And makes one little roome, an every where.
Let sea-discoverers to new worlds have gone,
Let Maps to other, worlds on worlds have showne,
Let us possesse one world, each hath one, and is one.

My face in thine eye, thine in mine appeares,
And true plaine hearts doe in the faces rest,
Where can we finde two better hemispheares
Without sharpe North, without declining West?
What ever dyes, was not mixt equally;
If our two loves be one, or, thou and I
Love so alike, that none doe slacken, none can die.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'The Good-morrow' and the aubade title; l. 8

    The aubade, a song of lovers parting at dawn, was a familiar lyric kind (compare Romeo and Juliet III.v). Donne's poem begins not with daybreak but with a question about the lovers' whole past, and the dawn it greets is a spiritual one: their 'waking soules' have only now come alive in love. The poem was first printed in 1633. Grierson found two distinct manuscript versions; the 1633 text, followed here ('countrey pleasures', 'snorted', 'one world', 'better'), he judged the racier and probably the earlier. The date is unknown; many critics associate it with the love poetry of the later 1590s.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  2. by my troth l. 1

    The mild oath sets the conversational, surprised tone characteristic of Donne's openings. 'Troth' (a variant of 'truth') means good faith, and was the word for a promise of marriage ('to plight one's troth', OED). The oath therefore quietly anticipates the poem's claim of mutual fidelity, while its colloquial abruptness breaks with the smooth openings of Elizabethan sonnets. Compare the brusque first lines of 'The Canonization' and 'The Sunne Rising'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'troth'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  3. wean'd ... suck'd ll. 2-3

    The speaker treats all earlier experience as infancy: not yet weaned, the lovers 'suck'd' on simple pleasures 'childishly'. The image makes their earlier affairs both innocent and contemptible, and their present love a kind of coming of age. The vocabulary of nursing also belongs to the poem's language of birth and waking. The questions are rhetorical: the speaker answers them himself in l. 5, 'T'was so'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  4. countrey pleasures l. 3

    On the surface the phrase means simple rural amusements, set against the sophistication of the lovers' present state. Modern editors have noted the possible sexual pun on the first syllable of 'country', exploited by Hamlet to Ophelia (Hamlet III.ii, 'country matters'). The reading is textual as well as interpretative: Grierson records that the other manuscript recension reads 'childish pleasures seelily' (that is, sillily), a tamer line. Readers who accept the pun see the stanza as dismissing earlier, purely physical affairs.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem; William Shakespeare, Hamlet III.ii

  5. the seaven sleepers den l. 4

    The legend of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus tells how seven young men fled persecution under Decius, were sealed in a cave, and woke under the Christian emperor Theodosius II. It was spread in the West by Gregory of Tours and Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend, and appears also in the Qur'an (Sura 18). The lovers ask whether their life before love was such a sleep. 'Snorted' (snored) is deliberately undignified; the alternative manuscript version reads 'slumbred'.

    Catholic Encyclopedia (1912), 'Seven Sleepers of Ephesus'; Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 'The Seven Sleepers'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'

  6. but a dreame of thee ll. 5-7

    'But this' means 'except for this love'; all other pleasures are mere fancies. The claim that every earlier beauty was only a dream of the present beloved draws loosely on Platonic thinking, in which earthly beauties are imperfect copies of one ideal form. It also returns to the poem's opposition between sleeping and waking: the earlier affairs belonged to the dream; this love is the waking. The stanza's last line is a hexameter, a longer close used at the end of each stanza.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  7. Stanza form st. 1, l. 7

    Each stanza has four alternately rhymed lines followed by a rhyming triplet; the closing line is lengthened to six stresses (an alexandrine), giving each stanza a weighty conclusion: 'a dreame of thee', 'each hath one, and is one', 'none can die'. The three stanzas move from past (what were we?), to present (good morrow), to future (can our love die?).

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  8. our waking soules ll. 8-9

    The greeting is addressed to the lovers' souls, not their bodies: the waking is spiritual. In l. 9 they 'watch' each other, but not as jealous lovers do, 'out of feare' of infidelity. The line alludes to the common Elizabethan figure of the suspicious lover keeping watch, and claims a love secure enough to be free of jealousy. Compare 'The Sunne Rising', where the lovers are spied on by the sun itself.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  9. one little roome, an every where ll. 10-11

    Love governs ('controules') all desire to look at anything else, and so makes the lovers' room contain everything. The idea of the lovers as a world complete in itself recurs in 'The Sunne Rising' ('This bed thy center is, these walls, thy spheare') and 'The Canonization'. 'An every where' turns an adverb into a noun, a characteristic compression. Behind it lies the Renaissance commonplace of man as a little world, a microcosm, mirroring the great world.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981); Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  10. sea-discoverers to new worlds l. 12

    The late sixteenth century was the age of Drake's circumnavigation (1577-80) and Ralegh's Guiana voyage (1595); Donne himself sailed on the Cadiz (1596) and Azores (1597) expeditions. 'Let' is concessive: let others go discovering. The lovers' world is preferred to the new worlds of the explorers, as in 'The Sunne Rising', where the lovers outshine 'both th'India's of spice and Myne'. Carey has stressed Donne's fascination with expansion and possession; here the imperial vocabulary is turned into a claim of private sufficiency.

    John Carey, John Donne: Life, Mind and Art (London: Faber, 1981); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  11. Let Maps to other, worlds on worlds have showne l. 13

    The line can mean that maps have shown other people one world after another, or that celestial maps show worlds beyond worlds (new stars and the spheres of the cosmos). Grierson notes that the 1633 printing may have dropped an 's' which most manuscripts give, and that a plural without 's' was still common. The comma after 'other' in this text makes 'to other' mean 'to other people'. Either way the line extends l. 12: explorers and cartographers multiply worlds; the lovers need only one.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  12. each hath one, and is one l. 14

    The line compresses the stanza's argument: the lovers together 'possesse one world', yet each also 'hath one' (the beloved) and 'is one' (a world to the other). The other manuscript recension reads 'our world', which Grierson thought less characteristic. Critics disagree whether the line emphasises union (two in one) or a delicate balance of two separate worlds; the ambiguity prepares for the final stanza, where the lovers are two hemispheres of a single globe.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  13. My face in thine eye ll. 15-16

    Gazing closely, each lover sees a tiny reflection of his or her face in the other's pupil, an image popularly called 'looking babies'. Donne uses it again in 'The Extasie' ('pictures in our eyes to get') and 'Witchcraft by a Picture'. The faces show 'true plaine hearts': honest, unconcealing, because the face is the mirror of the heart. The eyes thus become the 'hemispheares' of the next lines.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  14. two better hemispheares ll. 17-18

    The faces, or the reflecting eyes, are two hemispheres, the halves of the lovers' one world. Unlike the real globe, this world has no 'sharpe North' (cold, bitter) and no 'declining West' (where the sun sets, and so decline and death). The other recension reads 'fitter' for 'better'; Grierson argued that Donne means each is a whole and better world to the other, not merely that they fit together. The cosmic and geographical images of stanza 2 are thus brought down to the lovers' faces.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem

  15. What ever dyes, was not mixt equally l. 19

    Grierson glosses this from Aquinas, who argues (on the incorruptibility of the soul) that corruption occurs only where there is contrariety: a simple substance, or a compound without contrary elements, cannot perish. In Galenic medicine also, death followed imbalance among the four humours. The lovers' love, if perfectly mixed, or if the two loves are 'one', should therefore be immortal. Grierson also compares a sermon in which Donne argues for the immortality of resurrected bodies.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I, q. 75, a. 6; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  16. If ... none can die ll. 20-21

    The last two lines offer alternatives: either the two loves are one, or the lovers love so alike that neither slackens. 'Die' carries its usual Elizabethan pun on sexual climax, so the line can also mean that love will never be spent. Critics divide on the tone: some hear triumphant assurance, others an anxious 'if' that admits love's mortality. Gardner and later editors note that the whole argument depends on the scholastic premise of l. 19.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem

  17. The two versions of the poem ll. 3, 4, 14, 17

    Grierson found that the manuscripts give two distinct recensions. The 1633 edition and a few manuscripts read 'countrey pleasures childishly', 'snorted', 'one world' and 'better'; the more common version reads 'childish pleasures seelily', 'slumbred', 'our world' and 'fitter'. Grierson preferred the 1633 text as more vigorous and probably earlier; Gardner and later editors debate which represents Donne's revision. The variants show that Donne's lyrics circulated in manuscript, and were likely revised, long before they were printed.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. II, commentary on 'The Good-morrow'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and the Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), notes on the poem; Robin Robbins, ed., The Complete Poems of John Donne (Longman Annotated English Poets; Harlow: Pearson Longman, 2010), headnote and notes on the poem
