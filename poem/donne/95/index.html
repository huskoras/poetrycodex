Love

A nocturnall upon S. Lucies day, Being the shortest day.

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

TIs the yeares midnight, and it is the dayes,
Lucies, who scarce seaven houres herself unmaskes,
The Sunne is spent, and now his flasks
Send forth light squibs, no constant rayes;
The worlds whole sap is sunke:
The generall balme th'hydroptique earth hath drunk,
Whither, as to the beds-feet life is shrunke,
Dead and enterr'd; yet all these seeme to laugh,
Compar'd with mee, who am their Epitaph.

Study me then, you who shall lovers bee
At the next world, that is, at the next Spring:
For I am every dead thing,
In whom love wrought new Alchimie.
For his art did expresse
A quintessence even from nothingnesse,
From dull privations, and leane emptinesse
He ruin'd mee, and I am re-begot
Of absence, darknesse, death; things which are not.

All others, from all things, draw all that's good,
Life, soule, forme, spirit, whence they beeing have,
I, by loves limbecke, am the grave
Of all, that's nothing. Oft a flood
Have wee two wept, and so
Drownd the whole world, us two; oft did we grow
To be two Chaosses, when we did show
Care to ought else; and often absences
Withdrew our soules, and made us carcasses.

But I am by her death, (which word wrongs her)
Of the first nothing, the Elixer grown;
Were I a man, that I were one,
I needs must know, I should preferre,
If I were any beast,
Some ends, some means; Yea plants, yea stones detest,
And love, all, all some properties invest,
If I an ordinary nothing were,
As shadow, a light, and body must be here.

But I am None; nor will my Sunne renew.
You lovers, for whose sake, the lesser Sunne
At this time to the Goat is runne
To fetch new lust, and give it you,
Enjoy your summer all,
Since shee enjoyes her long nights festivall,
Let mee prepare towards her, and let mee call
This houre her Vigill, and her eve, since this
Both the yeares, and the dayes deep midnight is.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and occasion title, l. 1

    A nocturn was a division of the night office of prayer, so the title promises a liturgy for the darkest hour. Under the Julian calendar, still used in England in Donne's day, 13 December was reckoned the shortest day of the year: hence 'the shortest day'. The poem was first printed in 1633 and cannot be securely dated. Grierson was inclined to connect it with Lucy, Countess of Bedford, Donne's patron, perhaps in the year of her serious illness, 1612, while allowing that it 'might have been written to Ann More', the woman Donne married and who died in August 1617. Gosse, whom Grierson quotes, thought the lady's death 'assumed, not actual'. Other readers have taken the poem as a response to Anne Donne's death, which is possible but unproved. The 'shee' of the poem is never named, and many readers treat the death as at least partly imagined.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, on Twicknam Garden and the Nocturnall; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1

  2. Lucy unmasks l. 2

    Lucy of Syracuse, a virgin martyr of c. 304, was traditionally a saint of light and of eyes, her name being derived from Latin lux. Donne makes the saint a masked lady at a court entertainment who 'unmaskes' only briefly: the December day is a face uncovered for less than seven hours. The pun on the name also points toward the Lucy of the poem's occasion (whether the Countess of Bedford or another), so that the shortness of the saint's day mirrors the absence of the poet's light. The day is personified as 'shee', anticipating the dead woman of the last stanza whose 'long nights festivall' replaces her day.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, on the Nocturnall and Lady Bedford; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 1

  3. flasks ... light squibs ll. 3-4

    Endicott glosses 'flasks' as an obsolete form of 'flashes'; it may also keep the sense of powder-flasks, the containers of a gunner's powder, since 'squibs' were small fireworks or crackers that fizz and go out. The sun, 'spent' like a used-up charge, can manage only spurts of light, 'no constant rayes'. The image belongs to Donne's world of contemporary objects: the cosmic decline of the solstice is pictured as a firework display petering out. Compare 'A flaske of powder burne a day' in Donne's 'The Broken Heart', where Grierson defends 'flaske' against the 'flash' of later editions.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), ll. 3-4; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to The Broken Heart, l. 8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'squib'

  4. The general balm l. 6

    Renaissance physiology held that every living body contains a natural balsam, an innate preservative that keeps it fresh and wards off decay; Endicott cites Donne's verse letter to the Countess of Bedford, 'In every thing there naturally grows / A Balsamum to keep it fresh and new', and Grierson prints the sermon passages on the 'naturall Balsamum' that lie behind it. At the solstice the 'worlds whole sap is sunke': the vital moisture of nature has gone down into the ground. The stanza assembles a picture of a dying world, its sap, balm and light all withdrawn, only to say in its final line that all these 'seeme to laugh' compared with the speaker.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 6; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, note to 'To the Countesse of Bedford', 'In every thing there naturally grows', quoting Sermons; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  5. hydroptique l. 6

    'Hydroptic' (from Greek hydrops, dropsy) describes a sufferer from dropsy, the accumulation of fluid in the body, a disease traditionally said to produce a thirst that drinking only increases. The earth, swollen yet still thirsty, has drunk up the world's balm. Donne liked the figure: the dropsy of insatiable desire recurs elsewhere in his work. The medical register continues in the next line with the patient shrunk to the foot of the bed.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 6; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hydroptic'

  6. as to the beds-feet life is shrunke l. 7

    Helen Gardner, cited by Endicott, traced the image to Hippocrates' signs of approaching death: a patient who sinks down to the foot of the bed shows a 'still more dangerous symptom' (Adams's translation of the Prognostics). Life in the world has contracted into the earth's lowest part, as warmth retreats from a dying body. The next line adds 'Dead and enterr'd', the vocabulary of the burial service, preparing for the speaker's claim that he is their 'Epitaph'.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 7; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on the poem; Hippocrates, The Book of Prognostics, part 3, trans. Francis Adams

  7. who am their Epitaph l. 9

    An epitaph is the inscription on a tomb. All the dead things of the first stanza seem cheerful beside the speaker, who is the very summary of their deadness, written over them. The conceit turns him from a person into a sign, preparing for the next stanza's invitation to 'Study me', as one would read a text. The stanza's movement, from the year's midnight down to the single figure of the speaker, is typical of the poem's narrowing focus.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  8. Study me then, you who shall lovers bee ll. 10-11

    The address to lovers recalls the advice-to-lovers convention of 'The Canonization', where the saints of love become a 'patterne' for others. Here the speaker offers himself as a negative example. The 'next world' is wittily deflated into 'the next Spring', the season of renewed lovemaking that the solstice promises everyone except him; the final stanza returns to these lovers with the sun's journey to the Goat.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)

  9. every dead thing l. 12

    The editions of 1635 to 1669 print 'For I am a very dead thing' or similar; 1633 and some manuscripts read 'every'. Grierson kept 'every' as the harder and more meaningful reading: the speaker is the quintessence of all negations, 'absence, darknesse, death; things which are not', and finally of 'the first nothing'. 'Very' would make the line merely emphatic. Most modern editors follow 1633. The phrase sets up the alchemical conceit of the stanza, in which love distils a quintessence from nothing.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to l. 12; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Elegies and The Songs and Sonnets (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), commentary on the poem

  10. love wrought new Alchimie ... A quintessence ll. 13-15

    Alchemy sought to distil from base matter the quintessence, the 'fifth essence' beyond the four elements, thought to be latent in all things and to compose the heavenly bodies. 'Expresse' means to press out. Love's 'new' alchemy works on 'dull privations' and 'leane emptinesse', producing the quintessence of nothing. Grierson discusses the stanza's punctuation at length: following the 1719 text, the 'privations' and 'emptinesse' expand 'nothingnesse'; with the earlier pointing, 'From dull privations' would go with 'He ruin'd mee'. He calls the passage difficult in a difficult poem.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to ll. 14-18; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), ll. 14-15

  11. ruin'd ... re-begot ll. 17-18

    Endicott suggests that 'ruin'd' is used in an alchemical sense, the reduction of a substance to its elements before it is transformed, and that 'absence, darknesse, death' probably correspond to the three alchemical principles, salt, sulphur and mercury. The speaker is then 're-begot', reborn, but his new parents are negations. The line ends on 'things which are not', which may echo 1 Corinthians 1:28, where God chooses 'things which are not, to bring to nought things that are'.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 17; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to ll. 14-18; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 1:28

  12. loves limbecke l. 21

    The limbeck, or alembic, is the cap of a still in which vapours condense, a standard piece of alchemical apparatus. Where 'all others' draw life, soul, form and spirit from things, the speaker, distilled by love, is 'the grave / Of all, that's nothing': he receives only negation. The word continues the alchemy of the second stanza and prepares for the 'Elixer' of the fourth.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 21; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'limbeck'

  13. flood ... two Chaosses ... carcasses ll. 22-27

    Stanza three remembers the lovers' past as a series of small apocalypses. Their weeping was a Flood that 'Drownd the whole world, us two' (lovers as a world is a favourite Donne idea, as in 'The Good-morrow'); when they cared for anything else, each became a 'Chaos', the formless matter before creation; and absences 'Withdrew our soules', leaving 'carcasses', since the soul of each lover lay in the other. These negations prepare for the stanza that follows, in which her death reduces him to something below even chaos: 'the first nothing'.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; Bible (KJV), Genesis 7:19

  14. (which word wrongs her) l. 28

    The first explicit mention of her death comes with a correction. Readers take the aside in different ways: as a Christian reservation (she is not dead but translated to a higher life, which the last stanza's 'festivall' and 'Vigill' support), as a lover's refusal to apply a common word to her, or as a sign that the death is a poetic hypothesis rather than fact, which was Gosse's view, reported by Grierson. The parenthesis interrupts the line exactly as the event interrupts the poem.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, Introduction, quoting Gosse on the Nocturnall; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  15. the first nothing, the Elixer l. 29

    'Elixir' here means quintessence, as Grierson shows from the OED. The 'first nothing' is the void from which God created the world (creation ex nihilo). Donne wrote in his Essays in Divinity that this nothing was 'no creature' and so more incomprehensible than anything created, a passage Grierson quotes. The poem, he observes, turns on degrees of nothingness: love made the speaker nothing, but her death makes him the essence of the absolute nothing before creation, below even chaos (stanza 3), which was at least matter.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to ll. 14-18, on 'elixir' and the 'first nothing'; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 29; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:2

  16. man ... beast ... plants, yea stones ll. 30-34

    The lines rehearse the Aristotelian and scholastic hierarchy of souls. A man, with a rational soul, would know his preferences; a beast, with a sensitive soul, would choose ends and means; plants, with a vegetative soul, take what nourishes and reject what does not; even stones attract and repel. Grierson quotes a sermon in which Donne allows that stones may have life. Since the speaker has no such properties, he is below the whole chain. Grierson also rejects the Grolier Club punctuation of ll. 31-2 as unintelligible.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to ll. 31-2; N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 31

  17. an ordinary nothing ... As shadow ll. 35-36

    A shadow is a negation, yet it implies the presence of light and of a body to block it. Grierson quotes a sermon in which Donne argues that if there is no shadow when the sun shines, there is no body in that place either. The speaker is not even an 'ordinary nothing': with his light, the woman, gone, he cannot cast a shadow. The thought leads straight into 'But I am None', the poem's lowest point.

    H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to l. 35; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  18. my Sunne ... the lesser Sunne ll. 37-38

    'But I am None' may carry a pun on Donne's own name, as his poems often do (compare 'A Hymne to God the Father', which plays on 'Done'). His private sun, the woman, will not renew itself as the solar sun does after the solstice. Calling the real sun 'the lesser Sunne' inverts the order of the cosmos for love's sake, a move familiar from 'The Sunne Rising'. Some readers also hear the Christian Son behind 'Sunne', since the poem ends with a vigil.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem

  19. the Goat ll. 38-41

    The sun enters the zodiacal sign of Capricorn (the goat) at the winter solstice. Because the goat was the emblem of lechery, Donne imagines the sun travelling there 'to fetch new lust' for the lovers of the coming spring. The astronomical fact becomes an erotic errand from which the speaker is excluded: 'Enjoy your summer all'. Grierson argues that the full stop after 'Enjoy your summer all' (l. 41) is right, so that the following clause ('Since shee enjoyes...') explains the speaker's preparation, not the lovers' summer.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), l. 39; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to l. 41

  20. her Vigill, and her eve l. 44

    In the church calendar a vigil, or eve, is the night of prayer before a feast day. The speaker turns St Lucy's night into the vigil of the dead woman, whose 'long nights festivall' is her death, or her new life. He means to 'prepare towards her', that is, toward his own death and reunion. The religious vocabulary implicitly canonizes her, making her a second Lucy, and gives the poem its character as a 'nocturnal', a night office.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes to the poem; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 2, commentary, p. 44, note to l. 41

  21. Stanza form and circular close ll. 44-45, all stanzas

    Each of the five nine-line stanzas rhymes abbacccdd and mixes line lengths, with shorter lines in the middle of each stanza that seem to contract, like the light. The final line echoes the opening, 'Tis the yeares midnight, and it is the dayes', so that the poem enacts a single unmoving hour. The circular structure has been read as a sign that no progress or consolation is possible within the poem, though the vigil looks beyond it.

    N. J. Endicott, notes to 'A Nocturnal upon St Lucy's Day', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), rhyme-scheme field; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1 (text)
