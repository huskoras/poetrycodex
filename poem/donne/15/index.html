Love

(untitled)

by John Donne 1572–1631

Love

Batter my heart, three person'd God; for, you
As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend;
That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee, 'and bend
Your force, to breake, blowe, burn and make me new,
I, like an usurpt towne, to'another due,
Labour to'admit you, but Oh, to no end,
Reason your viceroy in mee, mee should defend,
But is captiv'd, and proves weake or untrue,
Yet dearely'I love you, and would be lov'd faine,
But am betroth'd unto your enemie,
Divorce mee, 'untie, or breake that knot againe,
Take mee to you, imprison mee, for I
Except you'enthral mee, never shall be free,
Nor ever chast, except you ravish mee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Batter my heart': the sonnet and its date l. 1

    The poem is untitled in this archive, as in the early editions, and is known by its first words. It was first printed among the 'Holy Sonnets' in the posthumous Poems of 1633 (as X) and renumbered XIV in 1635, the number Grierson kept. Helen Gardner dated most of the Holy Sonnets to 1609-11, the years of Donne's search for a career before ordination (1615); later scholars, including the editors of the Donne Variorum, treat her dating and her neat 'sequences' as less certain, since no occasion is recorded. The Holy Sonnets survive in several manuscript sequences, which the Variorum prints; the Westmoreland manuscript orders them differently from both the 1633 and 1635 editions. Whatever its date, it is the most violent of the Holy Sonnets: a prayer that God stop gently 'mending' the speaker and take him by force.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; Gary A. Stringer et al., eds, The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Holy Sonnets: Batter my heart', text and notes

  2. three person'd God l. 1

    'Three-personed' renders the credal formula of one God in three persons (Latin personae, Greek hypostaseis), as in the Athanasian Creed. Many readers since the twentieth century map the two triads of verbs onto the persons: 'knocke' and 'breake' to the Father, 'breathe' and 'blowe' to the Spirit (Latin spiritus, 'breath'; compare John 3:8 and 20:22), 'shine' and 'burn' to the Son (with a pun on sun/Son). Others, including Gardner, see the pattern as suggestive rather than exact. Either way the opening addresses the whole Godhead with the intimacy and bluntness of the Elizabethan lover's complaint.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; Bible (King James Version), John 20:22; John 3:8; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  3. knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend ll. 2-4

    The gentle verbs of l. 2 (knock, breathe, shine, mend) suggest a craftsman tapping, blowing on and polishing a dented pot; l. 4 answers each with a violent counterpart (break, blow, burn) and adds 'make me new', as if the vessel must be smashed and refounded in the fire. Behind it lie biblical images of God as potter and refiner (Jeremiah 18:1-6; Malachi 3:2-3) and the promise of a new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17; Revelation 21:5). 'Knocke' also recalls Revelation 3:20, where Christ stands at the door and knocks: the speaker complains that such courteous knocking is not enough. The monosyllabic, heavily stressed lines enact the battering the speaker demands.

    Bible (King James Version), Revelation 3:20; 21:5; Jeremiah 18:1-6; 2 Corinthians 5:17; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  4. Metre and stress ll. 1-4

    The sonnet is in iambic pentameter, but its opening lines are packed with stressed monosyllables: 'Batter my heart' begins with a reversed foot, and 'breake, blowe, burn' sets three heavy stresses side by side. The elisions marked in the early text (''and bend', 'to'another', 'dearely'I') show that the lines were meant to be read as ten syllables even when crowded. Critics have long praised Donne's way of letting sense and passion drive the metre; here the strain on the iambic pattern mimics the assault the speaker begs for. Students should read the lines aloud, keeping the elisions.

    A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants)

  5. That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee l. 3

    The line turns on a Christian paradox: to rise (in grace, and finally at the Resurrection) one must first be overthrown. Compare Luke 1:52 and the Pauline pattern of dying to sin in order to live (Romans 6:4-11). Some readers also hear the posture of a wrestler or a knight thrown in combat. The paradox prepares for the larger ones of the final couplet, where freedom comes through enthralment and chastity through ravishment.

    Bible (King James Version), Romans 6:4-11; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  6. an usurpt towne l. 5

    The second quatrain changes the conceit from metalwork to siege warfare. The soul is a town 'usurped' (seized without right) by the enemy, Satan, though it is 'due' to another, God. The allegory of the soul as a besieged castle or town is old (the morality play The Castle of Perseverance; Spenser's House of Alma in The Faerie Queene II.ix-xi) and was revived in Bunyan's later Holy War. The town 'labours' to admit its true lord but cannot open its own gates: a compressed statement of the Protestant view that the will cannot free itself without grace.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene, Book II, cantos ix-xi (the House of Alma)

  7. Reason your viceroy ll. 7-8

    A viceroy (French vice-roi) governs in the king's name. In Renaissance psychology reason was the ruling faculty placed in man by God. Here the deputy 'should defend' the town but is 'captiv'd': taken prisoner, and proving either 'weake' (too feeble to resist) or 'untrue' (disloyal, gone over to the enemy). The two adjectives keep open a theological question: is human reason merely damaged by the Fall, or actually corrupt? Donne leaves both possibilities in play.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'viceroy'; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  8. Volta and rhyme scheme l. 9

    Like most of the Holy Sonnets, the poem uses a Petrarchan octave (abba abba: you/mend/bend/new; due/end/defend/untrue) and a sestet rhyming cdcd ee, ending in an English couplet. The turn comes at l. 9 with 'Yet': after the confession of helplessness, the speaker declares love, and the poem's dominant metaphor becomes marital and erotic. The couplet then gathers the poem's paradoxes into two lines.

    Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Holy Sonnets: Batter my heart', text and notes

  9. betroth'd unto your enemie l. 10

    Betrothal was a binding contract, and breaking it required legal action, hence 'Divorce mee' and 'untie, or breake that knot' in l. 11. The biblical prophets describe Israel as God's bride gone astray and God as the husband who reclaims her: Hosea 2:19-20 ('I will betroth thee unto me for ever') and Jeremiah 3. The soul as bride of Christ also comes from the traditional allegorical reading of the Song of Songs. Donne's speaker, conventionally gendered female as the soul (anima) was, asks the divine lover to break the prior contract by force.

    Bible (King James Version), Hosea 2:19-20; Jeremiah 3:8, 14; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  10. knot l. 11

    'Knot' was standard for the tie of marriage or betrothal (OED). The speaker lists alternatives in descending order of legality: divorce (a legal dissolution), untying (a gentle release), and breaking (violence). The progression mirrors the poem's argument that gentle means have failed. The early editions print apostrophes ('mee, 'untie') to mark elision, so the line still scans as pentameter. The legal sequence also recalls the distinction between dissolving a contract and annulling a marriage.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'knot'; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I (text and variants); A. J. Smith, ed., John Donne: The Complete English Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1971), notes on the poem

  11. enthral ... free; chast ... ravish ll. 12-14

    'Enthral' means to make a thrall, a slave; 'ravish' means both to rape and to carry away in spiritual ecstasy (OED). The couplet echoes Pauline paradox (Romans 6:18-22, made free from sin and become servants to God) and the language of mystical writing, where the soul is 'ravished' by God; compare KJV Song of Songs 4:9, 'Thou hast ravished my heart'. Critics are divided on the effect. Older readings stress the boldness of Donne's religious wit; feminist and other recent critics have asked what it means to figure grace as sexual violence on a speaker who takes the female part, and whether the shock is meant to be uncomfortable. The poem leaves the paradox unresolved: the speaker can only ask to be overcome.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ravish'; Bible (King James Version), Romans 6:18-22; Song of Solomon 4:9; Helen Gardner, ed., John Donne: The Divine Poems (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952; 2nd edn 1978), introduction and notes on 'Batter my heart'; Gary A. Stringer et al., eds, The Variorum Edition of the Poetry of John Donne, vol. 7, part 1: The Holy Sonnets (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2005)
