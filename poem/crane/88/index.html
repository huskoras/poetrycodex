The Mind

XXI. A man said to the universe

by Stephen Crane 1871–1900

From War Is Kind (1899)

The Mind

A man said to the universe:
"Sir, I exist!"
"However," replied the universe,
"The fact has not created in me
"A sense of obligation."

Study notes by the editors

  1. War Is Kind, XXI l. 1

    Stephen Crane (1871-1900), the author of The Red Badge of Courage (1895), published two books of short, untitled, numbered poems, which he called 'lines': The Black Riders (1895) and War Is Kind (1899). This is no. XXI of the second. Their style is spare free verse, often a little parable or dialogue, and their subject is often the human ego confronting God, fate or an indifferent cosmos. Crane is said to have been introduced to Dickinson's poems by William Dean Howells shortly before he began writing them, and critics have long compared their compression and irony with hers.

    Stephen Crane, Prose and Poetry, ed. J. C. Levenson (Library of America, 1984); Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957); Stephen Crane, War Is Kind (New York: Frederick A. Stokes, 1899)

  2. A dialogue parable l. 1

    Crane's 'lines' often take the shape of a fable: 'A man' (anonymous and typical) speaks, and something answers. Giving the universe a voice is a comic device, since a universe that can reply is, paradoxically, one that could notice us, and it uses that voice to say that it does not care. The poem has no metre or rhyme; its effect depends on timing, like a joke, with the punchline in the last line.

    Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957); Stephen Crane, Prose and Poetry, ed. J. C. Levenson (Library of America, 1984)

  3. Sir, I exist! l. 2

    The man's statement is the minimum anyone can assert, recalling Descartes's 'I think, therefore I am', but here it is offered as a claim on attention. The polite 'Sir' treats the universe as a superior to be petitioned, perhaps a gentleman or an official. The exclamation mark suggests both pride and anxiety: the man insists on his existence because he fears it does not matter.

    Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957)

  4. However l. 3

    'However' is the language of official correspondence and polite refusal. The universe concedes the fact ('The fact', it says, not disputing it) and then rejects what was implied. The comedy lies in the gap between cosmic scale and office-clerk diction, and the tone is dry, not cruel. Readers often compare it to the universe answering in the voice of a civil servant turning down an application. The single word marks the turn of the poem.

    Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957); Stephen Crane, Prose and Poetry, ed. J. C. Levenson (Library of America, 1984)

  5. A sense of obligation l. 5

    Obligation is a moral and legal relationship: a debt, a duty owed. The universe says it owes the man nothing. This is the central idea of literary naturalism, which Crane helped found in America: nature and the cosmos are not hostile but simply indifferent to human claims. His story 'The Open Boat' (1897) says much the same of nature, where nature appears to the shipwrecked men as 'indifferent, flatly indifferent'. Some readers also hear a rebellion against the providential God of Crane's upbringing (his father was a Methodist minister), who was supposed to care for every sparrow.

    Stephen Crane, 'The Open Boat' (1897); Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957); Stephen Crane, Prose and Poetry, ed. J. C. Levenson (Library of America, 1984)

  6. The repeated quotation marks l. 5

    The universe's reply opens with a quotation mark on each new line ("However ... / "The fact ... / "A sense ...). Older printing practice often repeated opening quotation marks at the start of each line or paragraph of continuous speech. The archive keeps the 1899 punctuation. Readers should not take the marks as signs of separate speakers: the universe says all three lines.

    Stephen Crane, War Is Kind (1899), no. XXI; Stephen Crane, Prose and Poetry, ed. J. C. Levenson (Library of America, 1984)

  7. replied the universe l. 3

    The universe is personified, given speech and manners, and yet what it says denies any personal relationship. Crane often personifies abstract powers (God, fate, the sea) in order to make their indifference or menace concrete. Daniel Hoffman's study traces this in Crane's poems as a running quarrel with the God of his Methodist upbringing. The universe has manners, but no heart: it is courteous, articulate and completely unconcerned.

    Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957)

  8. The fact has not created in me l. 4

    The universe grants the fact ('The fact has not created in me') and refuses the inference. Philosophers call this the gap between 'is' and 'ought', the argument associated with David Hume that no statement of fact by itself implies a moral obligation. Crane was not writing philosophy, but the poem condenses the same point into a cosmic snub. Some readers find the poem bleak; others find it bracing and comic, a deflation of human self-importance rather than a lament. Readers such as Daniel Hoffman, who stress the irony in Crane's lines, are alert to the wry humour that keeps the poem from despair.

    David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739-40), 3.1.1; Daniel Hoffman, The Poetry of Stephen Crane (Columbia UP, 1957)
