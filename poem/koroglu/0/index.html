I Come, and I Say to Thee

by Köroğlu 17th century (traditional)

Translated by Alexander Chodzko (1842) · From Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842)

LivingNature

I come, and I say to thee. Prince, listen and learn how a noble horse may be known. Active and brisk, see if his nostrils are rapidly swelling and shrinking alternately; whether his slender limbs are like the limbs of the gazelle, ready to commence its course. His haunches must resemble those of a chamois: his tender mouth yields to the slightest motion of the bridle, like the mouth of a young camel. When he eats, his teeth grind the grain, and make them crack like a millstone in motion; and he swallows them like a famished wolf. His back ought to remind you exactly of that of a hare: his mane is soft and silky: his neck is lofty, formed in semblance of the peacock's. The best time to mount him is between his fourth and fifth year. His head, neat and small as the head of the great serpent, chah-maur: his eyes projecting like two apples: his teeth are like so many diamonds. The shape of his mouth ought to approach that of a male camel: his limbs are neatly turned and fashioned; their shape is rather rounded than elongated. When brought out of the stable he is playful and prances. His eyes are like the eyes of the eagle, and he walks along with the restlessness of a hungry wolf. His belly and his ribs ought to fill the girth exactly. A young man of good family lends an obedient ear to the words of his parents: he pays the greatest attention to his horse: he knows by heart its descent and its pure blood; often he tries the vigour of the joints of its knees. In one word, he ought to be what Mirza-Serraf was in his youth.

This translation is printed as prose in its source edition.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Roushan, the young man who will take the name Kurroglou, improvises this lesson on judging a noble horse for a prince who claims to know horses. Chodzko notes that Persian connoisseurs of horses attach great importance to this description.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Roushan's first song: the occasion opening

    In Chodzko's Meeting I, Roushan's father Mirza-Serraf, master of the stud to Sultan Murad, presents two ugly colts sired by a stallion that rose out of the Oxus; the prince, insulted, has his eyes put out with a red-hot iron. The blind father raises one colt in a dark stable for forty days and trains it into a marvel. Roushan then rides out to meet the prince by the river and mocks him: 'thou believest thyself a good judge of horses'. This song is his lesson. Chodzko collected the cycle orally in northern Persia from professional singers and printed it in prose; the improvisations were in what he calls Perso-Turkish (Azerbaijani Turkish), and he gives no original of this one. In the Anatolian versions, as the TDV article summarises them, the tyrant is the Bey of Bolu and the father his groom (seyis).

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15; Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002); Pertev Naili Boratav, Köroğlu Destanı (İstanbul, 1931)

  2. The Prince opening

    Chodzko names the ruler Sultan Murad, ruler of a province of Turkestan, who keeps Mirza-Serraf as Master of the Stud. In the Anatolian Köroğlu stories the tyrant is the Bey of Bolu, whose groom is blinded over a scrawny colt; Turkish scholarship from Pertev Naili Boratav (1931) onward treats these as branches of one tradition. The imperative 'listen and learn' is the heart of the insult: the ruler who prided himself on judging horses is sent back to school by the son of the man he maimed. The song is both instruction and revenge, as the narrative explains just before it: the father has told Roushan to show the prince the horse and bid him 'die of envy'.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Pertev Naili Boratav, Köroğlu Destanı (İstanbul, 1931); Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002)

  3. The noble horse and its signs opening

    The song is a didactic catalogue of the 'signs' by which breeding shows, each organ compared to an animal: gazelle, chamois, camel, wolf, hare, peacock, serpent, eagle. Chodzko notes that Persian horse-connoisseurs attach great importance to this description and appeal to it in disputes over their racers; in his introduction he adds that the descriptions of Kyrat in Meetings I and V are considered the surest authority in questions of hippology. The horse is the true hero of the cycle: Chodzko says it is the only thing Kurroglou constantly loves, and the TDV article calls Kırat the hero's chief helper, a marvellous and tireless animal. The poem is a horse-praise, a genre also found in Arabic and Persian poetry.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15; Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002)

  4. The swelling nostrils opening

    The first point is breathing: the nostrils should swell and shrink rapidly in turn. Large, mobile nostrils were valued across the horse cultures of the Near East as a sign of good wind, the capacity to gallop far without tiring, and of high spirit; the Arabian horse is still bred and judged for them. 'Active and brisk' sums up the impression. Chodzko gives only a prose translation, but the list form suggests a catalogue a singer could expand or shorten at will. Compare the later song in the archive, 'A Noble Horse Walks Boldly', on a horse as fiery as its rider.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Karl Reichl, Turkic Oral Epic Poetry: Traditions, Forms, Poetic Structure (New York: Garland, 1992)

  5. Gazelle, chamois, young camel opening

    Each comparison picks out one quality. The gazelle's slender legs, 'ready to commence its course', stand for speed and lightness; the gazelle (ceylan, ahu) is also the conventional image of beauty in Persian and Turkish love poetry, so the horse is praised in the language of the beloved. The chamois, a mountain goat-antelope, gives the powerful haunches needed for leaping over rocks, fitting a mountain bandit's horse. The 'tender mouth' that answers the slightest touch of the bridle, 'like the mouth of a young camel', means sensitivity to the rider's hand. Chodzko's 'chamois' may stand for some other wild mountain animal of Iran; the original is not given.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  6. Millstone and wolf: the appetite opening

    A hearty appetite was taken as a sign of health and stamina: the horse grinds his barley so hard that it cracks 'like a millstone in motion' and bolts it like a hungry wolf. The wolf returns later in the song ('the restlessness of a hungry wolf') for the horse's eager, prowling walk. The animal is well chosen for a bandit's mount: the wolf, which hunts by night and lives by raiding, is the bandit's own emblem in Turkic tradition, and Kurroglou's raids on caravans are described in similar terms. The homely millstone shows the singer's pleasure in sound as well as sight.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Karl Reichl, Turkic Oral Epic Poetry: Traditions, Forms, Poetic Structure (New York: Garland, 1992)

  7. The back of a hare opening

    The hare's back is short, strong and slightly arched, the frame of an animal built for sprinting and sudden turns. Horsemen still prize a short, strong back for carrying weight and for quick acceleration. Chodzko's 'ought to remind you exactly' keeps the didactic tone: the song is a set of rules as well as a praise. The animal comparisons in the song are typical of the descriptive catalogues of Turkic oral epic, in which a hero's horse is often praised point by point.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Karl Reichl, Turkic Oral Epic Poetry: Traditions, Forms, Poetic Structure (New York: Garland, 1992)

  8. Silky mane, peacock neck opening

    The soft, silky mane and the 'lofty' neck carried in the curve of a peacock's describe the arched, proudly held neck admired in Persian and Turkmen horses. The peacock (tavus) is the emblem of beauty and display in Persian and Turkish poetry, and also, in Islamic legend, a bird of Paradise. The image moves the catalogue from function to beauty: a noble horse should be not only fast and strong but splendid to look at, fit for the parade before a prince.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  9. When to mount him opening

    The only plainly practical rule in the song: a horse should be mounted, that is broken and ridden hard, between his fourth and fifth year, when bones and joints are mature. Riding too young was held to ruin a horse. The rule fits the story: Roushan's colt is trained under strict conditions, forty nights over rocks and forty over marshes, a method Chodzko says the Turkmen used before a raid. Its presence shows that the song was valued, as Chodzko says, as a real authority on horses and not only as poetry.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  10. chah-maur, the great serpent opening

    Chodzko leaves the word untranslated except as 'the great serpent'. It is evidently Persian shah-mar, literally 'king-snake' (shah, 'king', and mar, 'snake'), a name for a large serpent; the Turkish form şahmeran names the serpent-queen of Anatolian legend. The point of the comparison is a small, neat, finely shaped head, a mark of good breeding in Near Eastern horses. A snake's head is also alert and quick, so the image carries a hint of danger, apt for a war-horse.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892), 'shāh-mār'

  11. Eyes like apples, teeth like diamonds opening

    Large, prominent eyes were valued in Arab and Persian horses as signs of courage, intelligence and good sight, and the comparison with 'two apples' gives their round fullness. 'Teeth like so many diamonds' moves from description into the jewelled hyperbole of praise poetry. Chodzko's introduction says that even exaggeration, the essential of Asiatic poetry in his view, is here comparatively sober; this catalogue mostly bears him out, its comparisons pointing to real features of a good horse.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  12. Eagle eyes and the restless walk opening

    After the static points the song turns to behaviour: brought out of the stable the horse is playful and prances, with the sharp gaze of an eagle and the restless walk of a hungry wolf. The eagle, a bird of prey and an emblem of sovereignty, matches the horse to its warrior rider; in the next song of the Meeting Roushan compares himself to a famished eagle refusing the prince's bait. A horse that fidgets with energy was preferred to a placid one; the later song in the archive, 'A Noble Horse Walks Boldly', praises a horse eager to throw its rider.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Karl Reichl, Turkic Oral Epic Poetry: Traditions, Forms, Poetic Structure (New York: Garland, 1992)

  13. The young man of good family close

    Near the end the song shifts subject: the ideal is now the rider, a young man of good family who listens to his parents, tends his horse, knows its descent and 'pure blood' by heart, and tests the strength of its knees. Horse and master mirror each other; both are known by breeding and training. The lines also describe Roushan himself, an obedient son carrying out his blind father's instructions to the letter, and imply that the prince, who could not recognise a noble colt, lacks such knowledge. Pedigree was the pride of Turkmen horse-breeders, who kept the lines of their horses as carefully as their own.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  14. Mirza-Serraf close

    The last line, 'he ought to be what Mirza-Serraf was in his youth', is the sting of the song. It names the master of the stud whom the prince blinded for presenting the very colt now standing before him, and so reveals the singer's identity: the prince at once cries, 'This is the son of Mirza-Serraf. Ho, there! let him be arrested.' 'Mirza', from Persian amir-zada ('son of an emir'), was a title for a gentleman or secretary. In the story Mirza-Serraf is also a skilled astrologer who foresees his son's career. Roushan will soon take the name Kurroglou, 'son of the blind man' (Turkish kör, 'blind', oğlu, 'his son'), as Chodzko explains.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting I, pp. 17-24 and footnote p. 24; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), pp. 26 and 29 (the name Kurroglou, 'son of the blind man'); Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002)
