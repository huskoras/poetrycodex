Death & Dying

A True Warrior Must Lose His Life

by Köroğlu 17th century (traditional)

Translated by Alexander Chodzko (1842) · From Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842)

Death & DyingHistory & Politics

A true warrior must lose his life on the field of battle. The guns and the carbines thunder for him the death-song. O death! whom didst thou spare? The most cunning of all men, Babai-Amir, once run from thee, and endeavoured to escape. Didst thou not catch him, jumping upon him from an ambush? The heart of Kurroglou the ram fears thee not, provided his vows may be agreeable to God. Were even Hassan and Hussein, these footstools of the throne of God, on the seventh heaven (Ersh) spared by thee? No! thou hast made them martyrs at Kerbella.

This translation is printed as prose in its source edition.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A song on the death of warriors on the battlefield, which asks whether even Hassan and Hussein would have been spared by death.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Kurroglou's last song: the occasion opening

    In Chodzko's last Meeting (XIII), two khans, Behram Khan and Almas Khan, entertain Kurroglou overnight and then cut the leg-veins of his horse Kyrat as he sleeps. Finding the horse weltering in its blood, he weeps, bares his breast and asks only to sing; after a song against inconstant Fate he sings this one, says the prayer of the dying and lets them behead him. His head is taken to Isfahan to the shah. Chodzko's introduction says that without Kyrat 'he is nothing' and dies an hour after his horse. Anatolian versions end differently: there Köroğlu withdraws from the world, often linked with the coming of firearms. Chodzko's songs are prose versions of oral originals in Azerbaijani Turkish.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15; Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002); Pertev Naili Boratav, Köroğlu Destanı (İstanbul, 1931)

  2. Guns and the death-song opening

    Kurroglou imagines the fitting end of a warrior: on the battlefield, with guns and carbines thundering his dirge. The irony is that he is about to die not in battle but by treachery at night. Firearms are a marker of date: the cycle belongs to the age of muskets, the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when, according to the TDV article, a historical Köroğlu (named Rûşen in Ottoman archives of 1580-5) was active in Anatolia as a rebel. The best-known Turkish Köroğlu verse treats the gun as the end of heroism, since the brave can now be shot down from a distance by a coward ('Tüfenk icad oldu, mertlik bozuldu'). Chodzko's song, by contrast, accepts the gun as the warrior's music. Readers differ over how far the motif reflects real historical change.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002); Pertev Naili Boratav, Köroğlu Destanı (İstanbul, 1931); Türk Edebiyatı (turkedebiyati.org), page on Köroğlu with the text of the 'tüfenk icad oldu' koçaklama

  3. 'O death! whom didst thou spare?' opening

    The direct address to Death and the question 'whom didst thou spare?' introduce a short catalogue of the great and clever whom death overtook: the runner Babai-Amir, then Hassan and Hussein. The song just before it in the narrative does the same with Fate, naming Joseph, Solomon and Kay Kavus. The pattern, which European critics call ubi sunt from the Latin 'where are they?', is common in Persian and Turkish elegy and in the Qur'anic warnings that every soul shall taste death (Q 3:185). Its effect here is consolation: if even saints and the swiftest of men could not escape, a bandit need not be ashamed to die.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; The Qur'an, 3:185; Karl Reichl, Turkic Oral Epic Poetry: Traditions, Forms, Poetic Structure (New York: Garland, 1992)

  4. Babai-Amir, the runner opening

    Chodzko's footnote explains that Babai-Amir was the famous runner (courier) of Shah Abbas the Great (r. 1588-1629), said to cover forty farsakhs, about 160 English miles, in twenty-four hours. In old age, rich, he loaded his camels and set out on a journey to escape death; when he sat down, tired, to take breath, Ezrael, the angel of death, who could never catch him while he ran, killed him. The tale belongs to the family of fables about the vain flight from death, best known in English as the 'appointment in Samarra'. The song's 'jumping upon him from an ambush' makes Death a bandit like Kurroglou himself, waiting in hiding for his prey.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Abbas I'

  5. Death in ambush opening

    Kurroglou's own trade was ambush: Chodzko describes him plundering caravans on the road between Khoi and Erzurum from his fort at Chamly-bill, and in another song in the archive, 'The Spring Comes', he thanks the mountains for keeping the secret of his hiding places. Death the ambusher is therefore the one robber who can outwit him. The image also prepares the narrative irony: Kurroglou himself is about to be taken by stealth, by hosts who attack him in his sleep, not by an enemy in open battle.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  6. 'Kurroglou the ram' opening

    Chodzko's note on an earlier song explains the epithet: the Persians are very fond of ram-fighting and regard rams as patterns of courage and strength, hence the frequent comparison of warriors to rams. In Turkish the brave young man is a koç yiğit, 'a ram of a hero', and the heroic songs of the Anatolian minstrels, Köroğlu's above all, are called koçaklama, a type of koşma on war and valour. Here the hero names himself in the third person, as minstrel poets sign their songs, in a formula of self-praise: the ram's heart does not fear Death.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), p. 77, footnote on 'Kurroglou, the ram'; TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, 'Koşma' (koçaklama as the heroic type of koşma); Pertev Naili Boratav, Köroğlu Destanı (İstanbul, 1931)

  7. The condition: acceptable vows opening

    The fearless boast is qualified: Kurroglou does not fear death provided his vows are pleasing to God. A vow (Turkish adak, Arabic nadhr) is a promise made to God, often of an offering or a good deed in return for help; Chodzko's narrative stresses that Kurroglou keeps his knightly word above all else. The condition shows the mixture of violence and devotion typical of the hero: after the song he 'piously' says the prayer of the dying. In Chodzko's version he is a convert to Shi'a Islam, which leads to the martyrs named next.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  8. Hassan and Hussein close

    Chodzko's note: they are the sons of 'Ali and Fatima, the Prophet's daughter; one perished by poison, the other was murdered in the desert of Karbala. Hasan (d. 670) was believed by Shi'a tradition to have been poisoned; Husayn was killed with most of his family and followers at Karbala in Iraq in 680 (10 Muharram, AH 61) by the army of the Umayyad caliph Yazid. For Shi'a Muslims, and for the Alevi and Bektashi communities of Anatolia among whom Köroğlu's songs were sung, they are the supreme martyrs, mourned every year at Ashura. In Chodzko's narrative Kurroglou and his father turn Shi'a at Mashhad, and in an early song Kurroglou says he will go to Persia 'to establish there the worship of Ali'.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), pp. 26-9 (conversion at Mashhad); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Battle of Karbala'

  9. 'footstools of the throne of God' (Ersh) close

    The 'Ersh' of Chodzko's text is Arabic 'arsh, the Throne of God (Q 7:54); the 'footstool' recalls the kursi of the Throne verse, which 'extends over the heavens and the earth' (Q 2:255). In Islamic cosmology the Throne lies above the seven heavens, so Chodzko's 'seventh heaven (Ersh)' runs the two ideas together. Calling Hasan and Husayn its footstools places them at the highest rank of nearness to God, and makes Death's victory over them the strongest proof that no one escapes. Yunus Emre's ghazel 'That Mighty One of 'Be! and 'tis' in this archive likewise names the Throne and the Footstool ('Throne and Stool'), though not Hasan and Husayn.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; The Qur'an, 2:255; 7:54; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes

  10. Karbala close

    Karbala (Chodzko's 'Kerbella'), south-west of Baghdad and near the Euphrates, is the site of Husayn's death and shrine, one of the holiest places of Shi'a pilgrimage. To 'make martyrs' (shahid, Turkish şehit, literally 'witness') of the Prophet's grandsons is Death's greatest deed. The last word of the song links Kurroglou's coming death to theirs: he too is about to be killed by treachery, unresisting, after a night of hospitality. Chodzko's version thus closes the cycle as a kind of martyrdom, quite unlike the Anatolian tradition in which Köroğlu does not die but disappears.

    Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (1842), Meeting XIII, pp. 340-3, with Chodzko's footnotes p. 342; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Karbala'; Faruk Sümer and Nurettin Albayrak, 'Köroğlu', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 26 (2002)
