Love

Vita Nuova: 'My lady looks so gentle and so pure'

by Dante Alighieri 1265–1321

Translated by Dante Gabriel Rossetti (1861; text of the Ellis and Elvey edition, 1899) · From La Vita Nuova

Love

My lady looks so gentle and so pure
When yielding salutation by the way,
That the tongue trembles and has nought to say,
And the eyes, which fain would see, may not endure.
And still, amid the praise she hears secure,
She walks with humbleness for her array;
Seeming a creature sent from Heaven to stay
On earth, and show a miracle made sure.
She is so pleasant in the eyes of men
That through the sight the inmost heart doth gain
A sweetness which needs proof to know it by:
And from between her lips there seems to move
A soothing essence that is full of love,
Saying for ever to the spirit, “Sigh!”

Study notes by the editors

  1. Tanto gentile in the Vita Nuova l. 1

    In the prose that frames it, Dante describes how people ran to see Beatrice in the streets, how those she greeted could not raise their eyes, and how she went 'crowned and clothed with humility'. The sonnet is the summit of the 'praise style' (stilo de la loda) that Dante adopts in the middle of the Vita Nuova, after resolving to write only in praise of Beatrice rather than of his own suffering. Barbi numbers the chapter XXVI; Gorni's edition, chapter 17. Rossetti translated the whole Vita Nuova as a young man (chiefly 1845-49) and published it in The Early Italian Poets (1861). Robert Pogue Harrison calls the poem pivotal, the point where Beatrice's greeting becomes redemptive.

    Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI; Dante Alighieri, Vita Nova, ed. Guglielmo Gorni (Turin: Einaudi, 1996), ch. 17; Dante Alighieri, The New Life (La Vita Nuova), trans. Dante Gabriel Rossetti (Project Gutenberg text, with prefatory note on the date of translation); Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011

  2. looks (pare) l. 1

    Gianfranco Contini's famous essay of 1947 showed that almost every key word of this sonnet has shifted meaning since Dante. 'Parere', here and in 'par che sia' (l. 7) and 'par che... si mova' (l. 12), means 'to show itself, to be evidently': Beatrice does not merely seem noble, her nobility is made manifest. Rossetti, like most nineteenth-century readers, renders it as appearance ('looks', 'Seeming', 'there seems'), which introduces a note of doubt that Dante's lines do not have. The repeated verb ties the poem to its theme of manifestation and miracle.

    Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Donato Pirovano, 'Lettura di Tanto gentile e tanto onesta pare' (University of Turin, open-access PDF); Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17])

  3. gentle and so pure (gentile, onesta) l. 1

    Gentilezza, nobility of soul as opposed to birth, was the central value of the dolce stil novo, theorised in Guinizzelli's canzone 'Al cor gentil' and in Dante's 'Amore e 'l cor gentil sono una cosa' (VN XX). 'Onesta' derives from Latin honestas, outward dignity and seemliness that reveals inner worth. Contini stressed that both describe what appears in public, matching 'pare'. Rossetti's 'gentle and so pure' is beautiful but moves toward modern softness and moral purity, a characteristic Victorian shading.

    Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Donato Pirovano, 'Lettura di Tanto gentile e tanto onesta pare' (University of Turin, open-access PDF)

  4. salutation (saluto / salute) l. 2

    Earlier in the Vita Nuova (chs. III, X-XI) Beatrice's greeting brings the poet a bliss beyond which he desires nothing, and her denial of it plunges him into grief. Dante plays on saluto (greeting) and salute (health, salvation), both from Latin salus. The greeting is thus a quasi-sacramental act. Rossetti's 'yielding salutation by the way' keeps the formality but not the pun, which English cannot carry; his 'by the way' (in the street) comes from the prose context.

    Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI; Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011

  5. the tongue trembles l. 3

    The speechless, trembling beholder is a stilnovo topos; Cavalcanti's sonnet 'Chi è questa che vèn' (in the archive in Rossetti's version) says that in her presence 'none / Dare speak'. Dante's 'ogne lingua', every tongue, makes the effect universal, not just the lover's. Rossetti's 'the tongue' and 'has nought to say' soften the generalisation slightly. Critics often read the whole sonnet as Dante's answer to Cavalcanti's: the same effects, but turned from anguish to blessing.

    Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011; Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation)

  6. may not endure l. 4

    Dante's verb is 'ardire', to dare: the beholder's eyes are overcome by reverence. Rossetti's 'may not endure' suggests rather a physical dazzlement, as before the sun. Both senses belong to the tradition in which the lady's light is too strong for mortal sight. 'Fain' in Rossetti's line means 'gladly, willingly' (OED, fain, adv.). The prose of the chapter makes the same point in narrative form: those whom she greeted lowered their eyes and could not answer her.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fain, adv.'; Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17])

  7. secure l. 5

    Dante's line is 'Ella si va, sentendosi laudare': she goes on her way hearing herself praised. Rossetti's 'secure' (Latin securus, from se- 'without' and cura 'care') adds an interpretation: the praise does not disturb her (OED, secure, adj., sense 1). The motif of public praise defines the 'stilo de la loda', the new style announced in Vita Nuova XVIII-XIX with the canzone 'Donne ch'avete intelletto d'amore' (in the archive as 'Ladies that have intelligence in love').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'secure, adj.'; Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI; Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17])

  8. humbleness for her array l. 6

    Humility as a garment recalls the language of devotion to the Virgin, who in the Magnificat praises God for regarding 'the low estate [humilitatem] of his handmaiden' (Luke 1:48). Umiltà in Dante's usage also has the sense of benign graciousness, not self-abasement. Rossetti drops 'benignamente' and turns the clothing image into 'array', apparel. The prose of the chapter repeats the phrase, saying she went 'crowned and clothed with humility'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 1:48; Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI

  9. a miracle made sure ll. 7-8

    These lines make Beatrice a revelation: she has come down to earth to demonstrate a miracle, an image with Christological overtones (Harrison speaks of her as a Christ-like figure). Later in the Vita Nuova (XXIX) Dante says that she was herself 'a nine', a miracle whose root is the Trinity. In Contini's reading 'par' here again means 'evidently is', so the miracle is shown rather than suggested. Rossetti's 'Seeming' and 'made sure' (made certain, demonstrated) pull in opposite directions; 'made sure' is close to 'mostrare', but 'Seeming' introduces doubt.

    Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011; Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI

  10. the sestet l. 9

    'Mostrasi' (she shows herself) continues the chain of 'pare' and 'mostrare': the poem is entirely about appearance in the strong sense of manifestation. In Dante the sestet rhymes CDE EDC (mira, core, prova; mova, amore, sospira). Rossetti's sestet uses rhymes of his own (men, gain, by; move, love, Sigh), with 'move/love' an eye-rhyme, and indents some lines in the manner of the 1861 printing. His octave keeps Dante's ABBA ABBA. 'Pleasant' renders 'piacente', which has the stronger sense of 'pleasing, delightful'.

    Dante Alighieri, La Vita Nuova, ed. Michele Barbi (Florence: Bemporad, Società Dantesca Italiana, 1932), ch. XXVI; Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Dante Alighieri, The New Life (La Vita Nuova), trans. Dante Gabriel Rossetti (Project Gutenberg text, with prefatory note on the date of translation)

  11. through the sight to the heart l. 10

    The physiology of love in the Middle Ages, from Andreas Capellanus to the stilnovisti, held that love is born of sight: an image enters through the eyes and descends to the heart. Here, however, what passes is not desire but 'dolcezza', sweetness, a gift rather than a wound. Harrison notes that a modern translation which omits the heart and the soul loses the poem's spiritual physiology; Rossetti keeps both.

    Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011; Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17])

  12. known only by experience l. 11

    The appeal to experience (prova) is a lyric topos: only lovers understand love. Petrarch uses the same word in Rvf 1 ('ove sia chi per prova intenda amore'). Rossetti's line keeps the logic but his 'needs proof to know it by' is compressed to the point of obscurity: it means 'requires to be experienced in order to be known'. The thought also anticipates the Paradiso's insistence that the highest experiences exceed telling.

    Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Donato Pirovano, 'Lettura di Tanto gentile e tanto onesta pare' (University of Turin, open-access PDF)

  13. lips... a soothing essence ll. 12-13

    'Labbia' in Dante often means the face or countenance, not the lips (compare Inferno 7.7, 'quella 'nfiata labbia', 'that swollen face'); Rossetti's 'from between her lips' takes the narrower sense, and editors differ. 'Spirito' belongs to the medical-philosophical doctrine of spirits, the subtle vapours that carried perception and emotion between eye, heart and brain, which Cavalcanti uses obsessively. Rossetti's 'essence' blurs this technical sense into something more Victorian and ethereal. In Dante the spirit 'si mova', moves, from her face to the beholder's soul.

    Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17]); Donato Pirovano, 'Lettura di Tanto gentile e tanto onesta pare' (University of Turin, open-access PDF)

  14. the final 'Sigh!' l. 14

    The poem closes on a single imperative word. In Cavalcanti the sigh is the sign of love's destructive power; Harrison argues that here it is instead a sigh of spiritual infusion, full of hope rather than despair, and that this marks Dante's break from Cavalcanti's influence. Rossetti renders 'anima' (soul) as 'spirit', which confuses it with the 'spirito' of line 13, and adds 'for ever', making the effect perpetual. Many readers consider this the most perfect sonnet of the Vita Nuova.

    Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011; Gianfranco Contini, 'Esercizio d'interpretazione sopra un sonetto di Dante' (1947), repr. in Un'idea di Dante (Turin: Einaudi, 1976); Teodolinda Barolini, Dante's Lyric Poetry: Poems of Youth and of the 'Vita Nuova', trans. Richard Lansing (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 2014), commentary on 'Tanto gentile' (VN XXVI [17])
