Nature

In the Quiet Night

by Li Po 701–762

Translated by Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu (1929) · From The Jade Mountain (1929)

Nature

So bright a gleam on the foot of my bed —
Could there have been a frost already?
Lifting myself to look, I found that it was moonlight.
Sinking back again, I thought suddenly of home.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Li Bai's 'Quiet Night Thought' (Jing ye si) title

    The original, 靜夜思 Jing ye si ('Thoughts on a Quiet Night'), is a four-line poem of five characters per line by Li Bai (701-762), perhaps the best-known poem in Chinese and memorised by schoolchildren across East Asia. It is not a strictly regulated quatrain: Guo Maoqian included it in the Song-dynasty Yuefu shiji, and it ignores tonal rules while rhyming lines 1, 2 and 4. Bynner and Kiang translated the whole of the Qing anthology Three Hundred Tang Poems (Sun Zhu, 1763), so they render that anthology's text. Bynner (1881-1968), an American poet, worked from versions prepared by the scholar Jiang Kanghu (Kiang Kang-hu). Their method, set out in the book's introduction, favours a plain, unrhymed English line that follows the sense rather than the form. Here each Chinese line becomes one long English line, and the rhyme disappears.

    Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; 'Quiet Night Thought', Wikipedia (variant table: Yuefu shiji, Ming and Qing texts); 'Jiang Kanghu', Wikipedia

  2. Which text? 'bright moon' or 'looking at the moonlight' l. 1

    The Song-dynasty Shu printing of Li Bai's collected works and Guo Maoqian's Yuefu shiji read 牀前看月光 ('before the bed I look at the moonlight') in line 1 and 舉頭望山月 ('I raise my head and gaze at the mountain moon') in line 3. The now-standard 牀前明月光 ... 舉頭望明月, with 'bright moon' in both places, first appears in Ming anthologies (Li Panlong's Tangshi xuan is usually credited, following the Japanese scholar Morise Toshizō and Chen Shangjun) and was fixed by Three Hundred Tang Poems. Bynner's 'So bright a gleam' translates this later text. In Japan the older Song text, with 'mountain moon', remained the one usually taught, so Chinese and Japanese readers learn slightly different poems. Neither reading changes the poem's movement, but the Song text makes the speaker an active looker from the first line.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; 'Quiet Night Thought', Wikipedia (variant table: Yuefu shiji, Ming and Qing texts); Report on Morise Toshizō and Chen Shangjun's work on the Jing ye si variants, The News Lens (Chinese)

  3. 'bed' (chuang 牀) l. 1

    Bynner's 'the foot of my bed' renders 牀前 chuang qian, 'in front of the bed'. The usual reading pictures the poet indoors, moonlight falling through a window onto the floor beside the bed. Because Tang windows were small and often papered, some modern Chinese commentators have argued that chuang here means a folding camp-stool (胡牀) used outdoors, or the railing round a well (井欄), so that the poet sits outside under the sky. These proposals are debated and none is generally accepted; most annotated editions and translators keep 'bed'. The detail matters for the poem's drama: indoors, the speaker first sees only light on the floor, mistakes it for frost, and must look up to find the moon. Bynner's 'foot of my bed' even adds a precise spot not in the Chinese.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, ed. and trans., An Anthology of Chinese Literature: Beginnings to 1911 (New York: Norton, 1996); Mathias Gredig, analysis of 'Jing ye si' (Bern), on the poem's undetermined setting

  4. frost (shuang 霜) l. 2

    The Chinese line 疑是地上霜 means 'I suspect it is frost on the ground'. The comparison of moonlight to frost was already conventional in Chinese poetry, but here it is staged as a real moment of misperception between sleep and waking. Frost belongs to late autumn, the season of travel, separation and the year's decline, so the image adds a chill that prepares the turn to home. Bynner turns the statement of doubt (疑 yi, 'suspect, doubt') into a question, 'Could there have been a frost already?', and adds 'already', implying that the season has come sooner than expected. That addition has no exact counterpart in the Chinese but captures the half-awake surprise.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription

  5. the moon l. 3

    The Chinese reads 舉頭望明月 (or, in the earlier text, 望山月), 'raising my head I gaze at the bright (mountain) moon'. The moon is the classic emblem of reunion and absent loved ones, since the same moon is visible to the traveller and to those at home; full-moon festivals celebrate family gathering. Bynner reshapes the line: 'Lifting myself to look, I found that it was moonlight' makes it a discovery that solves the frost riddle, whereas the Chinese simply names the act of looking at the moon. He also loses the repetition of 'moon' between lines 1 and 3 in the standard text. The poem never names home's people; the moon does that work silently.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; 'Quiet Night Thought', Wikipedia (variant table: Yuefu shiji, Ming and Qing texts)

  6. parallel couplet: raise / lower the head ll. 3-4

    Lines 3 and 4 of the original form a parallel pair: 舉頭望明月，低頭思故鄉, 'raise head, gaze at bright moon; lower head, think of old home'. The verbs 'raise' and 'lower', 'gaze' and 'think', and the objects 'moon' and 'home' answer each other word for word. Lowering the head is both a return to the pillow and a gesture of sadness or reflection, so the outward look becomes inward memory. Bynner keeps the contrast with 'Lifting myself' and 'Sinking back again' but adds 'suddenly', which suggests a sudden pang; the Chinese has no such adverb, and its effect is rather of a quiet, inevitable sequence. Owen's account of Li Bai stresses this sort of apparent artlessness as a deliberate pose.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription

  7. homesickness (si gu xiang 思故鄉) l. 4

    故鄉 gu xiang is one's native place, the ancestral home where family graves and parents are. Tang poets were often away for years, as officials posted to the provinces, as exam candidates or, like Li Bai, as wanderers seeking patronage; poems of longing for home form a large conventional category. Li Bai was born probably in Central Asia (Suyab) and grew up in Sichuan, and he left Sichuan young and never returned, so 'home' for him is itself a complicated idea. The poem's last word in Chinese is 'home', and its lightness lies in naming the feeling without description. The poem is usually dated to his travels in the 720s, though there is no firm evidence.

    Li Bai, 'Jing ye si' (靜夜思), Chinese text in three recensions (Li Taibai wenji, Quan Tang shi, Tangshi sanbai shou), Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei; Stephen Owen, ed. and trans., An Anthology of Chinese Literature: Beginnings to 1911 (New York: Norton, 1996)

  8. Bynner and Kiang's method l. 3

    Each Chinese line has five characters; Bynner's line 3 has ten English words. The translation glosses the logic that the Chinese leaves implicit: the speaker lifts himself, looks, and 'found' the answer. Such expansions were deliberate. Bynner wished to give English readers natural speech rather than the clipped 'Chinese' manner made fashionable by Pound's Cathay (1915), and he worked closely with Jiang Kanghu over many years on the meaning of each poem. Later translators such as Owen have preferred to keep the poem's bareness, and Weinberger's study of translations of Wang Wei, which prints Bynner's version among many, is a good guide to how often such versions add explanatory words. Students comparing versions should notice how far 'meaning' in English depends on such additions.

    Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); 'Jiang Kanghu', Wikipedia
