Drinking Alone by Moonlight (I)

by Li Po 701–762

Translated by Arthur Waley (1919) · From More Translations from the Chinese (1919)

Living

A cup of wine, under the flowering trees;
I drink alone, for no friend is near.
Raising my cup I beckon the bright moon,
For he, with my shadow, will make three men.
The moon, alas, is no drinker of wine;
Listless, my shadow creeps about at my side.
Yet with the moon as friend and the shadow as slave
I must make merry before the Spring is spent.
To the songs I sing the moon flickers her beams;
In the dance I weave my shadow tangles and breaks.
While we were sober, three shared the fun;
Now we are drunk, each goes his way.
May we long share our odd, inanimate feast,
And meet at last on the Cloudy River of the sky.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Drinking Alone by Moonlight' (Yuexia duzhuo), no. 1 title, l. 1

    Li Bai (701-762) wrote a set of four poems titled 月下獨酌 Yuexia duzhuo, 'Drinking alone beneath the moon'; this is the first, a fourteen-line poem of five characters per line in the old-style (gushi) form, which allows changes of rhyme. Waley printed three of the four under the heading 'Three Poems' in More Translations from the Chinese (1919), translating each Chinese line by one English line with a stress-based rhythm rather than rhyme. Li Bai's reputation as the drinking 'immortal banished from heaven' made such poems central to his legend. Line 1 in Chinese reads 花間一壺酒, 'among flowers, a pot of wine': Waley's 'cup' and 'under the flowering trees' are free renderings, and the Chinese 'pot' (hu, a flask) becomes the cup that is raised in l. 3.

    Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  2. drinking alone l. 2

    The Chinese 獨酌無相親 says 'I pour alone, with no one close to me'. Wine-drinking among friends, with toasts, poetry games and music, was the normal setting for Tang poetry, so drinking alone signals estrangement. Li Bai's literary model is Tao Yuanming (365-427), whose twenty 'Drinking Wine' poems also present solitary drinking as a way out of the world's falseness (see the Tao Qian poem in this archive, 'I built my hut in a zone of human habitation'). Li Bai's poem turns loneliness into a comic party with invented companions. Waley's 'for no friend is near' turns the statement into a causal clause.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  3. moon, shadow and self make three ll. 3-4

    舉杯邀明月，對影成三人: 'raising my cup I invite the bright moon; facing my shadow, we make three people'. The conceit treats the moon and the shadow cast by moonlight as guests, creating company out of the conditions of solitude. It has a precedent in the Chinese habit of personifying the moon, but the arithmetic joke is Li Bai's own and became proverbial. Waley's 'For he, with my shadow, will make three men' makes the moon masculine, a choice the Chinese, which has no grammatical gender, does not force; in l. 9 he calls it 'her beams', so the moon's gender shifts within the translation. Owen reads the poem as Li Bai's characteristic staging of a self at play.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  4. the moon cannot drink ll. 5-6

    月既不解飲，影徒隨我身: 'the moon does not understand drinking; the shadow merely follows my body'. The joke has a philosophical edge: the companions are real only as extensions of the drinker. 徒 tu ('merely, in vain') is Waley's 'Listless', which gives the shadow a mood not quite in the Chinese; 'creeps about at my side' is his vivid gloss on 隨 sui, 'follow'. Waley's 'alas' (l. 5) also adds a sigh absent from the original's matter-of-fact tone.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500

  5. 'the moon as friend and the shadow as slave' l. 7

    The Chinese is 暫伴月將影, 'for a while I accompany the moon, together with the shadow'. Most editors take 將 jiang as a connective ('with, and'), so the line simply pairs the two companions. Waley's antithesis of 'friend' and 'slave' seems to draw on other senses of jiang ('to lead, command'), making the shadow a servant who must follow; the variant text 暫伴明月影 ('the bright moon's shadow') shows the line was already unsettled in transmission. Waley also drops 暫 zan, 'for a while', a word that anticipates the parting in l. 12. Students should treat Waley's reading as an interpretation rather than the plain sense.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, ed. and trans., An Anthology of Chinese Literature: Beginnings to 1911 (New York: Norton, 1996)

  6. carpe diem: enjoy pleasure while it is spring l. 8

    行樂須及春, 'making merry must catch the spring'. 行樂 'to take one's pleasure' belongs to a long tradition of poems, from the Han 'Nineteen Old Poems' onward, that urge enjoyment because human life is short; the archive holds Giles's version of one such poem as 'Carpe Diem' (classical-chinese-anonymous). Spring here is both the season of the flowers in l. 1 and the prime of life. Waley's 'I must make merry' keeps the obligation in 須 xu, 'must'.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, ed. and trans., An Anthology of Chinese Literature: Beginnings to 1911 (New York: Norton, 1996)

  7. parallel couplet: song and dance ll. 9-10

    我歌月徘徊，我舞影零亂. Each line has the same grammar (I + verb, companion + response), the moon answering the song and the shadow the dance. 徘徊 pai-huai, 'to pace to and fro, linger', suggests the moon wavering or hesitating as if listening, which Waley turns into 'flickers her beams'. 零亂 ling-luan, 'scattered, in disorder', becomes 'tangles and breaks', a good image of a shadow on uneven ground as the drunk man dances. Note that Waley's moon is 'he' in l. 4 and 'her' here; Chinese allows either, and the moon is traditionally yin (feminine) and associated with the goddess Chang'e.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500

  8. sober and drunk ll. 11-12

    醒時同交歡，醉後各分散: 'when sober, we share our joy together; after drunkenness, each goes his way'. The paradox is that drinking, meant to unite, ends the company: the drunk man falls asleep, the moon sets, the shadow vanishes. Waley's 'three shared the fun' is colloquial for 交歡, 'exchange delight'. The couplet also echoes the language of human partings, preparing the final vow of reunion.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  9. 'unfeeling' companionship (wuqing you 無情遊) l. 13

    永結無情遊, 'forever bind ourselves in a wanderer's friendship without feeling'. 無情 wuqing literally means 'without feelings'. One reading is literal and comic: the moon and shadow are insentient, so this is friendship with things, which Waley renders 'our odd, inanimate feast'. Another reading, favoured by many Chinese commentators, hears a Daoist ideal: a bond beyond ordinary human attachment, free of the passions that make human friendship painful. 遊 you is 'roaming, excursion', a Zhuangzi word for free and easy wandering, which Waley turns into 'feast'. The two senses can coexist, and the line is the poem's turn from play to something like spiritual longing.

    Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  10. Cloudy River (yunhan 雲漢): the Milky Way l. 14

    相期邈雲漢, 'we pledge to meet in the far-off Cloudy Han'. 雲漢 yunhan, the 'Cloudy Han River', is an old name for the Milky Way, seen as a heavenly counterpart of the Han River; Waley footnoted the phrase 'The Milky Way'. The word goes back to the Shijing ode 'Yun Han' (Mao 258). The Milky Way is also the river that separates the Weaver Girl and the Cowherd, who meet once a year, so a promised meeting there carries a faint note of lovers' reunion across distance. The final line lifts the poem from tipsy play to a cosmic, almost Daoist, rendezvous with the immortals among whom Li Bai liked to place himself.

    Arthur Waley, trans., More Translations from the Chinese (1919), Project Gutenberg ebook 16500; Li Bai, 'Yuexia duzhuo si shou' (月下獨酌四首), Quan Tang shi juan 182, Chinese Wikisource (with variants); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei
