The Prophet

by Alexander Pushkin 1799–1837

Translated by Ivan Panin (1888) · From Poems (Panin, 1888)

Religion

Tormented by the thirst for the spirit
I was dragging myself in a sombre desert,
And a six-winged seraph appeared
Unto me on the parting of the roads.
With fingers as light as a dream
Mine eyes he touched:
And mine eyes opened wise
Like the eyes of a frightened eagle;
He touched mine ears,
And they filled with din and ringing.
And I heard the trembling of the heavens
And the flight of the angel's wings,
And the creeping of the polyps in the sea,
And the growth of the vine in the valley.
And he took hold of my lips,
And out he tore my sinful tongue
With its empty and false speech.
And the fang of the wise serpent
Between my terrified lips he placed
With bloody hand.
And ope he cut with sword my breast,
And out he took my trembling heart,
And a coal with flaming blaze
Into the opened breast he shoved.
Like a corpse I lay in the desert.
And the voice of God unto me called:
Arise, O prophet, and listen, and guide.
Be thou filled with my will,
And going over land and sea
Fire with the word the hearts of men!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, occasion and translator l. 1

    Pushkin wrote 'Prorok' ('The Prophet') in 1826, while still in exile on his family estate of Mikhailovskoe; it was first printed in the journal Moskovskii vestnik in 1828 (no. 3). The poem is usually connected with the crushing of the Decembrist rising of December 1825, the execution of five of its leaders in July 1826, and the exile of Pushkin's friends to Siberia; on the testimony of M. P. Pogodin, it was the first of four planned poems, of which the others are lost. Its subject is the call of the poet as prophet, told through biblical scenes of prophetic vocation. Ivan Panin translated it literally in his Poems by Alexander Pushkin (Boston, 1888), and his introduction reads its last lines as the poet's complete surrender of self-will.

    'Prorok' (stikhotvorenie), Znanie.Rossiya, on dating, the Moskovskii vestnik publication and Pogodin's testimony of four poems; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; T. J. Binyon, Pushkin: A Biography (London: HarperCollins, 2002)

  2. thirst for the spirit l. 1

    The first line sets the speaker in a state of spiritual longing, the condition for vocation. The image recalls the Psalms, as in Psalm 63:1 ('my soul thirsteth for thee ... in a dry and thirsty land') and Psalm 42:2, and Christ's blessing on those who 'hunger and thirst after righteousness' (Matthew 5:6). Pushkin's 'духовной жаждою' means spiritual thirst; Panin's 'thirst for the spirit' slightly changes the sense to a thirst whose object is the spirit. 'Томим' (tormented, wearied) also appears in Pushkin's love lyric 'Ya vas lyubil' (pushkin/41), where Panin renders it 'tormented' too.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 63:1; Bible (KJV), Psalms 42:2; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  3. sombre desert l. 2

    The desert (пустыня, wilderness) is the classic place of prophetic and ascetic preparation: Moses at the burning bush, Elijah at Horeb, John the Baptist, and Christ's temptation. Pushkin's 'мрачной' (gloomy, dark) colours it with despair, and 'влачился' (dragged myself along, trudged wearily) is a verb of exhaustion, which Panin keeps well with 'I was dragging myself'. The desert returns in line 25 ('Like a corpse I lay in the desert'), framing the transformation.

    Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Bible (KJV), Matthew 4:1

  4. six-winged seraph l. 3

    In Isaiah 6, the prophet sees the Lord enthroned, with seraphim above him: 'each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly' (6:2). One seraph flies to Isaiah with a live coal from the altar and touches his lips, purging his sin (6:6-7), and Isaiah then accepts his mission. Pushkin keeps the seraph and the purifying fire but greatly expands the violence of the transformation. Commentators have long tied the poem to Isaiah's call. A late memoir attributed to A. O. Smirnova-Rosset, of uncertain reliability, instead links its origin with a reading of Ezekiel.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 6:2; 'Prorok' (stikhotvorenie), Znanie.Rossiya, on dating, the Moskovskii vestnik publication and Pogodin's testimony of four poems; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource

  5. the parting of the roads l. 4

    'Перепутье' means a crossroads or fork in the road, a traditional place of decision and of supernatural encounter in Russian folklore. The seraph appears to the speaker at the moment when he must choose a path. Panin's 'parting of the roads' recalls the King James phrase in Ezekiel 21:21, where the king of Babylon 'stood at the parting of the way, at the head of the two ways, to use divination'; it is an apt choice in a poem saturated with prophetic language. The image also anticipates the end: the prophet will be sent 'over land and sea', having taken the road of vocation.

    Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 21:21; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  6. With fingers as light as a dream l. 5

    The seraph's first touch is gentle, light as sleep or a dream ('сон' means both). 'Персты' (fingers) is the Slavonic form, replacing the everyday Russian 'пальцы'; Pushkin uses such words throughout ('зеницы' for eyes, 'уста' for lips, 'десница' for right hand, 'глагол' for word) to give the poem the sound of the Russian Bible and liturgy. The gentleness of these first transformations, eyes and ears, contrasts sharply with the violence that follows at the mouth and heart. Panin's 'as light as a dream' is exact.

    Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  7. Like the eyes of a frightened eagle ll. 7-8

    The opened eyes are 'вещие', prophetic, seeing what is hidden, and 'зеницы' (pupils, eyes) is again Slavonic. 'Отверзлись' (were opened) recalls the biblical opening of eyes, as in the healing of the blind. The simile of the eagle, proverbially keen-sighted, adds the startled wide-eyed look of a creature roused. Pushkin's 'орлица' is feminine, a she-eagle; Panin's 'eagle' loses the gender, which some readers associate with the protective mother eagle of Deuteronomy 32:11. Panin's 'opened wise' is his attempt at 'вещие' (prophetic, knowing).

    Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 32:11

  8. The cosmos heard ll. 11-14

    Lines 11-14 form a vertical catalogue of creation: 'И внял я неба содроганье, / И горний ангелов полёт, / И гад морских подводный ход, / И дольней лозы прозябанье' ('and I heard the shuddering of the sky, and the angels' flight on high, and the underwater course of sea creatures, and the growth of the vine in the valley'). The range runs from the heavens, through the angels and the depths of the sea, to the plant world on earth, so the prophet's hearing embraces the whole created order. 'Горний' (on high, celestial) and 'дольний' (of the vale, earthly) are paired Slavonic opposites. Panin's 'And ... And ... And' keeps the biblical parataxis of the Russian.

    Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  9. the polyps in the sea l. 13

    The Russian 'гад' (plural 'гады') is the Slavonic word for creeping things, reptiles and similar creatures, the word used in the Russian Bible for the 'creeping things' of Genesis 1. 'Гады морские' are therefore creatures of the sea in general, perhaps recalling Psalm 104:25 ('this great and wide sea, wherein are things creeping innumerable'). Panin's 'polyps' is oddly specific and introduces a zoological term; his 'creeping' is closer to the word's root sense, but 'ход' means movement or progress, not specifically creeping. The line's point is that the prophet hears what is normally inaudible and hidden, motion in the depths of the sea.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 104:25; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:24

  10. out he tore my sinful tongue ll. 15-17

    In Isaiah 6:5 the prophet cries that he is 'a man of unclean lips', and the seraph's coal purges them. Pushkin's seraph does far more: he seizes the lips ('приник ко мне к устам') and tears out the tongue. 'Празднословный' (idle-speaking) recalls Christ's warning about 'every idle word' (Matthew 12:36) and the Lenten prayer of St Ephrem, familiar in Orthodox liturgy, which asks to be spared 'the spirit of idle talk'. 'Лукавый' means crafty, deceitful. Panin's 'empty and false speech' is a fair paraphrase of both adjectives. The scene shows that the poet's old, worldly speech must be destroyed before prophetic speech is possible.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 6:5; Bible (KJV), Matthew 12:36; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  11. the fang of the wise serpent ll. 18-20

    The new tongue is 'жало мудрыя змеи', the sting of the wise serpent. The archaic genitive 'мудрыя' (for modern 'мудрой') is Church Slavonic. The serpent here is not the tempter of Genesis but the wisdom of Matthew 10:16, 'be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves', words spoken to apostles being sent into a hostile world. A forked serpent's tongue was popularly believed to be its sting ('жало'), so the image makes the prophet's speech both wise and piercing. Panin's 'fang' is inaccurate; 'sting' is standard. 'Десница' (right hand) and 'кровавой' (bloody) stress the violence.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 10:16; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  12. a coal with flaming blaze ll. 21-24

    Pushkin transforms the source decisively: 'И он мне грудь рассек мечом, / И сердце трепетное вынул, / И угль, пылающий огнём, / Во грудь отверстую водвинул' ('and he cut open my breast with a sword, and took out my trembling heart, and pushed a coal blazing with fire into the opened breast'). In Isaiah the coal purges lips; here it replaces the human heart altogether, a fusion with Ezekiel's promise of a 'new heart' (Ezekiel 36:26). 'Угль' is the Slavonic form of 'уголь' (coal). The trembling heart of human feeling becomes a fire, so the prophet will burn others' hearts in turn (last line).

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 6:6; Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 36:26; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  13. Like a corpse I lay l. 25

    After the operation, the speaker lies like a corpse in the desert. Biblical visionaries often fall as dead before the divine: Ezekiel falls on his face (Ezekiel 1:28) and John 'fell at his feet as dead' (Revelation 1:17). The line is also a death and rebirth: the man with ordinary eyes, ears, tongue and heart is dead, and only God's voice can raise the prophet. It is the poem's only short, self-contained sentence of stillness between the violent operations and the commanding voice. Panin translates it exactly.

    Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 1:28; Bible (KJV), Revelation 1:17; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  14. Arise, O prophet, and listen, and guide l. 27

    God's command echoes Ezekiel 2:1 ('Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee'). The Russian imperatives are in Church Slavonic form: 'восстань' (arise), 'виждь' (see), 'внемли' (hearken, attend), corresponding to the two senses the seraph transformed, sight and hearing. Panin renders 'внемли' as 'listen' but gives 'guide' where Pushkin has 'виждь' (see), which breaks the link with the opened eyes of lines 6-8 and may result from a misreading. Students relying on Panin should note the error, since 'see and hear' sums up the prophet's new perception before the mission of the final lines.

    Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 2:1; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  15. Be thou filled with my will l. 28

    The verb 'исполнись' (be filled, be fulfilled) can also mean 'carry out', so the line suggests both possession by divine will and its execution. Panin, in the introduction to his 1888 translation, treats this line as the climax of Pushkin's spiritual development, an ideal that ended, as he puts it, in the 'complete surrender of self-will'. Other readers stress the political context of 1826: after the Decembrists' defeat, the poet claims an authority higher than any tsar's. These readings are not exclusive, and the poem's power derives partly from its ambiguity between religious vocation and the Romantic idea of the poet as prophet.

    Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; T. J. Binyon, Pushkin: A Biography (London: HarperCollins, 2002)

  16. Fire with the word the hearts of men l. 30

    The famous last line joins the new heart of fire to the new speech of the serpent's sting: the prophet is to burn men's hearts with his word. 'Глагол' in modern Russian means 'verb', but in Church Slavonic it means word or utterance, especially divine utterance, so the line has a scriptural ring. Compare Jeremiah 23:29, 'Is not my word like as a fire? saith the Lord', and Jeremiah 20:9, where God's word is 'as a burning fire shut up in my bones'. The line became a watchword of the Russian idea of the poet's mission, and Lermontov answered it with his own bitter 'Prorok' (1841), in which the prophet is stoned and mocked.

    Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 23:29; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 20:9; Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  17. Form and Slavonic diction whole poem

    'Prorok' is a single paragraph of thirty lines of iambic tetrameter, with varied rhyme, so it moves without stanza breaks from desert to command. Its diction is deliberately archaic and scriptural: Slavonic forms such as 'персты', 'зеницы', 'уста', 'десница', 'угль', 'глагол' and the genitive 'мудрыя'. The many lines beginning 'И' (and) imitate biblical parataxis. Panin preserves this with his repeated 'And' and archaic 'mine eyes', but without metre and rhyme the English cannot convey the poem's cumulative, hammering rhythm. The poem was set to music by Rimsky-Korsakov among others.

    Pushkin, 'Prorok', Russian text, Wikisource; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  18. Isaiah or Ezekiel? l. 26

    Though contemporaries associated the poem with Isaiah, the structure follows a pattern found in Ezekiel: a vision, the prophet falling down, a voice commanding him to stand, and a mission to an obstinate people (Ezekiel 1:28-3:11). In Ezekiel 3:1-3 the prophet even eats the scroll that God gives him, an incorporation of the divine word comparable to Pushkin's new tongue and heart. A late memoir attributed to A. O. Smirnova-Rosset, of uncertain reliability, links the poem's origin with a reading of Ezekiel, while most commentary stresses Isaiah. Many readers see Pushkin as fusing several prophetic call narratives into a new myth of the poet.
