Love

Resigned Love

by Alexander Pushkin 1799–1837

Translated by Ivan Panin (1888) · From Poems (Panin, 1888)

Love

Thee I loved; not yet love perhaps is
In my heart entirely quenched
But trouble let it thee no more;
Thee to grieve with nought I wish.
Silent, hopeless thee I loved,
By fear tormented, now by jealousy;
So sincere my love, so tender,
May God the like thee grant from another.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its date and translator title, l. 1

    Pushkin wrote the eight lines in 1829, and they were published in 1830 in the almanac Severnye tsvety. The poem has no title in Russian and is known by its first words, 'Я вас любил' ('I loved you'). The title 'Resigned Love' is Panin's, given in his Poems by Alexander Pushkin (Boston, 1888), where it stands among the 'Poems of Love'. Panin (1855-1942) explains in his preface that he translated line for line, who says he 'tried neither for measure nor for rhyme', so his English is a crib that preserves the order of Pushkin's thought at the cost of music. The poem has been set to music by Dargomyzhsky, Alyabyev and Sheremetev, among many others.

    Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; 'I Loved You (poem)', Wikipedia, on date, publication and musical settings; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating

  2. The addressee ll. 1, 5

    Pushkin's biographer Pavel Annenkov first proposed that the poem was addressed to Anna Olenina, daughter of the president of the Academy of Arts, whom Pushkin courted and to whom he unsuccessfully proposed in 1828-29; other poems of 1828, such as 'Её глаза' ('Her eyes'), are associated with her. Tatiana Tsiavlovskaia regarded the Olenina identification as doubtful. The one piece of supporting evidence is the claim of Olenina's granddaughter, Olga Oom, that the poem stood in Olenina's album in Pushkin's hand with the dates 1829 and 1833 and the note 'plusqueparfait' ('long past'); the album is lost, so this cannot be checked. Students should treat the addressee as a biographical hypothesis; the poem itself names no one.

    'Ya vas lyubil... (stikhotvorenie)', Tsiklopediya, on the Olenina hypothesis and T. Tsiavlovskaia's doubts; T. J. Binyon, Pushkin: A Biography (London: HarperCollins, 2002); Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating

  3. Thee (Russian 'vas') ll. 1, 4

    Russian, like French, distinguishes the intimate 'ty' (thou) from the polite 'vy' (you). Pushkin's 'Ya vas lyubil' uses the polite form throughout, so the declaration of love is spoken with formal restraint, as if the speaker keeps his distance even in confession. Pushkin wrote a separate poem on exactly this distinction, 'Ты и вы' (1828), in which a woman's slip into the intimate 'ty' delights the speaker. Panin's 'Thee', archaic in 1888 and associated with prayer and intimate verse, cannot carry this nuance; in English, the plain 'you' (as in most modern translations) is nearer in tone. This is one place where a literal translation is less faithful than it seems.

    Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'

  4. not ... entirely quenched ll. 1-2

    The verb 'угаснуть' (to go out, be extinguished) treats love as a flame, one of the few metaphors in a poem famous for its near-absence of imagery. The hesitations ('ещё', still; 'быть может', perhaps; 'не совсем', not entirely) make the confession tentative: the speaker admits that love may survive while insisting it will cause no trouble. Pushkin's 'в душе моей' is 'in my soul', which Panin turns to 'In my heart'. The syntax of Panin's line ('not yet love perhaps is / In my heart entirely quenched') follows the Russian word order closely, but the English inversion sounds stilted.

    Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  5. trouble let it thee no more l. 3

    The verb 'тревожить' means to disturb, alarm, make anxious, and 'trouble' in Panin's archaic order ('trouble let it thee', let it trouble thee) is accurate in sense. The turn 'но' (but) moves from confession to renunciation: whatever remains of love, the speaker forbids it to affect the beloved. This selfless gesture, repeated in the next line, is what Panin's title 'Resigned Love' tries to capture. The following line, 'Я не хочу печалить вас ничем' ('I do not want to sadden you with anything'), Panin renders literally as 'Thee to grieve with nought I wish', where 'nought' means 'nothing'.

    Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trouble, v.'

  6. The repeated 'I loved you' ll. 1, 5, 7

    The poem's structure rests on three repetitions of 'Я вас любил': at the opening, at the start of the second quatrain ('Я вас любил безмолвно, безнадежно', 'I loved you silently, hopelessly'), and again in line 7 ('Я вас любил так искренно, так нежно', 'I loved you so sincerely, so tenderly'). The refrain gives the poem its ritual simplicity and makes each return add a new quality of the past love. Panin keeps the first two, but in line 7 writes 'So sincere my love, so tender', losing the third repetition and the pattern. Roman Jakobson used this poem as a test case for his 'poetry of grammar', arguing that its power lies in grammatical patterning, such as pronouns and verb forms, rather than in metaphor.

    Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  7. By fear tormented, now by jealousy l. 6

    The Russian construction 'то ... то ...' means 'now ... now ...' or 'at times ... at times ...', so the line describes the lover alternating between two states. 'Робость' is shyness, timidity, the diffidence of a lover who dares not speak; Panin's 'fear' is stronger and less specific. The alliteration and assonance of 'робостью ... ревностью' (robost'yu ... revnost'yu) bind the two feelings in sound. 'Томим' (tormented, wearied, languishing) is a word of the elegiac tradition. Panin keeps 'now' only before 'jealousy', so the balanced alternation is lost.

    Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'

  8. So sincere my love, so tender l. 7

    Line 7 sums up: the love was sincere ('искренно') and tender ('нежно'). The repeated 'так' (so) leads into the comparison of the last line ('как', as), so the syntax builds towards the wish that the beloved be loved 'so' by another. Panin's restructuring ('So sincere my love, so tender') removes the verb and the pronoun, and with them the third 'I loved you'. The two adverbs are typical of Pushkin's restraint: simple, abstract words that gain force from position rather than from imagery.

    Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'

  9. May God grant ... another l. 8

    The last line is the poem's crux. On one reading it is a generous blessing, the final step of renunciation: the speaker wishes the beloved a love equal to his. On another it carries a sting, since the comparison implies that such love is rare, and that she will be lucky if anyone ever loves her so well; the line then measures what she has lost. Critics have argued both ways, and many find the poem's power in holding the two together. Panin's 'May God the like thee grant from another' renders the comparison literally but awkwardly; 'from another' is unidiomatic for 'by another', and the passive 'to be loved' disappears.

    Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991

  10. Metre and rhyme ll. 1-8

    In Russian the poem is in iambic pentameter with an alternating rhyme scheme (быть может / не тревожит; совсем / ничем; and so on), the odd lines ending in feminine (two-syllable) rhymes. Its sound texture is notable, with dense patterning of the vowels and of the sounds 'l' and 'v' in 'Ya vas lyubil' and its echoes. Panin, as his preface says, set aside metre and rhyme altogether and translated line for line. The result is useful for checking what the Russian says, but it gives no idea of why Russian readers hold the poem to be one of the most perfect short lyrics in the language. Comparing Panin's crib with a rhymed version is a good classroom exercise.

    Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991; Pushkin, Polnoe sobranie sochinenii, 16 vols (Moscow-Leningrad: AN SSSR, 1937-59), vol. 3 (lyrics 1826-36), text and dating; Roman Jakobson, 'Poetry of Grammar and Grammar of Poetry' (Russian version 1961; English in Lingua 21, 1968), analysis of 'Ya vas lyubil'

  11. Panin's literal method whole poem

    Ivan Panin, a Russian émigré who settled in the United States, explains in his 1888 preface that he first rendered each line word for word and then arranged the words by ear, but never sought metre or rhyme. He claims fewer than five departures from the literal text (one transposition of two lines and some commas replaced by 'and') and says he kept Pushkin's punctuation, especially the dots. The method explains the inverted word order of this version ('Thee to grieve with nought I wish'), which follows Russian syntax. His introduction reads Pushkin's lyrics as a record of inner development, and 'Resigned Love' fits that scheme of growing self-renunciation.

    Pushkin, Poems, trans. with introduction and notes by Ivan Panin (Boston: Cupples and Hurd, 1888), Project Gutenberg ebook 54991
