Odes 3.30: Exegi Monumentum

by Horace (Quintus Horatius Flaccus) 65–8 BC

Translated by John Conington (3rd ed., before 1869) · From Odes, Book III

Living

And now 'tis done: more durable than brass
My monument shall be, and raise its head
O'er royal pyramids: it shall not dread
Corroding rain or angry Boreas,
Nor the long lapse of immemorial time.
I shall not wholly die: large residue
Shall 'scape the queen of funerals. Ever new
My after fame shall grow, while pontiffs climb
With silent maids the Capitolian height.
"Born," men will say, "where Aufidus is loud,
Where Daunus, scant of streams, beneath him bow'd
The rustic tribes, from dimness he wax'd bright,
First of his race to wed the Aeolian lay
To notes of Italy." Put glory on,
My own Melpomene, by genius won,
And crown me of thy grace with Delphic bay.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Odes 3.30: the epilogue and this translation l. 1

    Odes 3.30 closes the collection of three books of Odes that Horace published around 23 BC. Its metre, the stichic lesser Asclepiad, is the same as that of the first poem, Odes 1.1, so the collection is framed. Horace begins with exegi ('I have completed'), and claims that his poetry will outlast bronze and pyramids and that he will live in fame wherever Latin is spoken. Conington (translation first published 1863) uses four-line stanzas of pentameters rhymed abba, the rhyme scheme of Tennyson's In Memoriam (1850), though that poem is in tetrameters. Shorey collects many ancient and modern parallels.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; John Conington, trans., The Odes and Carmen Saeculare of Horace (London: George Bell, 1882), Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30

  2. 'My monument' ll. 1-2

    The Latin begins exegi monumentum aere perennius, 'I have finished a monument more lasting than bronze'. A monumentum (from monere, 'to remind') was anything that kept a memory alive, such as a tomb, statue or inscription. Shorey quotes Ruskin on poetry and architecture as the two conquerors of forgetting. Ovid ends his Metamorphoses with a direct imitation (iamque opus exegi). Shakespeare's Sonnet 55 ('Not marble nor the gilded monuments / Of princes shall outlive this powerful rhyme') takes up the same boast. Pushkin's 1836 poem is headed by Horace's two Latin words.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.871-9, Latin text (The Latin Library); William Shakespeare, Sonnet 55, Shakespeare's Sonnets (1609), Project Gutenberg

  3. 'more durable than brass' l. 1

    Aere perennius means 'more lasting than bronze'. Shorey glosses aere as statues and bronze tablets. Laws, treaties and honours were inscribed on bronze at Rome, and bronze statues stood for public fame, so Horace's poem claims to outlast the official records of the state. Latin aes covers copper, bronze and brass, and older English often called bronze 'brass', as in Shakespeare's 'brass eternal' (Sonnet 64). Conington's 'durable' is a weaker word than perennius, which means 'lasting through the years'.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. aes

  4. 'raise its head / O'er royal pyramids' l. 3

    The Latin is regalique situ pyramidum altius, 'and higher than the royal situs of the pyramids'. The word situs is disputed. Shorey takes it loosely as 'structure, pile', and records that others read it as 'decay', the mouldering of time, a sense situs often has, citing Martial 8.3.5. If 'decay' is meant, the pyramids are already falling into ruin, and the boast is sharper. The pyramids of Egypt were the classic royal tombs, and Egypt had become Roman only in 30 BC. Conington's 'raise its head / O'er royal pyramids' takes the structural sense.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  5. 'Corroding rain' l. 4

    The Latin is quod non imber edax, non Aquilo impotens / possit diruere, 'which no devouring rain, no uncontrolled north wind can destroy'. Edax means 'gluttonous, eating away'. Shorey glosses it as 'corroding' and notes Ovid's tempus edax rerum ('time, devourer of things', Metamorphoses 15.234) and his imitation of this passage at 15.872. He also cites Shakespeare's 'Devouring Time' (Sonnet 19). Conington's 'Corroding rain' is apt, since it suggests the chemical wearing away of stone and metal.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.871-9, Latin text (The Latin Library); Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  6. 'angry Boreas' l. 4

    Horace names the north wind Aquilo; Conington substitutes Boreas, its Greek name, which was more familiar in English poetry. Impotens means 'without self-control', and so 'raging, wild'. Shorey refers to his note on Odes 1.37.10, where the word describes Cleopatra's unrestrained ambition. The idea is that even the most violent forces of nature, rain and wind, cannot pull the poem down. Conington's 'angry' is a fair equivalent, though 'impotent' rage is a more pointed idea in Latin: force without self-mastery.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. impotens

  7. 'the long lapse of immemorial time' ll. 4-5

    The Latin is aut innumerabilis / annorum series et fuga temporum, 'or the countless succession of years and the flight of the seasons'. The long word innumerabilis spans much of a line, so that the verse seems to lengthen with the years it describes. Shorey compares other uses of fuga for the flight of time (Odes 2.14.1, 3.29.48). Conington's 'immemorial' means 'beyond memory', which is not quite 'countless', and his 'long lapse' renders series ... fuga as one flowing image.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'immemorial'

  8. 'I shall not wholly die' l. 6

    The Latin is non omnis moriar multaque pars mei / vitabit Libitinam, 'I shall not wholly die, and a great part of me will escape Libitina'. The phrase non omnis moriar became one of the most quoted in Latin. Shorey cites Ovid's imitations, parsque mei multa superstes erit (Amores 1.15.41-2) and parte tamen meliore mei (Metamorphoses 15.875), and Herrick's 'Thou shalt not All die'. Conington's 'large residue' for multa pars mei is a slightly prosaic word, suggesting what is left over.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.871-9, Latin text (The Latin Library); Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  9. 'the queen of funerals' l. 7

    Libitina was the goddess of funerals. Shorey explains that deaths were registered in her temple and that her name here stands, by metonymy, for death, or rather for the rites of death, avoiding a repetition of moriar. The undertakers (libitinarii) hired out their equipment at her grove. Horace means that the body will be carried out and buried, but most of him will escape this. Conington's 'queen of funerals' explains the name without giving it.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. Libitina

  10. 'Ever new / My after fame shall grow' ll. 7-8

    The Latin is usque ego postera / crescam laude recens, 'I shall go on growing, ever fresh with the praise of later times'. Shorey glosses usque as 'still' with crescam and postera as 'of after-days', and quotes Tennyson's line about praise that 'grows to guerdon after-days'. Recens, 'fresh', implies that the poems will not age. Conington's 'after fame' for postera laude is an old-fashioned compound.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30

  11. The Capitol, the pontiff and the Vestal ll. 8-9

    The Latin is dum Capitolium / scandet cum tacita virgine pontifex, 'as long as the pontiff climbs the Capitol with the silent Virgin'. The Capitol was the religious centre of Rome and the symbol of its permanence. The 'Virgin' is a Vestal, perhaps the chief Vestal, walking in ritual silence. Shorey notes a doubtful later tradition (in John Lydus) of an annual ascent of the Capitol by the Pontifex Maximus and chief Vestal to pray for the state. He regards the picture as symbolic. Conington's plurals, 'pontiffs' and 'maids', generalise the single ritual pair.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  12. 'where Aufidus is loud' l. 10

    The Latin is dicar, qua violens obstrepit Aufidus, 'I shall be spoken of, where the violent Aufidus roars'. The Aufidus, now the Ofanto, runs through Apulia near Venusia, where Horace was born, and was subject to floods. Shorey glosses obstrepit as 'brawls'. The construction is disputed: Shorey takes qua ('where') with dicar, so that Horace will be famous in his own region. Others take it with ex humili potens, 'having risen from humble birth where the Aufidus roars'. Shorey thinks the two readings come to much the same. Conington takes the second view, adding 'Born'.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  13. Daunus l. 11

    The Latin continues et qua pauper aquae Daunus agrestium / regnavit populorum, 'and where Daunus, poor in water, ruled over rustic peoples'. Daunus was a mythical early king of Apulia, which Horace calls 'Daunian' elsewhere (Odes 1.22.14, 4.14.26). Shorey glosses pauper aquae as 'parched' and compares Epode 3.16, siticulosae Apuliae ('thirsty Apulia'). The genitive with regnavit is a Greek construction, as Shorey notes. Conington's 'beneath him bow'd / The rustic tribes' adds a picture of submission that the Latin's simple 'ruled over' does not have.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. Daunus

  14. 'from dimness he wax'd bright' l. 12

    The Latin ex humili potens means 'grown powerful from a lowly state'. Shorey notes that Horace always anticipates sneers at his humble birth, since his father was a freedman, and compares Odes 2.20.5 and Epistles 1.20.20. Potens refers to Horace himself and to the power of the poet. Conington's 'from dimness he wax'd bright' turns social rank into light and obscurity. 'Wax'd' is the archaic past of wax, 'to grow' (OED), used as of the moon.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wax'

  15. 'to wed the Aeolian lay / To notes of Italy' ll. 13-14

    The Latin says Horace will be remembered as princeps, 'the first', to have 'brought down Aeolian song to Italian measures'. Aeolic was the Greek dialect of Lesbos, home of Alcaeus and Sappho, whose metres Horace adapted. Shorey notes that Horace ignores the few earlier experiments of Catullus, who had written two poems in Sapphics. Deduxisse may suggest spinning a fine thread (as in deducere carmen), or leading a colony to a new land. Conington's 'wed' introduces a marriage image of his own.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30

  16. 'Put glory on' ll. 14-15

    The Latin is sume superbiam / quaesitam meritis, 'assume the pride won by deserts'. Superbia usually means arrogance, a vice; here it is the proud bearing that true merit earns. Shorey notes that sume superbiam is the opposite of pone superbiam ('put aside your pride', Odes 3.10.9). Horace transfers the pride to the Muse, so the boast is shared. Conington's 'Put glory on' is a dignified paraphrase, and it treats the pride as a garment, matching the crown of the last line.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. superbia

  17. Melpomene l. 15

    Melpomene ('the singer', from Greek melpein, 'to sing') was one of the nine Muses. Later tradition assigned her to tragedy, but Horace invokes her as his own lyric Muse here and in Odes 1.24 and 4.3. Shorey cross-refers to these passages. The Latin adds volens ('graciously'), a formula of prayer that Shorey connects with the ritual volens propitiusque. Conington's 'My own' renders the intimacy, and 'of thy grace' gives volens.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Niall Rudd, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book III (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), on 3.30

  18. 'crown me of thy grace with Delphic bay' ll. 15-16

    The Latin is mihi Delphica / lauro cinge volens, Melpomene, comam, 'and graciously, Melpomene, bind my hair with Delphic laurel'. The laurel (bay) was sacred to Apollo, whose oracle was at Delphi, and was the prize of poets and victors. Shorey compares Lucretius's 'Delphic laurel of Phoebus'. The poem that began with a monument of bronze ends with a living crown. Conington's 'bay' is the usual English poetic word for laurel.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 3.30; Horace, Carmina, book 3, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bay'
