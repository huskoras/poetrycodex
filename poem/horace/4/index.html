Odes 1.5: Quis Multa Gracilis

by Horace (Quintus Horatius Flaccus) 65–8 BC

Translated by John Conington (3rd ed., before 1869) · From Odes, Book I

Living

What slender youth, besprinkled with perfume,
Courts you on roses in some grotto's shade?
Fair Pyrrha, say, for whom
Your yellow hair you braid,
So trim, so simple! Ah! how oft shall he
Lament that faith can fail, that gods can change,
Viewing the rough black sea
With eyes to tempests strange,
Who now is basking in your golden smile,
And dreams of you still fancy-free, still kind,
Poor fool, nor knows the guile
Of the deceitful wind!
Woe to the eyes you dazzle without cloud
Untried! For me, they show in yonder fane
My dripping garments, vow'd
To Him who curbs the main.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Odes 1.5: the Pyrrha ode and this translation l. 1

    In Odes 1.5 the speaker asks Pyrrha which slender youth is courting her now, pities the boy who trusts her, and ends by recalling his own escape from the 'sea' of her love. The metre is an Asclepiad stanza: two long lines, then a shorter and a shorter still. Editors number this variant differently (Page the fourth, Nisbet and Hubbard the third). The archive gives John Conington's translation (first published 1863; text of the 1882 Bell edition). He imitates the stanza's shape with two pentameters followed by two short lines rhymed abab. Milton's famous unrhymed version (1673) was made 'almost word for word' in a measure close to the Latin.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; John Conington, trans., The Odes and Carmen Saeculare of Horace (London: George Bell, 1882), Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); 'Odes 1.5', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet and Hubbard, Zielinski, Hoppin and others); John Milton, 'The Fifth Ode of Horace. Lib. I', Poems (1673), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room

  2. 'besprinkled with perfume' l. 1

    The Latin is perfusus liquidis urget odoribus, 'soaked in liquid perfumes, he presses you'. Liquidis odoribus means scented oils or unguents, poured over the hair at parties. Shorey compares Epode 13.9 for perfusus. Urget is a strong word for wooing ('presses, urges'), which Conington softens to 'Courts'. His 'besprinkled' is lighter than perfusus, which means 'drenched, poured over'. The adjective gracilis ('slender') marks the youth as young and delicate, an apt victim. In Latin the words of the first line are interleaved round te (Pyrrha), so that the boy is placed in the middle of the roses as he is in her arms.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  3. 'on roses' l. 2

    Horace's multa ... in rosa literally means 'on much rose'. Shorey takes it as 'on many a rose', a bed of roses. He compares Marlowe's 'There will I make thee beds of roses', but notes that phrases like potare in rosa ('to drink among roses') may refer to garlands. Roses were the flower of the Roman banquet and of Venus. Conington's 'Courts you on roses' takes the bed-of-roses view. The luxury of the setting prepares the contrast with the stormy sea that follows.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  4. 'in some grotto's shade' l. 2

    The Latin is grato, Pyrrha, sub antro, 'under a pleasant cave, Pyrrha'. Shorey explains sub as 'under the covert of', that is, inside. Artificial grottoes and caves were features of wealthy Roman villas and gardens, and in poetry they are places of love and of the Muses. The words grato ... sub antro enclose Pyrrha's name, so that the vocative sits inside the cave, like the girl herself. Conington's 'some grotto's shade' keeps the place but not the arrangement. 'Grotto' (from Italian grotta) gives the scene a slightly eighteenth-century garden air.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  5. Pyrrha l. 3

    Pyrrha is a Greek name formed from pyr, 'fire', and meaning 'flame-coloured, red-gold'. Shorey takes it as fictitious and as meaning flava, 'golden-haired', the fashionable colour; it matches flavam ... comam in the next line. The name may also recall the mythical Pyrrha, wife of Deucalion, who survived the great flood, a link some readers see with the poem's sea imagery and the speaker's escape from the waves. Horace's love-names in the Odes are usually Greek and often descriptive. Conington's 'Fair' is his own addition.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode; Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. Pyrrha

  6. 'for whom / Your yellow hair you braid' ll. 3-4

    The Latin is cui flavam religas comam, 'for whom do you bind back your yellow hair?' Shorey glosses religas as 'bind back', the simple knot of a woman's hair, and compares a Greek epigram asking 'for whom will you braid your hair now?' The question hints that Pyrrha's grooming is for whoever is her current lover, and that the speaker once had his turn. Flavam ('golden, yellow') repeats the sense of her name. Conington's 'braid' is a little more elaborate than religas, which describes a plain style, as the next words make clear.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  7. 'So trim, so simple!' l. 5

    The two words simplex munditiis have become proverbial for simple elegance. Munditia means neatness or refinement of dress, and simplex 'plain, uncomplicated'. Shorey quotes Milton's rendering 'plain in thy neatness' and Cicero's call for a neatness that is 'not odious or over-exquisite'. Many readers find an irony in simplex, which also means 'frank, guileless': Pyrrha looks innocent, and that is her danger. Conington's 'So trim, so simple!' turns the phrase into an exclamation of the speaker's admiration and keeps the double sense of 'simple'.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; John Milton, 'The Fifth Ode of Horace. Lib. I', Poems (1673), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  8. 'Lament that faith can fail, that gods can change' ll. 5-6

    The Latin is heu quotiens fidem / mutatosque deos flebit, 'alas, how often will he weep for faith and the changed gods'. Shorey explains fidem as 'thy faithlessness', or supplies mutatam ('changed') from the next word, so that both faith and gods have altered. The gods who change are the powers of love and fortune that once favoured him, and also, in the sea image that follows, the weather. Conington's version makes two clauses of it ('faith can fail ... gods can change'), which clarifies the sense but loses the compression of the Latin.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  9. 'Viewing the rough black sea' ll. 6-7

    The Latin is aspera / nigris aequora ventis, 'seas rough with black winds'. Shorey notes that nigris transfers the effect to the cause: the winds blacken the water. He compares Homer's sea darkening under the west wind (Iliad 7.64). The sea of love was a Greek commonplace, but Horace develops it through the whole ode, from the calm 'golden' surface to the votive tablet of the shipwrecked sailor. Conington moves the blackness from the winds to the sea itself ('the rough black sea') and omits the winds, which reappear in his 'tempests'.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  10. 'With eyes to tempests strange' l. 8

    The Latin emirabitur insolens means 'he, unused to it, will be utterly amazed'. Shorey notes that emirabitur occurs nowhere else in classical Latin, the prefix e- intensifying mirari ('to wonder'). He glosses insolens as 'unwonted to the sight', a novice at sea. Conington renders the novice's inexperience well with 'eyes to tempests strange', using 'strange' in its older sense of 'unaccustomed' (OED, strange adj.), but the astonishment of the rare verb is only implied.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strange'

  11. 'basking in your golden smile' l. 9

    The Latin is qui nunc te fruitur credulus aurea, 'who now, credulous, enjoys you, golden as you are'. Aurea agrees with te: Pyrrha herself is 'golden', all perfection in the boy's eyes, as Shorey explains, with parallels from Pindar and Shakespeare. Many readers also hear a link between aurea and aurae ('breeze') at line 11: the golden girl is a shifting wind. Conington moves the gold to her 'smile', a gloss rather than a translation. He keeps credulus in 'Poor fool' at line 11.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  12. 'still fancy-free, still kind' ll. 10-11

    The Latin is qui semper vacuam, semper amabilem / sperat, 'who hopes you will always be free, always lovable'. Shorey glosses vacuam as 'fancy free, and so ready to entertain him', that is, without another lover. Amabilem may mean both 'lovable' and 'kind'. The repeated semper ('always') shows the boy's naive faith in permanence. Conington's 'fancy-free', the same word Shorey uses in his note, is familiar from A Midsummer Night's Dream ('in maiden meditation, fancy-free'). 'Dreams' renders sperat ('hopes'), with a stronger hint of illusion.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fancy-free'

  13. 'the guile / Of the deceitful wind' ll. 11-12

    The Latin is nescius aurae / fallacis, 'not knowing the deceitful breeze'. Shorey glosses fallacis as 'shifting'. Aura is a light breeze, but it also means favour or popularity, as in aura popularis, so the word fits both sailing and love. The enjambment breaks the phrase across the stanza division, leaving the reader, like the boy, briefly unaware of the danger. Conington's 'guile' adds an idea of deliberate cunning; Horace's fallax can mean merely 'unreliable'.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  14. 'Woe to the eyes you dazzle without cloud / Untried!' ll. 12-13

    The Latin is miseri, quibus / intemptata nites, 'wretched those for whom you shine untried'. Shorey glosses intemptata as 'untried' and suggests nites may continue the sea metaphor, citing Lucretius on the treacherous smile of the calm sea. He also notes that nitor ('gleam') describes a woman's beauty in Odes 1.19. Intemptata, like the shining surface, describes a sea not yet tested by storms. Conington refers it to 'the eyes' of the lovers and adds 'without cloud', which makes the weather imagery explicit.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  15. The votive tablet ll. 13-16

    The Latin says that 'the sacred wall, with its votive tablet, shows that I have hung up my wet clothes to the god who rules the sea'. Shorey explains the custom: survivors of shipwreck dedicated a tablet or painting of the disaster in a temple, often of Neptune or Isis, with the clothes they had worn. The speaker thus presents himself as a survivor who has given up love. Conington's 'they show in yonder fane / My dripping garments' omits the tablet. 'Fane' is a poetic word for a temple (Latin fanum; OED, fane n.).

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fane'; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  16. 'To Him who curbs the main': god or goddess? l. 16

    The manuscripts and the ancient commentator Porphyrio read potenti ... maris deo, 'to the god powerful over the sea', meaning Neptune. Zielinski (1901) conjectured deae, 'goddess', so that the dedication is to Venus, born from the sea and protector of sailors. Nisbet and Hubbard (1970) favour deae, arguing that Neptune has no place in a metaphorical sea of love and comparing Odes 3.26, where Horace hangs up his lyre to Venus. Hoppin (1984) replies that deo can itself refer to a goddess. Most texts still print deo, and Conington's capitalised 'Him' follows it. 'The main' is the poetic word for the open sea.

    'Odes 1.5', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet and Hubbard, Zielinski, Hoppin and others); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode; Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5
