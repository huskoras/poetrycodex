Odes 1.11: Tu Ne Quaesieris

by Horace (Quintus Horatius Flaccus) 65–8 BC

Translated by John Conington (3rd ed., before 1869) · From Odes, Book I

Living

Ask not ('tis forbidden knowledge), what our destined term of years,
Mine and yours; nor scan the tables of your Babylonish seers.
Better far to bear the future, my Leuconoe, like the past,
Whether Jove has many winters yet to give, or this our last;
THIS, that makes the Tyrrhene billows spend their strength against
the shore.
Strain your wine and prove your wisdom; life is short; should hope
be more?
In the moment of our talking, envious time has ebb'd away.
Seize the present; trust to-morrow e'en as little as you may.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Odes 1.11: the 'carpe diem' ode and this translation l. 1

    Odes 1.11 is one of Horace's shortest odes and the source of the phrase carpe diem. The speaker tells Leuconoe not to ask the astrologers how long they will live, to accept whatever comes, and to make the most of today. The metre is the greater Asclepiad, a long line built round three choriambs, which Horace also uses in Odes 1.18 and 4.10. Conington (translation first published 1863) renders it in rhymed couplets of long trochaic lines, the metre of Tennyson's 'Locksley Hall'. Some lines are so long that the archive text wraps them.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; John Conington, trans., The Odes and Carmen Saeculare of Horace (London: George Bell, 1882), Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  2. '('tis forbidden knowledge)' l. 1

    The Latin is tu ne quaesieris, scire nefas, quem mihi, quem tibi / finem di dederint, 'do not ask (it is wrong to know) what end the gods have given me, what end you'. Nefas means what is forbidden by divine law, and Shorey translates scire nefas as ''tis wrong to know'. He notes that ne with the perfect subjunctive (quaesieris) is a sharper, more colloquial prohibition than the usual polite forms. Conington's parenthesis keeps the aside, and 'forbidden knowledge' brings in a faintly biblical sense of the fruit of knowledge.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. nefas

  3. 'what our destined term of years, / Mine and yours' ll. 1-2

    Horace asks quem mihi, quem tibi / finem di dederint, 'what end the gods have given to me, what end to you'. The repeated quem sets the two lives side by side, and the perfect dederint shows that the end is already fixed. Conington's 'destined term of years' paraphrases finem, making it a span rather than a final point. His 'Mine and yours' keeps the Latin's balance. Shorey's note collects other places where Horace says it is forbidden to know the future.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  4. 'the tables of your Babylonish seers' ll. 2-3

    The Latin is nec Babylonios / temptaris numeros, 'and do not try Babylonian numbers'. Shorey explains numeros as the calculations of the Chaldean astrologers, called mathematici at Rome. He compares Tacitus, Histories 1.22, on astrologers at the court of Otho. Astrology from Mesopotamia spread through the Hellenistic world and was popular, and periodically banned, in Rome. Merrill, commenting on Catullus 5, cites this line for the belief that exact numbers give power over a life. Conington's 'tables' (astrological charts) and 'Babylonish' (an archaic adjective, OED) make the practice sound foreign and quaint.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; E. T. Merrill, ed., Catullus (Boston: Ginn, 1893), commentary on poem 5; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  5. Leuconoe l. 3

    Leuconoe is otherwise unknown and probably fictitious, like most of the names in the Odes. It is formed from Greek leukos ('white, bright') and noos ('mind'). Scholars disagree about the point: it may suggest a bright or innocent mind, or, ironically, an empty or simple one, in a woman who worries over horoscopes. Some have also connected the name with Leuconoe, one of the daughters of Minyas in Ovid's Metamorphoses. Conington's 'my Leuconoe' supplies a possessive that the Latin lacks and makes the poem more intimate.

    R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode; Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  6. 'Better far to bear the future ... like the past' l. 3

    The Latin is ut melius, quidquid erit, pati, 'how much better to endure whatever shall be'. Shorey compares Aeneid 5.710, where all fortune is to be overcome by bearing it. Patience in the face of an unknown future was common ground for Epicurean and Stoic teaching, and Horace often presents the two together. Conington's 'like the past' is his own addition, which suggests that the future, once lived, will be no more terrible than what is already behind. The ode turns from prohibition to positive advice at this point.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  7. 'many winters yet to give, or this our last' l. 4

    The Latin is seu pluris hiemes seu tribuit Iuppiter ultimam, 'whether Jupiter has granted more winters, or this as the last'. Shorey notes that years are marked by winters or summers 'to suit the rhetorical color'. Here winter suits the scene of storm at sea that follows. He glosses tribuit as 'has assigned', like the Greek verbs for the Fates spinning a lot. Conington keeps 'winters', and 'Jove' for Iuppiter, and his 'this our last' makes the present winter possibly the final one.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  8. 'the Tyrrhene billows' ll. 5-6

    The Latin is quae nunc oppositis debilitat pumicibus mare / Tyrrhenum, 'which now wears out the Tyrrhenian sea on the rocks set against it'. The Tyrrhenian Sea lies between the west coast of Italy and Sardinia and Corsica. Shorey glosses debilitat as 'breaks the force of' and pumicibus as any wave-eaten, porous rock, comparing Lucretius. The line is often read as an image of time wearing everything down, or of the speaker and Leuconoe watching a winter storm from a villa by the sea. Conington's capitalised 'THIS' insists on the present winter.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  9. 'Strain your wine and prove your wisdom' l. 6

    The Latin is sapias, vina liques, 'be wise, strain the wine'. Shorey glosses liques as straining wine through a cloth or strainer (colum) to clear it of sediment, a step in preparing it for drinking. The pairing makes wisdom concrete and domestic. Sapere ('to be wise') also originally means 'to taste', a link some readers hear here. Conington's 'prove your wisdom' adds 'prove', making it a test, where Horace gives a bare imperative.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. sapio

  10. 'life is short; should hope be more?' ll. 6-7

    The Latin is spatio brevi / spem longam reseces, 'and since the span is short, prune back long hope'. Shorey takes spatio brevi as 'life's span being short' and spem longam as hopes that look far into the future. He quotes Cowley's version: 'from a short life cut off all hopes that grow too long'. Resecare is used of cutting back vines, so the imagery anticipates carpe ('pluck') in the last line. Conington's rhetorical question 'should hope be more?' gives the sense but loses the pruning image.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. reseco

  11. 'In the moment of our talking, envious time has ebb'd away' ll. 7-8

    The Latin is dum loquimur, fugerit invida / aetas, 'while we speak, envious time will have fled'. Shorey glosses fugerit as 'will be gone' and collects parallels from Persius, Boileau and Milton ('Fly, envious Time'). Time is invida because it grudges men their happiness. The future perfect fugerit makes the passing complete even as it is described, so the poem enacts its own point: speaking about time uses it up. Conington's 'ebb'd away' takes up the sea imagery of line 5, a good touch, but turns the future perfect into a simple past.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. G. M. Nisbet and Margaret Hubbard, A Commentary on Horace: Odes Book I (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), on the ode

  12. 'Seize the present': carpe diem l. 8

    Carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero is the poem's last line. Carpere means to pick or pluck fruit or flowers, and so to enjoy something as it comes to hand. Shorey notes both senses, 'catch as it flies' and 'pluck the flower of', and a comparable use in Persius 5.151, carpamus dulcia ('let us pluck life's sweets'). The image completes the vine imagery of vina liques and reseces. Conington's 'Seize the present', like the familiar 'seize the day', stresses urgency rather than gathering. Carpe diem has become a general motto, and the Epicurean context (enjoy what is present, do not depend on the future) is often forgotten.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. carpo

  13. 'trust to-morrow e'en as little as you may' l. 8

    The final phrase quam minimum credula postero means 'trusting as little as possible in the following (day)'. Shorey supplies diei with postero. The feminine credula reminds us that the advice is to Leuconoe, who has been trusting astrologers. The same word credulus is used of Pyrrha's naive lover in Odes 1.5. Shorey quotes FitzGerald's Omar Khayyam on tomorrow. Conington's 'e'en as little as you may' is an accurate version of quam minimum, though slower than the Latin.

    Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.11; Horace, Carmina, book 1, Latin text (The Latin Library); Paul Shorey and Gordon J. Laing, eds, Horace: Odes and Epodes, rev. edn (Boston: Sanborn, 1910), note on Odes 1.5
