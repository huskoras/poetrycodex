Nature

Snake

by D. H. Lawrence 1885–1930

From Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923)

NatureThe Mind

A snake came to my water-trough
On a hot, hot day, and I in pyjamas for the heat,
To drink there.

In the deep, strange-scented shade of the great dark carob-tree
I came down the steps with my pitcher
And must wait, must stand and wait, for there he was at the trough before me.

He reached down from a fissure in the earth-wall in the gloom
And trailed his yellow-brown slackness soft-bellied down, over the edge of the stone trough
And rested his throat upon the stone bottom,
And where the water had dripped from the tap, in a small clearness,
He sipped with his straight mouth,
Softly drank through his straight gums, into his slack long body,
Silently.

Someone was before me at my water-trough,
And I, like a second comer, waiting.

He lifted his head from his drinking, as cattle do,
And looked at me vaguely, as drinking cattle do,
And flickered his two-forked tongue from his lips, and mused a moment,
And stooped and drank a little more,
Being earth-brown, earth-golden from the burning bowels of the earth
On the day of Sicilian July, with Etna smoking.

The voice of my education said to me
He must be killed,
For in Sicily the black, black snakes are innocent, the gold are venomous.

And voices in me said, If you were a man
You would take a stick and break him now, and finish him off.

But must I confess how I liked him,
How glad I was he had come like a guest in quiet, to drink at my water-trough
And depart peaceful, pacified, and thankless,
Into the burning bowels of this earth?

Was it cowardice, that I dared not kill him?
Was it perversity, that I longed to talk to him?
Was it humility, to feel so honoured?
I felt so honoured.

And yet those voices:
If you were not afraid, you would kill him!

And truly I was afraid, I was most afraid,
But even so, honoured still more
That he should seek my hospitality
From out the dark door of the secret earth.

He drank enough
And lifted his head, dreamily, as one who has drunken,
And dickered his tongue like a forked night on the air, so black,
Seeming to lick his lips,
And looked around like a god, unseeing, into the air,
And slowly turned his head,
And slowly, very slowly, as if thrice adream,
Proceeded to draw his slow length curving round
And climb again the broken bank of my wall-face.

And as he put his head into that dreadful hole,
And as he slowly drew up, snake-easing his shoulders, and entered farther,
A sort of horror, a sort of protest against his withdrawing into that horrid black hole,
Deliberately going into the blackness, and slowly drawing himself after,
Overcame me now his back was turned.

I looked round, I put down my pitcher,
I picked up a clumsy log
And threw it at the water-trough with a clatter.

I think it did not hit him,
But suddenly that part of him that was left behind convulsed in undignified haste,
Writhed like lightning, and was gone
Into the black hole, the earth-lipped fissure in the wall-front,
At which, in the intense still noon, I stared with fascination.

And immediately I regretted it.
I thought how paltry, how vulgar, what a mean act!
I despised myself and the voices of my accursed human education.

And I thought of the albatross,
And I wished he would come back, my snake.

For he seemed to me again like a king,
Like a king in exile, uncrowned in the underworld,
Now due to be crowned again.

And so, I missed my chance with one of the lords
Of life.
And I have something to expiate;
A pettiness.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A snake comes to the poet's water-trough in Sicily. Voices tell him he should kill it, but he feels honoured by his visitor, and afterwards throws a log at it, a 'pettiness' he has to expiate. One of Lawrence's most anthologised poems.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sicily and publication l. 1

    Lawrence and his wife Frieda lived at the Fontana Vecchia in Taormina, Sicily, from 1920 to 1922. The poem records an encounter with a snake at his water-trough on a hot July day. It was first printed in The Dial (July 1921) and collected in Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923), Lawrence's great sequence of free-verse poems on animals and plants. The opening is matter-of-fact, almost comic (the poet in his pyjamas), before the encounter grows in moral and mythic weight.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Snake', text from Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; Wikipedia, 'Birds, Beasts and Flowers'

  2. Free verse l. 2

    'Snake' is in free verse. Lines expand and contract with the movement of the snake and of the poet's feelings, and repetition ('hot, hot', 'must wait, must stand and wait', 'slowly, very slowly') does the work that metre does in traditional verse. In 'Poetry of the Present', his preface to the American edition of New Poems (1920), Lawrence argued for a verse of the 'immediate present', without fixed form, following the instant of experience. The long sibilant lines describing the snake ('soft-bellied', 'slackness', 'sipped', 'Silently') imitate its movement.

    D. H. Lawrence, 'Poetry of the Present', preface to the American edition of New Poems (1920); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  3. the great dark carob-tree l. 4

    The carob (Ceratonia siliqua) is an evergreen tree of the Mediterranean with dense dark foliage and long brown seed pods. Tradition identified its pods with the 'locusts' eaten by John the Baptist (Matthew 3:4) and the 'husks' of the Prodigal Son parable (Luke 15:16), hence 'St John's bread'. Here its deep shade and scent create a setting of strangeness for the encounter.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'carob'; The Bible (KJV), Luke 15:16

  4. He sipped with his straight mouth l. 11

    Lawrence's observation is exact: the snake trails its 'yellow-brown slackness' over the edge of the trough, rests its throat on the stone, and sips from the small clear pool where the tap drips. 'Straight mouth' and 'straight gums' describe the lipless line of a snake's jaws. The sibilants make the reader hear the quiet drinking, and 'Silently' stands alone on its line, enacting the stillness.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Snake', text from Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923)

  5. a second comer l. 15

    The poet, owner of the trough, finds himself a 'second comer', waiting in a queue behind the animal. The reversal of human precedence is the poem's first moral gesture. It also introduces the idea of hospitality: the snake is a guest, or even the original resident, and the man is the late arrival in its world. The phrase 'Someone was before me' treats the snake as a person.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  6. as cattle do l. 16

    Comparing the snake to drinking cattle domesticates it: it lifts its head and gazes vaguely about as cows do at a pond. The repeated simile ('as cattle do', 'as drinking cattle do') slows the moment and resists the fear that the next stanzas will voice. The snake 'mused a moment', and is given a contemplative inner life. The flickering tongue is noted without alarm.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  7. Sicilian July ... Etna smoking l. 21

    Mount Etna, Europe's largest active volcano, dominates the view from Taormina and was smoking during Lawrence's time there. The snake is 'earth-brown, earth-golden from the burning bowels of the earth', so the volcano associates it with the earth's hidden fire and with the classical underworld, which ancient myth placed beneath Etna (the giant Typhon was imprisoned under it). The phrase 'burning bowels of the earth' recurs later, binding the snake to this chthonic world.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Snake', text from Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.346-358 (Typhoeus under Sicily)

  8. The voice of my education l. 22

    The 'voice of my education' says that gold snakes in Sicily are venomous and must be killed. Whether or not this local lore is accurate, its point is that it is received knowledge, not observation. A second set of voices appeals to manhood: 'If you were a man / You would take a stick'. Throughout his writing Lawrence opposed instinctive 'blood-consciousness' to the conventional mental consciousness taught by society; here the inner debate dramatises that conflict.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; D. H. Lawrence, Fantasia of the Unconscious (1922)

  9. like a guest l. 28

    The poet confesses that he liked the snake and was glad it came 'like a guest' to drink and depart 'peaceful, pacified, and thankless'. Hospitality to strangers was a sacred duty in the ancient Mediterranean world (Zeus Xenios protected guests), and the theme returns ('seek my hospitality'). 'Thankless' is not a reproach: the guest owes nothing, and the poet is honoured simply by its coming.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; Homer, Odyssey 9.266-271 (Zeus as protector of guests)

  10. cowardice ... perversity ... humility ... honoured l. 34

    The poet questions his own motives: was it cowardice, perversity or humility? He then simply restates the feeling ('I felt so honoured'). The voices return, and he admits he was afraid, but the honour outweighs the fear. This is the moral turning point: the poem is less about the snake than about the conflict within the man between awe and the impulse to dominate.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  11. dickered (textual error) l. 43

    The text in Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923), as reproduced by Representative Poetry Online, reads 'And flickered his tongue like a forked night on the air, so black'. 'Dickered' (which means to haggle) is clearly a scanning or transcription error for 'flickered'. The archive's line should be read with 'flickered', which also repeats the earlier 'flickered his two-forked tongue'. The simile itself is striking: the black forked tongue is like a sliver of forked night, perhaps a reversed lightning flash, against the bright noon.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Snake', text from Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  12. like a god, unseeing l. 45

    After drinking, the snake lifts its head 'as one who has drunken', a phrase with an archaic biblical ring, and looks around 'like a god, unseeing', majestic and indifferent to the human watcher. 'Thrice adream' uses the magical number three, as in charms and spells, and gives its slowness an enchanted quality. The god-like dignity prepares the later images of kingship.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  13. A sort of horror l. 52

    The moment of violence is triggered not by the snake's approach but by its withdrawal into 'that dreadful hole', 'that horrid black hole'. The poet's 'horror' is directed at the darkness of the earth it returns to, perhaps the unconscious, sexuality or death, which critics read in various ways (Freudian readings take the hole and the snake as sexual symbols). He acts only 'now his back was turned', which makes the act cowardly, as he then recognises.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  14. a clumsy log l. 56

    The language deflates the act: a 'clumsy log' thrown with 'a clatter' at the trough, not a heroic blow. It probably misses, but the snake's remaining body 'convulsed in undignified haste' and 'Writhed like lightning' into the 'earth-lipped fissure'. The poet has turned a god-like guest into a frightened animal, which is the source of his shame. 'Intense still noon' fixes the moment like a revelation.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Snake', text from Birds, Beasts and Flowers (1923)

  15. paltry, vulgar, mean l. 64

    The poet condemns himself in three adjectives that reverse the voices' claims about manliness: the act was paltry (trivial), vulgar (common, ill-bred) and mean (petty, base). The point is moral and social: killing the snake would have been not courage but conformity. The exclamation shows how quickly the feeling of honour returns once the act is done, and how violence has spoiled the encounter. The voices of masculine courage have produced only a mean little gesture.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  16. my accursed human education l. 65

    The 'voices of my education' from the earlier stanza are now 'accursed'. Lawrence's attack is on a civilisation that trains people to dominate and destroy what they fear. 'Accursed' also links to the biblical curse on the serpent in Genesis 3:14-15, which sets 'enmity' between humans and snakes. The poet sees that inherited enmity as the curse, not the serpent itself.

    The Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:14-15; D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  17. the albatross l. 66

    In Coleridge's 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' (1798), the mariner shoots an albatross, a bird of good omen that had befriended the ship, and brings a curse on himself and the crew. He is released only when he blesses the water-snakes 'unaware'. The allusion is especially apt because Coleridge's turning point is a blessing of sea-snakes. Lawrence's poet, having failed to bless his snake, recognises a comparable gratuitous crime against life, and the need for penance ('something to expiate').

    Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' (1798); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  18. a king in exile, uncrowned in the underworld l. 69

    The snake is now seen as a king exiled in the underworld, 'Now due to be crowned again'. The image suggests Pluto or Dis, the classical ruler of the underworld, and also Milton's Satan, the fallen serpent, whose dethronement Christian tradition celebrates. Lawrence reverses that tradition: the serpent is a dispossessed lord of life whom humanity should restore. Critics connect the image to Lawrence's interest in pre-Christian, chthonic religion, which he developed in his later work.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost, Book 9 (Satan in the serpent); D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  19. one of the lords / Of life l. 71

    The line-break after 'lords' isolates 'Of life' as its own line, emphasising that the snake represents life itself, not death or evil as in the Christian tradition. To miss one's 'chance' with such a lord is to fail in a sacramental encounter. The phrase is characteristic of Lawrence's vitalism, his belief in a sacred life-force that modern humanity has lost touch with.

    D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'

  20. something to expiate; / A pettiness l. 74

    'Expiate' (from Latin expiare, to atone, to purify by sacrifice) is a religious word, continuing the Ancient Mariner's penance. The anticlimax of the final line is deliberate: what must be atoned for is not murder but 'A pettiness', the small-minded failure to rise to the occasion. The understatement turns the poem's judgement on the poet's own smallness, in contrast to the snake's regal dignity.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'expiate'; D. H. Lawrence, The Poems, ed. Christopher Pollnitz (Cambridge UP, 2013), notes to 'Snake'
