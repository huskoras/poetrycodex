Faith, Idol, Ka'ba-Fane Am I

by Yunus Emre d. c. 1321

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1900) · From A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900)

Religion

Faith, idol, Ka'ba-fane am I; who smites the sphere a-spin am I;
The cloud upon the heavens' face, likewise the rains that rain am I.

The thunderbolt that flasheth forth, that, flashing, weaves itself in birth;
The poison-snake that deep in earth to creep and cower is fain am I.

The one who Hamza drew o'er Qaf, who swelled his hands and feet therewith;
Who many from their thrones drave off, the King of Wisdom's Rayne am I.

The cloud that doth to heaven ascend, the rain that doth to earth descend.
The haze and mist that doth before the sightless eyes remain am I.

Who buildeth bone and flesh and skin, who keepeth this a living thing,
Who lieth Wisdom's crib within and Power's milk doth drain am I.

Who summer brings and earth doth cheer, who makes our heart-house His repair;
Who doth consent to dam and sire, who knoweth Serfhood's gain am I.

Full many an one have I made thrall, I've lived a merry life withal;
What coalless burns, what leaps when hammer-blows on iron rain am I.

Let him come here who suffers woe that I to him a place may show;
My heart his home, mine eye his town; Time in the Cycle-train am I.

It is not Yunus sayeth this, 'tis Power's Tongue that speaketh thus;
Who credits not a paynim is, the First and Latest e'en am I.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A pantheistic ghazel in which the poet identifies himself with God and with all of creation. Gibb's notes explain the Qur'anic and Islamic allusions in the lines.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Yunus Emre and Gibb's translation couplet 1

    Yunus Emre, a dervish of central Anatolia (c. 1238-1320 by the usual dating), wrote mystical poems in plain Turkish, mostly in the syllabic metres of folk song, which made him one of the founders of Turkish poetry; his poems are still sung in Sufi gatherings. Gibb (1857-1901) printed this ghazel, with notes, in the first volume of his history of the Ottoman poets. Every couplet ends with 'am I', Gibb's rendering of a repeated end-word (redif): the speaker claims to be all things, but the last couplet reveals that the speaker is God's 'Tongue of Power' speaking through Yunus. Editors warn that later poets also signed as 'Yunus', so individual attributions are not always certain.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Yunus Emre'; Talat S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)

  2. Faith, idol, Ka'ba-fane couplet 1

    'Fane' is an archaic poetic word for a temple (OED, fane n.2, from Latin fanum). Gibb explains that the Ka'ba, the 'Cubical House' at Mecca towards which Muslims pray, stands for Islam, and the 'idol' for the other, conventionally false religions: Yunus identifies himself with every form of worship. The pairing of idol and Ka'ba is a commonplace of Persian Sufi poetry (compare Rumi and Hafiz), where the idol-temple and the mosque are equally veils over the one reality. The line is deliberately shocking for orthodox readers, and the poem only justifies it in the final couplet.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, 'fane, n.2'

  3. 'who smites the sphere a-spin' couplet 1

    In the Ptolemaic cosmology shared by Islamic scholars, the heavens are concentric spheres carrying the planets and stars, set in motion by a prime mover. Gibb's note reads the phrase as 'He who makes the spheres revolve'. 'A-spin' is Gibb's archaising coinage on the model of 'a-hunting', meaning 'into spinning'. The same couplet passes from the turning heavens to cloud and rain, so that the speaker is both the highest cause and the humblest weather: Gibb's opening chapter on Ottoman cosmology explains the system of spheres, seas and mountains behind such lines.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, 'a-, prefix1'

  4. Thunderbolt and poison-snake couplet 2

    The second couplet moves from life-giving rain (couplet 1) to the thunderbolt and the snake that hides in the earth. Pantheistic Sufism, in the tradition associated with Ibn 'Arabi and with Rumi, held that God manifests through both His 'beautiful' and His 'majestic' or terrible attributes, so destruction as well as mercy belongs to the One; Gibb draws on this distinction later in the poem (see 'What coalless burns'). Yunus's catalogue of creatures recalls the many Qur'anic signs (ayat) in nature, but the claim to be them goes beyond the Qur'an's language.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975), on the divine attributes of jamal and jalal

  5. Hamza and Mount Qaf couplet 3

    Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, the Prophet's uncle, died a martyr at Uhud (625), but a vast popular romance, the Hamzanama, made him a fantastic hero who travels to the lands of the jinn. Gibb's note says only that the line refers to 'an incident in the legendary history of Hamza'. For Qaf he refers to his account of Islamic cosmology: the earth is ringed by the mountains of Qaf and by seas, beyond which dwell the jinn. In this poem the divine speaker claims to be the power that carried the hero across the world's boundary, a mark of the romance's wide popularity.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), on Islamic cosmology and the mountains of Qaf

  6. 'who swelled his hands and feet' couplet 3

    Gibb's note covers the Hamza episode only in general terms, and he leaves 'swelled his hands and feet therewith' without comment; it presumably refers to a detail of the romance in which the hero's body is enlarged to accomplish his superhuman journey, but no source in this archive confirms that, and the phrase should be treated as unexplained. Students should note the limits of Gibb's apparatus here, and that the Hamzanama exists in many versions in Persian and Turkish. The line also illustrates the strain of Gibb's method: he kept the original rhyme and redif, at the cost of filler words like 'therewith'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes

  7. 'Rayne' couplet 3

    'Rayne' is a Middle English and early modern spelling recorded for 'reign' (and also for 'rein' and 'rain'); Gibb gives no note, but the sense 'the King of Wisdom's reign' fits the preceding half-line, 'Who many from their thrones drave off'. Gibb keeps a single rhyme through the ghazal (rain, fain, Rayne, remain, drain, gain, rain, train, I) as the Turkish does, and this odd spelling looks like a deliberately antique choice to distinguish it from the 'rains' of couplet 1. 'Drave' is the archaic past tense of 'drive'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'reign, n.'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes

  8. Wisdom's crib and Power's milk couplet 5

    The couplet moves from God as maker of the body ('Who buildeth bone and flesh and skin') to the image of the speaker as a baby in 'Wisdom's crib' drinking 'Power's milk'. Gibb gives no note; the image seems to make the infant's growth itself a manifestation of divine Wisdom and Power, two of the attributes of God. The capitalised abstractions, Wisdom, Power, Serfhood, are Gibb's way of marking Arabic theological terms in the Turkish (such as hikmet, wisdom, and kudret, power). The language prepares for couplet 6, where God condescends to physical birth.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes

  9. The heart as God's house couplet 6

    Gibb points to the Arabic saying 'the heart of the believer is the house of God'; hadith scholars do not accept it as a sound prophetic tradition, but it is everywhere in Sufi writing, together with the related saying in which God declares that neither heaven nor earth can contain Him, but the heart of His believing servant does. 'Repair' here means a place of resort or visiting (OED, repair n.1), so the line says that God makes the human heart His dwelling. The image answers the Ka'ba of couplet 1: the true Ka'ba is the heart.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, 'repair, n.1'; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (1975), on the heart as the dwelling of God

  10. Dam and sire; Serfhood couplet 6

    Gibb's note explains 'consent to dam and sire' as the universal One condescending to individual, physical birth. 'Dam and sire', words normally used of animals, emphasise the bodily nature of that birth. 'Serfhood' renders the condition of being a servant ('abd, Turkish kul) of God, the proper state of the human being in Islamic thought; to 'know Serfhood's gain' is to understand the value of humility and worship. The paradox is that the divine speaker also knows, from the inside, what it is to be a creature who worships.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes

  11. Light and fire couplet 7

    Gibb's notes read 'what coalless burns' as Light (nur) and the sparks leaping from iron under the hammer as Fire (nar). In Sufi thought light and fire often stand for the two kinds of divine attributes, the Beautiful (jemal) and the Awful or Majestic (jelal), a distinction Gibb discusses elsewhere in his volume. The smithy image is concrete and Anatolian; the light without fuel recalls the Qur'anic Light Verse (24:35), whose lamp is lit by an oil that 'would well-nigh give light though no fire touched it', a verse Gibb quotes in his note on the next ghazal.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Qur'an 24:35

  12. 'Time in the Cycle-train' couplet 8

    The couplet offers refuge to the sufferer: 'My heart his home, mine eye his town.' Gibb's note glosses 'Time in the Cycle-train' as the circling Time that brings healing on its wings. 'Cycle' is the turning of the heavens (devr), which in Persian and Turkish poetry usually brings misfortune; here it becomes the healing passage of time. 'Train' means a procession or sequence. The compound is typical of Gibb's practice, forcing a Turkish phrase into a hyphenated English one to keep the rhyme.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes

  13. The signature couplet couplet 9

    Ghazels conventionally name the poet in the last couplet. Yunus uses the convention to resolve the poem's scandal: the 'I' who claimed to be the idol, the Ka'ba and the universe is not the man Yunus but divine Power speaking through him. The idea recalls the Sufi doctrine that the annihilated mystic becomes a mouthpiece of God, and the famous cry of al-Hallaj (executed 922), 'I am the Truth' (ana'l-haqq), which later Sufis defended in just these terms. The 'Tongue of Power' appears again in the next Yunus ghazel in the archive ('Who makes the Tongue of Power to speak').

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (1975), on al-Hallaj

  14. 'paynim' and 'the First and Latest' couplet 9

    'Paynim' is an archaic word for a pagan or non-Christian, from Old French paienime (OED, paynim n.); Gibb uses it for the Turkish word for unbeliever. 'The First and the Last' echoes Qur'an 57:3, 'He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward', a verse that Yunus's next ghazel quotes directly ('The Outer and the Inner I, the Former and the Latter I'). The final half-line thus seals the poem's claim with scripture, while the threat to the disbeliever turns the poem's radical claim into a test of faith.

    Qur'an 57:3; Oxford English Dictionary, 'paynim, n.'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), section on Yunus Emre, and notes
