That Mighty One of 'Be! and 'Tis

by Yunus Emre d. c. 1321

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1900) · From A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900)

Religion

That Mighty One of 'Be! and 'tis,' that Lord of gracious sway am I.
That King who ere 'tis cut provides for each his bread each day am I.

The One who maketh man of seed, who maketh bird from egg proceed,
Who makes the Tongue of Power to speak, He who remembereth aye am I.

Who maketh some ascetics be, and some to work iniquity;
That Argument and Proof who veils their faults and flaws alway am I.

Who unto one doth horses give, doth wives and wealth and children give.
The while another lacks a groat, that One of gracious sway am I.

Who bringeth snow and doth earth freeze, who on each brute his food bestows,
Who worketh all these businesses, the Soul of souls in fay am I.

I am Eternity in sooth, that King, the Unconditioned Truth;
To-morrow Khizr may water dole, who wipes his sins away am I.

Know ye what from Four Things proceed, behold, I am the Sign indeed;
That God who builded Fire and Air and Earth and Water, yea, am I.

Who holds the veils of flesh and skin and bone and life and body e'en;
My works are Power and many an one, the Hid and Open aye am I.

The Outer and the Inner I, the Former and the Latter I;
Yea, I am He, and He is I, He whom they praise alway am I.

No truchman may between Us be, all wroughten there is clear to me;
Who giveth me this tongue is He; yon Sea that rolls each way am I.

He who did earth and sky create, who maketh Throne and Stool rotate;
Thousand and one His Names: Yunus, He of the Koran, yea, am I.

Codex Note

A ghazel built on the Qur'anic phrase 'Be! and it is', in which the poet speaks as God's creative power. Gibb's notes explain the allusions to the Light verse of the Qur'an and to the divine command.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its title and Gibb's text couplet 1

    Gibb printed this as the second of 'two of Yunus's pantheistic ghazels', straight after 'Faith, idol, Ka'ba-fane am I' (also in the archive). His note explains that 'Be! and it is', God's word of command and its instant effect, sums up the divine creative power and recurs throughout the Qur'an (he cites Q 2:117; it also appears at 3:47, 16:40 and 36:82); he compares Psalm 33:9, 'he spake, and it was done'. The speaker who claims to be that 'Mighty One' is not Yunus as a private man: as in the preceding ghazel, the claim is that God's own voice sounds through the dervish. Gibb's footnote on the Light verse (Q 24:35) belongs to that earlier poem's 'What coalless burns', not to this one. Yunus (c. 1240-1320) wrote in plain Anatolian Turkish; Gibb translated from the lithographed Divan of 1884-5.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 2:117 (and 3:47, 16:40, 36:82) (Gibb's numbering: ii, 111); The Bible (KJV), Psalm 33:9; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  2. The refrain 'am I' and the internal rhymes every couplet

    Gibb's preface says his translations try to reproduce the outer form of the original: its rhyme pattern, the syllable count where possible, and the movement of the accent. Here that means a fixed refrain, 'am I', closing every couplet, as the redif closes every couplet of a ghazel. The first line of each couplet has its own internal chime (seed / proceed, be / iniquity, freeze / bestows / businesses, sooth / Truth, create / rotate). Gibb notes that most of Yunus's ghazels use the favourite Turkish device of a thrice-repeated internal sub-rhyme, close to what learned poets called musammat verse, and that his rhymes are often loose or merely assonant. The relentless refrain turns the poem into a litany: each new divine work is claimed with the same two words, so that the whole of creation is gathered into one 'I'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Preface, p. ix (on reproducing the form, rhyme and syllable count of the originals); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  3. 'ere 'tis cut': daily bread couplet 1, l. 2

    Gibb's note glosses the line as the One 'who while the grain is yet growing assigns to each creature its daily portion'. The idea is the Qur'anic doctrine of rizq, God's provision: 'there is no creature on the earth but its sustenance depends on God' (Q 11:6). The portion is fixed before the harvest, indeed before the creature asks; Turkish piety still speaks of each person's appointed rızk. 'King' (Arabic malik, one of the divine Names) sets the image in a royal household, where a ruler feeds his dependants. The same theme of unequal distribution returns in couplet 4 ('lacks a groat').

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 11:6; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  4. Man from seed, bird from egg couplet 2

    The Qur'an repeatedly names the making of man 'from a drop' (nutfa) as a sign of God's power (e.g. Q 16:4, 76:2), and speaks of God bringing the living out of the dead (Q 6:95), which commentators illustrate by the chick from the egg. Gibb singles out this very formula when he describes Yunus's thought: the poet 'openly proclaims that he is God' who creates man from seed and brings the bird from the egg, and Gibb adds that Yunus's pantheism is frank, not hidden behind allegory as in later poets. The couplet thus chooses the humblest everyday wonders, birth and hatching, as proof of the one creative will.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; The Qur'an, 16:4; 76:2; 6:95

  5. 'the Tongue of Power' couplet 2

    'Power' here is the divine omnipotence (Arabic qudra, Turkish kudret). In the ghazel that Gibb prints just before this one ('Faith, idol, Ka'ba-fane am I'), the last couplet explains that it is not Yunus who speaks but 'Power's Tongue'. In this poem the speaker is the one 'who makes the Tongue of Power to speak': the voice is God's, Yunus is only its instrument. This is the usual defence of ecstatic Sufi utterance (shath): the 'I' of such lines belongs to God, not to the mystic's self, which has been annihilated (fanā'). Schimmel describes how such utterances, from al-Hallaj onward, were both admired and condemned. Compare couplet 10, 'Who giveth me this tongue is He'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  6. 'He who remembereth aye' couplet 2

    Gibb's note points to the Qur'anic promise 'Remember me, and I will remember you; thank me, and be not ungrateful' (Q 2:152; Gibb, using an older numbering, gives ii, 147). The verse underlies the Sufi practice of dhikr (Turkish zikir), the rhythmic repetition of God's Name in the dervish lodges where Yunus's poems were sung as hymns (ilahi). The speaker claims to be the remembering God, so the human practice of remembrance is answered from the divine side. 'Aye' is the archaic adverb 'always', one of Gibb's many deliberate archaisms.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 2:152 (Gibb's numbering: ii, 147); Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Oxford English Dictionary, 'aye, adv.'

  7. Ascetics and sinners: predestination couplet 3

    The 'ascetic' (Turkish zâhid) is the man of outward piety; in Persian and Turkish mystical poetry he is often a mild butt, contrasted with the lover. Here both piety and 'iniquity' are attributed to God's will, a statement of the doctrine of the divine decree (qadar). The theological difficulty is real, and Yunus did not avoid it: in the passage Gibb prints straight after the next ghazel, a mesnevi beginning 'O my God, if so be Thou should question me', the poet asks God why He created him so faulty and says that whatever he has done, God has done. Gibb introduces it as an attack on the crude ideas of God held by the ignorant or fanatical orthodox. Readers differ on whether such lines express fatalism or the mystic's sense that nothing exists apart from God.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; The Qur'an, 54:49 ('We have created everything by measure'); Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  8. 'Argument and Proof' who veils faults couplet 3

    'Argument and Proof' most likely renders the Arabic pair hujja and burhan, terms of theology meaning the conclusive evidence that establishes a truth; the Qur'an calls its message a 'proof (burhan) from your Lord' (Q 4:174). Gibb gives no note, so the exact Turkish words cannot be checked from this text. The second half of the line, the one 'who veils their faults and flaws', recalls the popular divine epithet Settâr (Arabic sattār), He who covers sins rather than exposing them; the theme is common in Turkish devotional language. Set beside the first half of the couplet, the line suggests that the God who decrees the sinner's sin also hides it in mercy.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 4:174; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  9. 'lacks a groat' couplet 4

    The groat (OED) was an English silver coin worth four pence, first issued in 1279 and struck regularly until the later seventeenth century, and proverbial for a trifling sum; Gibb prints no Turkish text, but his 'groat' evidently stands for some small coin of the original. The couplet returns to the theme of rizq (couplet 1): wealth and poverty, horses and family, are all apportioned by God. The tone is not social protest but acceptance; the same 'One of gracious sway' gives to one and withholds from another. Gibb's archaic diction ('doth', 'groat', 'the while') is meant to give a Victorian reader a sense of the age and rusticity of Yunus's Turkish, which Gibb calls the most rugged of any Turkish poet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'groat, n.'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes

  10. 'the Soul of souls in fay' couplet 5

    'Soul of souls' is a standard Persian and Turkish mystical title for God or the Beloved (jān-i jān, cân-ı cân): the life that animates every living thing. The couplet has just listed snow, frost and the feeding of every beast, so the phrase sums up the catalogue: all these 'businesses' are the work of one indwelling life. 'In fay' is the archaic English oath 'in faith', 'truly' (OED, fay, from Old French fei), used chiefly for the rhyme with 'am I'; Gibb fills out several lines with such tags ('in sooth', 'yea', 'aye'), which is the price of his strict rhyming.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'fay, n. (faith)'; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Preface, p. ix (on reproducing the form, rhyme and syllable count of the originals)

  11. 'Eternity' and 'the Unconditioned Truth' couplet 6

    Al-Haqq, 'the Real' or 'the Truth', is one of the Names of God and the favourite name for Him in Sufi writing; in Turkish it is simply Hak. Its most famous use is the cry attributed to the mystic al-Hallaj, executed in Baghdad in 922: 'Ana'l-Haqq', 'I am the Truth'. Gibb notes that Yunus elsewhere says he was Mansur (al-Hallaj) and also Mansur's executioners. 'Unconditioned' renders the philosophical notion of the absolute (mutlak), Being without any limit or attribute; 'Eternity' points to God as without beginning (ezel) and without end (ebed). On Yunus's own lips such claims are, in Sufi terms, the speech of the annihilated self.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  12. Khizr and the Water of Life couplet 6

    Gibb's long note calls Khizr 'a very vague personage' met everywhere in Oriental poetry: identified with the unnamed servant of God whom Moses follows in Qur'an 18:60-82, sometimes confused with Elijah and St George, guardian of the Stream of Life, which he alone found and drank in the Alexander legend, and so immortal. He appears to the pious in distress as an old man and vanishes when recognised. The name is linked to greenness (perhaps, Gibb suggests, evergreen and so immortal). In Turkey he is Hızır, celebrated with Ilyas at the spring festival of Hıdrellez. Gibb reads the line as saying that the Water of Life Khizr may offer 'to-morrow' is nothing to the speaker, who is the one who forgives Khizr his sins.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 18:60-82; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  13. The Four Things and the Sign couplet 7

    The 'Four Things' are named in the next line: Fire, Air, Earth and Water, the four elements of Greek natural philosophy (Empedocles, Aristotle) taken over by Islamic science, from which all bodies are compounded. Gibb's opening chapters on the Ottoman picture of the cosmos explain this system. 'Sign' renders the Qur'anic aya: everything in creation is a sign pointing to its Maker ('We will show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves', Q 41:53), and the same word means a verse of the Qur'an. The speaker claims to be not one sign among many but 'the Sign indeed', the meaning that all the elements spell out.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; The Qur'an, 41:53; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  14. The veils of flesh couplet 8

    In Sufi thought the created world, and the body above all, is a veil (hijab) between the soul and God; a well-known tradition speaks of God's veils of light and darkness. Here the veils of flesh, skin, bone, life and body are not obstacles set up by an enemy but garments the One Himself 'holds'. The second line answers the image with a Qur'anic pair: God is knower of 'the unseen and the seen' (al-ghayb wa'sh-shahada, Q 59:22), so He is at once 'the Hid and Open'. The same body that hides Him is one of the places where He appears, the idea modern Turkish scholars call tecelli, manifestation.

    Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); The Qur'an, 59:22; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  15. Outer and Inner, Former and Latter couplet 9

    The line translates almost word for word Qur'an 57:3, 'He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward, and He knows all things'. The four Names (al-Awwal, al-Akhir, al-Zahir, al-Batin) were a favourite text of the Sufis, for whom they showed that nothing lies outside God in time or in being. The pair 'Outer and Inner' (zahir and batin) also names the two levels of religion, the outward law and the inward truth, a distinction central to Anatolian dervish piety. Gibb's 'Former and Latter' keeps the rhyme on 'I' but blurs the plainer 'First and Last'.

    The Qur'an, 57:3; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes

  16. 'I am He, and He is I' couplet 9

    Gibb called this poem pantheistic and praised Yunus's frankness: unlike later poets, he does not hide the doctrine of the ultimate Unity behind allegory, and Gibb traced it to the lyric Divan of Jalal al-Din Rumi. Orthodox critics of Sufism condemned claims of union (ittihad) as heresy, the charge on which al-Hallaj died. Many modern scholars avoid the word 'pantheism', a European term: Tatcı describes Yunus's outlook as tevhid, in which being is one, the Absolute Being is God, and created things are manifestations (tecelli) of His Names and attributes; Schimmel similarly warns against reading Sufi 'unity of being' as simple identity of God and world. On that reading the 'I' that says 'I am He' is the self that has passed away in God. The refrain lets both readings stand.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Talât S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)

  17. 'truchman' couplet 10

    'Truchman' (OED) is an archaic English word for an interpreter, borrowed through medieval Latin and French from Arabic tarjuman, the same word that gives Turkish tercüman and English 'dragoman'. Gibb's choice is pointed: a word of the Levant trade, used of the interpreters at Ottoman courts, now denies that any interpreter is needed. Between the mystic and God nothing stands, neither priest nor scholar nor book, because 'all wroughten there' (all that is made) is plain to the speaker. The line also comments on the poem itself: the tongue that speaks is God's gift, so the poet is no translator of the divine word but its direct voice. 'Wroughten' is a pseudo-archaic participle of 'work'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'truchman, n.'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  18. The Sea that rolls each way couplet 10

    The sea is among the commonest images of Sufi poetry for the one divine Being: individual things are its waves, foam or drops, which rise, take shape and fall back without ever ceasing to be water. Yunus uses the image often in his Turkish poems, and Rumi, whom Gibb names as his master in thought, uses it constantly. 'That rolls each way' suggests a sea with no shore and no single current, moving in all directions at once: infinite, and present everywhere. Placed after 'Who giveth me this tongue is He', the image hints that the poem's own words are waves of that sea.

    Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Talât S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)

  19. Throne and Stool ('Arsh and Kursi) couplet 11

    Gibb's note gives the original words, 'Arsh and Kursi. The Qur'an speaks of God settling on the Throne ('arsh, e.g. Q 7:54), and the Throne verse says His Kursi, chair or footstool, 'extends over the heavens and the earth' (Q 2:255). In Islamic cosmology they lie above the seven heavens. Because Yunus speaks of them as rotating, Gibb suggests that the poet means them as the Ninth and Eighth Spheres, the outermost heavens of the Ptolemaic system, which turn the rest; he explains the system in his opening chapters. The couplet thus completes the poem's cosmic sweep, from grain and snow to the outer heavens.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 2:255; 7:54

  20. The thousand and one Names couplet 11

    The Qur'an says 'to God belong the most beautiful Names' (Q 7:180), and a well-known hadith counts ninety-nine of them; the litany of the Names is recited on prayer beads. Gibb's note records that some authorities count a thousand and one, the figure Yunus uses and a popular one in Turkish devotion (bin bir ad). Many of the poem's titles are among them: King (al-Malik), the Truth (al-Haqq), the First and Last, the Outer and Inner. In Sufi thought every created thing reflects one or more Names, which is why the speaker who claims all things can also claim all the Names.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 7:180; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  21. The signature: 'Yunus, He of the Koran' couplet 11, l. 2

    By convention the last couplet of a ghazel (the makta) contains the poet's pen name (mahlas); Yunus usually signs simply 'Yunus'. Gibb's note explains 'He of the Koran' as 'the Author of the Koran, i.e. God'. Read straight, the line makes the boldest claim in the poem: Yunus is God. Read against the previous ghazel's ending, 'It is not Yunus sayeth this, 'tis Power's Tongue', it can mean the reverse: what speaks under the name Yunus is God's word, and the human Yunus has vanished. Gibb also notes in his chapter that the received Divan, lithographed in 1884-5, can hardly be exactly what Yunus wrote, and modern editors warn that other poets, notably Âşık Yunus of Bursa, also signed 'Yunus'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [7], pp. 171-2, and Gibb's footnotes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606
