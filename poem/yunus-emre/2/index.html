Come and Let Us Two Be Comrades

by Yunus Emre d. c. 1321

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1900) · From A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900)

ReligionRelationships

Come and let us two be comrades, come and let us seek the Loved One;
Come and let us two be helpmates, come and let us seek the Loved One.

Come and guide me on our faring; to the Friend be our repairing;
Cast aside all dule and caring, come and let us seek the Loved One.

Let us quit the world together, cheated not, for it doth wither;
Let us two be parted never, come and let us seek the Loved One.

Soothly is the world unstable; ope thine eye, thy soul is sleeping;
Unto us be fere and comrade, come and let us seek the Loved One.

Ere the clutch of Fate impel us, ere the voice of Death do hail us,
Ere that 'Azra'il assail us, come and let us seek the Loved One.

Let us see the loyal lover tidings of The Truth to gather,
Let us find the lover Yunus, come and let us seek the Loved One.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A short invitation from the poet to a friend to set out together on the mystic path, in search of the Beloved. Gibb's notes explain that the 'Friend' and the 'Loved One' mean God.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and Gibb's text couplet 1

    Gibb printed this short ghazel after the two 'pantheistic' poems, describing it as apparently an invitation to some friend to follow the mystic life. His notes gloss 'the Loved One' and 'the Friend' as God, and 'the lover' as the mystic. Where the preceding poem speaks with God's voice, this one is human and companionable, the voice of a dervish urging a fellow traveller. Such poems were sung as hymns (ilahi) in the Sufi lodges of Anatolia and the Balkans, which is how Yunus's verse spread from the fourteenth century on, according to Tatcı. Gibb translated from the Divan lithographed in 1884-5; he does not print the Turkish, and attributions in that Divan are not all secure.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606; Talât S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)

  2. The refrain every couplet

    Each of the six couplets closes with 'come and let us seek the Loved One', Gibb's rendering of a repeated end-phrase (redif). Gibb's preface explains that he imitates the rhyme pattern and, where he can, the syllable count of his originals; his line is a long trochaic one of sixteen syllables in two halves of eight, which suggests the long syllabic lines of Turkish folk verse, though Gibb gives no Turkish text to confirm it. Gibb held that Yunus wrote only in native syllabic metres; the modern editor Mustafa Tatcı counts some 138 of 417 poems in the Persian quantitative metre (aruz), so the point needs checking against the original. The refrain suits communal singing in the dervish lodge.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Preface, p. ix (on reproducing the form, rhyme and syllable count of the originals); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  3. 'guide me on our faring' couplet 2

    'Faring' (OED, from 'fare', to go or travel) keeps the old sense of a journey. The journey is the Sufi Path (Arabic tariqa, Turkish tarikat or simply yol), which the seeker walks in stages under a spiritual guide (mürşid). Yunus himself had such a guide, Tapduk Emre, whose disciple he was, according to the Ottoman biographers Gibb summarises. Here the roles are shared: the speaker asks the friend to guide him, and both walk together. The image of the road and the travelling companion (yoldaş, 'road-mate') is basic to Anatolian dervish poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'faring, n.'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  4. Internal rhyme: faring, repairing, caring couplet 2

    The second couplet rhymes 'faring', 'repairing', 'caring' within the lines and then returns to the refrain; couplets 3 and 5 do the same (together / wither / never; impel us / hail us / assail us). Gibb notes that most of Yunus's ghazels use the favourite Turkish arrangement of a thrice-repeated internal sub-rhyme, in practice the same as the stanza-like form learned poets called musammat, and that Yunus's rhymes are often only assonant. Gibb's English copies this, including the near-rhymes. 'Repairing' means going to a place, as in 'repair to the court' (OED, repair v.1), not mending.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (London: Luzac, 1900), ch. on Yunus Emre, pp. 166-73, with Gibb's notes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Preface, p. ix (on reproducing the form, rhyme and syllable count of the originals); Oxford English Dictionary, 'repair, v.1'

  5. 'the Friend' couplet 2

    Gibb's note says simply that 'the Friend' is God. The Persian word dost, Turkish dost, is a standard name for God in Sufi poetry, alongside the Beloved (yâr, maşuk) and the Truth (Hak); Yunus uses it constantly. The term turns religion into a relationship of love and intimacy rather than fear: the seeker goes to God as to a friend's house. It also links the two friendships in the poem, the human comradeship of the two travellers and their shared love for the divine Friend.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Talât S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)

  6. 'dule' couplet 2

    'Dule' (OED, under dole n.2, 'grief, sorrow', of which dule is a Scots and northern form) belongs to the archaic diction Gibb uses to give his versions an antique, ballad-like flavour, here no doubt also for the rhyme. 'Dule and caring' means grief and worldly anxiety: the cares that bind the soul to the world. In Sufi teaching the seeker must cast them off, because sorrow over what passes is itself a form of attachment; the only grief the lover keeps is longing for the Friend.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'dole, n.2 (also dule)'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes

  7. Leaving the world couplet 3

    'Quit the world' means renunciation (Arabic zuhd, the first stage of the Path), not literal flight: the seeker detaches the heart from wealth, rank and pleasure. 'Cheated not' means 'not deceived by it', since the world is a cheat: the Qur'an calls the life of this world 'the enjoyment of delusion' (mata' al-ghurur, Q 57:20), and in the same verse compares it to vegetation that flourishes and then withers into straw, the image behind 'for it doth wither'. The couplet's last line, 'Let us two be parted never', turns renunciation into a pledge of companionship: what the two friends give up they replace with each other and with God.

    The Qur'an, 57:20; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes

  8. 'Soothly is the world unstable' couplet 4

    'Soothly' is the archaic adverb 'truly' (OED, from sooth, 'truth'). The idea that the world is fleeting (Turkish fâni dünya) is perhaps the most persistent theme of Yunus's poetry and of the Turkish hymns derived from it, rooted in the Qur'an's 'All that is on earth will perish, and there remains the face of your Lord' (Q 55:26-7). The world is 'unstable' in two senses: it changes, and it gives no firm footing to anyone who leans on it. The theme sets up the warnings of death in couplet 5.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'soothly, adv.'; The Qur'an, 55:26-27; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  9. The sleeping soul couplet 4

    Sufi teachers called the state of the worldly soul ghafla, Turkish gaflet, heedlessness or forgetfulness of God, and pictured it as sleep. The best-known form is the saying 'People are asleep, and when they die they wake up', often quoted as a saying of the Prophet and also reported as a saying of 'Ali; Mohammed Rustom has studied Mulla Sadra's commentary on it. Yunus's line urges the friend to wake now, before death forces the awakening. 'Ope' is the archaic short form of 'open'.

    Mohammed Rustom, 'Psychology, Eschatology, and Imagination in Mulla Sadra Shirazi's Commentary on the Hadith of Awakening', Islam & Science 5.1 (2007); Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes

  10. 'fere' couplet 4

    'Fere' (OED, fere n.1) is an old word for a companion, comrade or spouse, from Old English gefera, one who goes (faran) with another: a fellow traveller, exactly the Turkish yoldaş, 'road-companion'. It survives mainly in ballads and in Burns's 'Auld Lang Syne' ('my trusty fiere'). Gibb's 'fere and comrade' doubles the idea, as Yunus doubles 'comrades' and 'helpmates' in the opening couplet. The repetition shows that the poem is as much about friendship on the Path as about the goal.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'fere, n.1'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes

  11. Fate and Death couplet 5

    The fifth couplet builds a triple 'ere' (before) clause, each rhyming internally (impel us, hail us, assail us), and so turns the invitation into an urgent memento mori. 'Fate' probably stands for the appointed hour of death (Turkish ecel, from Arabic ajal), which the Qur'an says can be neither delayed nor hastened (Q 7:34); 'the voice of Death' that 'hails' is the summons no one can refuse. The climax, the Angel of Death himself, gives the abstract terms a face. The urgency is typical of Yunus, whose poems on death and the grave are among his most famous.

    The Qur'an, 7:34; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  12. 'Azra'il couplet 5

    Gibb's note identifies 'Azra'il as the Angel of Death. The Qur'an speaks of 'the angel of death who has been put in charge of you' (Q 32:11) without naming him; the name 'Azra'il, probably of Hebrew origin, comes from later tradition. In Turkish he is Azrail, a familiar and dreaded figure of popular religion and folk poetry: Chodzko's note to one of the Köroğlu songs in this archive calls him 'Ezrael'. Yunus's line pictures him as an assailant, the one who strikes when the term of life runs out.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; The Qur'an, 32:11; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  13. The lover couplet 6

    Gibb's note: 'the lover' is the mystic. Arabic 'ashiq, Turkish âşık, is the key word of Yunus's poetry, the one who loves God with a passion that consumes the self, as opposed to the zâhid, the cold ascetic. The same word later named the wandering minstrel-poets of Anatolia (âşık), who sang Köroğlu's songs among others; Chodzko records the northern Persian form for professional singers. 'Loyal' stresses fidelity: the true lover keeps faith in spite of suffering. The couplet asks that the two friends find such a lover to bring them news ('tidings') of the Truth.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Alexander Chodzko, Specimens of the Popular Poetry of Persia (London: Oriental Translation Fund, 1842), Introduction, pp. 3-15

  14. 'The Truth' couplet 6

    Gibb's capitals mark 'The Truth' as a divine Name, Arabic al-Haqq, Turkish Hak, the commonest name for God in Yunus. 'Tidings' casts the lover as a messenger who has been where the Beloved is and can report on Him, a familiar device of love poetry, where the lover waits for news of the absent beloved. The search for 'the Loved One' is thus partly a search for the right human guide, the true lover who already knows the way.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975); Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606

  15. The signature: 'the lover Yunus' couplet 6, l. 2

    As in the ghazel convention, the poet's name appears in the last couplet. Here the speaker seems to step outside himself: 'Let us find the lover Yunus', as if Yunus were the guide the friends should seek. Some readers take this as the humility of a dervish who speaks of himself in the third person; others as a sign that the poem is addressed by Yunus, as teacher, to his disciples. The phrase 'lover Yunus' (âşık Yunus) raises a further problem: the Divan contains poems by a later poet, Âşık Yunus of Bursa (d. early fifteenth century), who signed in the same way, and Tatcı notes that the two have been confused. Gibb, without the modern critical editions, does not discuss the question for this poem.

    Mustafa Tatcı, 'Yûnus Emre', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 600-606; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. I (1900), Ghazel [8], p. 173, and Gibb's footnotes; Talât S. Halman, ed., Yunus Emre and His Mystical Poetry (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1981)
