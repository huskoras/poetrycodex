Ode to Evening

by William Collins 1721–1759

From Odes on Several Descriptive and Allegorical Subjects (1746)

NatureTime & Brevity

If aught of oaten stop, or pastoral song,
May hope, chaste Eve, to soothe thy modest ear,
Like thy own brawling springs,
Thy springs, and dying gales;

O Nymph reserved, while now the bright-hair'd sun
Sits in yon western tent, whose cloudy skirts,
With brede ethereal wove,
O'erhang his wavy bed:

Now air is hush'd, save where the weak-eyed bat
With short shrill shriek flits by on leathern wing;
Or where the beetle winds
His small but sullen horn,

As oft he rises 'midst the twilight path,
Against the pilgrim borne in heedless hum:
Now teach me, maid composed,
To breathe some soften'd strain,

Whose numbers, stealing through thy darkening vale,
May not unseemly with its stillness suit;
As, musing slow, I hail
Thy genial loved return!

For when thy folding-star arising shows
His paly circlet, at his warning lamp
The fragrant Hours, and Elves
Who slept in buds the day,

And many a Nymph who wreathes her brows with sedge,
And sheds the freshening dew, and, lovelier still,
The pensive Pleasures sweet,
Prepare thy shadowy car.

Then let me rove some wild and heathy scene;
Or find some ruin, 'midst its dreary dells,
Whose walls more awful nod
By thy religious gleams.

Or, if chill blustering winds, or driving rain,
Prevent my willing feet, be mine the hut,
That, from the mountain's side,
Views wilds, and swelling floods,

And hamlets brown, and dim-discover'd spires;
And hears their simple bell, and marks o'er all
Thy dewy fingers draw
The gradual dusky veil.

While Spring shall pour his showers, as oft he wont,
And bathe thy breathing tresses, meekest Eve!
While Summer loves to sport
Beneath thy lingering light;

While sallow Autumn fills thy lap with leaves;
Or Winter, yelling through the troublous air,
Affrights thy shrinking train,
And rudely rends thy robes;

So long, regardful of thy quiet rule,
Shall Fancy, Friendship, Science, smiling Peace,
Thy gentlest influence own,
And love thy favourite name!

Codex Note

An unrhymed address to Evening, which becomes a tour of the landscape at dusk and through the seasons.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and the two texts ll. 1-2

    The ode first appeared in Odes on Several Descriptive and Allegoric Subjects, published by Millar in December 1746 with the date 1747. Collins revised it for Dodsley's Collection of Poems (1748), and Bronson, Falle (for RPO) and most modern editors print the revised text. The archive's version is a conflation: ll. 1-2 ('chaste Eve, to soothe thy modest ear') follow 1748, where 1746 read 'O pensive Eve, to sooth thine ear', but 'brawling springs' (l. 3), 'slept in buds' (l. 24), the stanza beginning 'Then let me rove' (ll. 29-32), 'Or, if ... Prevent' (ll. 33-4) and the last stanza follow 1746. Students comparing editions should expect these differences. 'Eve' is the evening, but the name also lets Milton's first woman hover behind the 'chaste' and 'modest' maid.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), pp. 53-5 (text and 1747 variants) and pp. 111-14 (notes); Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', ed. G. G. Falle (text of 1748, with publication note)

  2. oaten stop l. 1

    A 'stop' is a hole on a wind instrument, closed by the finger to change the note; an 'oaten' pipe is the shepherd's reed of classical pastoral (Virgil's avena). The RPO notes cite Comus 345, 'pastoral reed with oaten stops', and Lycidas has the 'Oaten Flute' (l. 33) and 'tender stops of various Quills' (l. 188). Collins opens with a modest conditional ('If aught ... May hope'): his song wishes only to be as soothing to Evening as her own streams and breezes.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on l. 1; John Milton, Comus 345 and Lycidas 33, 188 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stop, n.2' (finger-hole of a wind instrument)

  3. unrhymed stanza: Milton's Pyrrha ode ll. 1-4

    The ode has no rhyme. Its four-line stanza, two iambic pentameters followed by two trimeters, repeats the form Milton used for his version of Horace's ode to Pyrrha (Odes I.5), which tried to imitate the effect of Horace's Asclepiadean stanza in English. Bronson and the RPO notes both point this out. The shortening lines give each stanza a falling cadence, like dusk, and the whole poem is one long sentence through l. 20 and another to the end, its syntax as continuous as the fading light.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), Introduction (on the stanza) and p. 111; Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on the stanza; John Milton, 'The Fifth Ode of Horace, Lib. I' (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  4. brawling springs ll. 3-4

    'Brawling' means noisy, clamorous, of a stream running over stones. In the revised text of 1748 Collins wrote 'solemn springs'. A reviewer in the Athenaeum (1856), quoted by Bronson, argued that 'brawling' broke the deep calm of the poem, while 'solemn' replaced an outward fact with the poet's tranquil impression. Others might defend 'brawling' as precise observation of the sounds that stand out at dusk. The 'dying gales' are breezes falling away at evening; Evening's 'own' springs and gales are her natural music, which Collins hopes his song may match.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, note on l. 3 (quoting the Athenaeum, 5 Jan. 1856); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brawling, adj.'

  5. Joseph Warton's 'To Evening' l. 5

    Collins and Joseph Warton had planned a joint volume; in the end each published separately in December 1746, and Warton's Odes on Various Subjects includes a rhymed 'To Evening' ('Hail meek-ey'd maiden, clad in sober grey'). Bronson prints it for comparison; the two odes were composed close together, and which came first is not settled. The comparison shows Collins's originality: Warton's poem is a sequence of rural genre scenes, while Collins addresses Evening as a 'Nymph reserved', a shy goddess whose presence is felt in the quality of the light. 'Reserved' means modest, keeping herself apart.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), pp. 111-13 (text of Warton's 'To Evening' and T. Warton's note); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reserved, adj.'

  6. the sun in his western tent ll. 5-8

    The image combines the classical sun-god Apollo, 'bright-hair'd' (Phoebus was traditionally unshorn), with Psalm 19:4-5, where God has set 'a tabernacle for the sun', which comes out like a bridegroom from his chamber. The clouds are the tent's 'skirts' (hanging edges), woven ('wove') with 'brede ethereal', an embroidery of heavenly light. 'Brede' is an old form of 'braid', meaning embroidery or interwoven work (RPO and Bronson both gloss it). The sun's 'wavy bed' is the western sea, where he was imagined to sink at night. The stanza is a painterly sunset, and early readers compared the ode to Claude and Poussin.

    Psalm 19:4-5 (King James Version); W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, note on l. 7 ('brede = braid, embroidery'); p. 112 (Nathan Drake on Poussin and Claude); Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on l. 7

  7. the weak-eyed bat on leathern wing ll. 9-10

    Bronson compares Macbeth III.ii.40-43, where 'ere the bat hath flown / His cloister'd flight' the 'shard-borne beetle with his drowsy hums' rings night's peal: Collins takes both creatures from the passage. Dyce, quoted by Bronson, compares Spenser's 'lether-winged Batt, dayes enimy' (Faerie Queene II.xii); Shakespeare's fairies also war with bats 'for their leathern wings' (A Midsummer Night's Dream II.ii.4). 'Weak-eyed' records the belief that bats see poorly. The 'short shrill shriek' is onomatopoeic, with its run of sh and r sounds. Shakespeare's creatures foreshadow murder; Collins keeps their sounds and drops the menace.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, notes on ll. 9-14 (citing Dyce); Shakespeare, Macbeth III.ii (Folger Shakespeare); Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on l. 10 (Spenser)

  8. the beetle winds his horn ll. 11-14

    'Wind' here (rhyming with 'kind') means to blow a horn. Dyce, cited by Bronson, compares Lycidas 28, 'What time the Gray-fly winds her sultry horn'. The horn is 'small but sullen', a deep droning sound out of proportion to the insect. In the next stanza the beetle rises in the twilight path and flies 'Against the pilgrim', the traveller on foot, 'in heedless hum', careless of where it goes. Gray's Elegy (1751), 'the beetle wheels his droning flight', shares the same Shakespearean and Miltonic source; the twilight insect became a fixture of mid-century evening poetry.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, note on ll. 11-12 (citing Dyce); John Milton, Lycidas 28 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wind, v.2' (to blow a horn)

  9. numbers; genial ll. 17-20

    In the speaker's prayer ('Now teach me, maid composed, / To breathe some soften'd strain'), 'numbers' means verse, rhythmical lines, as the RPO note glosses it ('cadences'). The poem should steal through the darkening valley without breaking its stillness, 'not unseemly' (not unfittingly) suiting it. 'Genial' comes from Latin genialis, connected with the genius or generative spirit and with festivity; in eighteenth-century poetry it often means kindly, warm, fostering growth. The 'maid composed' is calm, collected. The main clause of the poem's first long sentence ('teach me') arrives only at l. 15, as Bronson notes.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on 'numbers'; W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, note on l. 1 ('The conclusion begins in l. 15'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genial, adj.' and 'number, n.' (metrical verse)

  10. the folding-star ll. 21-2

    The evening star rises as dusk falls and signals the hour for folding (penning) the flocks; Milton calls it 'The Star that bids the Shepherd fold' (Comus 93), and Virgil's sixth Eclogue ends as Vesper bids the shepherds pen their sheep. The star shows his 'paly circlet', a small pale disc ('paly' is a poetic form of 'pale'), and at his 'warning lamp' the attendants of Evening gather. Geoffrey Hartman made the evening star central to his reading of the poem, linking Hesperus, the star of the west, to the westward migration of poetry and imagination to England.

    John Milton, Comus 93 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Virgil, Eclogue VI.85-6 (The Latin Library); Geoffrey H. Hartman, 'Evening Star and Evening Land', in The Fate of Reading (Chicago, 1975)

  11. the Hours and the elves ll. 23-4

    The RPO note glosses the 'Hours' as the classical attendants of the day and the seasons; the Horai are often fragrant, flower-bearing figures, so 'fragrant Hours' marks the scents of evening. The elves who 'slept in buds the day' (1746; 1748 has 'flow'rs') are Shakespearean fairies, who in A Midsummer Night's Dream hide in acorn cups and sleep in flowers. Bronson thought the revision to 'flowers' a doubtful improvement, since half-opened buds are just the right dormitories for elves. The mixture of classical and English folk mythology is characteristic of Collins.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening', note on 'Hours'; W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 113, note on l. 24; Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream II.i-ii (Folger Shakespeare)

  12. the nymph crowned with sedge ll. 25-8

    Sedge is a coarse grass of riverbanks. River-nymphs (Naiads) were often pictured crowned with reeds or sedge; in The Tempest IV.i Iris calls the 'nymphs, call'd Naiads, of the windring brooks, / With your sedg'd crowns'. Collins's nymph 'sheds the freshening dew', so the falling of dew becomes a personified act. With the 'pensive Pleasures' (a Miltonic coinage recalling Il Penseroso) these attendants 'Prepare thy shadowy car', the chariot in which Evening rides, as Night and other deities ride in classical poetry.

    Shakespeare, The Tempest IV.i.128-30 (Folger Shakespeare); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sedge, n.1'; W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), Introduction (on Evening as person and as scene)

  13. the 1746 stanza and its 1748 replacement ll. 29-32

    The archive prints the original 1746 stanza: the speaker would roam a wild heath or find a ruin among 'dreary dells', whose walls 'nod' (lean, threaten to fall) more awfully in Evening's 'religious gleams'. The phrase recalls Il Penseroso 160, 'Casting a dimm religious light'. In 1748 Collins substituted a calmer stanza with a 'sheety lake', a 'time-hallow'd pile' and 'upland fallows grey' reflecting the last cool gleam. Bronson preferred the revision: the earlier stanza, he argued, spends three lines on the ruin and its 'wild', 'dreary' and 'awful' words disturb the poem's tranquillity. Readers interested in the rise of Gothic taste have often valued the first version for exactly those qualities.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), pp. 54 (variant) and 113 (note on ll. 29-32); Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening' (text of 1748); John Milton, Il Penseroso 160 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  14. the hut on the mountain side ll. 33-6

    'Prevent' (1746; 1748 'Forbid') means hinder. The hut on a hillside is a viewpoint: it 'views' and 'hears' and 'marks', so the building itself takes on the poet's senses. Thomas Campbell (Specimens of the British Poets, 1819), quoted by Bronson, praised the economy with which these few words open a wide landscape, as from Ben Lomond or Snowdon. 'Wilds' are uncultivated tracts; 'swelling floods' are rivers in spate. The scene is an imagined, composite prospect rather than a record of a particular place.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 114, note on ll. 35-6 (quoting Campbell, Specimens of the British Poets, 1819, v. 310); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'prevent, v.' (to hinder)

  15. dim-discover'd spires ll. 37-8

    'Discover'd' keeps its older sense, uncovered or revealed to sight: the spires are dimly made out. 'Brown' is a common eighteenth-century colour word for the dull tones of dusk or of thatch and earth. The 'simple bell' (unadorned, rustic) is probably the evening bell, the curfew that Milton's and Gray's evening poems also hear. Nathan Drake's remark (1798), quoted by Bronson, that the spirit of Poussin and Claude breathes through the ode suits this graded landscape of distances fading into one tone.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'discover, v.' (to reveal, disclose to view); W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 112 (quoting Nathan Drake, Literary Hours, 1798, p. 391)

  16. the dewy fingers and the gradual veil ll. 39-40

    Homer's formula for the dawn, rhododaktylos Eos, 'rosy-fingered Dawn', gives the goddess fingers that spread light; Collins's Evening has 'dewy fingers' that draw a veil, bringing the dew and the dark. 'Gradual' is a precise word: the veil is drawn by degrees, so the image renders the slow loss of light. Bronson cites Robert Willmott's remark that the ode is to be viewed like a picture; this stanza is its most painterly moment, and it reverses the sunset brilliance of the second stanza.

    Homer, Iliad I.477 (Greek text, Perseus Digital Library); W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 112 (quoting Willmott, 1854)

  17. Evening through the seasons ll. 41-8

    'Wont' means was accustomed (to do). Spring is masculine, pouring showers that bathe Evening's 'breathing tresses' (fragrant hair); Summer plays in her long light; 'sallow' (yellowish, wan) Autumn fills her lap with leaves. Winter 'yelling' through the 'troublous' (turbulent) air frightens her train and tears her robes: Bronson reads this as a figure for the short winter twilight, quickly giving way to night. The four seasons, each with its personified action, make Evening a constant presence across the year, which prepares the final 'So long'.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), p. 114, note on ll. 47-8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wont, v.' and 'sallow, adj.'

  18. the final stanza ll. 49-52

    This is the 1746 text: 'regardful of thy quiet rule' (attentive to her gentle reign), 'smiling Peace' and 'love thy favourite name'. In 1748 Collins wrote 'So long, sure-found beneath the sylvan shed', 'rose-lipp'd Health' and 'hymn thy fav'rite name'. 'Science' is learning; 'own' means acknowledge. Bronson thought the stanza a serious blemish in either version, a conventional parade of capitalised abstractions; he allowed that Peace, at least, has an evident connection with evening. Other readers see the ending as Collins's characteristic move from description to the allegorical powers that govern poetry, in keeping with the volume's 'Descriptive and Allegoric' title. In the war years 1745-6, 'smiling Peace' may also carry a public hope.

    W. C. Bronson, ed., Poems of William Collins (Athenaeum Press, Boston, 1898), pp. 55 (variant) and 114 (note on ll. 49-52); Representative Poetry Online, 'Ode to Evening' (text of 1748)
