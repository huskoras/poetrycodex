Nature

Frost at Midnight

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

From Poems of Coleridge, selected by Arthur Symons (1905)

Nature

The Frost performs its secret ministry,
Unhelped by any wind. The owlet's cry
Came loud--and hark, again! loud as before.
The inmates of my cottage, all at rest,
Have left me to that solitude, which suits
Abstruser musings: save that at my side
My cradled infant slumbers peacefully.
'Tis calm indeed! so calm, that it disturbs
And vexes meditation with its strange
And extreme silentness. Sea, hill, and wood,
This populous village! Sea, and hill, and wood,
With all the numberless goings-on of life,
Inaudible as dreams! the thin blue flame
Lies on my low-burnt fire, and quivers not;
Only that film, which fluttered on the grate,
Still flutters there, the sole unquiet thing.
Methinks, its motion in this hush of nature
Gives it dim sympathies with me who live,
Making it a companionable form,
Whose puny flaps and freaks the idling Spirit
By its own moods interprets, every where
Echo or mirror seeking of itself,
And makes a toy of Thought.

But O! how oft,
How oft, at school, with most believing mind,
Presageful, have I gazed upon the bars,
To watch that fluttering stranger! and as oft
With unclosed lids, already had I dreamt
Of my sweet birth-place, and the old church-tower,
Whose bells, the poor man's only music, rang
From morn to evening, all the hot Fair-day,
So sweetly, that they stirred and haunted me
With a wild pleasure, falling on mine ear
Most like articulate sounds of things to come!
So gazed I, till the soothing things, I dreamt,
Lulled me to sleep, and sleep prolonged my dreams!
And so I brooded all the following morn,
Awed by the stern preceptor's face, mine eye
Fixed with mock study on my swimming book:
Save if the door half opened, and I snatched
A hasty glance, and still my heart leaped up,
For still I hoped to see the stranger's face,
Townsman, or aunt, or sister more beloved,
My play-mate when we both were clothed alike!

Dear Babe, that sleepest cradled by my side,
Whose gentle breathings, heard in this deep calm,
Fill up the interspersed vacancies
And momentary pauses of the thought!
My babe so beautiful! it thrills my heart
With tender gladness, thus to look at thee,
And think that thou shalt learn far other lore,
And in far other scenes! For I was reared
In the great city, pent 'mid cloisters dim,
And saw nought lovely but the sky and stars.
But thou, my babe! shalt wander like a breeze
By lakes and sandy shores, beneath the crags
Of ancient mountain, and beneath the clouds,
Which image in their bulk both lakes and shores
And mountain crags: so shalt thou see and hear
The lovely shapes and sounds intelligible
Of that eternal language, which thy God
Utters, who from eternity doth teach
Himself in all, and all things in himself.
Great universal Teacher! he shall mould
Thy spirit, and by giving make it ask.

Therefore all seasons shall be sweet to thee,
Whether the summer clothe the general earth
With greenness, or the redbreast sit and sing
Betwixt the tufts of snow on the bare branch
Of mossy apple-tree, while the nigh thatch
Smokes in the sun-thaw; whether the eave-drops fall
Heard only in the trances of the blast,
Or if the secret ministry of frost
Shall hang them up in silent icicles,
Quietly shining to the quiet Moon.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication l. 1

    Coleridge was living in a cottage at Nether Stowey with his wife Sara and their son Hartley, then about seventeen months old; Wordsworth and Dorothy were nearby at Alfoxden. The poem was published by Joseph Johnson in 1798 and revised several times (in the Poetical Register of 1808-9 and in the collected editions of 1828 and 1829), the six-line ending of 1798 being dropped by 1829. The archive text is the revised version, which ends at 'the quiet Moon'. It is the most admired of the 'conversation poems', blank-verse meditations in a familiar voice addressed to a listener, here a sleeping infant.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Frost at Midnight', eds Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof (1798 text, Coleridge's note, notes on Ann Coleridge and on Cowper); David S. Miall, 'Frost at Midnight': variant readings 1798-1829 (University of Alberta); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), 'Frost at Midnight' with variants; Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989)

  2. secret ministry l. 1

    'Ministry' means service, and in a religious sense the office of a minister; Coleridge was himself a Unitarian preacher at this period. The phrase gives the frost a purpose without a visible agent, and the poem's theology of a God who 'teaches' through nature is already implied. The phrase returns in the final sentence, making the poem circular. Hoar-frost forms best on still, clear nights, which is why the next line adds 'Unhelped by any wind'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ministry'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'; M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)

  3. The owlet's cry ll. 2-3

    'Owlet' is a young or small owl, and in literary usage simply an owl. The repetition ('and hark, again! loud as before') makes the reader listen, while the frost's 'secret ministry' is soundless. The interjection gives the opening the feel of a live, present-tense meditation. Owls recur in Coleridge's poems of this period ('Christabel' opens at midnight with the owl's cry), and their calls serve as markers of night and solitude.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'owlet'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  4. inmates l. 4

    In older usage an 'inmate' is one who lives in a house with others, a lodger or member of the household. The word is neutral here, but it makes the cottage a small community from which the speaker is isolated by wakefulness. The household was not small: besides his wife and son, Coleridge often had guests at Nether Stowey, and in early 1798 Wordsworth and Dorothy were frequent visitors from nearby Alfoxden. The isolation is the chosen solitude of the late night, not loneliness.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'inmate'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  5. Abstruser musings l. 6

    'Abstruse' (Latin abstrusus, 'hidden, concealed') means hard to understand. The comparative suggests that solitude suits such thoughts; but the poem actually moves away from abstraction into memory and hope for the child. The phrase is often taken as self-mocking, given Coleridge's reputation for metaphysics. Coleridge's reading in 1797-98 included Hartley, Berkeley, Spinoza and the Neoplatonists, and in September 1798 he left for Germany, partly to study philosophy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abstruse'; Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989)

  6. My cradled infant l. 7

    Hartley was named after the philosopher David Hartley, whose associationist psychology Coleridge then admired. The father's hopes for him in this poem have often been set against Hartley's later life: he became a gifted but troubled poet who lived in the Lake District, where the poem imagines him wandering. Wordsworth's 'To H. C., Six Years Old' is also addressed to him.

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), 'Frost at Midnight' with variants

  7. so calm, that it disturbs ... extreme silentness ll. 8-10

    The silence 'vexes meditation' because there is nothing to resist or respond to; the mind turns inward. 'Silentness' is an uncommon variant of 'silence'. The next lines try to fill the silence with a list of what is present but unheard: 'Sea, hill, and wood ... With all the numberless goings-on of life, / Inaudible as dreams!' The repeated phrase 'Sea, and hill, and wood' sounds like an incantation against the quiet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'silentness'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  8. This populous village! l. 11

    The paradox of a 'populous' place that makes no sound continues the poem's play on presence and absence. The phrase may echo Milton's simile of Satan in Eden 'As one who long in populous city pent' (Paradise Lost 9.445), which Coleridge recalls more explicitly later in the poem ('In the great city, pent'). The exclamations imitate the movement of a mind registering its surroundings, a hallmark of the conversation-poem manner.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost 9.445-51 ('As one who long in populous city pent'), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  9. Only that film ... the sole unquiet thing l. 15

    Coleridge's note (1798) explains that 'in all parts of the kingdom these films are called strangers' and are supposed to portend the arrival of some absent friend. Cowper's The Task, Book 4 ('The Winter Evening'), has a passage on the 'sooty films' on the bars of the grate and the superstition they prompt, which Coleridge's poem seems to recall. The film is the only moving thing in a still world, and it becomes the link to memory in the next paragraph.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Frost at Midnight', eds Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof (1798 text, Coleridge's note, notes on Ann Coleridge and on Cowper); William Cowper, The Task (1785), Book 4, 'The Winter Evening' (the 'sooty films' on the grate); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), 'Frost at Midnight' with variants

  10. a companionable form l. 19

    The film seems to share the speaker's restlessness. 'Freaks' are whims or capricious movements; 'puny flaps' its small motions. Coleridge is analysing how a solitary mind projects its own moods onto objects, a process the next lines describe. The lines anticipate a theme central to Coleridge's later criticism, the difference between a fancy that merely plays with images and an imagination that finds true relation between mind and world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'freak'; M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)

  11. Echo or mirror seeking of itself, / And makes a toy of Thought ll. 22-23

    These lines replace a longer passage of 1798, which spoke of 'Idle thought' and 'fantastic playfulness'; the passage was reworked in 1808-9 and again in 1828. The final text compresses the idea: the mind finds itself everywhere and trivialises thought into a toy. Critics read the lines as Coleridge's critique of solipsistic fancy, set against the poem's later vision of nature as God's language, in which the mind receives meaning rather than projecting it.

    David S. Miall, 'Frost at Midnight': variant readings 1798-1829 (University of Alberta); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'; M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)

  12. Structure: the 'greater Romantic lyric' l. 23

    M. H. Abrams took this poem as a model of what he called the 'greater Romantic lyric': a speaker in a particular outdoor or domestic setting, whose meditation moves from the scene into memory and feeling and returns to the scene with a new understanding. The half-line indent at 'But O! how oft' marks the turn into the past. The verse is blank verse with a conversational, flexible syntax.

    M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  13. at school ... the bars l. 24

    After his father's death in 1781, Coleridge was sent to Christ's Hospital in the City of London. 'The bars' are the bars of the schoolroom grate where, as a boy, he watched for the same fluttering 'stranger'. 'Presageful' means full of foreboding or prediction. Charles Lamb, a schoolfellow, described Coleridge at school in his essays. Coleridge's memories of the school, its discipline and its loneliness for a country boy, recur in his letters and in Biographia Literaria.

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 1, on the Rev. James Bowyer of Christ's Hospital (Project Gutenberg text)

  14. my sweet birth-place, and the old church-tower l. 28

    The church of St Mary at Ottery, with its tall towers, dominated the small town; the boy at school dreamed of its bells ringing for the annual fair. 'Fair-day' is the day of the town fair. The bells as 'the poor man's only music' is a characteristic sympathetic touch. The memory of the fair and its bells is a memory within a memory: the boy at school is remembering an earlier childhood at home. The layered time-scheme is one of the poem's most admired features.

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), 'Frost at Midnight' with variants

  15. Most like articulate sounds of things to come l. 33

    'Articulate' means jointed into distinct words. The child hears meaning in the bells, as the boy at school reads meaning into the soot-film. The theme comes to its fullest form in the paragraph promising the infant 'that eternal language' of nature. The phrase also anticipates the boy's hope of a visitor: the bells seem to promise future happenings, as the soot-film promises a 'stranger'. The child's imagination is always reaching toward what is to come.

    M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  16. the stern preceptor l. 37

    In chapter 1 of Biographia Literaria Coleridge credits 'the Reverend James Bowyer' with training his taste and also remembers his discipline. 'Preceptor' means teacher. 'My swimming book' is the page blurring before eyes that are only pretending to study. Charles Lamb's essays on Christ's Hospital also remember Bowyer's severity. The memory of fear in the classroom sets up the contrast with the freedom imagined for Hartley.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 1, on the Rev. James Bowyer of Christ's Hospital (Project Gutenberg text); Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989)

  17. the stranger's face ... sister more beloved ll. 41-42

    The superstition becomes a child's longing. The sister is probably Ann (Nancy) Coleridge, who died in 1791. The 1829 text italicised 'stranger', stressing the link with the soot-film's folk name. The repeated hope ('still my heart leaped up, / For still I hoped') shows how intensely the lonely schoolboy longed for home. The sister's death gives the memory a retrospective sadness that the poem does not spell out.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Frost at Midnight', eds Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof (1798 text, Coleridge's note, notes on Ann Coleridge and on Cowper); David S. Miall, 'Frost at Midnight': variant readings 1798-1829 (University of Alberta); Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989)

  18. when we both were clothed alike l. 43

    The phrase identifies the sister as his playmate in early childhood, before boys were put into breeches. The detail is affectionate and exact, and it marks how far back the memory reaches. Coleridge was the youngest of a large family, and the 'sister more beloved' of the line was a companion of his early childhood in Ottery.

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  19. Dear Babe ... interspersed vacancies ll. 44-47

    'Interspersed vacancies' are the pauses scattered through thought; the child's breathing fills them as the film did. The address to a silent listener is typical of the conversation poems (compare 'This Lime-Tree Bower my Prison', addressed to Charles Lamb). The stillness of the sleeping child is the counterpart of the silent frost; both are presences that do not speak but fill the poet's attention.

    M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965); S. T. Coleridge, 'This Lime-Tree Bower my Prison' (1797; pub. 1800), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (1912)

  20. In the great city, pent 'mid cloisters dim l. 52

    'Pent' means confined. Milton's simile describes Satan's delight in Eden as like a city-dweller's in the country air. Coleridge had already used the phrase of Lamb, 'In the great City pent', in 'This Lime-Tree Bower my Prison' (1797). The 'cloisters dim' are those of Christ's Hospital, on the site of a former Franciscan friary. The contrast of city and country education is an eighteenth- and Romantic-period commonplace.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost 9.445-51 ('As one who long in populous city pent'), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; S. T. Coleridge, 'This Lime-Tree Bower my Prison' (1797; pub. 1800), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (1912); Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989)

  21. shalt wander like a breeze l. 54

    The landscape is not Somerset but the Lake District or some ideal upland; the Coleridges moved to Keswick in 1800, and Hartley did grow up among lakes and mountains. The clouds that 'image' (reflect, mirror) the lakes and crags make nature a set of mutual reflections, a contrast to the self-reflecting mind of the first paragraph. The breeze is a key Romantic image of free, unforced inspiration (compare Coleridge's 'The Eolian Harp').

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'

  22. that eternal language, which thy God / Utters ll. 60-61

    Berkeley argued that what we see is a set of signs by which God speaks to us, 'the universal language of the Author of nature' (New Theory of Vision; Alciphron). Coleridge adapts the idea: the 'lovely shapes and sounds intelligible' are words of a divine language through which the child will learn. 'Intelligible' means capable of being understood, perhaps with a philosophical sense of what is grasped by the intellect.

    George Berkeley, An Essay towards a New Theory of Vision (1709), sect. 147, and Alciphron (1732), Dialogue 4 (vision as the language of God), Project Gutenberg; Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)

  23. Himself in all, and all things in himself l. 62

    The line describes a God who is present in everything and contains everything. Coleridge in 1798 was still a Unitarian with pantheist sympathies; he later worried about pantheism. 'By giving make it ask' says that God's gifts awaken a desire for more, so that the child's spirit learns to seek. The formula is close to Paul's 'in him we live, and move, and have our being' (Acts 17:28), which Coleridge as a preacher knew well, and to Neoplatonic ideas of a world that comes from and returns to the One.

    Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1989); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Acts 17:28

  24. the redbreast sit and sing l. 67

    The final paragraph moves from summer to winter details: a robin among snow tufts on a mossy apple-tree, the thatch steaming in a sun-thaw, the eave-drops heard only in the pauses ('trances') of the wind. The precise rural observation answers the earlier promise that the child will learn from nature. 'The general earth' means the whole earth, the earth at large. The details are all small and domestic, a deliberate scaling down from the grand 'eternal language' of the previous paragraph.

    J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton UP, 2001), 'Frost at Midnight'; M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)

  25. The ending l. 74

    The original 1798 version continued for six more lines, imagining the baby stretching towards the icicles; Coleridge later explained that he omitted them because they destroyed the poem's return upon itself, and the cut stands in the 1829 text. The present ending fixes the poem in stillness: the eave-drops, frozen by 'the secret ministry of frost', shine 'quietly' to the 'quiet Moon'. Abrams treats the return to the opening scene, changed by the meditation, as the defining move of the greater Romantic lyric.

    David S. Miall, 'Frost at Midnight': variant readings 1798-1829 (University of Alberta); Representative Poetry Online, 'Frost at Midnight', eds Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof (1798 text, Coleridge's note, notes on Ann Coleridge and on Cowper); M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric', in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom (New York: Oxford UP, 1965)
