Nature

The Eolian Harp

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

From Poems of Coleridge, selected by Arthur Symons (1905)

Nature

My pensive Sara! thy soft cheek reclined
Thus on mine arm, most soothing sweet it is
To sit beside our cot, our cot o'ergrown
With white-flowered Jasmin, and the broad-leaved Myrtle,
(Meet emblems they of Innocence and Love!),
And watch the clouds, that late were rich with light,
Slow saddening round, and mark the star of eve
Serenely brilliant (such should wisdom be)
Shine opposite! How exquisite the scents
Snatched from yon bean-field! and the world so hushed!

The stilly murmur of the distant sea
Tells us of silence.

And that simplest lute,
Placed length-ways in the clasping casement, hark!
How by the desultory breeze caressed,
Like some coy maid half yielding to her lover,
It pours such sweet upbraiding, as must needs
Tempt to repeat the wrong! And now, its strings
Boldlier swept, the long sequacious notes
Over delicious surges sink and rise,
Such a soft floating witchery of sound
As twilight Elfins make, when they at eve
Voyage on gentle gales from Fairy-Land,
Where Melodies round honey-dropping flowers,
Footless and wild, like birds of Paradise,
Nor pause, nor perch, hovering on untamed wing!
O! the one life within us and abroad,
Which meets all motion and becomes its soul,
A light in sound, a sound-like power in light
Rhythm in all thought, and joyance every where--
Methinks, it should have been impossible
Not to love all things in a world so filled;
Where the breeze warbles, and the mute still air
In Music slumbering on her instrument.

And thus, my love! as on the midway slope
Of yonder hill I stretch my limbs at noon,
Whilst through my half-closed eye-lids I behold
The sunbeams dance, like diamonds, on the main,
And tranquil muse upon tranquillity;
Full many a thought uncalled and undetained,
And many idle flitting phantasies,
Traverse my indolent and passive brain,
As wild and various as the random gales
That swell and flutter on this subject lute!

And what if all of animated nature
Be but organic harps diversely framed,
That tremble into thought, as o'er them sweeps
Plastic and vast, one intellectual breeze,
At once the Soul of each, and God of all?

But thy more serious eye a mild reproof
Darts, O beloved woman! nor such thoughts
Dim and unhallowed dost thou not reject,
And biddest me walk humbly with my God.
Meek daughter in the family of Christ!
Well hast thou said and holily dispraised
These shapings of the unregenerate mind;
Bubbles that glitter as they rise and break
On vain Philosophy's aye-babbling spring.
For never guiltless may I speak of him,
The Incomprehensible! save when with awe
I praise him, and with Faith that inly feels;
Who with his saving mercies healed me,
A sinful and most miserable man,
Wildered and dark, and gave me to possess
Peace, and this cot, and thee, dear honoured Maid!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Eolian harp ll. 13-14

    Named after Aeolus, keeper of the winds, the instrument was fashionable in eighteenth-century houses; James Thomson wrote an 'Ode on Aeolus's Harp' (1748) and described one in The Castle of Indolence. Placed 'length-ways in the clasping casement', it sounds without a player as the breeze varies, producing chords that rise and fall unpredictably. For Coleridge and later Romantics it became the classic image of a passive receiver animated by an outside power: the poet's mind moved by impressions, or all living things moved by one divine breath. The poem's argument turns on how far that image can be pressed.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; M. H. Abrams, 'Coleridge's "A Light in Sound": Science, Metascience, and Poetic Imagination', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 116 (1972); James Thomson, 'Ode on Aeolus's Harp' (1748); The Castle of Indolence (1748), I.40-41

  2. My pensive Sara l. 1

    A manuscript version is dated 20 August 1795 at Clevedon, on the Bristol Channel, and the poem was published in Poems on Various Subjects (1796) as 'Effusion XXXV', with the note 'Composed August 20th, 1795, At Clevedon, Somersetshire'. Coleridge married Sara that October, and the couple spent their honeymoon in the rose-covered cottage. Sara is addressed throughout but never speaks; her 'more serious eye' finally rebukes the speaker's speculations. The marriage later failed, which colours biographical readings of the domestic peace the poem celebrates.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; Richard Holmes, Coleridge: Early Visions (London, 1989), ch. on Clevedon, 1795; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Eolian Harp'

  3. Jasmin ... Myrtle ... emblems ll. 4-5

    In classical tradition the myrtle was Venus's plant and was used in wedding garlands, while white flowers such as jasmine stood for purity. 'Meet' means fitting. The parenthesis, like the later '(such should wisdom be)' of the evening star, shows the young poet moralising the landscape as he describes it, a habit the poem's ending will question. The scents 'Snatched from yon bean-field' and the hushed world complete a scene of domestic and natural harmony.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Eolian Harp', text and notes

  4. the star of eve / Serenely brilliant ll. 7-9

    As the clouds lose the sunset light, the evening star appears; Coleridge's parenthesis '(such should wisdom be)' compares its calm brilliance to the ideal of wisdom, serene rather than flashy. The detail also places the poem precisely at dusk. Venus as evening star is the planet of love, linking with the myrtle of l. 4. The small moral asides of the opening paragraph show the poet already turning sense-impressions into meanings, a habit the poem pursues on a grand scale in the harp passages and then rebukes at the end.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Eolian Harp', text and notes

  5. The stilly murmur of the distant sea / Tells us of silence ll. 11-12

    'Stilly' is a poetic adjective meaning quiet or calm. The paradox is that the faint sound makes the surrounding silence perceptible, a sensory subtlety typical of the conversation poems. The line is left incomplete and the verse paragraph breaks, so that the page itself gives a silence before the harp is introduced. Coleridge's later poems repeatedly use such natural sounds at the edge of hearing, as in 'Frost at Midnight'.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; George McLean Harper, 'Coleridge's Conversation Poems', Quarterly Review 244 (1925); repr. in Spirit of Delight (1928)

  6. Like some coy maid half yielding to her lover ll. 16-18

    The harp's response to the wind is imagined as a courtship: the 'desultory' (fitful, wandering) breeze caresses it, and the strings reproach it so sweetly as to tempt a repetition of 'the wrong'. In a poem addressed to a new bride, the image is playful and sensuous. Critics note that it prepares the passive model of the mind in the later sections, in which thoughts 'Traverse my indolent and passive brain' as the breeze moves the strings.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Eolian Harp'

  7. sequacious l. 19

    From Latin sequax, 'inclined to follow', the word appears in Dryden's 'Song for St Cecilia's Day' (1687), where trees uprooted 'sequacious of the lyre' follow Orpheus. Coleridge's notes follow one another in continuous succession, and 'Over delicious surges sink and rise' gives them the movement of the sea. The passage then turns to fairy imagery, elfins voyaging on gales from Fairy-Land, and to birds of paradise.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sequacious'; John Dryden, 'A Song for St Cecilia's Day' (1687)

  8. Footless and wild, like birds of Paradise ll. 24-25

    Traders in New Guinea removed the feet of the skins sent to Europe, which gave rise to the belief that the birds were footless, lived on air and dew, and never alighted; Linnaeus named one species Paradisaea apoda, 'footless'. Coleridge's melodies, like the birds, never 'pause, nor perch' but hover 'on untamed wing'. The image of perpetual, unsettled motion suits both the harp's music and the speculative mind that the end of the poem will check.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bird of paradise'

  9. O! the one life within us and abroad ll. 27-33

    The passage first appeared in the errata to Sibylline Leaves (1817); the earlier texts went straight from the birds of paradise to the speaker on the hillside. It states the vision of a single life in mind and world, a 'light in sound' and 'sound-like power in light', which Abrams related to contemporary physics of light and sound and to Coleridge's later philosophy. Because the lines are late, critics debate their effect: they make the pantheistic idea more joyous and more central, and so make Sara's rebuke at the end harder to accept as final. Students should cite the poem as revised in 1817 and note the addition.

    S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), 'The Eolian Harp', with errata; M. H. Abrams, 'Coleridge's "A Light in Sound": Science, Metascience, and Poetic Imagination', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 116 (1972); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Eolian Harp'

  10. my indolent and passive brain ll. 39-43

    In 1795 Coleridge was an admirer of David Hartley, whose Observations on Man (1749) explained thought as the association of sensations transmitted by vibrations in the nerves; he named his first son Hartley in 1796. The image of the brain as a 'subject lute' (an instrument subject to the wind) fits this mechanistic psychology. Coleridge's later criticism, especially in Biographia Literaria (1817), rejected the passive model in favour of an active imagination, so the passage is often read as recording a position he outgrew.

    David Hartley, Observations on Man (1749); S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), chs. 5-7 on Hartley; M. H. Abrams, 'Coleridge's "A Light in Sound": Science, Metascience, and Poetic Imagination', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 116 (1972)

  11. organic harps diversely framed ll. 44-48

    The hypothetical 'what if' extends the harp image to all 'animated nature'. 'Organic' here means having organs, living. The 'intellectual breeze' that sweeps over them is a single universal mind, 'At once the Soul of each, and God of all'. The idea comes close to pantheism, and editors have compared Spinoza and the Neoplatonists as well as Priestley's Unitarian thought, which Coleridge then shared. The question form keeps the speculation tentative, but it is exactly the kind of thought Sara's look rebukes.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; M. H. Abrams, 'Coleridge's "A Light in Sound": Science, Metascience, and Poetic Imagination', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 116 (1972); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Eolian Harp'

  12. Plastic l. 48

    The word does not mean malleable here but actively forming. The Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth, in The True Intellectual System of the Universe (1678), argued for a 'plastic nature', a power through which God shapes the world without intervening at every point; Coleridge read Cudworth closely in the mid-1790s, and Bristol Library borrowing records are usually cited for this. The breeze thus shapes the harps it plays, and the passage blends Cudworth's Platonism with the more materialist necessitarianism of Hartley and Priestley.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'plastic'; Ralph Cudworth, The True Intellectual System of the Universe (1678), I.iii; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes

  13. biddest me walk humbly with my God ll. 49-53

    Micah 6:8 asks what the LORD requires but 'to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God'. Sara, 'Meek daughter in the family of Christ', speaks only through her eye and through the scriptural phrase the speaker gives her. The poet now calls his ideas 'shapings of the unregenerate mind', 'Bubbles' on the spring of 'vain Philosophy'. Critics disagree about the retraction. Some take it as sincere piety; others see it as social and domestic deference that does not refute the vision, or as a sign of Coleridge's lifelong struggle between speculative philosophy and Christian faith. The 1817 addition of the 'one life' lines intensified this tension.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Micah 6:8; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Eolian Harp'; M. H. Abrams, 'Coleridge's "A Light in Sound": Science, Metascience, and Poetic Imagination', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 116 (1972)

  14. The Incomprehensible l. 60

    The Creed of St Athanasius, appointed to be said in the Church of England's Book of Common Prayer, declares the Father, Son and Holy Ghost 'incomprehensible', in the older sense of 'not to be contained or limited'. Coleridge, then a Unitarian who rejected the Trinity, nonetheless uses its language to rebuke his own speculation: God can be spoken of only 'with awe' and with 'Faith that inly feels'. The closing lines turn to personal salvation and domestic thanks, 'Peace, and this cot, and thee, dear honoured Maid!', returning to the cottage of the opening.

    The Book of Common Prayer (1662), Athanasian Creed ('Quicunque vult'); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes

  15. Conversation poem whole poem

    The term 'conversation poem' was popularised by George McLean Harper in 1925, from Coleridge's subtitle for 'The Nightingale: A Conversation Poem' (1798). The group includes 'This Lime-Tree Bower', 'Frost at Midnight' and 'Fears in Solitude'. Typically the poem begins in a quiet present scene, rises through meditation to a large speculative or visionary moment, and returns to the scene and the addressee. 'The Eolian Harp' shows the pattern clearly, from the cottage to the 'one intellectual breeze' and back to 'this cot'. The informal syntax, with exclamations and parentheses, is part of the effect.

    George McLean Harper, 'Coleridge's Conversation Poems', Quarterly Review 244 (1925); repr. in Spirit of Delight (1928); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. I, 'The Eolian Harp', textual notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Eolian Harp', text and notes
