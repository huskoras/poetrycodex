Kubla Khan

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

Arts & SciencesMythology & FolkloreThe Mind

In Xanadu did Kubla Khan
A stately pleasure-dome decree:
Where Alph, the sacred river, ran
Through caverns measureless to man
Down to a sunless sea.
So twice five miles of fertile ground
With walls and towers were girdled round;
And there were gardens bright with sinuous rills,
Where blossomed many an incense-bearing tree;
And here were forests ancient as the hills,
Enfolding sunny spots of greenery.

But oh! that deep romantic chasm which slanted
Down the green hill athwart a cedarn cover!
A savage place! as holy and enchanted
As e’er beneath a waning moon was haunted
By woman wailing for her demon-lover!
And from this chasm, with ceaseless turmoil seething,
As if this earth in fast thick pants were breathing,
A mighty fountain momently was forced:
Amid whose swift half-intermitted burst
Huge fragments vaulted like rebounding hail,
Or chaffy grain beneath the thresher’s flail:
And ’mid these dancing rocks at once and ever
It flung up momently the sacred river.
Five miles meandering with a mazy motion
Through wood and dale the sacred river ran,
Then reached the caverns measureless to man,
And sank in tumult to a lifeless ocean;
And ’mid this tumult Kubla heard from far
Ancestral voices prophesying war!
The shadow of the dome of pleasure
Floated midway on the waves;
Where was heard the mingled measure
From the fountain and the caves.
It was a miracle of rare device,
A sunny pleasure-dome with caves of ice!

A damsel with a dulcimer
In a vision once I saw:
It was an Abyssinian maid,
And on her dulcimer she played,
Singing of Mount Abora.
Could I revive within me
Her symphony and song,
To such a deep delight ’twould win me,
That with music loud and long,
I would build that dome in air,
That sunny dome! those caves of ice!
And all who heard should see them there,
And all should cry, Beware! Beware!
His flashing eyes, his floating hair!
Weave a circle round him thrice,
And close your eyes with holy dread,
For he on honey-dew hath fed,
And drunk the milk of Paradise.

Codex Note

Composed, Coleridge claimed, in an opium dream and broken off by a knock at the door, the poem is a fragment about its own visionary power — the ‘pleasure-dome’ a monument to an imagination that can never quite be rebuilt in waking words.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and the 1816 preface l. 38; subtitle

    Coleridge's prefatory note says that in summer 1797 he was staying at a lonely farmhouse between Porlock and Linton, took an anodyne, and fell asleep while reading in Purchas's Pilgrimage. He believed he composed two to three hundred lines in sleep, began writing them down on waking, and was then called away by a visitor from Porlock for over an hour, after which he remembered only scattered lines. The poem was printed in 1816 with 'Christabel' and 'The Pains of Sleep', with the subtitle 'A Vision in a Dream'. The date is disputed: the Crewe manuscript says autumn 1797; Elisabeth Schneider argued for a later date, and some editors favour a date as late as spring 1798. Many scholars treat the preface as partly a literary fiction that frames the poem as a fragment.

    S. T. Coleridge, Christabel; Kubla Khan, a Vision; The Pains of Sleep (1816), prefatory note to 'Kubla Khan'; Elisabeth Schneider, Coleridge, Opium and Kubla Khan (Chicago, 1953); J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  2. Xanadu l. 1

    Shangdu ('Upper Capital') was built from 1256 by Kublai and described by Marco Polo, whose account Samuel Purchas adapted. Purchas spelled it 'Xamdu'; Coleridge's 'Xanadu' gives it three syllables and an exotic music. Through the poem's fame the name has come to mean any place of magnificent luxury. Lowes traced how Coleridge's reading in travel literature, above all Purchas, supplied the poem's geography.

    Samuel Purchas, Purchas his Pilgrimage (1613; 3rd edn 1617), bk 4, p. 472 of the 1617 edition, on Cublai Can and Xamdu; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; The Travels of Marco Polo, bk 1, on Chandu (Shangdu)

  3. Kubla Khan l. 1

    Kublai ruled the Mongol empire from 1260 and conquered Song China, establishing the Yuan dynasty. To Europeans he was known chiefly through Marco Polo, who served at his court. In the poem he is an emperor-creator who can 'decree' a paradise into existence, a figure for absolute power and for artistic creation; the second half sets the inspired poet beside him. 'Khan' means ruler. The inverted syntax ('did Kubla Khan... decree') gives the opening a ceremonial, incantatory weight.

    Samuel Purchas, Purchas his Pilgrimage (1613; 3rd edn 1617), bk 4, p. 472 of the 1617 edition, on Cublai Can and Xamdu; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4

  4. stately pleasure-dome decree l. 2

    The preface quotes Purchas: 'Here the Khan Kubla commanded a palace to be built, and a stately garden thereunto'. Purchas also mentions a moveable 'house of pleasure'. 'Decree' implies creation by word alone, like God's fiat in Genesis. The compound 'pleasure-dome' fuses sensual delight and architectural grandeur, and the dome becomes the poem's central symbol, later rebuilt 'in air' by the poet's song. Critics have linked the walled garden to accounts of earthly paradise in Milton's Eden (Paradise Lost 4).

    S. T. Coleridge, Christabel; Kubla Khan, a Vision; The Pains of Sleep (1816), prefatory note to 'Kubla Khan'; Samuel Purchas, Purchas his Pilgrimage (1613; 3rd edn 1617), bk 4, p. 472 of the 1617 edition, on Cublai Can and Xamdu; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'

  5. Alph, the sacred river l. 3

    In classical myth the river Alpheus in the Peloponnese pursued the nymph Arethusa under the sea and resurfaced in the fountain of Arethusa at Syracuse (Virgil, Aeneid 3.694-96; Ovid, Metamorphoses 5; Pausanias). Lowes connected Coleridge's river, sinking into 'caverns measureless to man' and bursting up as a fountain, with that story and with travellers' accounts of the Nile's hidden sources. The name also suggests 'alpha', the first letter, and so a primal source of life or language. 'Sacred' marks the river as a divine or visionary force.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Virgil, Aeneid 3.692-96; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of S. T. Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  6. twice five miles of fertile ground ll. 6-7

    Purchas's account describes Kubla's park as many miles of fertile ground enclosed by a wall, with meadows, springs and streams. Coleridge's preface paraphrases it as 'ten miles of fertile ground were inclosed with a wall'. The odd arithmetic of 'twice five' has an incantatory, ritual quality. The enclosure ('girdled round') sets the ordered, artificial garden against the 'savage' chasm that follows, a contrast critics read as order versus energy, or art versus wild nature.

    S. T. Coleridge, Christabel; Kubla Khan, a Vision; The Pains of Sleep (1816), prefatory note to 'Kubla Khan'; Samuel Purchas, Purchas his Pilgrimage (1613; 3rd edn 1617), bk 4, p. 472 of the 1617 edition, on Cublai Can and Xamdu; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'

  7. that deep romantic chasm ll. 12-13

    'Romantic' in the 1790s meant wild, picturesque, like the scenery of romances. 'Athwart' means across, slantwise, and 'cedarn' means of cedar trees, a Miltonic adjective (Comus has 'cedarn alleys'). The exclamation 'But oh!' marks a sudden shift of tone from the stately description to excitement. Many critics read the chasm and the fountain as images of creative energy breaking out from below the controlled surface of the mind; some, especially in psychoanalytic criticism, read the chasm as sexually charged.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'romantic, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'athwart, adv. and prep.'; Milton, Comus (A Masque), l. 990; Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4

  8. woman wailing for her demon-lover ll. 14-16

    The simile draws on the ballad tradition of the demon lover, a revenant or devil who carries a woman away (compare the ballad 'The Daemon Lover', Child 243) and on Gothic fiction of the 1790s. The 'waning moon' adds an ominous, magical atmosphere. 'Holy and enchanted' sets sacred and demonic side by side. The line makes the chasm a site of desire, loss and supernatural power, and readers relate it to the poem's later figures of the Abyssinian maid and the frenzied poet.

    F. J. Child, The English and Scottish Popular Ballads, no. 243, 'James Harris (The Daemon Lover)'; Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  9. mighty fountain momently forced ll. 17-24

    'Momently' means from moment to moment, continually. The earth is imagined as breathing in 'fast thick pants', a bodily, almost sexual image of labour or exertion. The rocks vault 'like rebounding hail, / Or chaffy grain beneath the thresher's flail', homely similes from farming set against the exotic setting; a flail is a hand tool for beating grain from the husk. Lowes traced the details to travel accounts of springs and geysers, especially Bartram's Travels on Florida springs. Many readers take the eruption as a figure for inspiration.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'momently, adv.'; William Bartram, Travels through North and South Carolina, Georgia, East and West Florida (1791)

  10. Five miles meandering with a mazy motion l. 25

    The line is a famous example of sound echoing sense: 'meandering', 'mazy', 'motion' and 'miles' slow the rhythm after the violent fountain. 'Meander' comes from the Maeander, a winding river in Asia Minor (Greek Maiandros), so the word itself carries a classical river. 'Mazy' means labyrinthine. Coleridge's verse throughout mixes tetrameter and pentameter, with irregular rhyme, giving the effect of an incantation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'meander, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mazy, adj.'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  11. sank in tumult to a lifeless ocean ll. 26-28

    The river's course from source to underground sea has been read as an allegory of life (birth, turbulent youth, meandering maturity, death) or of the creative process, rising from the unconscious and returning to it. 'Sunless' (l. 5) and 'lifeless' frame the garden's sunny spots in darkness. The 'caverns measureless to man' mark limits of human knowledge. Readers should be cautious with neat allegories: Coleridge called the poem a psychological curiosity rather than a work of poetic merit (1816 preface).

    S. T. Coleridge, Christabel; Kubla Khan, a Vision; The Pains of Sleep (1816), prefatory note to 'Kubla Khan'; Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'

  12. Ancestral voices prophesying war ll. 29-30

    The line introduces history and danger into the dream-paradise. Historically, Kublai's reign was full of war, including failed invasions of Japan, and his ancestry led back to Genghis Khan. Critics read the voices in many ways: as the inevitable violence of empire, as a hint of the political anxieties of the 1790s (Britain was at war with France), or as a reminder that every paradise is threatened. The line is notably unexplained, and Coleridge never returns to it, which supports readings of the poem as a series of juxtaposed images.

    Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'

  13. a miracle of rare device... caves of ice ll. 31-36

    'Device' means design or contrivance, and 'rare' means exceptional. The shadow 'floated midway on the waves' and the 'mingled measure' of fountain and caves suggest a harmony of opposites, warmth and cold, light and dark, pleasure and danger. Coleridge's later theory defined the imagination as the power that reconciles 'opposite or discordant qualities' (Biographia Literaria ch. 14), and critics often read the sunny dome with caves of ice as an emblem of that power. Lowes found sources for ice caverns in travel accounts.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 14; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'device, n.'

  14. a damsel with a dulcimer... an Abyssinian maid ll. 37-39

    Abyssinia is the old name for Ethiopia. Coleridge had read James Bruce's Travels to Discover the Source of the Nile (1790), which described Abyssinia, and Lowes traced connections to it. A dulcimer is a stringed instrument struck with small hammers, though the name is also used in the Bible for an instrument in Nebuchadnezzar's orchestra (Daniel 3:5). The new stanza changes from third to first person and from description to desire, so the maid becomes the source of the song the poet longs to revive.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Bible (KJV), Daniel 3:5; James Bruce, Travels to Discover the Source of the Nile (1790); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dulcimer, n.'

  15. Mount Abora l. 41

    In Paradise Lost 4.280-84 Milton lists rival paradises, including 'Mount Amara', where Abyssinian kings guarded their heirs, 'by some supposed / True Paradise under the Ethiop line'. The Crewe manuscript of the poem gives a form close to Milton's name, and editors think Coleridge altered it to 'Abora' in revision, perhaps for sound or to avoid a direct allusion. The maid thus sings of a lost paradise, which links her song to Kubla's garden. Lowes also discussed river names in Bruce's Abyssinia as possible sources.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 4.280-84; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  16. Could I revive within me ll. 42-47

    The conditional 'Could I' (if I could) admits that he cannot: the vision is lost, like the interrupted dream of the preface. 'Symphony' (Greek symphonia, sounding together) means harmony. The idea that music can build structures has classical precedent: Amphion raised the walls of Thebes by playing the lyre. The stanza turns the poem into a meditation on inspiration and its loss, which is why many critics read the whole as a poem about poetry.

    Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'symphony, n.'; Horace, Ars Poetica 394-96 (Amphion)

  17. build that dome in air l. 46

    The poet becomes a rival to Kubla: the emperor built by decree, the poet would build by music, 'in air', a phrase that suggests both the medium of sound and something insubstantial, a castle in the air. 'All who heard should see them there' fuses hearing and sight, a synaesthetic claim for poetry's power. The repetition of 'That sunny dome! those caves of ice!' echoes the end of the first section, binding the two halves of the poem.

    Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  18. Beware! His flashing eyes, his floating hair! ll. 49-50

    The image recalls the classical idea of poetic frenzy (furor poeticus), in which the poet is possessed by a god. Plato's Ion (533e-534b) compares inspired poets to Bacchic worshippers out of their senses. 'Flashing eyes' and 'floating hair' are the signs of the bard, a figure also prominent in Gray's 'The Bard' (1757), whose prophet has hair streaming 'like a meteor'. The listeners' fear expresses the double nature of inspiration, both holy and threatening.

    Plato, Ion 533e-534b; Thomas Gray, 'The Bard' (1757), ll. 19-20; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'

  19. Weave a circle round him thrice l. 51

    Drawing a circle three times around a person is a traditional act of magic or exorcism, meant to bind a spirit. Compare the witches' chant in Macbeth: 'Thrice to thine, and thrice to mine, / And thrice again, to make up nine' (1.3). 'Close your eyes with holy dread' indicates awe before something sacred that must not be looked at. The ritual sets the poet apart from the community, sanctified and dangerous.

    Shakespeare, Macbeth 1.3.35-37; Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'

  20. honey-dew... milk of Paradise ll. 53-54

    Plato's Ion (534a) says inspired poets are like Bacchants who draw milk and honey from rivers when possessed, but not when in their right minds; Lowes and later editors cite the passage. The Bible's promised land flows 'with milk and honey' (Exodus 3:8), and manna tasted 'like wafers made with honey' (Exodus 16:31). 'Honey-dew' is also a real sweet deposit on leaves, once thought to fall from heaven. Some readers also connect these lines with opium. The poem ends on 'Paradise', returning to the theme of lost paradise.

    Plato, Ion 534a; Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:8; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston, 1927), chs. on 'Kubla Khan'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'honeydew, n.'

  21. The Crewe manuscript l. 47

    The Crewe manuscript, given to Mrs Crewe and given by the Marquess of Crewe to the British Museum in 1962 and now British Library Add MS 50847, ends with a note saying the poem, with much more not recoverable, was composed in a sort of reverie brought on by two grains of opium taken to check a dysentery, at a farmhouse near Culbone Church, in the fall of 1797. It does not mention the visitor from Porlock. Its readings include a form close to Milton's 'Amara' for 'Abora'. Scholars use it to question the 1816 preface's account and to date the poem.

    J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'; Elisabeth Schneider, Coleridge, Opium and Kubla Khan (Chicago, 1953); Representative Poetry Online, 'Kubla Khan', editorial note (eds. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof)

  22. Fragment or complete poem? l. 36

    The first 36 lines describe Kubla's creation; the last 18 describe the poet's longing to recreate it, ending in a vision of the inspired bard. Humphry House argued in 1953 that the poem is a complete whole about poetic creation, its ending an achieved conditional rather than a failure. Others, following the preface, read it as a true fragment whose incompleteness is part of its meaning. Lines 35-36, a rhymed couplet summing up the dome, close the first part neatly. The varying line lengths and the indentation of shorter lines in printed texts mark the shifting music of the verse.

    Humphry House, Coleridge: The Clark Lectures 1951-52 (London, 1953), ch. 4; S. T. Coleridge, Christabel; Kubla Khan, a Vision; The Pains of Sleep (1816), prefatory note to 'Kubla Khan'; Elisabeth Schneider, Coleridge, Opium and Kubla Khan (Chicago, 1953); J. C. C. Mays, ed., The Collected Works of S. T. Coleridge: Poetical Works (Princeton, 2001), notes to 'Kubla Khan'
