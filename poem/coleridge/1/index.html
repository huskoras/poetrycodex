Nature

Work Without Hope

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

NatureLivingThe Mind

All Nature seems at work. Slugs leave their lair—
The bees are stirring—birds are on the wing—
And Winter slumbering in the open air,
Wears on his smiling face a dream of Spring!
And I the while, the sole unbusy thing,
Nor honey make, nor pair, nor build, nor sing.

Yet well I ken the banks where amaranths blow,
Have traced the fount whence streams of nectar flow.
Bloom, O ye amaranths! bloom for whom ye may,
For me ye bloom not! Glide, rich streams, away!
With lips unbrightened, wreathless brow, I stroll:
And would you learn the spells that drowse my soul?
Work without Hope draws nectar in a sieve,
And Hope without an object cannot live.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Everything in nature is busy — bees, birds, slugs — while the poet alone stands idle. The sonnet’s ache is that hope and labour need each other: ‘Work without Hope draws nectar in a sieve.’

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and publication l. 1; title

    The poem's subtitle in collected editions, 'Lines composed 21st February 1825', gives the date of composition; Coleridge was then 52, living with the Gillmans at Highgate. It was first printed in 1828, in the annual The Bijou (under the title 'Lines Composed on a Day in February') and in the Poetical Works of that year. The RPO editors note the irony that 1825 was also the year of Aids to Reflection, the prose book that founded his reputation in America. The title, which states the conclusion in advance, came later.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Work without Hope', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof; The Bijou; or Annual of Literature and the Arts (London: Pickering, 1828), 'Lines Composed on a Day in February' (Poetess Archive text); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. 1, 'Work without Hope' and notes; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  2. Slugs, bees, birds ll. 1-2

    The opening is deliberately unpoetic: slugs come first, then bees, then birds, as if the speaker were reporting the first stirrings of a mild February. 'Lair' is usually used of wild beasts, so applying it to slugs is gently mock-heroic. The dashes give the lines a breathless, notebook-like quality. All these creatures are doing something purposive, unlike the speaker. The plain, almost prosaic diction is typical of Coleridge's late 'conversational' manner.

    J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. 1, 'Work without Hope' and notes

  3. Winter dreaming of Spring ll. 3-4

    The personification is paradoxical. Winter is 'slumbering', yet he too is part of the busy scene, for his sleeping face 'wears' a smile because he dreams of Spring. The image makes even inactivity hopeful, which sharpens the contrast with the speaker, whose idleness has no such dream. The phrase 'in the open air' adds the sense of an unusually mild late-winter day.

    J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. 1, 'Work without Hope' and notes

  4. 'the sole unbusy thing' ll. 5-6

    Line 6 answers the catalogue of lines 1-2 point by point: honey (bees), pairing and building (birds), singing (birds, and poets). 'Nor ... nor ... nor' is an emphatic negative series in the older manner. 'Sing' carries the poet's own vocation: Coleridge had long lamented his failure to complete major poetic work. The poem has been compared with George Herbert's 'Employment (I)', where the speaker complains 'All things are busie; onely I / Neither bring hony with the bees'; Coleridge admired Herbert, and the parallel is close.

    George Herbert, 'Employment (I)', in The Works of George Herbert, ed. F. E. Hutchinson (Oxford, 1941); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  5. Ken l. 7

    'Ken' as a verb, from Old English cennan and Old Norse kenna, survived in Scots and northern dialect, and in literary English as an archaism. The 'Yet' that opens the second section turns from idleness to knowledge: the speaker knows perfectly well where beauty and inspiration are to be found, but that knowledge is useless to him. The choice of an archaic word also gives the line the ring of ballad or proverb, anticipating the gnomic couplet at the end.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ken'; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. 1, 'Work without Hope' and notes

  6. Amaranths l. 7; l. 9

    Greek amarantos means 'unfading'. In poetic tradition the amaranth is the flower of immortality; Milton describes it as once blooming in Eden near the tree of life and then removed to Heaven after the Fall, where the angels weave it into crowns (Paradise Lost 3.352-59). 'Blow' means bloom. The amaranths and the 'streams of nectar' (the gods' drink) stand for imperishable beauty, poetic fame, and perhaps love, all of which the speaker knows but cannot share: 'For me ye bloom not!'

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (1667), 3.352-59 (the 'immortal amarant'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'amaranth'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  7. Fount of nectar l. 8

    In Greek myth nectar and ambrosia are the food and drink of the gods, which confer immortality. A 'fount' of nectar suggests also the Muses' springs, Hippocrene and Castalia, sources of poetic inspiration. The speaker has 'traced' the fountain to its source, so the failure is not ignorance; it is exclusion. The word 'nectar' returns in line 13 in the sieve image, linking the two halves of the poem.

    Homer, Iliad 5.339-42 (the gods eat no bread and drink no wine but are immortal); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  8. Unbrightened lips, wreathless brow l. 11

    Wreaths of laurel crowned victorious poets in classical tradition, and garlands of flowers crowned lovers and revellers; amaranth itself was woven into heavenly crowns in Milton. The speaker's brow is bare, his lips 'unbrightened' (unlit by smile or song, perhaps by kisses). He merely 'strolls', a low-key verb of aimless walking. The line gathers the negations of line 6 into a self-portrait: neither lover nor laurelled poet, the speaker is simply unoccupied, wandering without a goal.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (1667), 3.352-59 (the 'immortal amarant'); J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  9. Spells that drowse l. 12

    Using 'drowse' as a transitive verb ('to make drowsy') is unusual and slightly archaic. 'Spells' suggests enchantment, but the answer given in the final couplet is not magic but moral psychology. Readers often connect the line with Coleridge's long struggle with opium addiction and depression, and with the 'Dejection' ode (1802), where he describes a 'grief without a pang'. The question form makes the couplet that follows sound like a proverb or moral.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'drowse'; S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802), l. 21; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary

  10. Nectar in a sieve l. 13

    Carrying water in a sieve is a proverbial image of useless labour. In classical myth the Danaids, daughters of Danaus who murdered their husbands, were condemned in the underworld to fill a leaking vessel forever; Plato's Gorgias (493b) uses the leaky jar and sieve as an image of the insatiable soul. Coleridge's version gives the image his own key word: what runs away is 'nectar', the very sweetness the speaker longs for. The line is the poem's best-known, and its phrase gave Kamala Markandaya the title of her novel Nectar in a Sieve (1954), which carries the couplet as its epigraph.

    Plato, Gorgias 493a-c; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sieve'

  11. Hope without an object l. 14

    The couplet is a chiasmus of ideas: Work without Hope / Hope without an object. Biographical readers have connected the missing 'object' with Coleridge's long, unrequited love for Sara Hutchinson, though the poem itself leaves it general. Psychologically the claim is that hope cannot sustain itself on nothing; it must be directed at a person, goal or good. Some readers find the ending despairing, others a lucid diagnosis that implies its own remedy.

    J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), vol. 1, 'Work without Hope' and notes; Representative Poetry Online, 'Work without Hope', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof

  12. Form: an inverted sonnet ll. 1-14

    The poem is a sonnet only loosely. Its fourteen lines fall into a first section of six lines (rhymed ababbb) describing nature's work and the speaker's idleness, and a second of eight lines in couplets. Some critics describe it as an inverted or reversed sonnet, with the sestet before the octave; the scheme is ababbbccddeeff, as RPO prints it. The series of couplets makes the end sound increasingly epigrammatic, culminating in the proverb-like final pair.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Work without Hope', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), poem and commentary
