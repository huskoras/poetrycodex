Nature

This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

From Poems of Coleridge, selected by Arthur Symons (1905)

Nature

Well, they are gone, and here must I remain,
This lime-tree bower my prison! I have lost
Beauties and feelings, such as would have been
Most sweet to my remembrance even when age
Had dimmed mine eyes to blindness! They, meanwhile,
Friends, whom I never more may meet again,
On springy heath, along the hill-top edge,
Wander in gladness, and wind down, perchance,
To that still roaring dell, of which I told;
The roaring dell, o'erwooded, narrow, deep,
And only speckled by the mid-day sun;
Where its slim trunk the ash from rock to rock
Flings arching like a bridge--that branchless ash,
Unsunned and damp, whose few poor yellow-leaves
Ne'er tremble in the gale, yet tremble still,
Fanned by the water-fall! and there my friends
Behold the dark green file of long lank weeds,
That all at once (a most fantastic sight!)
Still nod and drip beneath the dripping edge
Of the blue clay-stone.

Now, my friends emerge
Beneath the wide wide Heaven--and view again
The many-steepled tract magnificent
Of hilly fields and meadows, and the sea,
With some fair bark, perhaps, whose sails light up
The slip of smooth clear blue betwixt two Isles
Of purple shadow! Yes! they wander on
In gladness all; but thou, me thinks, most glad,
My gentle-hearted Charles! for thou hast pined
And hungered after Nature, many a year,
In the great City pent, winning thy way
With sad yet patient soul, through evil and pain
And strange calamity! Ah! slowly sink
Behind the western ridge, thou glorious Sun!
Shine in the slant beams of the sinking orb,
Ye purple heath-flowers! richlier burn, ye clouds
Live in the yellow light, ye distant groves!
And kindle, thou blue Ocean! So my friend
Struck with deep joy may stand, as I have stood,
Silent with swimming sense; yea, gazing round
On the wide landscape, gaze till all doth seem
Less gross than bodily; and of such hues
As veil the Almighty Spirit, when yet he makes
Spirits perceive his presence.

A delight
Comes sudden on my heart, and I am glad
As I myself were there! Nor in this bower,
This little lime-tree bower, have I not marked
Much that has soothed me. Pale beneath the blaze
Hung the transparent foliage; and I watched
Some broad and sunny leaf, and loved to see
The shadow of the leaf and stem above,
Dappling its sunshine! And that walnut-tree
Was richly tinged, and a deep radiance lay
Full on the ancient ivy, which usurps
Those fronting elms, and now, with blackest mass--
Makes their dark branches gleam a lighter hue
Through the late twilight: and though now the bat
Wheels silent by, and not a swallow twitters,
Yet still the solitary humble-bee
Sings in the bean-flower! Henceforth I shall know
That Nature ne'er deserts the wise and pure;
No plot so narrow, be but Nature there,
No waste so vacant, but. may well employ
Each faculty of sense, and keep the heart.
Awake to Love and Beauty! and sometimes
'Tis well to be bereft of promised good,
That we may lift the soul, and contemplate
With lively joy the joys we cannot share.
My gentle-hearted Charles! when the last rook
Beat its straight path along the dusky air
Homewards, I blest it! deeming, its black wing
(Now a dim speck, now vanishing in light)
Had cross'd the mighty orb's dilated glory,
While thou stood'st gazing; or when all was still,
Flew creeking o'er thy head, and had a charm
For thee, my gentle-hearted Charles, to whom
No sound is dissonant which tells of Life.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The occasion (summer 1797) l. 1

    Coleridge's headnote says that 'long-expected friends' arrived in June 1797 and that an accident on the morning of their arrival disabled him from walking. A letter to Southey of July 1797 is more specific: his wife Sara had spilt a skillet of boiling milk on his foot. The friends were Charles Lamb, down from London, and William and Dorothy Wordsworth, who were about to settle at Alfoxden a few miles away. Left behind, Coleridge composed the poem in a garden bower beside his cottage. A shorter draft went to Southey (the letter is now in the Morgan Library) and another to Charles Lloyd. The poem was first printed in Southey's Annual Anthology (1800) and revised for Sibylline Leaves (1817). The opening 'Well,' sets the casual, spoken tone of the 'conversation poems'.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'This Lime-tree Bower my Prison', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof, notes; Morgan Library & Museum, 'This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison: Coleridge in Isolation' (on the manuscript letter to Southey)

  2. lime-tree bower ... prison l. 2

    The lime-tree here is the linden, a common English shade tree. A 'bower' is a garden arbour, a place of retreat in pastoral and romance; to call it a 'prison' is half-humorous self-pity, and the poem's whole movement undoes the paradox. By line 48 the same place is 'This little lime-tree bower', in which he has 'marked / Much that has soothed me'. Physical confinement becomes imaginative freedom: he follows the walkers in his mind, and then finds that the narrow plot itself repays attention. The Annual Anthology title added 'A Poem Addressed to Charles Lamb, of the India House, London'; the words were dropped in later editions.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'lime'; Wikipedia, 'This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison' (summarizing Rosemary Ashton, Richard Holmes and others)

  3. springy l. 7

    In the manuscript letter to Southey Coleridge added the gloss 'Elastic, I mean': the heath gives under the walker's foot and springs back. The OED records 'springy' in this sense of elastic or resilient. The detail is imagined, not seen. Coleridge projects into the walkers' bodies a sensation he cannot share, which is the method of the poem's first movement (ll. 1-20), a guided tour conducted in absence. Note also 'Friends, whom I never more may meet again' (l. 6), a melodramatic touch that the poem's growing cheerfulness quietly corrects.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'springy'

  4. the roaring dell ll. 9-11

    'Of which I told' shows that Coleridge had described the place to his guests beforehand. 'Still' carries the older sense 'always, continually' (OED), so the dell is never silent; the next line repeats 'The roaring dell' as if savouring it. The adjectives 'o'erwooded, narrow, deep' and 'only speckled by the mid-day sun' make it a place of near darkness, which the poem contrasts with the open 'wide wide Heaven' of line 22. In the letter to Southey the dell was 'rifted' and many an ash 'Twists its wild limbs beside the ferny rock'; revision concentrates the scene into the single branchless ash.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'still'

  5. the branchless ash ll. 12-16

    The leaves 'Ne'er tremble in the gale, yet tremble still, / Fanned by the water-fall': a small paradox, since sheltered from the wind they move all the same. Readers have taken the tree as an emblem of the poet himself, cut off from the breezes the others enjoy yet still moved by a hidden source, and it anticipates the closing claim that no plot is so narrow that Nature is absent. Moving air as a figure for inspiration runs through Coleridge's poetry, from 'The Eolian Harp' to 'Dejection'; Abrams traced the pattern in his essay on the 'correspondent breeze'.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  6. long lank weeds ll. 17-20

    Coleridge's footnote names the plant as Asplenium scolopendrium, 'called in some countries the Adder's Tongue, in others the Hart's Tongue', adding that William Withering's botany reserves 'Adder's Tongue' for Ophioglossum. The fern's long strap-shaped fronds hang from the wet rock; the 'fantastic sight' is that they all nod and drip together 'beneath the dripping edge / Of the blue clay-stone' (the 1800 text had 'dim'). The care for correct naming shows how seriously Coleridge took exact natural observation.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'This Lime-tree Bower my Prison', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof, notes

  7. the wide wide Heaven l. 22

    The second verse paragraph opens with release: from the enclosed dell the friends climb out to a panoramic view of 'hilly fields and meadows, and the sea'. Doubling 'wide' imitates the sense of expansion. The same unusual repetition occurs in The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, written in the same months and printed in Lyrical Ballads (1798): 'Alone on a wide wide sea'. There immensity is desolating; here it is liberating. 'Many-steepled tract' (printed 'track' in 1800) evokes the church towers of the Somerset parishes seen from the hills; the sea is the Bristol Channel.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads (1798)

  8. My gentle-hearted Charles l. 29

    The epithet 'gentle-hearted' recurs three times (ll. 29, 70, 77) and annoyed its subject. In August 1800, after the poem appeared in print, Lamb begged Coleridge not to make him ridiculous 'by terming me gentle-hearted in print', since 'gentle' 'almost always means poor-spirited'. Late in life Coleridge wrote beside the poem in his 1834 Poetical Works that Charles and Mary Lamb were 'dear to my heart'. Addressing a named friend is a defining device of the conversation poems: the poem is meant to be overheard by Lamb as well as read by us.

    Charles Lamb to Coleridge, August 1800, in The Best Letters of Charles Lamb, ed. E. G. Johnson (1892); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; E. V. Lucas, The Life of Charles Lamb, vol. 2 (1905)

  9. In the great City pent l. 31

    Lamb, a London clerk, has 'pined / And hungered after Nature, many a year'. The phrase recalls Paradise Lost IX.445-51, where Satan, seeing Eve in the garden, is compared to one 'long in populous City pent' who goes out on a summer morning to breathe the air of the countryside. Coleridge borrows the townsman, not the menace, but the allusion gives Lamb's day in the hills the quality of an escape from confinement that matches Coleridge's own 'prison'. 'Frost at Midnight' (1798) uses the same word of Coleridge's London schooldays: 'pent 'mid cloisters dim'.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost, Book 9, ll. 445-51 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  10. strange calamity ll. 32-33

    In September 1796 Mary Lamb, suffering a mental breakdown, killed their mother with a knife. Charles, then twenty-one, undertook to be her guardian so that she would not be confined permanently to an asylum, and he looked after her for the rest of his life. Coleridge's 'through evil and pain / And strange calamity' alludes to this discreetly; the letter to Southey read 'sad yet bowed soul' where the printed text has 'sad yet patient soul'. Knowing the event gives weight to the wish that Lamb may stand 'Struck with deep joy' on the hill.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'This Lime-tree Bower my Prison', ed. Kathleen Coburn and R. S. Woof, notes; E. V. Lucas, The Life of Charles Lamb, vol. 2 (1905); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  11. the sunset invocation ll. 33-38

    Here the absent poet behaves almost like a celebrant, calling on the landscape: 'slowly sink', 'Shine', 'richlier burn', 'Live', 'kindle'. The verbs are those of fire and light, and the scene rises to a sunset that will leave Lamb 'Silent with swimming sense'. The rhetoric recalls the psalms' summons to sun, moon and all creation to praise God (Psalm 148). It is the climax of the imagined journey, after which (l. 45) the mind returns to the bower.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Bible, King James Version, Psalm 148

  12. Less gross than bodily ... veil the Almighty Spirit ll. 41-44

    In the letter to Southey the passage read 'a living thing / That acts upon the mind, and with such hues / As clothe th' Almighty Spirit', with the note 'You remember I am a Berkleyan'. George Berkeley argued that material things exist only in being perceived and that nature is a language in which God speaks to us; 'Frost at Midnight' calls nature 'that eternal language, which thy God / Utters'. The change from 'clothe' to 'veil' (made in the errata of Sibylline Leaves) turns the colours into a covering that hides God while signalling his presence. Readers differ on how pantheistic the passage is: the revised wording keeps God more clearly distinct from the landscape.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; George Berkeley, A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710); M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric' (1965), in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom

  13. the turn back to the bower ll. 45-47

    The conversation poems commonly follow an out-and-back pattern, from a present scene through memory or imagination and back to the scene with new understanding. M. H. Abrams made this the model of what he called the 'greater Romantic lyric', with this poem, 'Frost at Midnight' and 'Tintern Abbey' as examples. Here delight arrives 'As I myself were there', and with it Coleridge notices what he had overlooked: transparent lime leaves, shadows of leaf and stem, the walnut tree, the ivy. The label 'conversation poem' comes from Coleridge's subtitle for 'The Nightingale' (Lyrical Ballads, 1798).

    M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric' (1965), in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom; Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads (1798)

  14. the ivy that usurps the elms ll. 55-58

    'Usurps' is a political verb: the ivy has taken over the trees it climbs. Yet the effect is not sinister. The ivy's 'blackest mass' makes the elms' dark branches look lighter by contrast 'Through the late twilight'. The passage is one of several in which Coleridge records exact effects of light (dappling, transparency, gleam), the kind of observation found in his notebooks; critics such as Rosemary Ashton see the poem perfecting the plain, observant style begun in 'The Eolian Harp'.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison' (summarizing Rosemary Ashton, Richard Holmes and others)

  15. humble-bee ll. 60-61

    'Humble-bee' is the older English name of the bumble-bee (OED), from its humming. With the bat wheeling 'silent' and no swallow twittering, the bee's sound is the last sign of life in the garden, and it leads straight to the moral 'Henceforth I shall know'. Some readers take the solitary bee as a figure for the poet, alone but still singing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'humble-bee'; Wikipedia, 'This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison' (summarizing Rosemary Ashton, Richard Holmes and others)

  16. the lesson of the bower ll. 61-69

    'Henceforth I shall know' announces knowledge the poem has earned. The confidence that 'Nature ne'er deserts the wise and pure' anticipates Wordsworth's 'Nature never did betray / The heart that loved her' in 'Tintern Abbey' (1798), written a year later within the same friendship. The second lesson, that it is sometimes well 'to be bereft of promised good' in order to 'contemplate / With lively joy the joys we cannot share', makes the poem an exercise in disinterested sympathy. The stops in 'but. may' (l. 64) and 'keep the heart.' (l. 65) in this text look like misprints; the sense runs on: keep the heart awake to Love and Beauty.

    Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads (1798); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  17. the last rook ll. 70-75

    Coleridge imagines that the rook he sees crossing the sunset is the same bird Lamb sees, so a single creature joins the separated friends in one act of perception. The blessing recalls the turning point of the Ancient Mariner, written in the same months, where the Mariner blesses the water-snakes 'unaware'. 'The mighty orb's dilated glory' is the setting sun, apparently enlarged near the horizon; '(Now a dim speck, now vanishing in light)' records the bird disappearing into the glare.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads (1798)

  18. creeking l. 76

    'Creeking' is an old spelling of 'creaking' (OED). In a footnote Coleridge says that some months after writing the line he was pleased to find that William Bartram, in his Travels through North and South Carolina (1791), had noticed the same thing in the savanna crane, whose quill-feathers creak 'as the joints or working of a vessel in a tempestuous sea'. Bartram's Travels was a favourite book of Coleridge and Wordsworth in these years. The point is that even this harsh sound 'had a charm' for Lamb.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; William Bartram, Travels through North and South Carolina (1791) (Documenting the American South); Oxford English Dictionary, 'creak'

  19. No sound is dissonant l. 78

    The poem ends not on the poet but on his friend, crediting Lamb with the generous perception the poem has been learning. 'Dissonant' is a musical term, and the line resolves the harsh 'creeking' into harmony. The idea that all things share one life appears in 'The Eolian Harp', whose lines on 'the one Life within us and abroad' were added in 1817. The final return to the addressee and to the evening scene is the conversation poem's characteristic close, with the speaker's mind changed since the start. Lamb himself disliked being praised in these terms; see the note on 'gentle-hearted'.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric' (1965), in From Sensibility to Romanticism, ed. F. W. Hilles and H. Bloom
