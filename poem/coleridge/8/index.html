The Mind

Dejection: An Ode

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

From Poems of Coleridge, selected by Arthur Symons (1905)

The Mind

I

Well! If the Bard was weather-wise, who made
The grand old ballad of Sir Patrick Spence,
This night, so tranquil now, will not go hence
Unroused by winds, that ply a busier trade
Than those which mould yon cloud in lazy flakes,
Or the dull sobbing drafty that moans and rakes
Upon the strings of this Æolian lute,
Which better far were mute.
For lo! the New-moon winter-bright!
And overspread with phantom light,
(With swimming phantom light o'erspread
But rimmed and circled by a silver thread)
I see the old Moon in her lap, foretelling
The, coming-on of rain and squally blast.
And oh that even now the gust were swelling,
And the slant night-shower driving loud and fast!
Those sounds which oft have raised me, whilst they awed,
And sent my soul abroad,
Might now perhaps their wonted impulse give,
Might startle this dull pain, and make it move and live!

II

A grief without a pang, void, dark, and drear,
A stifled, drowsy, unimpassioned grief,
Which finds no natural outlet, no relief,
In word, or sigh, or tear--

O Lady! in this wan and heartless mood,
To other thoughts by yonder throstle woo'd,

All this long eve, so balmy and serene,
Have I been gazing on the western sky,
And its peculiar tint of yellow green:
And still I gaze--and with how blank an eye
And those thin clouds above, in flakes and bars,
That give away their motion to the stars;
Those stars, that glide behind them or between,
Now sparkling, now bedimmed, but always seen
Yon crescent Moon, as fixed as if it grew
In its own cloudless, starless lake of blue;
I see them all so excellently fair,
I see, not feel, how beautiful they are!

III

My genial spirits fail;
And what can these avail
To lift the smothering weight from off my breast?
It were a vain endeavour,
Though I should gaze for ever
On that green light that lingers in the west:
I may not hope from outward forms to win
The passion and the life, whose fountains are within.

IV

O Lady! we receive but what we give,
And in our life alone does Nature live:
Ours is her wedding-garment, ours her shroud!
And would we aught behold, of higher worth,
Than that inanimate cold world allowed
To the poor loveless, ever-anxious crowd,
Ah! from the soul itself must issue forth
A light, a glory, a fair luminous cloud--
Enveloping the Earth--
And from the soul itself must there be sent
A sweet and potent voice, of its own birth,
Of all sweet sounds the life and element!

V

O pure of heart! thou need'st not ask of me
What this strong music in the soul may be!
What, and wherein it doth exist,
This light, this glory, this fair luminous mist,
This beautiful and beauty-making power.
Joy, virtuous Lady! Joy that ne'er was given,
Save to the pure, and in their purest hour,
Life, and Life's effluence, cloud at once and shower,
Joy, Lady! is the spirit and the power,
Which wedding Nature to us gives in dower,
A new Earth and new Heaven,
Undreamt of by the sensual and the proud--
Joy is the sweet voice, Joy the luminous cloud--
We in ourselves rejoice!
And thence flows all that charms or ear or sight,
All melodies the echoes of that voice,
All colours a suffusion from that light.

VI

There was a time when, though my path was rough,
This joy within me dallied with distress,
And all misfortunes were but as the stuff
Whence Fancy made me dreams of happiness:
For hope grew round me, like the twining vine,
And fruits, and foliage, not my own, seemed mine.
But now afflictions bow me down to earth:
Nor care I that they rob me of my mirth
But oh! each visitation
Suspends what nature gave me at my birth,
My shaping spirit of Imagination.
For not to think of what I needs must feel,
But to be still and patient, all I can;
And haply by abstruse research to steal
From my own nature all the natural man--
This was my sole resource, my only plan:
Till that which suits a part infects the whole,
And now is almost grown the habit of my soul.

VII

Hence, viper thoughts, that coil around my mind,
Reality's dark dream!
I turn from you, and listen to the wind,
Which long has raved unnoticed. What a scream
Of agony by torture lengthened out
That lute sent forth! Thou Wind, that rav'st without,
Bare crag, or mountain-tairn, or blasted tree,
Or pine-grove whither woodman never clomb,
Or lonely house, long held the witches' home,
Methinks were fitter instruments for thee,
Mad Lutanist! who in this month of showers,
Of dark-brown gardens, and of peeping flowers,
Mak'st Devils' yule, with worse than wintry song,
The blossoms, buds, and timorous leaves among.
Thou Actor, perfect in all tragic sounds!
Thou mighty Poet, even to frenzy bold!
What tell'st thou now about?
'Tis of the rushing of an host in rout,
With groans of trampled men, with smarting wounds--
At once they groan with pain, and shudder with the cold!
But hush! there is a pause of deepest silence!
And all that noise, as of a rushing crowd,
With groans, and tremulous shudderings-all is over--
It tells another tale, with sounds less deep and loud!
A tale of less affright,
And tempered with delight,
As Otway's self had framed the tender lay,
'Tis of a little child
Upon a lonesome wild,
Not far from home, but she hath lost her way:
And now moans low in bitter grief and fear,
And now screams loud, and hopes to make her mother hear.

VIII

Tis midnight, but small thoughts have I of sleep:
Full seldom may my friend such vigils keep!
Visit her, gentle Sleep! with wings of healing,
And may this storm be but a mountain-birth,
May all the stars hang bright above her dwelling,
Silent as though they watched the sleeping Earth!
With light heart may she rise,
Gay fancy, cheerful eyes,
Joy lift her spirit, joy attune her voice;
To her may all things live, from pole to pole,
Their life the eddying of her living soul!
O simple spirit, guided from above,
Dear Lady! friend devoutest of my choice,
Thus mayest thou ever, evermore rejoice.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and publication st. I

    The original 'Letter to Sara Hutchinson', written at Keswick on 4 April 1802, poured out Coleridge's unhappy marriage, his hopeless love for Sara (sister of Wordsworth's future wife) and his sense of failing powers. He pared it down, changed the addressee more than once, and published it as 'Dejection: An Ode' in the Morning Post on 4 October 1802, the day of Wordsworth's wedding and the seventh anniversary of his own. It was reprinted in Sibylline Leaves (1817). Printed versions carry an epigraph from the ballad of Sir Patrick Spence, omitted in this text. The opening 'Well!' keeps the conversational voice of the earlier conversation poems even within the ode form. Richard Holmes prefers the ode as a work of art while finding the letter closer to Coleridge's inner life.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'Dejection: An Ode' (summarizing Richard Holmes and George Watson)

  2. Sir Patrick Spence st. I, l. 2

    In the ballad (first printed in Percy's Reliques, 1765; Child no. 58) the king sends Sir Patrick to sea in winter; a sailor warns that he saw the new moon with the old moon in her arms and fears a deadly storm, and the ship is lost. Coleridge's epigraph quotes these lines. The 'Bard' who made the ballad is 'weather-wise' because the sign was a traditional forecast of bad weather. The irony is that Coleridge hopes the omen is true: where the ballad's storm destroys, he wants a storm that may 'startle this dull pain, and make it move and live'.

    Wikipedia, 'Sir Patrick Spens' (Child 58; first printed in Percy's Reliques, 1765); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  3. drafty st. I, l. 6

    In E. H. Coleridge's edition, based on the 1834 Poetical Works, the line reads 'Or the dull sobbing draft, that moans and rakes', 'draft' being a variant spelling of 'draught', a current of air. The letter to Sotheby and the Morning Post read 'drones' for 'moans'. This text's 'drafty' gives no sense as a noun and is best read as a printing slip. The point is the contrast between a feeble draught that merely 'rakes' the strings of the wind-harp and the strong wind that would rouse the poet.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'draught'

  4. Æolian lute st. I, l. 7

    The instrument is named after Aeolus, keeper of the winds in Homer and Virgil. Coleridge had used it in 'The Eolian Harp' (1795) to wonder whether all of animated nature might be 'organic harps' swept by one intellectual breeze. Here the harp first produces only a dull moan, so that it 'better far were mute'; in stanza VII the storm makes it scream, and the wind becomes a 'Mad Lutanist'. Abrams's essay 'The Correspondent Breeze' takes this poem as a central example of the Romantic link between outer wind and inner renewal, and notes the irony that the wind-instrument model of mind is exactly what the poem's claim of self-generated joy denies.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  5. the old Moon in her lap st. I, l. 13

    When the new crescent is low, the rest of the lunar disc can often be seen dimly lit by 'earthshine', light reflected from the earth. Popular lore read the sight as 'the old moon in the new moon's arms' and a sign of storms, as in the ballad. Coleridge's 'phantom light' and 'swimming phantom light' describe the ghostly glow, 'rimmed and circled by a silver thread' of the crescent. Note the comma in 'The, coming-on of rain' in this text, evidently a misprint.

    Wikipedia, 'Sir Patrick Spens' (Child 58; first printed in Percy's Reliques, 1765); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  6. A grief without a pang st. II, l. 1

    Coleridge distinguishes this grief from the kind that finds 'natural outlet' in 'word, or sigh, or tear'. It is 'void, dark, and drear', 'stifled, drowsy, unimpassioned', and so close to the old theological sin of acedia or sloth. In 1814 he quoted these lines in his 'Essays on the Fine Arts' to illustrate a point of aesthetics: that we can judge an object beautiful even when great depression of spirits prevents us from feeling pleasure in it. The stanza thus prepares the famous line 'I see, not feel, how beautiful they are'.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'acedia'

  7. O Lady! st. II, l. 5

    E. H. Coleridge's line-notes show the shifting addressee. The original letter spoke to Sara Hutchinson; the version sent to William Sotheby in July 1802 addressed Wordsworth by name; a Coleorton manuscript has 'O William'; the Morning Post disguised him as 'Edmund'; Sibylline Leaves (1817) settled on an unnamed 'Lady'. The changes mean that the final stanza's blessing, now for a woman, was once a tribute to Wordsworth as a poet whose joy Coleridge has lost. Readers therefore debate whether the ode is a love poem, a poem of friendship, or a dialogue with Wordsworth's 'Intimations' ode.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'Dejection: An Ode' (summarizing Richard Holmes and George Watson)

  8. throstle st. II, l. 6

    'Throstle' is the old and dialect name for the song thrush (OED). In the letter to Sotheby the bird 'pipes within the larch-tree', and the passage goes on through 'this sweet primrose-month', placing the scene in April at Keswick. The bird's song, like the sky, is an outward beauty that should rouse joy but cannot reach the speaker. The thrush's repeated phrases made it a familiar bird of English pastoral; 'woo'd' implies a courtship the speaker cannot return.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'throstle'; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  9. I see, not feel st. II, l. 18

    The long sentence of stanza II catalogues an exquisite sky (the 'peculiar tint of yellow green', clouds 'in flakes and bars', the crescent moon) only to end in this confession; 'how blank an eye' anticipates it. The Morning Post italicized 'see' and 'feel'. Wordsworth quoted the line in his pamphlet on the Convention of Cintra (1809). The line is often set against Wordsworth's 'Tintern Abbey', where the eye and the heart work together, and against the 'Intimations' ode, whose 'there hath past away a glory from the earth' Coleridge is answering. Critics such as Abrams read the poem as testing the claim that the mind half-creates what it perceives.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  10. genial spirits st. III, l. 1

    'Genial' derives from Latin genialis, belonging to one's genius or to generation; the OED records the sense 'productive, generative'. Milton's blinded Samson says 'So much I feel my genial spirits droop' (Samson Agonistes, l. 594), and Wordsworth, at the end of 'Tintern Abbey' (1798), says that without his sister he might 'Suffer my genial spirits to decay'. Coleridge's flat statement 'My genial spirits fail' thus answers both: the Miltonic despair, and Wordsworth's confidence that his own spirits are safe.

    John Milton, Samson Agonistes, l. 594 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads (1798); Oxford English Dictionary, 'genial'

  11. fountains within st. III, ll. 7-8

    This couplet states the poem's thesis, which stanza IV develops: no amount of gazing at the green light in the west will produce joy, because feeling springs from within. The image of the inner fountain has biblical roots (e.g. John 4:14, 'a well of water springing up into everlasting life'), but the claim is psychological. M. H. Abrams's account of Romantic theory in The Mirror and the Lamp turns on this shift from a mind that reflects the world to a mind that projects light onto it.

    Bible, King James Version, John 4:14; M. H. Abrams, The Mirror and the Lamp: Romantic Theory and the Critical Tradition (1953)

  12. we receive but what we give st. IV, l. 1

    The Coleorton manuscript and the Fine Arts essay italicize 'receive' and 'give'. The lines are often read as Coleridge's reply to Wordsworth's faith in a nature that gives to the receptive mind. In the letter to Sotheby they were addressed to Wordsworth ('O Wordsworth! we receive...'), which sharpens the argument into a dialogue between the two poets. Critics disagree whether the claim is a considered idealist doctrine (consistent with Coleridge's study of Kant and Schelling) or the expression of a mood, since the poem's outward storm still moves him in stanza VII.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  13. wedding-garment ... shroud st. IV, l. 3

    In Matthew 22:11-12 a guest at the king's wedding feast is cast out for having no 'wedding garment'. Coleridge reverses the image: it is we who provide Nature's wedding-garment, as well as her shroud when the mind is dead. The image of a marriage between mind and nature is also close to Wordsworth's 'Prospectus' to The Recluse, in which the discerning intellect is 'wedded to this goodly universe'; it returns in stanza V, where joy is 'wedding Nature to us' and brings 'in dower / A new Earth and new Heaven'.

    Bible, King James Version, Matthew 22:11-12; William Wordsworth, Prospectus to The Recluse, in Knight, ed., Poetical Works, vol. 5 (1896); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  14. a light, a glory, a fair luminous cloud st. IV, l. 8

    'Glory' in Coleridge's period can mean a halo or ring of light, as well as splendour; the KJV often speaks of the 'glory of the Lord' appearing in a cloud (Exodus 16:10). The image reverses the opening, where clouds and moonlight outside left him unmoved: now the light comes from within. Stanza V repeats the terms ('This light, this glory, this fair luminous mist'; 'Joy the luminous cloud') with the incantatory insistence of a hymn. Compare Wordsworth's 'glory' that has passed from the earth in the 'Intimations' ode.

    Bible, King James Version, Exodus 16:10; Oxford English Dictionary, 'glory'

  15. O pure of heart st. V, l. 1

    Joy is 'ne'er ... given, / Save to the pure, and in their purest hour'. The Beatitude promises vision to the pure in heart; Coleridge makes purity the condition of joy, and joy the condition of true seeing. The implication, never quite stated, is that the speaker is no longer pure, in contrast to the addressee (Wordsworth in some versions, the Lady in the final one). 'Virtuous Lady' was 'blameless Poet' in the letter to Sotheby and 'virtuous Edmund' in the Morning Post.

    Bible, King James Version, Matthew 5:8; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  16. Joy st. V, l. 9

    Stanza V is a hymn to Joy, repeated five times. 'Life, and Life's effluence, cloud at once and shower' makes it both a radiance and a fertilizing rain ('effluence' was misprinted 'effulgence' in 1817 and corrected by Coleridge). The insistence has been read as heroic affirmation, or as an attempt to talk himself into a state he lacks. Coleridge's 'Joy' resembles Schiller's 'An die Freude' only distantly; it is closer to the spiritual delight in Wordsworth's lyrics of 1802.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  17. A new Earth and new Heaven st. V, l. 11

    Revelation 21:1 (compare Isaiah 65:17) promises a renewed creation after the old has passed away; Revelation 21 also describes the holy city 'as a bride adorned for her husband', which links with the poem's wedding imagery and the 'dower' that Nature brings. Coleridge internalizes the apocalypse: the new world is a way of seeing, 'Undreamt of by the sensual and the proud'. The Fine Arts version read 'A new heaven and new earth', closer to scripture. Abrams later built Natural Supernaturalism (1971) on this Romantic secularizing of apocalyptic marriage.

    Bible, King James Version, Revelation 21:1-2; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; M. H. Abrams, Natural Supernaturalism (1971)

  18. shaping spirit of Imagination st. VI, l. 11

    In Biographia Literaria (1817) Coleridge coins 'esemplastic' (from Greek eis hen plattein, to shape into one) for the imagination (ch. 10) and in ch. 13 defines secondary imagination as the power that 'dissolves, diffuses, dissipates, in order to re-create'. 'Shaping' is the plain English for the same idea; compare Theseus in A Midsummer Night's Dream on lovers' 'shaping fantasies'. Stanza VI contrasts this with 'Fancy', which once made 'dreams of happiness' from misfortune, an early form of his later distinction between fancy and imagination. Coleridge quoted the couplet on hope and the twining vine in Biographia Literaria.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), chs. 10 and 13; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream, 5.1.5 (MIT Complete Works)

  19. abstruse research st. VI, l. 14

    In these years Coleridge read widely in German philosophy and wrote long philosophical letters. Here he presents the work as a strategy 'not to think of what I needs must feel', an attempt 'to steal / From my own nature all the natural man', until the anaesthetic 'infects the whole'. 'Natural man' echoes 1 Corinthians 2:14, where 'the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God'; Coleridge uses it of his ordinary human feelings. The Morning Post omitted stanzas VI and VII as too personal; Coleridge quoted the lines on abstruse research in a letter to Josiah Wedgwood of 20 October 1802.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Bible, King James Version, 1 Corinthians 2:14

  20. Hence, viper thoughts st. VII, l. 1

    'Hence' (go away) is the formula of exorcism and of Milton's 'L'Allegro' ('Hence loathed Melancholy'). The thoughts are 'viper' thoughts because they coil round the mind; 'Reality's dark dream' describes the waking nightmare of his circumstances. The turn ('I turn from you, and listen to the wind') fulfils the wish of stanza I: the storm has arrived, and the lute now screams. The ode's irregular stanzas, varying line-length and sudden shifts of address belong to the Pindaric tradition of English odes from Cowley to Gray.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; John Milton, 'L'Allegro', l. 1 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  21. mountain-tairn st. VII, l. 7

    Coleridge's own note: a tairn is 'a small lake, generally if not always applied to the lakes up in the mountains', which feed the lakes in the valleys. The word comes from Old Norse tjörn (OED, 'tarn'). The note adds that his address to the storm-wind 'will not appear extravagant' to anyone who has heard it at night in mountain country. The list of places the wind might better play on (crag, tarn, blasted tree, pine-grove, witches' house) is a catalogue of Gothic sublimity.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, 'tarn'

  22. the wind as Mad Lutanist, Actor, Poet st. VII, ll. 11-17

    The wind 'Mak'st Devils' yule' (a devils' Christmas, a riot of misrule) among the spring blossoms, in 'this month of showers' (April). It is then 'Actor, perfect in all tragic sounds' and 'mighty Poet, even to frenzy bold', recalling the poet's 'fine frenzy' in A Midsummer Night's Dream. The wind does what Coleridge cannot: it creates, telling two tales, first a battle rout, then a lost child. Abrams reads this as the 'correspondent breeze' that, despite the poem's argument, does stir the poet's imagination, so that the poem enacts a partial recovery it denies.

    M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960); Oxford English Dictionary, 'yule'; Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream, 5.1.12 (MIT Complete Works); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  23. Otway's self st. VII, l. 27

    Otway, author of The Orphan and Venice Preserv'd, was a byword for tenderness and pathos, and legend said he died starving. But the line is a disguise. In the letter to Sotheby it read 'As thou thyself had framed', addressing Wordsworth, and in the Morning Post 'As Edmund's self'. The tale that follows, of a little child lost on a lonesome wild, crying for her mother, recalls Wordsworth's 'Lucy Gray', written in 1799 and printed in Lyrical Ballads (1800). The substitution of Otway when the addressee became a lady hides the dialogue with Wordsworth that runs through the ode.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wordsworth, Lyrical Ballads, with Other Poems (1800), vol. 2

  24. a mountain-birth st. VIII, l. 4

    Horace mocks poets who promise great things and produce trifles: parturient montes, nascetur ridiculus mus. Coleridge turns the proverb into a blessing: may the storm be a 'mountain-birth', all noise and no harm, for his friend. The final stanza moves from his own sleeplessness ('Full seldom may my friend such vigils keep!') to prayer for her sleep and waking joy.

    Horace, Ars Poetica, l. 139 (The Latin Library); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem

  25. the final blessing st. VIII, ll. 10-14

    The image of the soul as a whirlpool or eddy that draws all life into its motion gives to the friend the creative joy described in stanza V. In the Morning Post this blessing was addressed to 'Edmund', a 'lofty Poet' and 'Brother and Friend of my devoutest choice', i.e. Wordsworth, and was signed with a Greek-letter signature, a pun on Coleridge's initials S.T.C. 'O simple spirit, guided from above' and the double 'ever, evermore rejoice' close the poem in the form of a benediction, generosity replacing the self-absorption of the opening.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford, 1912), notes and line-notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'Dejection: An Ode' (summarizing Richard Holmes and George Watson)
