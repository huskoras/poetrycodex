The Mind

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

by Samuel Taylor Coleridge 1772–1834

From Poems of Coleridge, selected by Arthur Symons (1905)

The Mind

PART I

It is an ancient Mariner,
And he stoppeth one of three.
"By thy long grey beard and glittering eye,
Now wherefore stopp'st thou me?

"The Bridegroom's doors are opened wide,
And I am next of kin;
The guests are met, the feast is set:
May'st hear the merry din."

He holds him with his skinny hand,
"There was a ship," quoth he.
"Hold off! unhand me, grey-beard loon!"
Eftsoons his hand dropt he.

He holds him with his glittering eye
The Wedding-Guest stood still,
And listens like a three years' child:
The Mariner hath his will.

The Wedding-Guest sat on a stone:
He cannot choose but hear;
And thus spake on that ancient man,
The bright-eyed Mariner.

"The ship was cheered, the harbour cleared,
Merrily did we drop
Below the kirk, below the hill,
Below the lighthouse top.

The sun came up upon the left,
Out of the sea came he!
And he shone bright, and on the right
Went down into the sea.

Higher and higher every day,
Till over the mast at noon--"
The Wedding-Guest here beat his breast,
For he heard the loud bassoon.

The bride hath paced into the hall,
Red as a rose is she;
Nodding their heads before her goes
The merry minstrelsy.

The Wedding-Guest he beat his breast,
Yet he cannot choose but hear;
And thus spake on that ancient man,
The bright-eyed Mariner.

"And now the Storm-blast came, and he
Was tyrannous and strong:
He struck with his o'ertaking wings
And chased us south along.

With sloping masts and dipping prow,
As who pursued with yell and blow
Still treads the shadow of his foe,
And forward bends his head,
The ship drove fast, loud roared the blast,
And southward aye we fled.

And now there came both mist and snow,
And it grew wondrous cold:
And ice, mast-high, came floating by,
As green as emerald.

And through the drifts the snowy clifts
Did send a dismal sheen:
Nor shapes of men nor beasts we ken--
The ice was all between.

The ice was here, the ice was there,
The ice was all around:
It cracked and growled, and roared and howled,
Like noises in a swound!

At length did cross an Albatross,
Thorough the fog it came;
As if it had been a Christian soul,
We hailed it in God's name.

It ate the food it ne'er had eat,
And round and round it flew.
The ice did split with a thunder-fit;
The helmsman steered us through!

And a good south wind sprung up behind;
The Albatross did follow,
And every day, for food or play,
Came to the mariners' hollo!

In mist or cloud, on mast or shroud,
It perched for vespers nine;
Whiles all the night, through fog-smoke white,
Glimmered the white moon-shine."

"God save thee, ancient Mariner!
From the fiends, that plague thee thus!--
Why look'st thou so?"--With my cross-bow
I shot the Albatross.

PART II

The Sun now rose upon the right:
Out of the sea came he,
Still hid in mist, and on the left
Went down into the sea.

And the good south wind still blew behind,
But no sweet bird did follow,
Nor any day for food or play
Came to the mariners' hollo!

And I had done a hellish thing,
And it would work 'em woe:
For all averred, I had killed the bird
That made the breeze to blow.
Ah wretch! said they, the bird to slay,
That made the breeze to blow!

Nor, dim nor red, like God's own head,
The glorious Sun uprist:
Then all averred, I had killed the bird
That brought the fog and mist.
'Twas right, said they, such birds to slay,
That bring the fog and mist.

The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew,
The furrow followed free;
We were the first that ever burst
Into that silent sea.

Down dropt the breeze, the sails dropt down,
'Twas sad as sad could be;
And we did speak only to break
The silence of the sea!

All in a hot and copper sky,
The bloody Sun, at noon,
Right up above the mast did stand,
No bigger than the Moon.

Day after day, day after day,
We stuck, nor breath nor motion;
As idle as a painted ship
Upon a painted ocean.

Water, water, every where,
And all the boards did shrink;
Water, water, every where
Nor any drop to drink.

The very deep did rot: O Christ!
That ever this should be!
Yea, slimy things did crawl with legs
Upon the slimy sea.

About, about, in reel and rout
The death-fires danced at night;
The water, like a witch's oils,
Burnt green, and blue and white.

And some in dreams assured were,
Of the Spirit that plagued us so;
Nine fathom deep he had followed us
From the land of mist and snow.

And every tongue, through utter drought,
Was withered at the root;
We could not speak, no more than if
We had been choked with soot.

Ah! well a-day! what evil looks
Had I from old and young!
Instead of the cross, the Albatross
About my neck was hung.

PART III

There passed a weary time. Each throat
Was parched, and glazed each eye.
A weary time! a weary time!
How glazed each weary eye,
When looking westward, I beheld
A something in the sky.

At first it seemed a little speck,
And then it seemed a mist;
It moved and moved, and took at last
A certain shape, I wist.

A speck, a mist, a shape, I wist!
And still it neared and neared:
As if it dodged a water-sprite,
It plunged and tacked and veered.

With throats unslaked, with black lips baked,
We could nor laugh nor wail;
Through utter drought all dumb we stood!
I bit my arm, I sucked the blood,
And cried, A sail! a sail!

With throats unslaked, with black lips baked,
Agape they heard me call:
Gramercy! they for joy did grin,
And all at once their breath drew in,
As they were drinking all.

See! see! (I cried) she tacks no more!
Hither to work us weal;
Without a breeze, without a tide,
She steadies with upright keel!

The western wave was all a-flame,
The day was well nigh done!
Almost upon the western wave
Rested the broad bright Sun;
When that strange shape drove suddenly
Betwixt us and the Sun.

And straight the Sun was flecked with bars,
(Heaven's Mother send us grace!)
As if through a dungeon-grate he peered
With broad and burning face.

Alas! (thought I, and my heart beat loud)
How fast she nears and nears!
Are those her sails that glance in the Sun,
Like restless gossameres?

Are those her ribs through which the Sun
Did peer, as through a grate?
And is that Woman all her crew?
Is that a Death? and are there two?
Is Death that Woman's mate?

Her lips were red, her looks were free,
Her locks were yellow as gold:
Her skin was as white as leprosy,
The Night-mare Life-in-Death was she,
Who thicks man's blood with cold.

The naked hulk alongside came,
And the twain were casting dice;
"The game is done! I've won! I've won!"
Quoth she, and whistles thrice.

The Sun's rim dips; the stars rush out:
At one stride comes the dark;
With far-heard whisper, o'er the sea,
Off shot the spectre-bark.

We listened and looked sideways up!
Fear at my heart, as at a cup,
My life-blood seemed to sip!
The stars were dim, and thick the night,
The steersman's face by his lamp gleamed white;
From the sails the dew did drip--
Till clomb above the eastern bar
The horned Moon, with one bright star
Within the nether tip.

One after one, by the star-dogged Moon,
Too quick for groan or sigh,
Each turned his face with a ghastly pang,
And cursed me with his eye.

Four times fifty living men,
(And I heard nor sigh nor groan)
With heavy thump, a lifeless lump,
They dropped down one by one.

The souls did from their bodies fly,--
They fled to bliss or woe!
And every soul, it passed me by,
Like the whizz of my cross-bow!

PART IV

"I fear thee, ancient Mariner!
I fear thy skinny hand!
And thou art long, and lank, and brown,
As is the ribbed sea-sand.

I fear thee and thy glittering eye,
And thy skinny hand, so brown."--
Fear not, fear not, thou Wedding-Guest!
This body dropt not down.

Alone, alone, all, all alone,
Alone on a wide wide sea!
And never a saint took pity on
My soul in agony.

The many men, so beautiful!
And they all dead did lie:
And a thousand thousand slimy things
Lived on; and so did I.

I looked upon the rotting sea,
And drew my eyes away;
I looked upon the rotting deck,
And there the dead men lay.

I looked to heaven, and tried to pray;
But or ever a prayer had gusht,
A wicked whisper came, and made
My heart as dry as dust.

I closed my lids, and kept them close,
And the balls like pulses beat;
For the sky and the sea, and the sea and the sky,
Lay like a load on my weary eye,
And the dead were at my feet.

The cold sweat melted from their limbs,
Nor rot nor reek did they:
The look with which they looked on me
Had never passed away.

An orphan's curse would drag to hell
A spirit from on high;
But oh! more horrible than that
Is a curse in a dead man's eye!
Seven days, seven nights, I saw that curse,
And yet I could not die.

The moving Moon went up the sky,
And no where did abide:
Softly she was going up,
And a star or two beside--

Her beams bemocked the sultry main,
Like April hoar-frost spread;
But where the ship's huge shadow lay,
The charmed water burnt alway
A still and awful red.

Beyond the shadow of the ship,
I watched the water-snakes:
They moved in tracks of shining white,
And when they reared, the elfish light
Fell off in hoary flakes.

Within the shadow of the ship
I watched their rich attire:
Blue, glossy green, and velvet black,
They coiled and swam; and every track
Was a flash of golden fire.

O happy living things! no tongue
Their beauty might declare:
A spring of love gushed from my heart,
And I blessed them unaware:
Sure my kind saint took pity on me,
And I blessed them unaware.

The selfsame moment I could pray;
And from my neck so free
The Albatross fell off, and sank
Like lead into the sea.

PART V

Oh sleep! it is a gentle thing,
Beloved from pole to pole!
To Mary Queen the praise be given!
She sent the gentle sleep from Heaven,
That slid into my soul.

The silly buckets on the deck,
That had so long remained,
I dreamt that they were filled with dew;
And when I awoke, it rained.

My lips were wet, my throat was cold,
My garments all were dank;
Sure I had drunken in my dreams,
And still my body drank.

I moved, and could not feel my limbs:
I was so light--almost
I thought that I had died in sleep;
And was a blessed ghost.

And soon I heard a roaring wind:
It did not come anear;
But with its sound it shook the sails,
That were so thin and sere.

The upper air burst into life!
And a hundred fire-flags sheen,
To and fro they were hurried about!
And to and fro, and in and out,
The wan stars danced between.

And the coming wind did roar more loud,
And the sails did sigh like sedge;
And the rain poured down from one black cloud;
The Moon was at its edge.

The thick black cloud was cleft, and still
The Moon was at its side:
Like waters shot from some high crag,
The lightning fell with never a jag,
A river steep and wide.

The loud wind never reached the ship,
Yet now the ship moved on!
Beneath the lightning and the Moon
The dead men gave a groan.

They groaned, they stirred, they all uprose,
Nor spake, nor moved their eyes;
It had been strange, even in a dream,
To have seen those dead men rise.

The helmsman steered, the ship moved on;
Yet never a breeze up blew;
The mariners all 'gan work the ropes,
Where they were wont to do;
They raised their limbs like lifeless tools--
We were a ghastly crew.

The body of my brother's son
Stood by me, knee to knee:
The body and I pulled at one rope
But he said nought to me.

"I fear thee, ancient Mariner!"
Be calm, thou Wedding-Guest!
'Twas not those souls that fled in pain,
Which to their corses came again,
But a troop of spirits blest:

For when it dawned--they dropped their arms,
And clustered round the mast;
Sweet sounds rose slowly through their mouths,
And from their bodies passed.

Around, around, flew each sweet sound,
Then darted to the Sun;
Slowly the sounds came back again,
Now mixed, now one by one.

Sometimes a-dropping from the sky
I heard the sky-lark sing;
Sometimes all little birds that are,
How they seemed to fill the sea and air
With their sweet jargoning!

And now 'twas like all instruments,
Now like a lonely flute;
And now it is an angel's song,
That makes the heavens be mute.

It ceased; yet still the sails made on
A pleasant noise till noon,
A noise like of a hidden brook
In the leafy month of June,
That to the sleeping woods all night
Singeth a quiet tune.

Till noon we quietly sailed on,
Yet never a breeze did breathe:
Slowly and smoothly went the ship,
Moved onward from beneath.

Under the keel nine fathom deep,
From the land of mist and snow,
The spirit slid: and it was he
That made the ship to go.
The sails at noon left off their tune,
And the ship stood still also.

The Sun, right up above the mast,
Had fixed her to the ocean:
But in a minute she 'gan stir,
With a short uneasy motion--
Backwards and forwards half her length
With a short uneasy motion.

Then like a pawing horse let go,
She made a sudden bound:
It flung the blood into my head,
And I fell down in a swound.

How long in that same fit I lay,
I have not to declare;
But ere my living life returned,
I heard and in my soul discerned
Two voices in the air.

"Is it he?" quoth one, "Is this the man?
By him who died on cross,
With his cruel bow he laid full low
The harmless Albatross.

The spirit who bideth by himself
In the land of mist and snow,
He loved the bird that loved the man
Who shot him with his bow."

The other was a softer voice,
As soft as honey-dew:
Quoth he, "The man hath penance done,
And penance more will do."

PART VI

FIRST VOICE

"But tell me, tell me! speak again,
Thy soft response renewing--
What makes that ship drive on so fast?
What is the ocean doing?"

SECOND VOICE

"Still as a slave before his lord,
The ocean hath no blast;
His great bright eye most silently
Up to the Moon is cast--

If he may know which way to go;
For she guides him smooth or grim.
See, brother, see! how graciously
She looketh down on him."

FIRST VOICE

"But why drives on that ship so fast,
Without or wave or wind?"

SECOND VOICE

"The air is cut away before,
And closes from behind.

Fly, brother, fly! more high, more high!
Or we shall be belated:
For slow and slow that ship will go,
When the Mariner's trance is abated."

I woke, and we were sailing on
As in a gentle weather:
'Twas night, calm night, the moon was high,
The dead men stood together.

All stood together on the deck,
For a charnel-dungeon fitter:
All fixed on me their stony eyes,
That in the Moon did glitter.

The pang, the curse, with which they died,
Had never passed away:
I could not draw my eyes from theirs,
Nor turn them up to pray.

And now this spell was snapt: once more
I viewed the ocean green,
And looked far forth, yet little saw
Of what had else been seen--

Like one, that on a lonesome road
Doth walk in fear and dread,
And having once turned round walks on,
And turns no more his head;
Because he knows, a frightful fiend
Doth close behind him tread.

But soon there breathed a wind on me,
Nor sound nor motion made:
Its path was not upon the sea,
In ripple or in shade.

It raised my hair, it fanned my cheek
Like a meadow-gale of spring--
It mingled strangely with my fears,
Yet it felt like a welcoming.

Swiftly, swiftly flew the ship,
Yet she sailed softly too:
Sweetly, sweetly blew the breeze--
On me alone it blew.

Oh! dream of joy! is this indeed
The light-house top I see?
Is this the hill? is this the kirk?
Is this mine own countree?

We drifted o'er the harbour-bar,
And I with sobs did pray--
O let me be awake, my God!
Or let me sleep alway.

The harbour-bay was clear as glass,
So smoothly it was strewn!
And on the bay the moonlight lay,
And the shadow of the Moon.

The rock shone bright, the kirk no less,
That stands above the rock:
The moonlight steeped in silentness
The steady weathercock.

And the bay was white with silent light
Till rising from the same,
Full many shapes, that shadows were,
In crimson colours came.

A little distance from the prow
Those crimson shadows were:
I turned my eyes upon the deck--
Oh, Christ! what saw I there!

Each corse lay flat, lifeless and flat,
And, by the holy rood!
A man all light, a seraph-man,
On every corse there stood.

This seraph-band, each waved his hand:
It was a heavenly sight!
They stood as signals to the land,
Each one a lovely light;

This seraph-band, each waved his hand,
No voice did they impart--
No voice; but oh! the silence sank
Like music on my heart.

But soon I heard the dash of oars,
I heard the Pilot's cheer;
My head was turned perforce away,
And I saw a boat appear.

The Pilot and the Pilot's boy,
I heard them coming fast:
Dear Lord in Heaven! it was a joy
The dead men could not blast.

I saw a third--I heard his voice:
It is the Hermit good!
He singeth loud his godly hymns
That he makes in the wood.
He'll shrieve my soul, he'll wash away
The Albatross's blood.

PART VII

This Hermit good lives in that wood
Which slopes down to the sea.
How loudly his sweet voice he rears!
He loves to talk with marineres
That come from a far countree.

He kneels at morn, and noon, and eve--
He hath a cushion plump:
It is the moss that wholly hides
The rotted old oak-stump.

The skiff-boat neared: I heard them talk,
"Why, this is strange, I trow!
Where are those lights so many and fair,
That signal made but now?"

"Strange, by my faith!" the Hermit said--
"And they answered not our cheer!
The planks looked warped! and see those sails,
How thin they are and sere!
I never saw aught like to them,
Unless perchance it were

Brown skeletons of leaves that lag
My forest-brook along;
When the ivy-tod is heavy with snow,
And the owlet whoops to the wolf below,
That eats the she-wolf's young."

"Dear Lord! it hath a fiendish look-
(The Pilot made reply)
I am a-feared"--"Push on, push on!"
Said the Hermit cheerily.

The boat came closer to the ship,
But I nor spake nor stirred;
The boat came close beneath the ship,
And straight a sound was heard.

Under the water it rumbled on,
Still louder and more dread:
It reached the ship, it split the bay;
The ship went down like lead.

Stunned by that loud and dreadful sound,
Which sky and ocean smote,
Like one that hath been seven days drowned
My body lay afloat;
But swift as dreams, myself I found
Within the Pilot's boat.

Upon the whirl, where sank the ship,
The boat spun round and round;
And all was still, save that the hill
Was telling of the sound.

I moved my lips--the Pilot shrieked
And fell down in a fit;
The holy Hermit raised his eyes,
And prayed where he did sit.

I took the oars: the Pilot's boy,
Who now doth crazy go,
Laughed loud and long, and all the while
His eyes went to and fro.
"Ha! ha!" quoth he, "full plain I see,
The Devil knows how to row."

And now, all in my own countree,
I stood on the firm land!
The Hermit stepped forth from the boat,
And scarcely he could stand.

"O shrieve me, shrieve me, holy man!"
The Hermit crossed his brow.
"Say quick," quoth he, "I bid thee say
What manner of man art thou?"

Forthwith this frame of mine was wrenched
With a woful agony,
Which forced me to begin my tale;
And then it left me free.

Since then, at an uncertain hour,
That agony returns:
And till my ghastly tale is told,
This heart within me burns.

I pass, like night, from land to land;
I have strange power of speech;
That moment that his face I see,
I know the man that must hear me:
To him my tale I teach.

What loud uproar bursts from that door!
The wedding-guests are there:
But in the garden-bower the bride
And bride-maids singing are:
And hark the little vesper bell,
Which biddeth me to prayer!

O Wedding-Guest! this soul hath been
Alone on a wide wide sea:
So lonely 'twas, that God himself
Scarce seemed there to be.

O sweeter than the marriage-feast,
Tis sweeter far to me,
To walk together to the kirk
With a goodly company!--

To walk together to the kirk,
And all together pray,
While each to his great Father bends,
Old men, and babes, and loving friends,
And youths and maidens gay!

Farewell, farewell! but this I tell
To thee, thou Wedding-Guest!
He prayeth well, who loveth well
Both man and bird and beast.

He prayeth best, who loveth best
All things both great and small;
For the dear God who loveth us,
He made and loveth all.

The Mariner, whose eye is bright,
Whose beard with age is hoar,
Is gone: and now the Wedding-Guest
Turned from the bridegroom's door.

He went like one that hath been stunned,
And is of sense forlorn:
A sadder and a wiser man,
He rose the morrow morn.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and this text l. 1

    Coleridge began the poem in November 1797 on a walk over the Quantocks with William and Dorothy Wordsworth, and it opened Lyrical Ballads (1798) as 'The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere', in deliberately archaic spelling and diction. He modernised much of it for the 1800 edition and, for Sibylline Leaves (1817), added the Latin epigraph from Thomas Burnet and the prose gloss printed in the margins, which glosses the action in the voice of a pious antiquarian editor. Line references in these notes follow the standard 1834 numbering (625 lines); the text here, from Arthur Symons's 1905 selection, follows the later wording but omits both epigraph and gloss, and runs a few lines longer after about l. 300, so a printed line count may differ slightly from the reference given. Readers should know the gloss exists: many interpretations, for example of the Polar Spirit or the moon, lean on it, and some critics argue the gloss is a further fiction rather than the author's key.

Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner', headnote and commentary

  2. one of three l. 2

    The frame narrative is a ballad device: an old man stops a young one on his way to a marriage, and the merriment inside the hall ('the loud bassoon', the bride 'Red as a rose') interrupts the tale. The guest is chosen, not random; Part VII says the Mariner knows at sight 'the man that must hear me'. Critics have read the pairing as setting two kinds of communion against each other: the social sacrament of marriage and the solitary religious experience the Mariner brings back. The poem ends with the Mariner preferring the walk 'together to the kirk' to the marriage-feast, and the guest turning away from the bridegroom's door, so the frame is changed by the tale.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  3. glittering eye ll. 3, 13

    The guest throws off the 'skinny hand' but cannot resist the eye, and 'listens like a three years' child'. Coleridge was interested in Franz Anton Mesmer's 'animal magnetism', which was much discussed in Britain in the 1780s and 1790s, and editors have connected the fascinating eye with that vogue, as well as with the older folklore of the evil eye and the serpent's power to charm its prey. The eye recurs through the poem: the dying crew curse him 'with his eye', the dead men's eyes fix on him, and the poem ends with the Mariner 'whose eye is bright'. Looking, cursing and blessing are tied together throughout.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1927), Part I; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner', headnote and commentary

  4. loon; Eftsoons ll. 11-12

    Coleridge's ballad diction mixes genuine old words with pseudo-medieval colour. 'Loon' (Scots and northern English) is a rogue, idler or simpleton. 'Eftsoons', literally 'soon after', had dropped out of ordinary use by the seventeenth century but was familiar from Spenser's Faerie Queene, where it is frequent. Other archaisms in the poem include 'wist' (knew), 'ken' (see, know), 'clomb' (climbed), 'uprist' (rose up), 'shrieve' (absolve after confession) and 'trow' (believe). The 1798 version carried far more of this archaic spelling, and early reviewers, Southey among them, found the poem strange and obscure, which partly explains Coleridge's revisions of 1800.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eftsoons'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  5. like a three years' child l. 15

    In the Fenwick notes of 1843 Wordsworth recalled that he supplied two or three lines near the opening, including 'And listened like a three years' child: / The Mariner had his will' (he misdates the walk to spring 1798). Coleridge himself acknowledged a debt to Wordsworth for two lines in Part IV ('As is the ribbed sea-sand') in a footnote in Sibylline Leaves. The simile makes the hold of the tale almost hypnotic: an adult wedding guest becomes a small child who cannot choose but hear. It also anticipates the poem's closing simplicity, a moral fit for a child, which some critics take at face value and others regard as deliberately inadequate to what has been told.

Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  6. The sun came up upon the left ll. 25-28

    Coleridge never names places, but the sun gives exact bearings. Sailing south from Britain, a ship has the rising sun on its left (east). The storm drives it to the polar ice; after the Albatross guides it out, the sun rises 'upon the right' (Part II), so the ship is now heading north up the Pacific side of South America. 'Higher and higher every day, / Till over the mast at noon' marks the approach to the equator, where the becalmed ship lies under a sun directly overhead. Lowes traced how Coleridge assembled this geography from his wide reading of travel and voyage literature, including Cook's voyages and accounts of the southern ice.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1927), Part I; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  7. Albatross ll. 63-66

    Wordsworth told Isabella Fenwick that he proposed the crime that brings spectral persecution on the Mariner: he had been reading Shelvocke's Voyage Round the World, where the second captain, Simon Hatley, shot a black albatross that had followed the ship in bad weather, believing it an ill omen. Wordsworth suggested the killing and the tutelary spirits who avenge it. The bird is hailed 'As if it had been a Christian soul', fed by the crew, and greeted 'in God's name'; it thus becomes a guest and almost a fellow Christian, which makes its killing a violation of hospitality as well as of nature. Whether it is a symbol (of Christ, nature, or the spirit of love) or simply a creature is a long-running debate.

    Isabella Fenwick's notes of Wordsworth's recollections (1843) on the genesis of the poem, transcribed by John Dobson; George Shelvocke, A Voyage Round the World by Way of the Great South Sea (London, 1726), account of the albatross shot by Simon Hatley off Cape Horn; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  8. With my cross-bow / I shot the Albatross ll. 79-82

    The stanza withholds a reason: the guest asks why the Mariner looks so stricken, and the answer falls abruptly into the last line of Part I. The absence of motive is the poem's central crux. Robert Penn Warren, in his influential 1946 essay, read the act as a re-enactment of the Fall, a crime of the perverse will against the 'One Life', with the moon and the wind as symbols of the imagination. Empson resisted the Christian scheme, reading the poem instead as an expression of guilt over European maritime expansion and the slave trade. Jerome McGann argued that the poem's layered texts and gloss deliberately present a history of interpretation rather than a single meaning. Students should notice that the crew's verdicts on the act shift with the weather (Part II), complicating any simple moral reckoning.

    Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); William Empson and David Pirie, eds., Coleridge's Verse: A Selection (London: Faber, 1972), Empson's introduction; Jerome J. McGann, 'The Meaning of the Ancient Mariner', Critical Inquiry 8.1 (1981)

  9. For all averred ll. 91-102

    'Averred' means declared, affirmed. In two balanced stanzas the sailors call the Mariner a wretch for killing the bird 'That made the breeze to blow', then, when the sun rises 'like God's own head', say it was right to kill birds 'That bring the fog and mist'. The marginal gloss of 1817 makes the point explicit, saying the crew thus make themselves accomplices in the crime. The repetition shows a morality based on consequences and superstition; it also explains, within the poem's logic, why the whole crew die while the Mariner, the principal, is left to a worse fate of living on.

    S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aver'

  10. Water, water, every where ll. 119-122

    The repetition imitates the endless view of salt water, and 'all the boards did shrink' shows the ship's planks shrinking in the heat. The paradox of thirst amid water had a long history in sea narratives, but Coleridge's plain ballad phrasing made it proverbial; the usual misquotation smooths away his abrupt 'Nor any drop to drink'. Spiritually, the image fits the poem's pattern of deprivation in the midst of plenty: the dead calm is a drought of grace as well as of water, ended in Part V only after the Mariner blesses the water-snakes and the rain falls.

    Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  11. slimy things did crawl with legs ll. 123-126

    Lowes connected these images with descriptions in voyage literature of the becalmed sea seeming to stagnate and of marine organisms swarming at the surface. At this stage the creatures are disgusting to the Mariner, 'slimy things'; in Part IV he still sees 'a thousand thousand slimy things' that live on while his comrades die. The turn of the poem comes when he sees the same sea-creatures, the water-snakes, as beautiful and blesses them 'unaware'. The language of slime and rot thus measures his spiritual condition rather than the creatures themselves.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1927), Part I; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  12. Instead of the cross, the Albatross / About my neck was hung ll. 139-142

    The cross a sailor might wear as a sign of salvation is replaced by the sign of his crime; the rhyme of 'cross' and 'Albatross' (already linked in 'did cross an Albatross') presses the association between the bird and Christ, which Christian readings exploit. The image recalls the custom of hanging a dead animal on a dog that has killed livestock. It is reversed at the end of Part IV, when the Mariner prays and 'The Albatross fell off, and sank / Like lead into the sea'. The modern idiom for a burdensome guilt or encumbrance derives from this passage.

    Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'albatross'

  13. Death and Life-in-Death ll. 190-198

    The spectre-ship that sails without wind is a hulk of bare ribs through which the sun peers 'as through a dungeon-grate'. Its crew are two allegorical figures. The woman, with red lips, golden hair and skin 'white as leprosy', combines erotic allure with disease, a 'Night-mare' in the old sense of an incubus that sits on the sleeper. Her prize is the condition the Mariner then endures: alive, but unable to pray or to die. The 1798 version describes Death more grotesquely, as a skeleton with jet-black bones; Coleridge cut the passage in later editions. The dicing echoes medieval and ballad imagery of Fortune and of soldiers casting lots.

    Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads, with a Few Other Poems (Bristol and London, 1798), 'The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere'; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner', headnote and commentary

  14. The horned Moon, with one bright star / Within the nether tip ll. 209-211

    Sailors held a star 'dogging' the moon to be an ill omen, and the 1817 gloss calls it so. Commentators since the nineteenth century have pointed out that no star could appear within the dark part of the moon's disc, since the moon would hide it; some see a deliberate supernatural sign, others a slip. Lowes found possible sources in astronomical reports and in Coleridge's reading, and he cited records of apparent 'stars' seen within the moon's crescent. Either way, the moon is one of the poem's governing presences, presiding over the curse here and over the Mariner's release in Part IV.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1927), Part I; S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  15. long, and lank, and brown, / As is the ribbed sea-sand ll. 226-227

    In Sibylline Leaves Coleridge added a footnote acknowledging that he owed these two lines to Wordsworth, whose other contributions to the poem's early shape he recalled in the Fenwick note (without mentioning these lines). 'Ribbed' describes the ripples left in sand by receding water, and it also suggests the ribs of an emaciated body, and the 'ribs' of the spectre-ship through which the sun peered. The guest's renewed fear pauses the narrative at the start of Part IV, reminding the reader that the man speaking survived, and the Mariner's reply ('This body dropt not down') introduces his solitary ordeal.

    S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Isabella Fenwick's notes of Wordsworth's recollections (1843) on the genesis of the poem, transcribed by John Dobson; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  16. a curse in a dead man's eye ll. 257-262

    An orphan's curse was proverbially powerful, since orphans had God as their protector (compare the biblical commands to defend the fatherless, as in Exodus 22:22-24). The Mariner sets the dead men's silent curse above it. The number seven, the length of the curse, has scriptural resonance as the days of creation and of mourning. The stanza is six lines long, extending the ballad quatrain as the ordeal is prolonged; 'And yet I could not die' states the Life-in-Death condition. The release depends on the moon's rising in the next stanzas and on the blessing that follows.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Exodus 22:22-24; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  17. The moving Moon went up the sky ll. 263-271

    The stanza is in calm contrast to the stasis before it: the moon moves and 'no where did abide', with a star or two beside her. In Sibylline Leaves Coleridge's marginal gloss here describes the Mariner, in his loneliness, yearning towards the journeying moon and the stars, which still sojourn yet still move onward and are everywhere at home. Warren made the moon and its light the symbol of the imagination and of grace throughout the poem. Under the moonlight the sea outside the ship's shadow looks like hoar-frost, while the water in the shadow 'burnt alway / A still and awful red'.

    S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  18. the water-snakes ... I blessed them unaware ll. 272-291

    The 'slimy things' of Part II are now 'happy living things' with 'rich attire' of blue, green, velvet black and golden fire. The blessing comes 'unaware', from 'A spring of love' rather than an act of will, and the poem attributes it equally to 'my kind saint'. Warren read the moment as the operation of grace, answering the gratuitous crime with a gratuitous act of love, and many critics connect it with Coleridge's idea of the 'One Life' in all creatures. Notice the exact reversal of the earlier failure: in Part IV he 'tried to pray' but a 'wicked whisper' dried his heart; now 'The selfsame moment I could pray'. The release is partial, though, and penance continues.

    Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  19. silly buckets l. 297

    The word has puzzled readers. Its root, Old English sǣlig, meant 'happy, blessed' (compare German selig), and over centuries it came to mean innocent, simple, helpless, and finally foolish. Coleridge's buckets have stood empty and useless through the drought, and editors gloss 'silly' as 'useless' or 'empty', while noting the older sense of 'blessed', apt for buckets now to be filled by heaven-sent rain. The stanza turns the dream into fact: the Mariner dreams the buckets are full of dew, and wakes to rain. Sleep is attributed to 'Mary Queen', in keeping with the poem's Catholic medieval setting.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'silly'; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner', headnote and commentary

  20. Two voices in the air ll. 393-409

    The Polar Spirit that 'bideth by himself / In the land of mist and snow' has driven the ship from beneath; the 1817 gloss calls him one of the invisible inhabitants of this planet, neither departed souls nor angels, and cites Josephus and the Platonist Michael Psellus as authorities on such daemons. The second voice, 'soft as honey-dew', is gentler and introduces the idea of penance, the Catholic sacramental sequence of contrition, confession and satisfaction that the Mariner's later shriving by the Hermit continues. The line 'He loved the bird that loved the man' compresses the poem's chain of love between creatures.

    S. T. Coleridge, Sibylline Leaves (1817), text with marginal gloss and footnotes; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; J. C. C. Mays, ed., Poetical Works, The Collected Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge 16 (Princeton, 2001), 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner', headnote and commentary

  21. Like one, that on a lonesome road ll. 446-451

    The spell broken, the Mariner still dreads what is behind him, and the simile gives his state the shape of folk fear: the walker who has once turned round, seen something, and walks on without daring to look again. The stanza was quickly famous. Mary Shelley has Victor Frankenstein quote it as he flees through Ingolstadt after creating his creature (vol. I, ch. 4 in 1818; ch. 5 in 1831), and her novel elsewhere echoes the Rime through Walton's polar voyage and Victor's isolation. Coleridge, a friend of her father William Godwin, had recited the poem in the Godwin household when she was a child, according to family tradition.

    Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818; rev. 1831), ch. 5 (1831 text); Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'

  22. the Hermit good ... He'll shrieve my soul ll. 508-513

    Arriving in the Pilot's boat, the Hermit lives in the wood by the sea, kneels on a moss-covered stump, and loves to talk with sailors from far countries. He stands for an unworldly, nature-loving piety that the poem treats with affection. 'Shrieve' is the Middle English form of 'shrive', from Old English scrīfan, to impose penance. When the Mariner begs 'O shrieve me', the Hermit's demand 'What manner of man art thou?' forces the tale out of him; the confession brings relief, but not final absolution, since the agony returns 'at an uncertain hour' and compels him to tell the story again and again.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shrive'; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  23. I pass, like night, from land to land ll. 586-590

    The penance never ends: an agony returns at uncertain hours, and he must find the one listener who 'must hear me'. The image of the wanderer passing 'like night, from land to land' recalls the legend of the Wandering Jew, condemned to wander until the Second Coming, which Coleridge knew and to which Lowes and later critics compare the Mariner. Many readers also see an image of the poet, driven to speak and gifted with a 'strange power' that holds his audience, and some biographical critics link the Mariner's guilt and isolation with Coleridge's own. The tale itself is a 'teaching' ('To him my tale I teach'), which leads into the poem's much-debated moral.

    John Livingston Lowes, The Road to Xanadu: A Study in the Ways of the Imagination (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1927), Part I; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946)

  24. He prayeth best, who loveth best ll. 612-617

    In Table Talk (31 May 1830) Coleridge recalled that Anna Laetitia Barbauld told him the poem's faults were that it was improbable and had no moral. He replied that it had too much moral, and that its chief fault was the open intrusion of the moral sentiment into a work of pure imagination; it ought to have had no more moral than the Arabian Nights tale of the merchant whose thrown date-shell puts out the eye of a genie's son, so that the genie claims his life. Critics divide: some take the closing stanzas as the poem's sincere centre, others think the neat moral is the Mariner's inadequate reduction of an experience that exceeds it, since the tale's punishments far outrun the crime and include innocent men.

    S. T. Coleridge, Specimens of the Table Talk, ed. H. N. Coleridge (1835), entry for 31 May 1830; David S. Miall, 'Mariner: The Poem's Moral' (University of Alberta teaching site), on Table Talk, 31 May 1830; Robert Penn Warren, 'A Poem of Pure Imagination: An Experiment in Reading', with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1946); Jerome J. McGann, 'The Meaning of the Ancient Mariner', Critical Inquiry 8.1 (1981)

  25. A sadder and a wiser man ll. 622-625

    The poem ends not with the Mariner but with his listener, who turns from the bridegroom's door 'like one that hath been stunned'. 'Forlorn' here has its older sense of 'deprived of, lost'. The last couplet suggests that the tale's knowledge is a kind of fall: wisdom comes with sadness, as in Ecclesiastes 1:18 ('he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow'). The frame thus repeats the poem's pattern of isolation from community, though now as a beginning of understanding rather than as a curse. The phrase became proverbial.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Ecclesiastes 1:18; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'forlorn'; Ernest Hartley Coleridge, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. I, text and notes of 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner'
