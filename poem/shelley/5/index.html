Nature

Mont Blanc

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From Poems of 1816

Nature

The everlasting universe of things
Flows through the mind, and rolls its rapid waves,
Now dark—now glittering—now reflecting gloom—
Now lending splendour, where from secret springs
The source of human thought its tribute brings
Of waters,—with a sound but half its own,
Such as a feeble brook will oft assume
In the wild woods, among the mountains lone,
Where waterfalls around it leap for ever,
Where woods and winds contend, and a vast river
Over its rocks ceaselessly bursts and raves.

Thus thou, Ravine of Arve—dark, deep Ravine—
Thou many-coloured, many-voiced vale,
Over whose pines, and crags, and caverns sail
Fast cloud-shadows and sunbeams: awful scene,
Where Power in likeness of the Arve comes down
From the ice-gulfs that gird his secret throne,
Bursting through these dark mountains like the flame
Of lightning through the tempest;—thou dost lie,
Thy giant brood of pines around thee clinging,
Children of elder time, in whose devotion
The chainless winds still come and ever came
To drink their odours, and their mighty swinging
To hear—an old and solemn harmony;
Thine earthly rainbows stretched across the sweep
Of the ethereal waterfall, whose veil
Robes some unsculptured image; the strange sleep
Which when the voices of the desert fail
Wraps all in its own deep eternity;—
Thy caverns echoing to the Arve’s commotion,
A loud, lone sound no other sound can tame;
Thou art pervaded with that ceaseless motion,
Thou art the path of that unresting sound—
Dizzy Ravine! and when I gaze on thee
I seem as in a trance sublime and strange
To muse on my own separate fantasy,
My own, my human mind, which passively
Now renders and receives fast influencings,
Holding an unremitting interchange
With the clear universe of things around;
One legion of wild thoughts, whose wandering wings
Now float above thy darkness, and now rest
Where that or thou art no unbidden guest,
In the still cave of the witch Poesy,
Seeking among the shadows that pass by
Ghosts of all things that are, some shade of thee,
Some phantom, some faint image; till the breast
From which they fled recalls them, thou art there!

Some say that gleams of a remoter world
Visit the soul in sleep,—that death is slumber,
And that its shapes the busy thoughts outnumber
Of those who wake and live.—I look on high;
Has some unknown omnipotence unfurled
The veil of life and death? or do I lie
In dream, and does the mightier world of sleep
Spread far around and inaccessibly
Its circles? For the very spirit fails,
Driven like a homeless cloud from steep to steep
That vanishes among the viewless gales!
Far, far above, piercing the infinite sky,
Mont Blanc appears,—still, snowy, and serene—
Its subject mountains their unearthly forms
Pile around it, ice and rock; broad vales between
Of frozen floods, unfathomable deeps,
Blue as the overhanging heaven, that spread
And wind among the accumulated steeps;
A desert peopled by the storms alone,
Save when the eagle brings some hunter’s bone,
And the wolf tracts her there—how hideously
Its shapes are heaped around! rude, bare, and high,
Ghastly, and scarred, and riven.—Is this the scene
Where the old Earthquake-daemon taught her young
Ruin? Were these their toys? or did a sea
Of fire envelope once this silent snow?
None can reply—all seems eternal now.
The wilderness has a mysterious tongue
Which teaches awful doubt, or faith so mild,
So solemn, so serene, that man may be,
But for such faith, with nature reconciled;
Thou hast a voice, great Mountain, to repeal
Large codes of fraud and woe; not understood
By all, but which the wise, and great, and good
Interpret, or make felt, or deeply feel.

The fields, the lakes, the forests, and the streams,
Ocean, and all the living things that dwell
Within the daedal earth; lightning, and rain,
Earthquake, and fiery flood, and hurricane,
The torpor of the year when feeble dreams
Visit the hidden buds, or dreamless sleep
Holds every future leaf and flower;—the bound
With which from that detested trance they leap;
The works and ways of man, their death and birth,
And that of him and all that his may be;
All things that move and breathe with toil and sound
Are born and die; revolve, subside, and swell.
Power dwells apart in its tranquillity,
Remote, serene, and inaccessible:
And THIS, the naked countenance of earth,
On which I gaze, even these primaeval mountains
Teach the adverting mind. The glaciers creep
Like snakes that watch their prey, from their far fountains,
Slow rolling on; there, many a precipice,
Frost and the Sun in scorn of mortal power
Have piled: dome, pyramid, and pinnacle,
A city of death, distinct with many a tower
And wall impregnable of beaming ice.
Yet not a city, but a flood of ruin
Is there, that from the boundaries of the sky
Rolls its perpetual stream; vast pines are strewing
Its destined path, or in the mangled soil
Branchless and shattered stand; the rocks, drawn down
From yon remotest waste, have overthrown
The limits of the dead and living world,
Never to be reclaimed. The dwelling-place
Of insects, beasts, and birds, becomes its spoil;
Their food and their retreat for ever gone,
So much of life and joy is lost. The race
Of man flies far in dread; his work and dwelling
Vanish, like smoke before the tempest’s stream,
And their place is not known. Below, vast caves
Shine in the rushing torrents’ restless gleam,
Which from those secret chasms in tumult welling
Meet in the vale, and one majestic River,
The breath and blood of distant lands, for ever
Rolls its loud waters to the ocean waves,
Breathes its swift vapours to the circling air.

Mont Blanc yet gleams on high—the power is there,
The still and solemn power of many sights,
And many sounds, and much of life and death.
In the calm darkness of the moonless nights,
In the lone glare of day, the snows descend
Upon that Mountain; none beholds them there,
Nor when the flakes burn in the sinking sun,
Or the star-beams dart through them:—Winds contend
Silently there, and heap the snow with breath
Rapid and strong, but silently! Its home
The voiceless lightning in these solitudes
Keeps innocently, and like vapour broods
Over the snow. The secret strength of things
Which governs thought, and to the infinite dome
Of heaven is as a law, inhabits thee!
And what were thou, and earth, and stars, and sea,
If to the human mind’s imaginings
Silence and solitude were vacancy?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication ll. 1-11

    Shelley, Mary Godwin and Claire Clairmont toured Chamonix from about 21 to 27 July 1816, during the summer spent near Byron on Lake Geneva. The poem was first printed as the conclusion of the Shelleys' History of a Six Weeks' Tour (1817), subtitled 'Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; the printed date of June 23 conflicts with the travel record, and editors place composition in late July. The preface says the poem was composed under the immediate impression of the scene and claims to imitate its 'untameable wildness and inaccessible solemnity'. A rough earlier version survives in the Scrope Davies Notebook, discovered in a London bank vault in 1976.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); The Shelley-Godwin Archive, 'Mont Blanc' (Scrope Davies Notebook version, BL Loan MS 70/8, and published text); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  2. The everlasting universe of things / Flows through the mind ll. 1-6

    The first section is a single extended figure. The 'universe of things' streams through the mind like a great river, and the mind's own contribution, 'from secret springs', is like a brook that joins it 'with a sound but half its own'. The image belongs to a philosophical debate Shelley knew from Locke, Hume, Berkeley and Sir William Drummond: whether we know a world outside the mind, or only the impressions that flow through it. Wasserman read the poem as Shelley's working-out of a sceptical idealism, while other critics stress its uncertainty. The landscape that follows, the Arve rushing through its ravine, literally enacts the opening figure.

    Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959)

  3. Ravine of Arve l. 12

    Shelley's letter to Peacock describes the road along the Arve, swollen with rain and roaring through a gorge. The poem's second section addresses the ravine as 'thou', making it the outward counterpart of the mind in section I: just as thought receives the stream of things, the ravine is the 'path' of the river's ceaseless sound and motion. The Arve's 'commotion' and its 'giant brood of pines' make the valley a scene of the natural sublime, in the sense of Burke's Enquiry (1757): vastness, obscurity, power and danger producing awe.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757)

  4. Power ll. 16-17

    Shelley avoids the word 'God'. Power comes down 'in likeness of' the Arve, as the Holy Spirit descends in the likeness of a dove (Luke 3:22), from 'ice-gulfs' that hide its 'secret throne'. The poem never sees Power directly, only its manifestations, and section IV states that 'Power dwells apart in its tranquillity, / Remote, serene, and inaccessible'. Critics disagree whether Power is a sceptic's name for unknown natural necessity, a neutral force indifferent to humanity, or something like Shelley's 'Intellectual Beauty' (the hymn of the same summer). Its final form is 'The secret strength of things / Which governs thought'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Luke 3:22; Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  5. unsculptured image ll. 25-27

    The 'ethereal waterfall' (in the letters, the falls of the valley such as those Shelley saw near Servoz) is veiled in spray with rainbows, and the veil suggests a robed statue beneath. 'Unsculptured' implies both natural and inexpressible: the image beneath has never been shaped, so the veil may cover nothing, or something beyond representation. The figure of the veil recurs in Shelley, most famously in the sonnet 'Lift not the painted veil which those who live / Call Life', and in this poem in 'The veil of life and death' (l. 54).

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'

  6. my own separate fantasy ll. 35-40

    The lines turn the poem from description to self-reflection, and they echo Wordsworth's language in 'Tintern Abbey' of what the eye and ear 'half create, / And what perceive'. 'Fantasy' here means the imagination or the image-making faculty. 'Renders and receives' describes the mind as both giving back and taking in. The passage is notoriously hard to construe: its wandering thoughts float over the ravine and rest in 'the still cave of the witch Poesy', and the syntax of ll. 41-48 is disputed. Shelley's reworking of Wordsworth's ideas of mind and nature is central to readings of the poem.

    William Wordsworth, 'Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey', Lyrical Ballads (1798); Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  7. the still cave of the witch Poesy l. 44

    The cave recalls Plato's allegory of the cave (Republic VII), in which prisoners see only shadows of real things; it also suggests the oracular caves of classical prophecy. Poesy, the poetic faculty, is a 'witch' because it conjures images. The poet's thoughts seek among the shadows 'some shade of thee, / Some phantom, some faint image' of the ravine, until the mind recalls them and the ravine is suddenly present: 'thou art there!' The passage is Shelley's account of how poetic images both represent and fall short of their objects, a theme he developed in A Defence of Poetry (1821).

    Plato, Republic VII (514a-520a), the allegory of the cave; Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  8. Some say that gleams of a remoter world / Visit the soul in sleep ll. 49-57

    The 'some say' introduces a received belief, possibly the Platonic or Wordsworthian idea (the 'Immortality Ode') that intimations of another world reach the soul in sleep or childhood. The poet then asks whether some 'unknown omnipotence' has drawn back 'The veil of life and death', or whether he lies in dream. The spirit 'fails', driven like a cloud among 'viewless gales' (invisible winds; 'viewless' is a poetic word found in Shakespeare and Milton). The uncertainty is characteristic: Shelley's poem moves by questions, not assertions, which is why critics have read it both as sceptical and as quasi-religious.

    Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959)

  9. The veil of life and death l. 54

    Veils in Shelley usually mark the limit of human knowledge; to lift one is either to see truth or to discover emptiness. The image links section III to the veiled waterfall of section II and anticipates the mountain's appearance, 'Far, far above', which is the poem's nearest approach to an unveiled vision. Mont Blanc itself, 'still, snowy, and serene', is seen but does not explain itself.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'

  10. And the wolf tracts her there l. 69

    The 1817 printing in History of a Six Weeks' Tour reads 'tracts', which the archive keeps. Editors have either emended it to 'tracks' or explained 'tracts' as an older form of 'tracks' or 'traces', meaning that the wolf follows the eagle to scavenge the hunter's bone. The detail makes the 'desert peopled by the storms alone' a place where only predators and the dead remain, preparing for the hideously heaped shapes of the next lines. Students should note which text their edition follows.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'Mont Blanc'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tract, v.'

  11. the old Earthquake-daemon ll. 71-75

    'Daemon' in its Greek sense is a spirit, neither good nor evil. The questions glance at contemporary geological debates: whether mountains were raised by subterranean fire and upheaval (the Plutonist view associated with James Hutton) or deposited by water. Shelley's letter from Chamonix discusses Buffon's 'sublime but gloomy theory' that the earth will at last be frozen, which also informs section IV's advancing glaciers. 'None can reply, all seems eternal now' expresses the limits of such speculation: the mountain gives no answer.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; James Hutton, Theory of the Earth (1795)

  12. awful doubt, or faith so mild ... But for such faith ll. 76-79

    The 'mysterious tongue' of the wilderness speaks to some of 'awful doubt' (awe-inspiring scepticism) and to others of a mild faith. Line 79 is a crux. 'But for' can mean 'only by means of', so that such faith reconciles man with nature, or 'were it not for', so that faith is the obstacle and doubt the path to reconciliation. The second reading fits Shelley's hostility to conventional religion: a sceptical awe would reconcile humanity with nature, but a mild faith prevents it. Editors and critics have argued for both readings, and the line is best read as deliberately poised.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  13. to repeal / Large codes of fraud and woe ll. 80-83

    'Repeal' is a parliamentary word: the mountain's silent lesson abolishes oppressive laws and creeds, which for Shelley included both despotic government and the religion of a vengeful creator. That week at Chamonix Shelley entered his name in hotel registers with the Greek word atheos ('atheist'), an act reported by later visitors such as Southey, so the political and religious reading of the lines is well grounded. The voice is 'not understood / By all': only 'the wise, and great, and good' can 'Interpret, or make felt, or deeply feel' it, three different relations to truth (the philosopher's, the poet's, the feeling heart's).

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'Mont Blanc'; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959)

  14. the daedal earth l. 86

    The adjective derives from Daedalus, the legendary craftsman, via Latin daedalus, 'cunningly wrought'. Lucretius opens De rerum natura with an invocation to Venus, for whom 'daedala tellus' sends up sweet flowers. Shelley admired Lucretius, the poet of materialist philosophy, and uses 'daedal' in several poems, including Prometheus Unbound. The allusion is apt here: section IV lists the living world of fields, lakes and creatures, which 'Are born and die' in endless revolution, a Lucretian picture of a nature without providence.

    Lucretius, De rerum natura I.7 ('daedala tellus'), Latin text; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'daedal'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  15. Power dwells apart in its tranquillity ll. 96-97

    The line sets an immovable source against everything that moves: all things 'revolve, subside, and swell', but Power is 'Remote, serene, and inaccessible'. Many commentators compare the Epicurean gods described by Lucretius, who live in tranquil detachment from human affairs. The mountain itself, 'the naked countenance of earth', is said to 'Teach the adverting mind' (the mind that turns towards it, attends). What it teaches is ambiguous: indifference, or a power so far beyond human values that it neither punishes nor rewards.

    Lucretius, De rerum natura I.7 ('daedala tellus'), Latin text; Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  16. The glaciers creep / Like snakes that watch their prey ll. 100-120

    In his letter to Peacock Shelley described the glaciers advancing on the valley, overthrowing forests and fields, and he mentioned Buffon's theory of a future frozen globe. The poem turns those observations into a vision of nature's destructive power. The glaciers are a 'flood of ruin' that 'Rolls its perpetual stream', destroying the 'dwelling-place / Of insects, beasts, and birds' and making 'The race / Of man' flee. The phrase 'their place is not known' echoes Psalm 103:16 ('the place thereof shall know it no more'). Yet the same ice feeds 'one majestic River, / The breath and blood of distant lands', so destruction and life come from one source.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Psalm 103:16; Job 7:10; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  17. The secret strength of things / Which governs thought ll. 139-141

    Section V returns to the summit, where snows fall unseen and winds and lightning act 'innocently' in silence. Shelley stresses that none beholds them: the mountain's power works without witnesses. The 'secret strength of things' resembles the Lucretian or Newtonian idea of natural law, but Shelley also says it 'governs thought', closing the circle with the poem's opening, in which the universe flows through the mind. 'Infinite dome / Of heaven' recalls the 'dome' imagery of his other poems, such as the 'dome of many-coloured glass' in Adonais.

    Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  18. If to the human mind's imaginings / Silence and solitude were vacancy? ll. 142-144

    The ending is one of the most discussed in Romantic poetry. One reading is humanist: the mountain matters only because the imagination fills its silence with meaning, so mind confers value on nature. Another is sceptical: the question admits that silence and solitude may be vacancy, and that the imagination may be projecting. A third, Wasserman's, takes the line as an affirmation that the mind's imaginings reveal the power genuinely present in things. The rhetorical question, without a question of fact, leaves the poem poised between these, and its last word, 'vacancy', is a hard word to end on.

    Earl R. Wasserman, Shelley: A Critical Reading (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1971), chapter on 'Mont Blanc'; Harold Bloom, Shelley's Mythmaking (New Haven: Yale, 1959); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes

  19. Form: irregular rhyme whole poem

    The poem looks like blank verse, but rhymes occur at irregular intervals and sometimes at a distance (as 'sweep' and 'sleep', ll. 25-27, or 'tame' and 'came' within section II), so the ear hears an order that it cannot predict. Critics connect this with the preface's claim to imitate the scene's 'wildness'; it also suggests the Pindaric ode, and some readers hear an answer to Coleridge's 'Hymn Before Sun-rise, in the Vale of Chamouni' (1802), whose pious apostrophe to the mountain Shelley's sceptical poem revises. The five sections progress from mind and ravine to the summit's secret power.

    [Mary and Percy Shelley], History of a Six Weeks' Tour (London: Hookham and Ollier, 1817), Preface and 'Mont Blanc. Lines written in the Vale of Chamouni'; Letter IV (Shelley to Peacock, July 1816); S. T. Coleridge, 'Hymn Before Sun-rise, in the Vale of Chamouni' (Morning Post, 1802); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., 2002), 'Mont Blanc', notes
