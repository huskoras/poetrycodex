Sonnet: England in 1819

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From Poems of 1819

History & Politics

An old, mad, blind, despised, and dying king,—
Princes, the dregs of their dull race, who flow
Through public scorn,—mud from a muddy spring,—
Rulers who neither see, nor feel, nor know,
But leech-like to their fainting country cling,
Till they drop, blind in blood, without a blow,—
A people starved and stabbed in the untilled field,—
An army, which liberticide and prey
Makes as a two-edged sword to all who wield,—
Golden and sanguine laws which tempt and slay;
Religion Christless, Godless—a book sealed;
A Senate,—Time’s worst statute, unrepealed,—
Are graves from which a glorious Phantom may
Burst, to illumine our tempestuous day.

Study notes by the editors

  1. England in 1819: occasion and publication l. 1

    Shelley wrote the sonnet in Italy between September and December 1819, in the wake of the Peterloo Massacre (16 August 1819) and the government's repressive response. He sent it to Leigh Hunt with a letter dated 23 December 1819, saying he did not expect Hunt to publish it but that he might show it to whom he pleased. Given the prosecutions for seditious libel then under way, it could not safely be printed; Mary Shelley published it in the Poetical Works of 1839 and supplied the title, the poem being untitled in the letter. Chandler takes the poem's title as the starting point for his study of 1819 as a self-conscious historical moment.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; 'England in 1819', Wikipedia (summary of publication history and readings); James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904), text and Mary Shelley's notes

  2. An old, mad, blind, despised, and dying king l. 1

    George III (reigned 1760-1820) suffered recurrent episodes of mental illness, and from 1811 was permanently incapacitated; his son governed as Prince Regent. By 1819 he was also blind and secluded at Windsor. He died on 29 January 1820, within weeks of the poem. The line's five adjectives pile up without a verb, beginning the long list of subjects that delays the main verb to l. 13. Shelley's line has an analogue in the pathos of Lear, the old mad king, though here pity is withheld.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  3. Princes, the dregs of their dull race l. 2

    The 'Princes' are the sons of George III, notorious for debt, mistresses and scandal: chiefly the Prince Regent (later George IV), but also the Dukes of York, Clarence, Cumberland and others. 'Dregs' are the sediment at the bottom of a vessel; the 'race' (family line) is 'dull', stupid and spiritless. The metaphor flows into the next line: they move 'Through public scorn' like 'mud from a muddy spring', the polluted source of a corrupt stream.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dreg, n.'

  4. mud from a muddy spring l. 3

    The figure of fountain and stream was a commonplace for the relation of a ruler to the nation and of a parent to offspring (compare Proverbs 25:26, 'a troubled fountain, and a corrupt spring'). Shelley turns it against hereditary monarchy: the source is muddy, so its outflow is mud. The repetition 'mud... muddy' gives the line an insulting, spitting energy.

    Bible (KJV), Proverbs 25:26; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  5. Rulers who neither see, nor feel, nor know l. 4

    Psalms 115 and 135 deride idols that 'have eyes, but they see not... ears, but they hear not'. Shelley's triad turns the formula on the governing class: blind to suffering, without sympathy, ignorant. Here 'Rulers' probably means ministers and the governing oligarchy (Liverpool's government, with Castlereagh and Sidmouth), distinct from the royal princes. The charge of not seeing links them to the 'blind' king of l. 1 and anticipates 'blind in blood' in l. 6.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 115:4-6; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998)

  6. leech-like to their fainting country cling l. 5

    Leeches were used medicinally for bloodletting; 'leech' was also an old word for a physician. The rulers who pretend to be the country's doctors are in fact parasites, drawing blood until the patient faints. Shelley's political prose of the same year, A Philosophical View of Reform, attacks the national debt and taxation as means by which the wealthy live off the labour of the poor; the image condenses that argument.

    Shelley, A Philosophical View of Reform (written 1819-20); The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'leech, n.1 and n.2'

  7. Till they drop, blind in blood, without a blow l. 6

    Leeches drop off their host when gorged with blood. 'Blind in blood' plays on their being sightless and bloated, and perhaps on the blood they have shed. 'Without a blow' is often read as Shelley's characteristic hope for a non-violent transformation, consistent with the passive resistance urged in The Mask of Anarchy; others take it as contempt (the rulers will collapse without anyone needing to strike them). Either way the line predicts the system's self-destruction.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  8. A people starved and stabbed in the untilled field l. 7

    A peaceful crowd of some 60,000 gathered in St Peter's Field to hear Henry Hunt speak for parliamentary reform; the Manchester yeomanry and hussars charged with sabres, killing about fifteen and injuring hundreds. Shelley heard the news in Italy in September and responded with The Mask of Anarchy. The people were 'starved' by economic depression and the Corn Laws, and 'stabbed' by the sabres; 'untilled' suggests both the unemployment of the starving (land and labour left idle) and the ironic name 'field' for an urban space.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); Shelley, The Mask of Anarchy (written 1819, published 1832)

  9. liberticide l. 8

    'Liberticide' (Latin libertas + -cida, killer, on the model of 'homicide', 'regicide') means the destruction of liberty. The word had currency in the political vocabulary of the French Revolution and its British sympathizers. Here it is a noun, paired with 'prey', that forms the subject of the singular verb 'Makes': liberty-killing and plunder make the army (the relative 'which') a two-edged sword to all who wield it. Some editors note the syntax of ll. 8-9 is compressed and has been variously parsed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'liberticide, n.'; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  10. a two-edged sword to all who wield l. 9

    The 'two-edged sword' is biblical (Hebrews 4:12; Revelation 1:16), and here suggests a weapon that cuts both ways: an army used against the people may turn on its masters, a warning heard in Jesus' saying that 'all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword' (Matthew 26:52). Radicals of the period opposed a standing army in peacetime as an instrument of tyranny. The line thus repeats the poem's pattern of oppression containing the seeds of its own downfall.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 4:12; Bible (KJV), Matthew 26:52; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes

  11. Golden and sanguine laws which tempt and slay l. 10

    'Golden' suggests laws bought or serving wealth (and the corruption of paper money and taxes Shelley attacked in his prose); 'sanguine' (Latin sanguis, blood) means bloody, with the capital statutes and the repression of 1819 in view, perhaps also the 'Six Acts' passed in December 1819 to curb meetings and the press. 'Tempt and slay' matches the pair of adjectives: gold tempts, blood slays. Some readers take the laws as tempting the poor into crime and then hanging them.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sanguine, adj.'

  12. Religion Christless, Godless l. 11

    Shelley, expelled from Oxford in 1811 for The Necessity of Atheism, was no defender of Christianity, yet he often contrasted the ethical teaching of Jesus with the institutional Church allied to the state. The Church of England, with bishops in the House of Lords, supported repressive government. The charge 'Christless, Godless' uses Christian standards to condemn official religion: a church that blesses a government which starves and sabres the poor has, in Shelley's view, lost both the Christ of the Gospels and any God worth the name. The word-pair, with its heavy stresses, is the bluntest phrase in the poem.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Shelley, 'Essay on Christianity' (c. 1817), in Shelley's Prose, ed. D. L. Clark (1954)

  13. a book sealed l. 11

    In Isaiah 29:11 the vision becomes 'as the words of a book that is sealed', which the learned cannot read; in Revelation 5:1 a book 'sealed with seven seals' can be opened only by the Lamb. Shelley's phrase suggests that official religion keeps the true meaning of Scripture closed to the people, or that the Bible has become dead letter to the Church itself. The apocalyptic context of Revelation also prepares for the 'glorious Phantom' bursting from the grave at the poem's close.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 29:11; Bible (KJV), Revelation 5:1; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes

  14. A Senate,—Time's worst statute, unrepealed l. 12

    'Senate' gives Parliament a Roman name, implying an aristocratic body. The appositive 'Time's worst statute, unrepealed' is the poem's best-known crux. Many readers identify a particular law, usually one denying Catholics political rights, not repealed until Catholic Emancipation in 1829. Chandler argues instead that the phrase describes the Senate itself: the unreformed Parliament, with its rotten boroughs, is time's worst statute, an outdated institution that should be repealed. The dash punctuation in the archive's text supports reading the phrase in apposition to 'Senate'.

    James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); 'England in 1819', Wikipedia (summary of publication history and readings); The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  15. Are graves from which... l. 13

    The sonnet is one sentence. Lines 1-12 form a list of noun phrases (king, princes, rulers, people, army, laws, religion, senate) with no main verb, until l. 13 supplies 'Are graves'. The delay enacts the oppressive weight of the catalogue, and the sudden verb turns description into prophecy: these institutions are dead things from which new life may break out. Chandler and others note how the grammar converts a list of grievances into an apocalyptic expectation.

    James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  16. a glorious Phantom may / Burst ll. 13-14

    The 'Phantom' is unnamed: freedom, revolution, a spirit of reform or a resurrected England. The image of bursting from the grave echoes Christ's resurrection and apocalyptic prophecy, giving secular hope a religious shape; compare the 'Shape' that rises to protect the people in The Mask of Anarchy and the spirit of the 'Ode to the West Wind', both of 1819. 'Phantom' may also hint at the uncertainty or insubstantiality of such hope; 'may' qualifies the prophecy. The enjambment 'may / Burst' itself bursts across the line ending.

    The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; James Chandler, England in 1819: The Politics of Literary Culture and the Case of Romantic Historicism (University of Chicago Press, 1998); Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley, 'Ode to the West Wind' (1819)

  17. The sonnet form ll. 1-14

    The rhyme scheme (king/spring/cling; flow/know/blow; field/wield/sealed/unrepealed; prey/slay/may/day) fits neither Petrarchan nor Shakespearean models: six lines on two rhymes are followed by eight on two more, and the volta comes late, at l. 13. Shelley's sonnets are often formally irregular (compare 'Ozymandias'). The final rhyme 'day', with 'tempestuous', closes on a stormy present lit by the hoped-for Phantom. The closing couplet (may / day) at last gives a clean pair of adjacent rhymes after the interlaced pattern before it, so the form itself seems to settle into place at the moment of prophecy, as the verb 'Burst' opens l. 14.

    'England in 1819', Wikipedia (summary of publication history and readings); The Poems of Shelley, vol. 3: 1819-1820, ed. Jack Donovan, Cian Duffy, Kelvin Everest and Michael Rossington (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2011), headnote and notes; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem
