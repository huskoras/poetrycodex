Nature

To a Skylark

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From Poems of 1820

Nature

Hail to thee, blithe Spirit!
Bird thou never wert,
That from Heaven, or near it,
Pourest thy full heart
In profuse strains of unpremeditated art.

Higher still and higher
From the earth thou springest
Like a cloud of fire;
The blue deep thou wingest,
And singing still dost soar, and soaring ever singest.

In the golden lightning
Of the sunken sun,
O’er which clouds are bright’ning.
Thou dost float and run;
Like an unbodied joy whose race is just begun.

The pale purple even
Melts around thy flight;
Like a star of Heaven,
In the broad daylight
Thou art unseen, but yet I hear thy shrill delight,

Keen as are the arrows
Of that silver sphere,
Whose intense lamp narrows
In the white dawn clear
Until we hardly see—we feel that it is there.

All the earth and air
With thy voice is loud,
As, when night is bare,
From one lonely cloud
The moon rains out her beams, and Heaven is overflowed.

What thou art we know not;
What is most like thee?
From rainbow clouds there flow not
Drops so bright to see
As from thy presence showers a rain of melody.

Like a Poet hidden
In the light of thought,
Singing hymns unbidden,
Till the world is wrought
To sympathy with hopes and fears it heeded not:

Like a high-born maiden
In a palace-tower,
Soothing her love-laden
Soul in secret hour
With music sweet as love, which overflows her bower:

Like a glow-worm golden
In a dell of dew,
Scattering unbeholden
Its aereal hue
Among the flowers and grass, which screen it from the view!

Like a rose embowered
In its own green leaves,
By warm winds deflowered,
Till the scent it gives
Makes faint with too much sweet those heavy-winged thieves:

Sound of vernal showers
On the twinkling grass,
Rain-awakened flowers,
All that ever was
Joyous, and clear, and fresh, thy music doth surpass:

Teach us, Sprite or Bird,
What sweet thoughts are thine:
I have never heard
Praise of love or wine
That panted forth a flood of rapture so divine.

Chorus Hymeneal,
Or triumphal chant,
Matched with thine would be all
But an empty vaunt,
A thing wherein we feel there is some hidden want.

What objects are the fountains
Of thy happy strain?
What fields, or waves, or mountains?
What shapes of sky or plain?
What love of thine own kind? what ignorance of pain?

With thy clear keen joyance
Languor cannot be:
Shadow of annoyance
Never came near thee:
Thou lovest—but ne’er knew love’s sad satiety.

Waking or asleep,
Thou of death must deem
Things more true and deep
Than we mortals dream,
Or how could thy notes flow in such a crystal stream?

We look before and after,
And pine for what is not:
Our sincerest laughter
With some pain is fraught;
Our sweetest songs are those that tell of saddest thought.

Yet if we could scorn
Hate, and pride, and fear;
If we were things born
Not to shed a tear,
I know not how thy joy we ever should come near.

Better than all measures
Of delightful sound,
Better than all treasures
That in books are found,
Thy skill to poet were, thou scorner of the ground!

Teach me half the gladness
That thy brain must know,
Such harmonious madness
From my lips would flow
The world should listen then—as I am listening now.

Study notes by the editors

  1. composition and occasion st. 1, l. 1

    Mary Shelley's note on the poems of 1820 records that the poem was inspired while 'wandering among the lanes whose myrtle-hedges were the bowers of the fire-flies' near Leghorn, when they heard a skylark singing. The skylark (Alauda arvensis) sings while rising high into the sky, often until it is invisible, which gives the poem its controlling idea of a voice heard but not seen. 'Hail to thee' is a formula of greeting to a superior being, as in the angel's 'Hail' to Mary (Luke 1:28); 'blithe' means joyful, light-hearted.

    Mary Shelley, 'Note on Poems of 1820', in Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Hutchinson (1904); P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  2. Bird thou never wert st. 1, l. 2

    Critics have long debated the line. Some take it literally as Platonist: the song comes from a higher realm and the bird is only its vehicle. Others take it as hyperbole, a way of saying the singer's art transcends its nature. Wordsworth's skylark poems keep the bird bound to its nest; Shelley's goes the other way. 'Wert' is an archaic second-person past form of 'be'.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  3. unpremeditated art st. 1, l. 5

    In Paradise Lost 9.24 Milton describes his heavenly muse dictating to him in sleep, inspiring 'Easy my unpremeditated verse'. Shelley borrows the phrase for the bird, making it the model of the inspired poet. The idea fits the Defence of Poetry, which insists that poetry is not produced by labour and study but comes from inspiration. The paradox of 'art' that is unpremeditated (art usually implying skill and design) is the stanza's point.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 9.21-24 ('my unpremeditated Verse'); P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840)

  4. the stanza st. 2, l. 6

    The short lines, three stresses each, rising and quick, mimic the lark's darting ascent; the final long line spreads out like the song lingering in the air. The contrast between brevity and expansiveness gives each stanza a rise and release. The rhyme scheme ababb lets the last line echo the fourth. The poem moves from address (st. 1-6), through a series of similes (st. 7-12), to questions and reflections on human limits (st. 13-21).

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  5. cloud of fire st. 2, l. 8

    In Exodus the Lord goes before the Israelites 'by day in a pillar of a cloud' and 'by night in a pillar of fire'. Shelley fuses the two in one image of light and lift. The comparison is the first of many that try and fail to say what the bird is: the poem's method is to multiply similes because no single one is adequate.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 13:21; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  6. an unbodied joy st. 3, l. 15

    The adjective was a favourite of Shelley's: abstractions without bodies recur in his work. The line suggests the ideal of a spirit freed from mortality. 'Whose race is just begun' adds the image of a runner starting a race, connected to Psalm 19:5, where the sun 'rejoiceth as a strong man to run a race'. The sunset setting (st. 3-4) means the lark flies in the last light, so its joy contrasts with the falling day.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:5; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  7. even st. 4, l. 16

    'Even' for evening is archaic and poetic. The stanza describes the lark invisible in the evening light, 'Like a star of Heaven, / In the broad daylight': a star is present in daytime but cannot be seen. The lark too is heard but not seen. The paradox of invisible presence governs the next stanzas. The colour words (pale, purple) also mark the poem's fascination with fading, half-visible light.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'even'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  8. that silver sphere st. 5, l. 22

    The 'silver sphere' whose 'intense lamp narrows / In the white dawn clear' is usually identified as Venus as the morning star, which is the last star visible as day breaks. Some commentators suggest the moon, but the moon is mentioned separately in the next stanza, which favours Venus. 'Keen as are the arrows' links the planet to Artemis/Diana, goddess of the moon and an archer, which may be why some readers prefer the moon. The point is the paradox in 'we hardly see—we feel that it is there'.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  9. What thou art we know not st. 7, l. 31

    The admission prepares four long similes (st. 8-11), each introduced by 'Like': a poet, a high-born maiden, a glow-worm, a rose. Each presents something hidden that yet communicates itself (through song, music, light, scent). The method has been called the poem's characteristic Shelleyan 'failure' of definition: the lark's essence can be approached only through comparisons that never quite reach it.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  10. the Poet hidden in light st. 8, l. 36

    This is the first and most important simile, because it states Shelley's theory of the poet. The poet is invisible, his influence unacknowledged, but his songs make the world 'wrought / To sympathy'. The Defence of Poetry compares the poet to 'a nightingale, who sits in darkness and sings to cheer its own solitude', whose listeners are moved without knowing why. 'Wrought' means worked, brought to a state.

    P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  11. high-born maiden st. 9, l. 41

    The maiden in a tower is a stock figure of medieval romance and its Gothic revivals. Her song, like the lark's, is unseen but escapes its confinement. 'Bower' in older usage meant a lady's private chamber. The 'secret hour' and 'love-laden soul' introduce desire, which the poem will later contrast with the lark's love without 'sad satiety'. The image makes the lark's song an expression of private feeling that becomes public by overflow.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bower'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  12. glow-worm st. 10, l. 46

    The glow-worm (in fact a beetle) shines in the dark. Mary Shelley's note on the Leghorn summer mentions the fireflies flashing in the myrtle hedges, so the image may come from the walk that inspired the poem. 'Aereal hue' (the archive keeps the older spelling) means an airy or ethereal colour. 'Unbeholden' means unseen, though it can also suggest 'not indebted'.

    Mary Shelley, 'Note on Poems of 1820', in Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Hutchinson (1904); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aerial'

  13. the rose and the bees st. 11, l. 51

    The simile is sensual and slightly disquieting: 'deflowered' and 'thieves' suggest violation, and 'faint with too much sweet' an excess that becomes pain. Critics have noted that this stanza anticipates the poem's later theme of joy mixed with suffering ('Our sweetest songs are those that tell of saddest thought'). The bees, laden with pollen, are 'heavy-winged', in contrast to the light-winged lark.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  14. Sprite or Bird st. 13, l. 61

    After declaring 'Bird thou never wert' (st. 1), the poet now allows both possibilities. The shift marks the poem's movement from rapture to questioning: from st. 13 onwards the poet asks the lark to 'teach' him the source of its joy. 'Sprite' is a doublet of 'spirit' (both from Latin spiritus). The request 'Teach us' frames the bird as a teacher and the poet as a pupil, a reversal of the usual relation between poet and subject.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sprite'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  15. Chorus Hymeneal st. 14, l. 66

    Classical weddings were celebrated with a hymenaios, a song invoking Hymen. Triumphal chants were sung for victorious generals in Rome. Shelley says that these human festive forms, compared with the lark's song, are 'but an empty vaunt' (a hollow boast), lacking something essential. 'Want' means lack. The two forms together stand for the highest occasions of shared human joy, love and victory, and both are found wanting beside the solitary bird.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hymeneal'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  16. satiety st. 16, l. 80

    The idea that fulfilment kills desire is a commonplace from Lucretius onwards, and Shelley treats it often. 'Joyance' (st. 16) is an archaic word for joy, used by Spenser; 'languor' is the listlessness that follows passion. The lark knows love without that after-sadness, which makes it a figure of an ideal state unavailable to humans. The stanza thus prepares the turn to human limitation in the stanzas that follow.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'satiety'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'joyance'

  17. We look before and after st. 18, l. 86

    In the soliloquy 'How all occasions do inform against me' (Hamlet 4.4, found only in the Second Quarto), Hamlet says God gave humans reason, 'Looking before and after', that should not rust unused. Shelley turns the capacity into a curse: because we look before and after, we 'pine for what is not'. The lark lives in the present; humans, conscious of past and future, cannot. This stanza is the emotional centre of the poem.

    Shakespeare, Hamlet 4.4 (Q2 only), Folger Shakespeare; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'

  18. Our sweetest songs st. 18, l. 90

    The line names a Romantic paradox: the lark's song is pure joy, but human beauty depends on pain. The stanza's progression ('sincerest laughter / With some pain is fraught') leads to it. The next stanza adds that even if we were free of hate, pride and fear, we could not reach the lark's joy. The line echoes widely in later poetry; Keats's 'Ode on Melancholy' (1819) makes a related claim that melancholy dwells with beauty.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; 'To a Skylark', Wikipedia (composition near Livorno, June 1820; publication with Prometheus Unbound)

  19. the closing prayer st. 21, l. 101

    'Harmonious madness' recalls the Platonic idea of the poet's divine madness (Plato, Phaedrus 245a; Ion), in which inspiration overcomes reason. The final line turns from bird to audience: 'The world should listen then—as I am listening now.' The poet's ambition is for influence, a concern of all Shelley's later writing. The lark is 'scorner of the ground', a final epithet that sums up its freedom.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'To a Skylark'; P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840)
