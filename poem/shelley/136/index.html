The Mask of Anarchy

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From The Mask of Anarchy (1832)

History & Politics

As I lay asleep in Italy
There came a voice from over the Sea,
And with great power it forth led me
To walk in the visions of Poesy.

I met Murder on the way—
He had a mask like Castlereagh—
Very smooth he looked, yet grim;
Seven blood-hounds followed him:

All were fat; and well they might
Be in admirable plight,
For one by one, and two by two,
He tossed them human hearts to chew
Which from his wide cloak he drew.

Next came Fraud, and he had on,
Like Eldon, an ermined gown;
His big tears, for he wept well,
Turned to mill-stones as they fell.

And the little children, who
Round his feet played to and fro,
Thinking every tear a gem,
Had their brains knocked out by them.

Clothed with the Bible, as with light,
And the shadows of the night,
Like Sidmouth, next, Hypocrisy
On a crocodile rode by.

And many more Destructions played
In this ghastly masquerade,
All disguised, even to the eyes,
Like Bishops, lawyers, peers, or spies.

Last came Anarchy: he rode
On a white horse, splashed with blood;
He was pale even to the lips,
Like Death in the Apocalypse.

And he wore a kingly crown;
And in his grasp a sceptre shone;
On his brow this mark I saw—
‘I AM GOD, AND KING, AND LAW!’

With a pace stately and fast,
Over English land he passed,
Trampling to a mire of blood
The adoring multitude.

And a mighty troop around,
With their trampling shook the ground,
Waving each a bloody sword,
For the service of their Lord.

And with glorious triumph, they
Rode through England proud and gay,
Drunk as with intoxication
Of the wine of desolation.

O’er fields and towns, from sea to sea,
Passed the Pageant swift and free,
Tearing up, and trampling down;
Till they came to London town.

And each dweller, panic-stricken,
Felt his heart with terror sicken
Hearing the tempestuous cry
Of the triumph of Anarchy.

For with pomp to meet him came,
Clothed in arms like blood and flame,
The hired murderers, who did sing
‘Thou art God, and Law, and King.

‘We have waited, weak and lone
For thy coming, Mighty One!
Our purses are empty, our swords are cold,
Give us glory, and blood, and gold.’

Lawyers and priests, a motley crowd,
To the earth their pale brows bowed;
Like a bad prayer not over loud,
Whispering—‘Thou art Law and God.’—

Then all cried with one accord,
‘Thou art King, and God, and Lord;
Anarchy, to thee we bow,
Be thy name made holy now!’

And Anarchy, the Skeleton,
Bowed and grinned to every one,
As well as if his education
Had cost ten millions to the nation.

For he knew the Palaces
Of our Kings were rightly his;
His the sceptre, crown, and globe,
And the gold-inwoven robe.

So he sent his slaves before
To seize upon the Bank and Tower,
And was proceeding with intent
To meet his pensioned Parliament

When one fled past, a maniac maid,
And her name was Hope, she said:
But she looked more like Despair,
And she cried out in the air:

‘My father Time is weak and gray
With waiting for a better day;
See how idiot-like he stands,
Fumbling with his palsied hands!

‘He has had child after child,
And the dust of death is piled
Over every one but me—
Misery, oh, Misery!’

Then she lay down in the street,
Right before the horses’ feet,
Expecting, with a patient eye,
Murder, Fraud, and Anarchy.

When between her and her foes
A mist, a light, an image rose,
Small at first, and weak, and frail
Like the vapour of a vale:

Till as clouds grow on the blast,
Like tower-crowned giants striding fast,
And glare with lightnings as they fly,
And speak in thunder to the sky,

It grew—a Shape arrayed in mail
Brighter than the viper’s scale,
And upborne on wings whose grain
Was as the light of sunny rain.

On its helm, seen far away,
A planet, like the Morning’s, lay;
And those plumes its light rained through
Like a shower of crimson dew.

With step as soft as wind it passed
O’er the heads of men—so fast
That they knew the presence there,
And looked,—but all was empty air.

As flowers beneath May’s footstep waken,
As stars from Night’s loose hair are shaken,
As waves arise when loud winds call,
Thoughts sprung where’er that step did fall.

And the prostrate multitude
Looked—and ankle-deep in blood,
Hope, that maiden most serene,
Was walking with a quiet mien:

And Anarchy, the ghastly birth,
Lay dead earth upon the earth;
The Horse of Death tameless as wind
Fled, and with his hoofs did grind
To dust the murderers thronged behind.

A rushing light of clouds and splendour,
A sense awakening and yet tender
Was heard and felt—and at its close
These words of joy and fear arose

As if their own indignant Earth
Which gave the sons of England birth
Had felt their blood upon her brow,
And shuddering with a mother’s throe

Had turned every drop of blood
By which her face had been bedewed
To an accent unwithstood,—
As if her heart had cried aloud:

‘Men of England, heirs of Glory,
Heroes of unwritten story,
Nurslings of one mighty Mother,
Hopes of her, and one another;

‘Rise like Lions after slumber
In unvanquishable number,
Shake your chains to earth like dew
Which in sleep had fallen on you—
Ye are many—they are few.

‘What is Freedom?—ye can tell
That which slavery is, too well—
For its very name has grown
To an echo of your own.

‘’Tis to work and have such pay
As just keeps life from day to day
In your limbs, as in a cell
For the tyrants’ use to dwell,

‘So that ye for them are made
Loom, and plough, and sword, and spade,
With or without your own will bent
To their defence and nourishment.

‘’Tis to see your children weak
With their mothers pine and peak,
When the winter winds are bleak,—
They are dying whilst I speak.

‘’Tis to hunger for such diet
As the rich man in his riot
Casts to the fat dogs that lie
Surfeiting beneath his eye;

‘’Tis to let the Ghost of Gold
Take from Toil a thousandfold
More than e’er its substance could
In the tyrannies of old.

‘Paper coin—that forgery
Of the title-deeds, which ye
Hold to something of the worth
Of the inheritance of Earth.

‘’Tis to be a slave in soul
And to hold no strong control
Over your own wills, but be
All that others make of ye.

‘And at length when ye complain
With a murmur weak and vain
’Tis to see the Tyrant’s crew
Ride over your wives and you
Blood is on the grass like dew.

‘Then it is to feel revenge
Fiercely thirsting to exchange
Blood for blood—and wrong for wrong—
Do not thus when ye are strong.

‘Birds find rest, in narrow nest
When weary of their winged quest;
Beasts find fare, in woody lair
When storm and snow are in the air.

‘Asses, swine, have litter spread
And with fitting food are fed;
All things have a home but one—
Thou, Oh, Englishman, hast none!

‘This is Slavery—savage men,
Or wild beasts within a den
Would endure not as ye do—
But such ills they never knew.

‘What art thou Freedom? O! could slaves
Answer from their living graves
This demand—tyrants would flee
Like a dream’s dim imagery:

‘Thou art not, as impostors say,
A shadow soon to pass away,
A superstition, and a name
Echoing from the cave of Fame.

‘For the labourer thou art bread,
And a comely table spread
From his daily labour come
In a neat and happy home.

Thou art clothes, and fire, and food
For the trampled multitude—
No—in countries that are free
Such starvation cannot be
As in England now we see.

‘To the rich thou art a check,
When his foot is on the neck
Of his victim, thou dost make
That he treads upon a snake.

Thou art Justice—ne’er for gold
May thy righteous laws be sold
As laws are in England—thou
Shield’st alike the high and low.

‘Thou art Wisdom—Freemen never
Dream that God will damn for ever
All who think those things untrue
Of which Priests make such ado.

‘Thou art Peace—never by thee
Would blood and treasure wasted be
As tyrants wasted them, when all
Leagued to quench thy flame in Gaul.

‘What if English toil and blood
Was poured forth, even as a flood?
It availed, Oh, Liberty,
To dim, but not extinguish thee.

‘Thou art Love—the rich have kissed
Thy feet, and like him following Christ,
Give their substance to the free
And through the rough world follow thee,

‘Or turn their wealth to arms, and make
War for thy beloved sake
On wealth, and war, and fraud—whence they
Drew the power which is their prey.

‘Science, Poetry, and Thought
Are thy lamps; they make the lot
Of the dwellers in a cot
So serene, they curse it not.

‘Spirit, Patience, Gentleness,
All that can adorn and bless
Art thou—let deeds, not words, express
Thine exceeding loveliness.

‘Let a great Assembly be
Of the fearless and the free
On some spot of English ground
Where the plains stretch wide around.

‘Let the blue sky overhead,
The green earth on which ye tread,
All that must eternal be
Witness the solemnity.

‘From the corners uttermost
Of the bounds of English coast;
From every hut, village, and town
Where those who live and suffer moan
For others’ misery or their own,

‘From the workhouse and the prison
Where pale as corpses newly risen,
Women, children, young and old
Groan for pain, and weep for cold—

‘From the haunts of daily life
Where is waged the daily strife
With common wants and common cares
Which sows the human heart with tares—

‘Lastly from the palaces
Where the murmur of distress
Echoes, like the distant sound
Of a wind alive around

‘Those prison halls of wealth and fashion,
Where some few feel such compassion
For those who groan, and toil, and wail
As must make their brethren pale—

‘Ye who suffer woes untold,
Or to feel, or to behold
Your lost country bought and sold
With a price of blood and gold—

‘Let a vast assembly be,
And with great solemnity
Declare with measured words that ye
Are, as God has made ye, free—

‘Be your strong and simple words
Keen to wound as sharpened swords,
And wide as targes let them be,
With their shade to cover ye.

‘Let the tyrants pour around
With a quick and startling sound,
Like the loosening of a sea,
Troops of armed emblazonry.

‘Let the charged artillery drive
Till the dead air seems alive
With the clash of clanging wheels,
And the tramp of horses’ heels.

‘Let the fixed bayonet
Gleam with sharp desire to wet
Its bright point in English blood
Looking keen as one for food.

Let the horsemen’s scimitars
Wheel and flash, like sphereless stars
Thirsting to eclipse their burning
In a sea of death and mourning.

‘Stand ye calm and resolute,
Like a forest close and mute,
With folded arms and looks which are
Weapons of unvanquished war,

‘And let Panic, who outspeeds
The career of armed steeds
Pass, a disregarded shade
Through your phalanx undismayed.

‘Let the laws of your own land,
Good or ill, between ye stand
Hand to hand, and foot to foot,
Arbiters of the dispute,

‘The old laws of England—they
Whose reverend heads with age are gray,
Children of a wiser day;
And whose solemn voice must be
Thine own echo—Liberty!

‘On those who first should violate
Such sacred heralds in their state
Rest the blood that must ensue,
And it will not rest on you.

‘And if then the tyrants dare
Let them ride among you there,
Slash, and stab, and maim, and hew,—
What they like, that let them do.

‘With folded arms and steady eyes,
And little fear, and less surprise,
Look upon them as they slay
Till their rage has died away.

Then they will return with shame
To the place from which they came,
And the blood thus shed will speak
In hot blushes on their cheek.

‘Every woman in the land
Will point at them as they stand—
They will hardly dare to greet
Their acquaintance in the street.

‘And the bold, true warriors
Who have hugged Danger in wars
Will turn to those who would be free,
Ashamed of such base company.

‘And that slaughter to the Nation
Shall steam up like inspiration,
Eloquent, oracular;
A volcano heard afar.

‘And these words shall then become
Like Oppression’s thundered doom
Ringing through each heart and brain,
Heard again—again—again—

‘Rise like Lions after slumber
In unvanquishable number—
Shake your chains to earth like dew
Which in sleep had fallen on you—
Ye are many—they are few.’

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Peterloo ll. 1-4

    On 16 August 1819 a peaceful crowd of perhaps 60,000 gathered in St Peter's Field, Manchester, to hear the radical Henry Hunt speak for parliamentary reform; local yeomanry and then regular hussars charged with sabres, killing some fifteen to eighteen people and injuring hundreds. The press named it 'Peterloo' in mockery of Waterloo. Shelley, living near Livorno, heard in early September and wrote to Ollier that his indignation was still boiling. He sent the poem to Leigh Hunt for The Examiner, but Hunt, fearing prosecution for seditious libel, held it back until 1832, the year of the Reform Act. The dream-vision opening ('a voice from over the Sea') frames the poem as a visionary report from exile.

    Shelley to Charles Ollier, 6 September 1819, in The Letters of Percy Bysshe Shelley, ed. F. L. Jones (Oxford, 1964), vol. II; Leigh Hunt, Preface to Percy Bysshe Shelley, The Masque of Anarchy (London: Edward Moxon, 1832); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; David S. Miall, 'The Mask of Anarchy' (University of Alberta teaching site: text, context and notes)

  2. Mask / masque title; l. 6

    Shelley's manuscript title is 'The Mask of Anarchy', and Hunt printed it in 1832 as 'The Masque of Anarchy'. Both senses operate. A masque was an aristocratic entertainment of allegorical figures in procession, and Shelley turns it into a 'ghastly masquerade' (l. 27) in which Murder, Fraud and Hypocrisy wear the faces of ministers. The masks also reverse the expected direction of disguise: the vices are not hidden behind respectable faces, but the respectable faces are revealed as masks of vice. The device owes something to the popular satirical prints of the period, in which politicians appeared as allegorical monsters.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'; Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819

  3. Murder ... Castlereagh ll. 5-13

    Castlereagh (1769-1822) was the government's chief spokesman in the House of Commons and had been Chief Secretary for Ireland when the 1798 rebellion was put down with great severity; radicals regarded him as the embodiment of reaction at home and abroad, through the Congress of Vienna and the Holy Alliance. Shelley makes him Murder, 'Very smooth' yet grim, feeding human hearts to seven fat bloodhounds; editors have tried to identify the hounds with the allied powers of the post-Napoleonic settlement, though the reference is not certain. Byron attacked Castlereagh with similar violence in the dedication to Don Juan written the year before.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980); Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'

  4. Fraud ... Eldon ll. 14-21

    The ermined gown is the robe of the Lord Chancellor. Eldon (1751-1838) was known for tears on the bench and for interminable delays in Chancery. After Harriet Shelley's suicide in 1816, her family petitioned to keep her children from Shelley, citing his atheism and his views on marriage, and in March 1817 Eldon ruled against him; Shelley had already cursed him in the poem 'To the Lord Chancellor'. Here the Chancellor's tears turn to millstones that dash out children's brains, a grotesque image that mixes private grievance with public charge, since the legal system was seen as crushing the poor.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980)

  5. Hypocrisy ... Sidmouth ... crocodile ll. 22-25

    As Home Secretary (1812-22) Sidmouth was responsible for internal security: the suspension of habeas corpus in 1817, the use of informers and agents provocateurs, and the congratulations sent to the Manchester magistrates after Peterloo. 'Clothed with the Bible' glances at his support for the Church Building Act of 1818, which granted a million pounds for new Anglican churches in the growing towns, while the poor went hungry. The crocodile, which proverbially weeps as it devours, completes the image of piety masking cruelty. The following stanza extends the parade to 'Bishops, lawyers, peers, or spies'.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'; Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819

  6. spies l. 29

    In 1817 a government agent, W. J. Richards, known as Oliver, encouraged plans for an armed rising in Derbyshire; its leaders were hanged and beheaded in November 1817, and the exposure of Oliver's role in the radical press caused a scandal. Shelley responded with his pamphlet An Address to the People on the Death of the Princess Charlotte, which contrasted national mourning for the princess with the executions. The line's flat list sets the established professions (church, law, aristocracy) beside paid informers, as equally disguised instruments of 'Destructions'.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980); P. B. Shelley, An Address to the People on the Death of the Princess Charlotte (1817)

  7. Anarchy ... Like Death in the Apocalypse ll. 30-33

    In Revelation 6:8 the fourth horseman, on a pale horse, is named Death, and Hell follows him. Shelley's Anarchy is the climax of the procession, crowned and sceptred, and the irony is pointed: conservatives used 'anarchy' for the threat of revolution, but here the ruling order itself is lawless violence, trampling 'The adoring multitude'. The inscription on his brow, 'I AM GOD, AND KING, AND LAW', recalls the names written on the harlot's forehead and on the rider's vesture in Revelation (compare 17:5 and 19:16) and parodies the alliance of throne, altar and courts. The anarchist philosopher William Godwin, Shelley's father-in-law, had argued that government itself is the chief source of disorder.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Revelation 6:8; 17:5; 19:16; Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  8. I AM GOD, AND KING, AND LAW l. 37

    The phrase is repeated and reshuffled by the hired soldiers ('Thou art God, and Law, and King'), by lawyers and priests ('Thou art Law and God'), and by all ('Thou art King, and God, and Lord'), until the litany ends in a parody of the Lord's Prayer, 'Be thy name made holy now'. The rearrangements suggest that the terms are interchangeable masks of one power. Scrivener reads the poem as an expression of Shelley's philosophical anarchism, derived from Godwin, in which these institutions are forms of a single domination. The later stanzas define a true 'Law' against this one, in the 'old laws of England'.

    Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Matthew 6:9

  9. the Bank and Tower ... pensioned Parliament ll. 82-85

    In December 1816 a breakaway group from a radical meeting at Spa Fields, London, marched towards the City with talk of seizing the Tower and the Bank; the government made much of the episode to justify repression. Shelley reverses the charge: the real conspirators against the nation's wealth and arms are its rulers. The 'pensioned Parliament' refers to the placemen and holders of government pensions and sinecures who, radicals complained, filled the unreformed House of Commons. The procession is about to take its seat when Hope interrupts it.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980)

  10. a maniac maid ... Hope ll. 86-101

    Her father Time is old, 'idiot-like' and palsied, worn out with waiting for a better day; all his children but her are dead. The image reverses the classical Time who devours his children (Kronos/Saturn) into a feeble parent who has outlived them. Hope's act, lying down in the street before the cavalry, is an early figure for the non-violent resistance the poem later recommends to the crowd. Some critics have seen in it a memory of the women at Peterloo, several of whom were among the killed and wounded. Her transformation into 'Hope, that maiden most serene' (l. 128) marks the turn of the poem.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819

  11. a Shape arrayed in mail ll. 102-125

    The Shape is never named. Critics variously identify it as the spirit of Liberty, of the people's collective will, or of revolutionary thought; its effect is psychological rather than physical, since men feel its presence but see 'empty air', and the consequence is the birth of 'Thoughts'. The helmet's planet 'like the Morning's' is Venus as morning star, Lucifer, 'light-bearer', a sign of a new day, and it rains 'crimson dew' through its plumes. The stanza of similes that follows ('As flowers beneath May's footstep waken') belongs to Shelley's recurring imagery of regeneration, close to that of Prometheus Unbound, written in the same year.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'

  12. A planet, like the Morning's l. 115

    In the KJV, Isaiah 14:12 addresses the fallen king of Babylon as 'O Lucifer, son of the morning', a text later applied to Satan; Shelley, like Blake, tended to reverse the orthodox valuation and treat the light-bringer as a figure of rebellion against tyranny. In The Revolt of Islam (1817) the morning star appears as a positive symbol in the opening canto. Here the planet on the Shape's helmet promises the dawn that follows the night of the procession, and its light, filtered through plumes, falls as 'crimson dew', an image that anticipates the refrain's chains shaken off 'like dew'.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version (1611), Isaiah 14:12; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'

  13. Rise like Lions after slumber ll. 151-155, 368-372

    Milton's Areopagitica (1644) pictures England as a noble nation 'rousing herself like a strong man after sleep, and shaking her invincible locks', the figure of Samson. Shelley's lions waking and shaking off chains 'like dew' combine strength with ease: the chains are as insubstantial as the dew that fell in the night of sleep, so oppression depends on the people's passivity. The refrain frames the long speech of the Earth (ll. 147-372) and gives the poem its most famous line, 'Ye are many, they are few', which has been taken up by later radical and labour movements.

    John Milton, Areopagitica (1644); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980)

  14. Ye are many, they are few l. 155

    The idea had a long radical pedigree; it is close to David Hume's observation, in 'Of the First Principles of Government' (1741), that the many are governed by the few only through opinion, and to the writings of Paine and Godwin. Shelley gives it the force of a slogan by its brevity and its place as the extra fifth line of the refrain stanza. It has often been quoted at political rallies, and Jeremy Corbyn quoted the closing stanza at Glastonbury in 2017, and some commentators have suggested that the title of Labour's 2017 manifesto, 'For the Many, Not the Few', echoes it, though that wording had stood in the party's Clause IV since 1995. Its meaning in context, though, is followed by a call to non-violent assembly rather than armed rising.

    David Hume, 'Of the First Principles of Government', Essays, Moral and Political (1741); Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  15. Paper coin, that forgery ll. 180-183

    The Bank Restriction Act of 1797 suspended the convertibility of banknotes into gold to finance the war against France; it was ended only by Peel's Act of 1819, passed shortly before Peterloo. Cobbett's Paper against Gold (1810-11) argued that paper money and the national debt transferred wealth from labourers to fundholders. Shelley develops the same case in A Philosophical View of Reform (1819-20). Here the 'Ghost of Gold', paper credit, takes from toil a thousandfold more than gold ever did, and paper money is called a forgery of the title-deeds to the people's 'inheritance of Earth'.

    William Cobbett, Paper against Gold (1810-11; collected 1815); P. B. Shelley, A Philosophical View of Reform (written 1819-20; published 1920); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  16. Leagued to quench thy flame in Gaul ll. 241-246

    'Gaul' is the Latin name for France. In Shelley's radical reading, the coalitions formed against France from 1792 were a league of kings to crush the Revolution's liberty; English 'toil and blood' were poured out for that end, and the national debt contracted to pay for it weighed on the poor. Shelley elsewhere, notably in the preface to The Revolt of Islam, analysed how the Revolution's excesses had produced despair among its former sympathisers, and he insists here that liberty was dimmed but not put out.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; P. B. Shelley, Preface to The Revolt of Islam (1818)

  17. Let a great Assembly be ll. 262-266

    The remedy proposed is not insurrection but a lawful mass meeting, of the kind the Manchester crowd had attended, gathered from every corner of the country, from workhouses, prisons and even palaces. The assembly is to declare 'with measured words' that its members are free; its words should wound like swords and protect like 'targes'. The poem thus defends the right of public assembly, which the government's Six Acts of December 1819 would restrict. Scholars disagree whether this programme is a realistic political strategy, a visionary ideal, or a deliberately restrained proposal fitted to a newspaper audience.

    Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  18. targes l. 301

    'Targe' is an old word for a shield, especially the small round shield of the Highland soldier, and the diminutive 'target' first meant the same. The image balances the previous line, in which words are 'Keen to wound as sharpened swords', so that speech serves both as attack and as defence. It fits the poem's trust in persuasion: the assembled people resist by declaration and by their moral authority, not by arms.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'targe'; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  19. With folded arms and steady eyes ll. 340-347

    The Earth's speech tells the crowd to stand 'calm and resolute, / Like a forest close and mute', to let the soldiers 'Slash, and stab, and maim, and hew', and to watch until their rage dies away; the shed blood will then speak 'In hot blushes on their cheek', women will scorn them, and soldiers will desert to the people. Some readers see a principled doctrine of passive resistance, later admired by campaigners for non-violent protest. Others, such as Paul Foot, read the advice tactically, as a way of seizing moral advantage, and note the threat of retribution ('A volcano heard afar'). The stanza's plain diction turns Peterloo itself into a model rather than a defeat.

    Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980); Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  20. The old laws of England ll. 331-335

    Radicals of the period often appealed to the 'ancient constitution' and to Magna Carta and the Bill of Rights as guarantees of freedom from arbitrary power, claiming that reform restored rather than overthrew English liberty. Shelley's Earth personifies these laws as gray-headed elders whose voice must echo 'Liberty', and lays the blame for any bloodshed on whoever first violates them. The appeal to law is tactical as well as sincere: it puts the people on the side of legality and the magistrates outside it. It contrasts with Anarchy's motto 'LAW' at the start of the poem.

    Michael Henry Scrivener, Radical Shelley: The Philosophical Anarchism and Utopian Thought of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Princeton, 1982), ch. on the poems of 1819; Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes

  21. Eloquent, oracular; / A volcano heard afar ll. 360-363

    The image mixes the Delphic oracle, whose priestess was said to prophesy from vapours rising out of the earth, with the volcano, a favourite Romantic figure for revolution. Shelley had visited Vesuvius in December 1818 and wrote about it in his letters. The volcano is 'heard afar', so the effect is a menacing promise rather than an explosion within the poem. The stanza thus qualifies the non-violent advice that precedes it, a balance critics read differently.

    Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Paul Foot, Red Shelley (London: Sidgwick & Jackson, 1980)

  22. Metre and stanza ll. 1-4

    The poem's 372 lines use a short, emphatic line of seven or eight syllables, often trochaic in rhythm, rhymed in couplets (aabb), with some stanzas extended to five lines by a third rhyme. The measure resembles popular broadside verse and hymns, and Shelley wrote to Hunt in late 1819 of his wish to produce a volume of popular songs for the people; poems such as 'Song to the Men of England' belong to the same project. Hunt's 1832 preface praised the poem's directness. The plain style contrasts with the dense symbolism of Prometheus Unbound, composed in the same year.

    Leigh Hunt, Preface to Percy Bysshe Shelley, The Masque of Anarchy (London: Edward Moxon, 1832); Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat, eds., Shelley's Poetry and Prose, Norton Critical Edition (2nd ed., New York, 2002), 'The Mask of Anarchy', headnote and notes; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1904; rev. 1914), 'The Mask of Anarchy', with Mary Shelley's 'Note on Poems of 1819'
