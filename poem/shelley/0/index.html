Ozymandias

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

History & PoliticsTime & BrevityArts & Sciences

I met a traveller from an antique land,
Who said—“Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand,
Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown,
And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,
Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,
The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed;
And on the pedestal, these words appear:
My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;
Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare
The lone and level sands stretch far away.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Empire reduced to a shattered face in the sand. The sculptor’s mockery outlasts the king it served, and Shelley lets the desert have the last word on every tyrant’s boast: ‘Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!’

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ozymandias: occasion and publication l. 1

    Shelley and the banker-poet Horace Smith each wrote a sonnet on the same subject during Smith's stay with the Shelleys at Marlow in the Christmas season of 1817. Hunt printed Shelley's in The Examiner on 11 January 1818 under the pen-name 'Glirastes'; Smith's followed on 1 February. Hutchinson records the Examiner printing, the reprint with Rosalind and Helen (1819), and a Bodleian manuscript. The opening words, 'I met a traveller', set up a frame: everything after them is reported speech, so the king's boast reaches us at third hand (sculptor, traveller, poet). That distancing is part of the irony. Readers in 1818 would also have heard a comment on contemporary power, coming two years after Napoleon's fall and in the repressive Regency years.

    P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes; 'Ozymandias', Wikipedia (composition with Horace Smith; Examiner publication, 11 Jan. 1818, signed 'Glirastes'); Horace Smith, 'On a Stupendous Leg of Granite' (Examiner, 1 Feb. 1818), summarised in 'Ozymandias (Smith)', Wikipedia

  2. antique land l. 1

    Shelley leaves the place unnamed, which makes the scene emblematic rather than topographical. His knowledge was literary: Diodorus Siculus's description of the tomb of 'Osymandyas' at Thebes, and the travel literature that followed Napoleon's Egyptian expedition (1798). The traveller stands in for that whole body of reports. The interest of the British Museum in Egyptian colossi was topical: the great head of Ramesses II (the 'Younger Memnon') had been acquired by the consul Henry Salt and was being shipped to England when the sonnets were written (it reached London in 1818, and the British Museum acquired it from Salt in 1821); there is no evidence Shelley saw it. 'Antique' in its older sense meant both 'ancient' and, by association, 'grotesque'.

    Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933); 'Younger Memnon' (British Museum EA 19), Wikipedia: head of Ramesses II from the Ramesseum, shipped for Henry Salt, in London 1818, acquired by the Museum 1821; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'antique'

  3. trunkless legs l. 2

    The fragmentary image opens the description with absurdity: what was meant to proclaim power now stands as two stumps. Diodorus describes a seated colossus whose foot alone exceeded seven cubits; Shelley's standing legs and fallen face are invented, and give the poem its central contrast between what endures (stone, the sculptor's art) and what does not (the king's empire). The ellipsis that follows in this text marks the traveller's pause as his eye moves from the legs to the face half buried in sand.

    Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933); P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  4. visage l. 4

    The word is slightly elevated and literary, fitting the description of a monument. The face is the only part of the king that is 'read' in the poem, and it is read for character: frown, wrinkled lip, and sneer. Shelley makes the broken face more expressive than the whole statue would have been, since fragmentation has left only the signs of arrogance. Compare Diodorus, who praises the statue's craftsmanship and makes no mention of expression; the 'sneer' is Shelley's addition.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'visage'; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933)

  5. sneer of cold command l. 5

    The hard c sounds ('cold command') harden the line. Shelley's politics are in play: he repeatedly presents tyranny as a passion that survives in its symbols, as in the sonnet 'England in 1819' and The Mask of Anarchy (both 1819). The frown, lip and sneer form a rising triad that the next lines explain: the sculptor accurately read these passions in his royal sitter, and their trace outlasts both men.

    P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes; 'Ozymandias', Wikipedia (composition with Horace Smith; Examiner publication, 11 Jan. 1818, signed 'Glirastes')

  6. the sculptor's reading l. 6

    The line makes art an act of interpretation. The sculptor is a reader of character, and the stone is his text. The passions 'yet survive' because they are 'stamped on these lifeless things', which sets living feeling against dead material. The irony is double: the king commissioned the statue to perpetuate his glory, but what it actually perpetuates is a critical portrait of his pride, made by a subordinate artisan. Many critics take this as Shelley's claim for the power of art and the artist over political might, a theme he states openly in A Defence of Poetry, where poets are called the unacknowledged legislators of the world.

    P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840); P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  7. mocked ... fed l. 8

    This is the poem's main syntactic crux. Most editors read 'survive' (l. 7) as governing two objects: the passions survive the hand (the sculptor's, which 'mocked' them) and the heart (the king's, which 'fed' them, i.e. nourished them). 'Mock' in the older sense meant to imitate or represent, as a mock-up does; it already carried the modern sense of ridicule, and both apply: the sculptor imitated the king's passions and, through the sneer, exposed them. An alternative reading makes 'hand' and 'heart' both the sculptor's. The compression is typical of the sonnet's density and has been much discussed in classroom anthologies.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mock'; P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ozymandias', edition notes

  8. Ozymandias l. 10

    Diodorus Siculus (first century BC) describes the tomb of 'Osymandyas' at Thebes (the Ramesseum) and gives the inscription in Greek. Modern Egyptology identifies the name with Ramesses II's prenomen User-maat-re. Shelley probably had no clear notion of the historical king: the Greek name gave him a sonorous, exotic figure of absolute monarchy. The title 'King of Kings' was a real formula of ancient Near Eastern rulers and is used in the Bible of Artaxerxes and Nebuchadnezzar and, finally, of Christ. Shelley's readers would have caught the blasphemous presumption of a mortal claiming it.

    Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933); 'Younger Memnon' (British Museum EA 19), Wikipedia: head of Ramesses II from the Ramesseum, shipped for Henry Salt, in London 1818, acquired by the Museum 1821; Bible (KJV), Ezra 7:12; Daniel 2:37; Revelation 19:16

  9. the inscription l. 11

    In Diodorus the inscription reads, roughly: 'King of Kings am I, Osymandyas. If anyone would know how great I am and where I lie, let him surpass one of my works.' Shelley turns the challenge into a command to despair. In context the meaning reverses itself: the Mighty should despair not because they cannot match him but because nothing he made remains. The irony depends entirely on the setting, since the words themselves have not changed. Horace Smith's companion sonnet quotes the same inscription and spells out the moral at length; Shelley's restraint, ending on landscape, is generally judged the stronger.

    Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933); Horace Smith, 'On a Stupendous Leg of Granite' (Examiner, 1 Feb. 1818), summarised in 'Ozymandias (Smith)', Wikipedia; 'Ozymandias', Wikipedia (composition with Horace Smith; Examiner publication, 11 Jan. 1818, signed 'Glirastes')

  10. pedestal ... these words appear l. 9

    A 'legend' here would mean an inscription (Latin legenda, 'things to be read'), which keeps up the poem's theme of reading: the sculptor reads the king, the traveller reads the pedestal, and we read the sonnet. The revised wording 'these words appear' is plainer and makes the inscription seem to surface from the sand. The pedestal itself is the place a monument names its subject, and so the place where the claim to permanence is made.

    P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'legend'

  11. Nothing beside remains l. 12

    The full stop in mid-line (a caesura) marks the real turn of the poem, coming after line 11 rather than at the conventional volta after line 8. The short declarative sentence contrasts with the inflated rhetoric of the inscription. The passage has often been compared with the biblical theme of vanity ('all is vanity', Ecclesiastes 1:2) and with the classical topos of fallen cities, though Shelley offers no religious consolation: what lasts is sand.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:2; P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  12. colossal Wreck l. 13

    The Greek kolossos meant a large statue, and the Colossus of Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders, was itself destroyed by an earthquake within decades of completion, so the adjective carries its own lesson. 'Wreck' is unusual for a statue: it belongs more often to ships, and suggests a disaster rather than slow erosion. The capitals in this text (Wreck, Works, Mighty) give the nouns emblematic weight.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'colossal'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wreck'

  13. lone and level sands l. 14

    The last two and a half lines move from the human scale of the statue to the impersonal scale of the desert. The paired alliterations slow the reading. Nothing in the closing lines is said by the king or the sculptor: the landscape has the last word, and 'stretch far away' leaves the sonnet open-ended rather than closed by a couplet. Many readers find here a sublime of absence, in which emptiness is more powerful than any monument.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ozymandias', edition notes; P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  14. the sonnet form l. 12

    The octave/sestet division is blurred: the sentence about the sculptor runs over line 8, and the true turn comes at 'Nothing beside remains' in line 12. The rhymes are loose and some are slant ('stone/frown', 'appear/despair'). Commentators often read the broken, overlapping rhyme pattern as mirroring the broken statue: the traditional form, like the monument, survives only in pieces. Shelley's freedom with the sonnet contrasts with Smith's more regular companion poem.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ozymandias', edition notes; 'Ozymandias', Wikipedia (composition with Horace Smith; Examiner publication, 11 Jan. 1818, signed 'Glirastes'); Horace Smith, 'On a Stupendous Leg of Granite' (Examiner, 1 Feb. 1818), summarised in 'Ozymandias (Smith)', Wikipedia

  15. the rival sonnet l. 2

    Smith's poem, printed three weeks after Shelley's and later retitled 'On a Stupendous Leg of Granite, Discovered Standing by Itself in the Deserts of Egypt', ends by imagining some hunter in a ruined London wondering at fragments of a vanished city. Reading both sonnets side by side shows how much of Shelley's power comes from what he leaves out: no future, no explicit lesson, only the inscription and the desert. The phrase 'King of Kings' is common to both, being drawn from Diodorus.

    Horace Smith, 'On a Stupendous Leg of Granite' (Examiner, 1 Feb. 1818), summarised in 'Ozymandias (Smith)', Wikipedia; 'Ozymandias', Wikipedia (composition with Horace Smith; Examiner publication, 11 Jan. 1818, signed 'Glirastes'); Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 1.47, trans. C. H. Oldfather (Loeb Classical Library, 1933)
