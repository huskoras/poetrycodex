Nature

Ode to the West Wind

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

NatureThe Mind

I
O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn’s being,
Thou, from whose unseen presence the leaves dead
Are driven, like ghosts from an enchanter fleeing,

Yellow, and black, and pale, and hectic red,
Pestilence-stricken multitudes: O thou,
Who chariotest to their dark wintry bed

The wingèd seeds, where they lie cold and low,
Each like a corpse within its grave, until
Thine azure sister of the Spring shall blow

Her clarion o’er the dreaming earth, and fill
(Driving sweet buds like flocks to feed in air)
With living hues and odours plain and hill:

Wild Spirit, which art moving everywhere;
Destroyer and preserver; hear, oh hear!

V
Make me thy lyre, even as the forest is:
What if my leaves are falling like its own!
The tumult of thy mighty harmonies

Will take from both a deep, autumnal tone,
Sweet though in sadness. Be thou, Spirit fierce,
My spirit! Be thou me, impetuous one!

Drive my dead thoughts over the universe
Like withered leaves to quicken a new birth!
And, by the incantation of this verse,

Scatter, as from an unextinguished hearth
Ashes and sparks, my words among mankind!
Be through my lips to unawakened Earth

The trumpet of a prophecy! O Wind,
If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Shelley begs the wild autumn wind to scatter his words like leaves and sparks. The famous close turns despair toward prophecy: ‘If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?’

Study notes by the editors

  1. composition and occasion l. 1 (section I)

    Shelley's own note says the poem was 'conceived and chiefly written in a wood that skirts the Arno, near Florence', on a day of the warm, rain-bringing wind, and that a violent storm of hail, thunder and lightning followed at sunset. The year 1819 was one of personal and political crisis: his son William died in June, and the Peterloo Massacre in Manchester (August) prompted The Mask of Anarchy and 'England in 1819'. Readers since have balanced a political reading (the wind as revolutionary force spreading the poet's ideas) with a personal one (the poet's depression and hope of renewal). The apostrophe 'O wild West Wind' sets the tone of urgent invocation.

    Shelley's own note to 'Ode to the West Wind' (Prometheus Unbound volume, 1820), reprinted in Hutchinson (1904); 'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  2. terza rima and the stanza l. 2 (section I)

    Terza rima, the interlocking form of Dante's Divine Comedy, is fitting for a poem about forward motion: each tercet's middle rhyme drives the next. Closing every section with a couplet gives each a sonnet-like shape. Note that the archive text prints only sections I and V of the five; the omitted sections II-IV address the wind's action on clouds and on the sea before the poet turns, in IV, to his own weakness. Harold Bloom described the poem as a fusion of the public Pindaric ode with the personal Horatian ode.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations; Dante, Inferno 3.112-117 (souls like falling autumn leaves), Digital Dante (Columbia University)

  3. breath l. 1 (section I)

    The word-play on breath, wind and spirit runs through the ode: 'Wild Spirit' (l. 13), 'Be thou, Spirit fierce, / My spirit!' (ll. 61-62). The same identity is ancient: in Genesis 1:2 the 'Spirit of God' moving on the waters translates the Hebrew ruach, which also means wind, and in John 3:8 'the wind bloweth where it listeth'. Shelley, a declared atheist, takes over the language of inspiration (literally, 'breathing in') and redirects it to a natural force.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:2; John 3:8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spirit'

  4. leaves like ghosts l. 3 (section I)

    Comparing the dead to falling leaves is one of the oldest epic similes (Iliad 6; Aeneid 6.309-10). Dante uses it of the damned souls crowding Charon's boat (Inferno 3.112-117), and Milton of the fallen angels lying 'Thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks / In Vallombrosa' (Paradise Lost 1.302-3); Vallombrosa is in the hills near Florence, where Shelley was writing. The 'enchanter' adds a touch of romance magic: the wind is a sorcerer driving spirits before it.

    Dante, Inferno 3.112-117 (souls like falling autumn leaves), Digital Dante (Columbia University); Milton, Paradise Lost 1.301-4 (Vallombrosa simile)

  5. hectic l. 4 (section I)

    'Hectic' (from Greek hektikos, 'habitual, consumptive') was the medical term for the recurring fever of wasting diseases, and 'hectic red' was the flush on a consumptive's cheek. The four colours (yellow, black, pale, red) have sometimes been read as the colours of the human races, or as the stages of a disease. Either way, the dead leaves become a sick crowd, which leads into 'Pestilence-stricken multitudes'. Keats, a consumptive, was dying as this poem was published, and Shelley would mourn him in Adonais (1821).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hectic'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  6. Pestilence-stricken multitudes l. 5 (section I)

    The phrase completes the turn from nature to humanity begun by 'ghosts'. 'Multitudes' was a loaded word in post-war England, when mass meetings for reform were being dispersed by force; in The Mask of Anarchy (1819) Shelley addresses the people as 'Ye are many'. The wind sweeps away the sick and dead to make room for new life: the political reading sees here a cleansing revolutionary force.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); P. B. Shelley, Complete Poetical Works, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904; repr. Project Gutenberg), headnotes and textual notes

  7. chariotest l. 6 (section I)

    Shelley makes a verb from the noun 'chariot', with the archaic second-person ending -est. The chariot suggests a classical god of the winds, or a triumphal procession, and contrasts with the 'dark wintry bed' to which the seeds are carried. The seeds are buried 'each like a corpse within its grave', but the burial is a planting: the image of the seed that dies and is raised anticipates the hope of the close.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chariot'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  8. the buried seed l. 8 (section I)

    St Paul writes that what is sown 'is not quickened, except it die' (1 Corinthians 15:36), and Christ says that unless a grain of wheat dies it 'abideth alone' (John 12:24). Shelley borrows this resurrection logic for a natural cycle, without the Christian promise. The word 'quicken' returns in section V ('to quicken a new birth'), so the image frames the whole ode.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:36; John 12:24

  9. azure sister of the Spring l. 9 (section I)

    In classical poetry the west wind (Greek Zephyros, Latin Favonius) is the warm wind of spring. Shelley makes the autumn and spring winds siblings: the same west wind both destroys and revives. 'Azure', sky-blue, contrasts with the dark and sickly colours of the leaves. Her trumpet-call ('clarion') wakes the earth from its dream of winter. The feminine pronoun also sets up a family of natural powers, in keeping with the poem's animism.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'azure'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  10. clarion l. 10 (section I)

    The clarion recalls the last trumpet of 1 Corinthians 15:52, at whose sound 'the dead shall be raised'. Shelley's resurrection is seasonal, not eschatological. The image prepares the 'trumpet of a prophecy' at the poem's end, so that the first and last sections are linked by trumpet calls: the spring wind's in nature, the poet's in human affairs. The word also had military associations (a clarion called troops to battle).

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:52; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clarion'

  11. Wild Spirit l. 13 (section I)

    'Which art' echoes the Lord's Prayer ('Our Father which art in heaven', Matthew 6:9), and the phrase recalls the spirit 'moving' on the waters of Genesis 1:2. The ode takes the form of a prayer throughout: invocation, a list of the deity's powers, and the refrain 'hear, oh hear!'. Shelley turns the liturgical form toward nature, and later toward himself.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:9; Genesis 1:2

  12. Destroyer and preserver l. 14 (section I)

    This paradox is the poem's central idea. The wind strips the trees and scatters the seeds that will grow again. Some readers compare the pairing with the Hindu gods Shiva (destroyer) and Vishnu (preserver), with whom Shelley may have been familiar through the Orientalist writing of his time; this is a comparison rather than a demonstrated source. Politically, the phrase implies that revolution must destroy before it renews. The refrain 'hear, oh hear!' ends sections I to III.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  13. Make me thy lyre l. 57 (section V)

    The Aeolian (wind) harp was placed in an open window so that the breeze made it sound; Coleridge's 'The Eolian Harp' (1795) made it an emblem of the mind. Shelley's Defence of Poetry compares man to 'an instrument' over which impressions pass 'like the alternations of an ever-changing wind over an Aeolian lyre'. Here the poet wants to be played as the forest is played, but the Defence adds that the mind also responds actively; the ode moves from passive instrument to active prophet.

    P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840); 'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes)

  14. my leaves are falling l. 58 (section V)

    The pun on leaves as pages runs through the final section: the 'dead thoughts' are driven 'like withered leaves' over the universe. Shelley was only twenty-seven, but section IV speaks of a life that has fallen 'upon the thorns of life' and is 'chained and bowed'. His work had a small readership, and the hope here is that even dead pages can be scattered and fertilise a new age. The 'deep, autumnal tone' acknowledges a mature, sadder music.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  15. Be thou me l. 62 (section V)

    The repetition 'Be thou, Spirit fierce, / My spirit! Be thou me' moves from possession to identity. Some critics read it as the high point of Romantic self-assertion, others as self-annihilation, the poet surrendering his personality to a natural force. The odd pronoun 'me' (where grammar might expect 'I') makes the merging physical. 'Impetuous', rushing forward (Latin impetus, 'attack'), is the wind's defining quality.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'impetuous'

  16. quicken l. 64 (section V)

    'Quicken' meant to make alive, and also referred to the first movement of a child in the womb, so 'quicken a new birth' joins agriculture and pregnancy. The word recalls St Paul's 'that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die' (1 Corinthians 15:36), linking back to the seeds of section I. The leaves rot and become the soil of new life: Shelley's hope that his work, ignored now, will feed later readers and reforms.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quicken'; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:36

  17. incantation l. 65 (section V)

    With this word the ode becomes self-conscious: its verse is the instrument by which the poet hopes to command the wind. This recalls the 'enchanter' of l. 3 (the same Latin root, cantare, 'to sing'), so that the poet now takes the enchanter's place. The ode thus claims for poetry the power of a spell, a claim Shelley makes more broadly in A Defence of Poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'incantation'; P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840)

  18. unextinguished hearth l. 66 (section V)

    The image combines dead matter (ashes) and living fire (sparks), echoing the destroyer and preserver of section I. A spark carried by the wind can start a blaze: the metaphor of ideas as sparks had strong revolutionary associations in the period. 'Unextinguished' insists that the poet's inspiration, though dimmed, is still alive. Shelley's Defence calls the mind in creation 'a fading coal' which some invisible influence, 'like an inconstant wind', awakens to brightness.

    P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations

  19. trumpet of a prophecy l. 69 (section V)

    Biblical prophets are told to lift up their voices 'like a trumpet' (Isaiah 58:1), and trumpets announce the end in Revelation. Shelley's prophecy is secular: the coming of a better political and moral order. The phrase answers the spring wind's 'clarion' in section I, so the poet takes on the wind's role. The line runs over into 'O Wind', breaking the sentence and the couplet pattern in a burst of urgency.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 58:1; 'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes)

  20. If Winter comes ... l. 70 (section V)

    The final line is among the most quoted in English. Read confidently, it states the law of the seasons as a law of history: oppression and despair must give way to renewal. Read closely, it is a question, not a statement, and follows a poem of desperate prayer; several critics stress the uncertainty, the hope that has to be asked for. The seasonal cycle had already been made political in the reform movement: Shelley's question invites the reader to supply the answer.

    'Ode to the West Wind', Wikipedia (composition near Florence 1819; Peterloo context; terza rima; Bloom on Pindaric and Horatian odes); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Ode to the West Wind', annotations
