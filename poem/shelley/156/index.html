Time & Brevity

Mutability (We are as clouds…)

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From Complete Poetical Works, Oxford ed. Hutchinson (1914)

Time & Brevity

We are as clouds that veil the midnight moon;
How restlessly they speed, and gleam, and quiver,
Streaking the darkness radiantly!—yet soon
Night closes round, and they are lost for ever:

Or like forgotten lyres, whose dissonant strings
Give various response to each varying blast,
To whose frail frame no second motion brings
One mood or modulation like the last.

We rest.—A dream has power to poison sleep;
We rise.—One wandering thought pollutes the day;
We feel, conceive or reason, laugh or weep;
Embrace fond woe, or cast our cares away:

It is the same!—For, be it joy or sorrow,
The path of its departure still is free:
Man’s yesterday may ne’er be like his morrow;
Nought may endure but Mutability.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Early poem, 1814–15. Published 1816.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and publication title; l. 1

    Shelley published this poem in Alastor; or, The Spirit of Solitude: and Other Poems (London: Baldwin, Cradock and Joy, 1816). Its exact date is uncertain: the Longman editors (Matthews and Everest) put it in the winter of 1815-16, chiefly on grounds of stylistic maturity, though the opening image could equally describe a night in 1814 (so Anna Mercer notes). The poem belongs with Alastor's preoccupation with loss and the failure of the mind to hold its own experiences. 'Mutability' is an old word (Latin mutabilis, 'changeable') with a long poetic history, most famously in Spenser's Two Cantos of Mutabilitie; Shelley's later lyric 'The flower that smiles to-day' (1821) is also often printed as 'Mutability' and should not be confused with this one.

    Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes; G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; 'Mutability (poem)', Wikipedia (summary of publication and Frankenstein use); Anna Mercer, 'On This Day in 1815: the Shelleys and ‘Mutability’', British Association for Romantic Studies blog

  2. Clouds over the moon l. 1

    The image is carefully double. The clouds 'veil' the moon, so they are obstacles to light, yet they are also lit by it ('gleam', 'radiantly'), so their brief beauty is borrowed. The three verbs of line 2, 'speed, and gleam, and quiver', give restless motion, brightness and trembling in quick succession, and the dash in line 3 marks the turn from radiance to extinction. 'Lost for ever' anticipates the closing claim that nothing returns in the same form. Clouds are among Shelley's favourite figures for transient being; compare the later 'The Cloud' (1820), where the cloud, by contrast, boasts that it changes but cannot die.

    G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes

  3. The forgotten lyres (Aeolian harp) ll. 5-8

    An Aeolian harp was a box strung with strings and set in a window so that the wind would sound it; it was a favourite Romantic figure for the mind or soul responding to outside impulse, as in Coleridge's 'The Eolian Harp' (1795). Shelley darkens the figure. His lyres are 'forgotten', their strings 'dissonant', and each gust ('blast') draws a different response; their 'frail frame' cannot repeat any 'mood or modulation' (both are musical terms: mode or key, and change of key). The point is that the self has no stable tone of its own. Shelley returns to the image in the Defence of Poetry (written 1821), where man is 'an instrument' over which impressions pass 'like the alternations of an ever-changing wind over an Æolian lyre'.

    G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; S. T. Coleridge, 'The Eolian Harp' (1795), in Poetical Works, ed. E. H. Coleridge (Oxford, 1912); P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840)

  4. Mood, modulation l. 8

    Both nouns work musically and psychologically at once. 'Mood' is an emotional state, but in older usage the word also overlaps with 'mode', the musical scale (the 'Dorian mood / Of flutes' in Milton, Paradise Lost 1.549-51). 'Modulation' means the passage from one key to another, or more generally the varying of pitch and tone. 'Second motion' means a second impulse of the wind. Shelley's claim is that no two gusts produce the same tune: experience never repeats. The alliterating m-sounds bind the line into a small musical phrase of its own.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'modulation'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mood'; John Milton, Paradise Lost (1667), 1.549-51 ('the Dorian mood / Of flutes')

  5. The third stanza: short clauses ll. 9-12

    After two stanzas of extended simile the poem turns to the literal human case, and the syntax changes accordingly. 'We rest.' and 'We rise.' are sentence-units of two words, each followed by a dash and a clause showing how little control we have: a single dream poisons sleep, one stray thought 'pollutes' a whole day. Line 11 then piles up verbs of mental life (feeling, conceiving, reasoning) and of emotion (laughing, weeping) as if all were equivalent. This is the stanza Mary Shelley quoted, with the fourth (eight lines in all), in Frankenstein, and its parallelism suits Victor's mood of exhausted fatalism there.

    G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), vol. 2, ch. 2 (ch. 10 in the 1831 numbering); the Creature’s echo is in ch. 15 of the 1831 text

  6. Pollutes l. 10

    In Shelley's time 'pollute' still carried its older sense of ritual or moral defilement (from Latin polluere, to soil or defile), rather than the modern environmental one. The hyperbole is the point: one 'wandering' thought, not even a deliberate one, is enough to stain a whole day. The pairing with 'poison' in the previous line makes sleep and waking equally vulnerable to contamination from within.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pollute'; G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'

  7. Fond woe l. 12

    The older meaning of 'fond' is 'foolish' (Middle English fonned, 'infatuated'), and it shades into 'doting'. 'Embrace fond woe' therefore describes a self-indulgent or foolish attachment to sorrow, the opposite of 'cast our cares away'. Both choices, the poem goes on to say, come to 'the same', since whatever we choose will not last. The phrase prepares for the stanza's claim that joy and sorrow are equally fugitive.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fond'; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes

  8. 'It is the same!' l. 13

    The line is the poem's logical hinge and has been read in two ways. On a bleak reading, 'the same' means that all human choices are equally futile. On a more consoling one, it means that sorrow, like joy, is bound to depart, so that change also frees us from grief. The next line supports the second reading: 'The path of its departure still is free' means that the way out is always open for any feeling to leave. In Frankenstein the Creature later echoes the line ('the path of my departure was free'), quoting back to Victor words Victor had mused over on Montanvert. Critics differ on how far the poem's stoicism is despairing or quietly liberating.

    Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), vol. 2, ch. 2 (ch. 10 in the 1831 numbering); the Creature’s echo is in ch. 15 of the 1831 text; G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; 'Mutability (poem)', Wikipedia (summary of publication and Frankenstein use)

  9. The path of its departure still is free l. 14

    'Still' carries its older sense 'always, continually' rather than 'yet'. 'Its' refers back to 'joy or sorrow' taken as a single thing: whichever it is, the road by which it leaves us lies open. The phrasing echoes the earlier images of clouds speeding away and of wind passing over strings; feelings, like weather, are always in transit. The line thus quietly reverses the opening image of loss: departure is no longer only a deprivation but also a release.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still'; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes

  10. Yesterday and morrow (textual variant) l. 15

    The line restates the lyre image in the language of time: no day of a man's life repeats any other. The 1816 text reads 'may' (as does Frankenstein); Mary Shelley, quoting the line in her later novel Lodore (1835), reportedly altered it to 'can'. The difference matters slightly: 'may ne'er be' allows a possibility, 'can ne'er be' asserts a law. The archive follows the 1816 reading. The feminine rhyme 'sorrow / morrow' is an ancient English pairing that sounds almost proverbial here.

    Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes; Mary Shelley, Lodore (1835), ch. 49

  11. 'Nought may endure but Mutability' l. 16

    The final line compresses an old philosophical commonplace. The Heraclitean doctrine that all things flow, and Pythagoras' long speech on universal change in Ovid's Metamorphoses (book 15), lie behind it, as does Spenser's Mutabilitie Cantos, in which the Titaness Mutabilitie claims rule over the world before Nature judges that things change only to return to themselves. Shelley allows no such reassurance: Mutability alone 'endures'. The capital turns the abstraction into a presiding power, as in Spenser. Mary Shelley's Frankenstein quotes stanzas 3 and 4, ending with this line, without naming the author.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.60-478 (speech of Pythagoras on change); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene, Book VII ('Two Cantos of Mutabilitie', 1609); G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), vol. 2, ch. 2 (ch. 10 in the 1831 numbering); the Creature’s echo is in ch. 15 of the 1831 text

  12. Quoted in Frankenstein ll. 9-16

    In the novel Victor, seeking comfort in Alpine scenery after the deaths of William and Justine, ascends the glacier of Montanvert near Chamonix and reflects on how fragile human feelings are, and then recites stanzas 3 and 4 of this poem. The Creature appears immediately after. Percy Shelley is not named. The quotation ties the poem to the Shelleys' shared reading and writing in these years; Anna Mercer discusses the poem as part of that collaboration. The Frankenstein text differs slightly in punctuation and capitals.

    Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), vol. 2, ch. 2 (ch. 10 in the 1831 numbering); the Creature’s echo is in ch. 15 of the 1831 text; Anna Mercer, 'On This Day in 1815: the Shelleys and “Mutability”', British Association for Romantic Studies blog; 'Mutability (poem)', Wikipedia (summary of publication and Frankenstein use)

  13. Form and rhyme ll. 1-16

    The poem's structure is strictly logical: two stanzas of simile (clouds, lyres), one of literal application to human life, and one of conclusion. The pentameter is regular but often loosened by extra syllables; line 3, 'Streaking the darkness radiantly', opens with a trochee and ends on a weak stress, enacting the brief streak of light. The feminine rhymes in stanzas 1 and 4 ('quiver / ever', 'sorrow / morrow') leave lines hanging on an unstressed beat, appropriate to a poem about instability. The many dashes and exclamations are characteristic of early Shelley and of the 1816 printing.

    G. M. Matthews and Kelvin Everest, eds., The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes to 'Mutability'; Thomas Hutchinson, ed., The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley (Oxford, 1914), 'Mutability' with textual notes
