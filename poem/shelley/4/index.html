Religion

Hymn to Intellectual Beauty

by Percy Bysshe Shelley 1792–1822

From Poems of 1816

Religion

The awful shadow of some unseen Power
Floats though unseen among us,—visiting
This various world with as inconstant wing
As summer winds that creep from flower to flower,—
Like moonbeams that behind some piny mountain shower,
It visits with inconstant glance
Each human heart and countenance;
Like hues and harmonies of evening,—
Like clouds in starlight widely spread,—
Like memory of music fled,—
Like aught that for its grace may be
Dear, and yet dearer for its mystery.

Spirit of BEAUTY, that dost consecrate
With thine own hues all thou dost shine upon
Of human thought or form,—where art thou gone?
Why dost thou pass away and leave our state,
This dim vast vale of tears, vacant and desolate?
Ask why the sunlight not for ever
Weaves rainbows o’er yon mountain-river,
Why aught should fail and fade that once is shown,
Why fear and dream and death and birth
Cast on the daylight of this earth
Such gloom,—why man has such a scope
For love and hate, despondency and hope?

No voice from some sublimer world hath ever
To sage or poet these responses given—
Therefore the names of Demon, Ghost, and Heaven.
Remain the records of their vain endeavour,
Frail spells—whose uttered charm might not avail to sever,
From all we hear and all we see,
Doubt, chance, and mutability.
Thy light alone—like mist o’er mountains driven,
Or music by the night-wind sent
Through strings of some still instrument,
Or moonlight on a midnight stream,
Gives grace and truth to life’s unquiet dream.

Love, Hope, and Self-esteem, like clouds depart
And come, for some uncertain moments lent.
Man were immortal, and omnipotent,
Didst thou, unknown and awful as thou art,
Keep with thy glorious train firm state within his heart.
Thou messenger of sympathies,
That wax and wane in lovers’ eyes—
Thou—that to human thought art nourishment,
Like darkness to a dying flame!
Depart not as thy shadow came
Depart not—lest the grave should be,
Like life and fear, a dark reality.

While yet a boy I sought for ghosts, and sped
Through many a listening chamber, cave and ruin,
And starlight wood, with fearful steps pursuing
Hopes of high talk with the departed dead.
I called on poisonous names with which our youth is fed;
I was not heard—I saw them not—
When musing deeply on the lot
Of life, at that sweet time when winds are wooing
All vital things that wake to bring
News of birds and blossoming,—
Sudden, thy shadow fell on me;
I shrieked, and clasped my hands in ecstasy!

I vowed that I would dedicate my powers
To thee and thine—have I not kept the vow?
With beating heart and streaming eyes, even now
I call the phantoms of a thousand hours
Each from his voiceless grave: they have in visioned bowers
Of studious zeal or love’s delight
Outwatched with me the envious night—
They know that never joy illumed my brow
Unlinked with hope that thou wouldst free
This world from its dark slavery,
That thou—O awful LOVELINESS,
Wouldst give whate’er these words cannot express.

The day becomes more solemn and serene
When noon is past—there is a harmony
In autumn, and a lustre in its sky,
Which through the summer is not heard or seen,
As if it could not be, as if it had not been!
Thus let thy power, which like the truth
Of nature on my passive youth
Descended, to my onward life supply
Its calm—to one who worships thee,
And every form containing thee,
Whom, SPIRIT fair, thy spells did bind
To fear himself, and love all human kind.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hymn to Intellectual Beauty: composition and publication l. 1

    Shelley composed the Hymn in the summer of 1816 in Switzerland, during the months spent near Byron on Lake Geneva; the period also produced 'Mont Blanc'. He sent a fair copy to Leigh Hunt, who lost it, and a second copy was published in The Examiner on 19 January 1817; it was reprinted in the Rosalind and Helen volume (1819). A further fair copy in Mary Shelley's hand was found in 1976 in the 'Scrope Davies notebook', with substantial variants. The poem is a religious 'hymn' addressed to a non-religious power, a secular sceptic's devotional ode; critics stress the influence of Wordsworth's Immortality Ode and Rousseau's Julie, ou la Nouvelle Héloïse, which Shelley read on the same trip.

    'Hymn to Intellectual Beauty', Wikipedia (composition and publication history, Scrope Davies notebook); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes; The Complete Poetical Works of Percy Bysshe Shelley, ed. Thomas Hutchinson (Oxford, 1904), text and Mary Shelley's notes

  2. awful shadow of some unseen Power l. 1

    'Awful' keeps its older sense of 'awe-inspiring', not 'very bad'. The Power is never seen directly; only its 'shadow' visits us, and even that 'floats though unseen'. Shelley's language is deliberately tentative ('some'), refusing to name a deity. The Platonic resonance is strong (shadows of a higher reality, as in the cave of Republic 7), though Shelley read the Symposium only in August 1817 and the Phaedrus in 1818, after the Hymn was written, and Barrell argued that the poem is not strictly Platonic. 'Power' also links the Hymn to 'Mont Blanc', written in the same summer.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'awful, adj.'; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Joseph Barrell, Shelley and the Thought of His Time (Yale, 1947); Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley

  3. with as inconstant wing / As summer winds ll. 3-4

    The first stanza is a chain of similes (summer winds, moonbeams, hues and harmonies of evening, clouds, remembered music), each describing something fleeting, delicate and beautiful. The accumulation itself conveys the elusiveness of the Power, which can only be approached by analogy. 'Inconstant' introduces the poem's central problem: the visitations are intermittent, and the speaker cannot command their return.

    Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes

  4. The stanza st. 1-7

    Each of the seven stanzas has twelve lines with the same rhyme scheme (abbaaccbddee) and the same pattern of line lengths, mixing pentameters, shorter tetrameter lines and a long fifth line that swells to six stresses (here 'Like moonbeams that behind some piny mountain shower'). The pattern loosely recalls the Pindaric or irregular ode tradition and the stanza of Wordsworth's Immortality Ode, while the title calls it a 'hymn'. The shortening lines in the middle of each stanza produce an effect of breath catching or flickering, suited to the inconstant visitations described.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes; Stuart Curran, Poetic Form and British Romanticism (Oxford, 1986), chapter on the ode

  5. Spirit of BEAUTY / Intellectual Beauty l. 13

    'Intellectual' here means belonging to the realm of the intellect or spirit as opposed to the senses, an older philosophical sense derived from Platonism (OED). The title evokes Spenser's Fowre Hymnes (1596), which include 'An Hymne in Honour of Beautie' and 'An Hymne of Heavenly Beautie', Renaissance Neoplatonic praises of beauty as the reflection of the divine. Shelley borrows the form of devotional address but withholds belief in a God behind the Beauty. The capitals (BEAUTY, LOVELINESS, SPIRIT) mark the moments of direct invocation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'intellectual, adj.'; Edmund Spenser, Fowre Hymnes (1596); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes

  6. dost consecrate l. 13

    'Consecrate' (Latin consecrare, to make holy) belongs to the language of worship: the Spirit hallows whatever human thought or form it shines upon. The verb sets the pattern of the poem, which repeatedly borrows religious vocabulary (hymn, vow, dedicate, worship, spells) to describe an experience that rejects orthodox religion. Shelley echoes Wordsworth's 'The consecration, and the Poet's dream' ('Elegiac Stanzas', Peele Castle, 1807, l. 16).

    Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'consecrate'; Wordsworth, 'Elegiac Stanzas Suggested by a Picture of Peele Castle' (1807), l. 16; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  7. This dim vast vale of tears l. 17

    'Vale of tears' translates the Latin lacrimarum valle of the Salve Regina, a medieval Marian prayer, and derives from the 'valley of Baca' (weeping) in Psalm 84:6 (Psalm 83:7 in the Vulgate numbering), rendered in the Latin Vulgate as 'valle lacrimarum'. The phrase became proverbial for earthly life as exile and suffering. Shelley uses Christian words to describe the condition of life when the Spirit is absent, without the Christian promise of a heavenly home. The long fifth line enlarges the sense of desolation.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 84:6; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vale, n.1' (vale of tears); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  8. the names of Demon, Ghost, and Heaven l. 27

    No revelation from 'some sublimer world' has answered the questions of stanza 2; therefore the words religion and superstition supply (demon, ghost, heaven) merely record 'vain endeavour'. Line 27 is one of two lines (with l. 58) that Shelley corrected after the Examiner printing, and the Scrope Davies copy differs from the printed texts in many places, so editors compare the early witnesses; the archive follows the Rosalind and Helen text. 'Demon' may carry the Greek sense of daimon, an intermediary spirit, rather than devil. Editors (Reiman and Fraistat; the Longman editors) discuss the variants.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes; 'Hymn to Intellectual Beauty', Wikipedia (composition and publication history, Scrope Davies notebook)

  9. Frail spells l. 29

    Religious names are reduced to magic 'spells' whose 'uttered charm' cannot 'sever' doubt, chance and mutability from what we perceive. The word is ironic, since at the end of the poem the Spirit itself is said to have bound the poet with 'spells' (l. 83): Shelley replaces one enchantment with another, but one that operates through feeling and not dogma.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley

  10. Doubt, chance, and mutability l. 31

    Mutability, the law of change and decay, is a major Renaissance theme (Spenser's 'Mutabilitie Cantos') and a recurrent one in Shelley. His short poem 'Mutability' ('We are as clouds that veil the midnight moon'), published with Alastor in 1816, ends 'Nought may endure but Mutability'. Here doubt (intellectual uncertainty), chance (lack of providence) and mutability define a world without a guaranteed metaphysical order, the condition the Hymn accepts as its starting point.

    Shelley, 'Mutability' (1816), in Complete Poetical Works, ed. Hutchinson; Spenser, The Faerie Queene, 'Two Cantos of Mutabilitie' (1609); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  11. strings of some still instrument ll. 33-34

    The image is of the wind-harp or Aeolian harp, a stringed box placed in a window, which sounds when the breeze crosses it. It was a favourite Romantic emblem of the passive, receptive mind, most famously in Coleridge's 'The Eolian Harp' (1795). Shelley returns to it in 'A Defence of Poetry' and the 'Ode to the West Wind' ('Make me thy lyre'). 'Still' (silent until the wind comes) stresses that the instrument has no music of its own: grace and truth come only when the Spirit's light visits.

    S. T. Coleridge, 'The Eolian Harp' (1795); Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (1821), opening paragraphs; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  12. Love, Hope, and Self-esteem l. 37

    Shelley pairs Self-esteem with Love and Hope, as if a secular version of the theological virtues (faith, hope and charity) in 1 Corinthians 13. The word is positive, meaning a just valuation of oneself; editors compare Raphael's advice to Adam in Paradise Lost 8, that nothing profits more than 'self-esteem, grounded on just and right'. These states, too, are inconstant: they 'like clouds depart / And come'.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 8.571-73; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:13; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  13. Man were immortal, and omnipotent l. 39

    The conditional ('Man were immortal... Didst thou... Keep... firm state within his heart') is the poem's boldest statement. Divinity is relocated from heaven into the human mind: what makes gods is not a separate being but the constant presence of beauty, love and hope. The 'glorious train' pictures the Spirit as a monarch with a retinue holding 'firm state' (court) in the heart, a contrast with the inconstant visits of stanza 1. Critics read this as an early form of the meliorist vision of Prometheus Unbound.

    Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes

  14. Thou messenger of sympathies l. 42

    'Sympathies' in Shelley's usage means affinities and fellow-feeling, the emotional bonds between people. The Spirit is their messenger (Greek angelos, 'angel', means messenger, so the phrase quietly secularizes an angelic role). 'Wax and wane' compares the coming and going of love in lovers' eyes to the phases of the moon. Shelley's essay 'On Love' (written c. 1818, published posthumously) develops the idea of love as a sympathy seeking its counterpart.

    Shelley, 'On Love' (1818), in Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Reiman and Fraistat; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'angel'

  15. Like darkness to a dying flame l. 45

    The Spirit is 'to human thought... nourishment, / Like darkness to a dying flame'. Darkness does not literally feed a flame, so readers have offered several explanations: a flame appears most brilliant against darkness, so the Spirit sets off and intensifies thought; or the image is of a dying candle that seems to flare as night closes in. Some critics regard the simile as a deliberately unsettling paradox, consistent with a Power that is 'unknown and awful'. Editors note the difficulty without settling it.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley

  16. While yet a boy I sought for ghosts l. 49

    The fifth stanza turns to personal narrative. Shelley's friends and biographers Thomas Jefferson Hogg (an Oxford friend) and Thomas Medwin (a cousin and schoolfellow) describe his youthful fascination with chemistry, magic and attempts to raise spirits. 'Hopes of high talk with the departed dead' refers to such necromantic experiments, influenced by gothic fiction. The 'poisonous names' are the names of demons or the spells of superstition, and perhaps also the religious teaching fed to children; the boy calls on them in vain.

    T. J. Hogg, The Life of Percy Bysshe Shelley (1858); Thomas Medwin, The Life of Percy Bysshe Shelley (1847); Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  17. I called on poisonous names with which our youth is fed l. 53

    The line links the boy's invocation of spirits to what 'our youth is fed', implying that the names come from nursery superstition and religious instruction alike. Read with l. 27, it extends Shelley's scepticism from ghosts to the vocabulary of Christianity. The metaphor of feeding on poison also contrasts with the Spirit's true 'nourishment' in l. 44. Editors note that Shelley's attack is cautious enough to have been printed by Hunt in 1817.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley

  18. I shrieked, and clasped my hands in ecstasy! l. 60

    The Spirit's shadow falls suddenly in spring, not when sought but when the boy was musing on life. The response (shrieking, clasping the hands) imitates the language of evangelical conversion narratives, but the revelation is of beauty, not of God. 'Ecstasy' (Greek ekstasis, standing outside oneself) suggests rapture beyond the self. Some readers find the line overwrought; others see its awkwardness as a mark of sincerity. The structure, a childhood epiphany followed by a vow, parallels Wordsworth's dedication scene in The Prelude Book 4, though Shelley could not have read that poem, which was published in 1850.

    Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'ecstasy'; Wordsworth, The Prelude (1850), Book 4, the dawn-walk dedication

  19. I vowed that I would dedicate my powers l. 61

    The sixth stanza converts the experience into a vocation: like a priest or knight, the poet dedicates his 'powers' to the Spirit and asks whether he has kept the vow. The 'phantoms of a thousand hours' are his past selves or past studies, called from their graves as witnesses, in contrast to the ghosts he once tried to raise. Shelley's sense of a dedicated life also appears in the Dedication to Laon and Cythna (1817), which recalls a similar youthful moment of resolve.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; Shelley, Dedication to Laon and Cythna (1817), stanzas 3-5; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley

  20. visioned bowers / Of studious zeal or love's delight ll. 65-67

    'Visioned' means seen in a vision, imagined, and 'bowers' are secluded arbours, a romance word. The hours have 'Outwatched with me the envious night', staying awake until dawn in study or love; the night is 'envious' because it seeks to end or overshadow these pleasures. The lines suggest that the poet's devotion has been sustained by both intellectual labour and erotic love, which Shelley sees as two modes of the same pursuit of beauty.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes

  21. O awful LOVELINESS l. 71

    Eighteenth-century aesthetics, especially Burke's Philosophical Enquiry (1757), distinguished the beautiful (small, smooth, pleasing) from the sublime (vast, terrifying, awe-inspiring). Shelley's oxymoron 'awful LOVELINESS' unites them, as the opening 'awful shadow' did. The hope expressed here is political as well as personal: that the Spirit would free 'This world from its dark slavery', anticipating the revolutionary hopes of Laon and Cythna and Prometheus Unbound.

    Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757), in Works, vol. 1 (Project Gutenberg); Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  22. The day becomes more solemn and serene l. 73

    As noon passes and autumn arrives, the day gains a harmony and lustre 'not heard or seen' in summer. Critics have long compared Wordsworth's 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality' (1807), whose last stanza finds a 'sober colouring' in the clouds round the setting sun and a 'philosophic mind' replacing youthful vision. Shelley similarly asks that his mature life be granted the 'calm' that the Spirit's visits once brought in youth. The difference is that Shelley's consolation does not rest on a belief in pre-existence or immortality.

    Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality' (1807), stanza 11; 'Hymn to Intellectual Beauty', Wikipedia (composition and publication history, Scrope Davies notebook); Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem

  23. on my passive youth l. 79

    'Passive' implies receptivity, being acted upon, the condition of the wind-harp in stanza 3. The simile 'like the truth / Of nature' suggests a natural revelation rather than a supernatural one. Shelley now asks that this power 'supply' his 'onward life' with calm: the movement from youthful passivity to adult dedication is the poem's narrative arc. The prayer is modest: not for renewed ecstasy but for the autumnal serenity described at the opening of the stanza, a mature steadiness grounded in the earlier visitations.

    Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem; The Poems of Shelley, vol. 1: 1804-1817, ed. Geoffrey Matthews and Kelvin Everest (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1989), headnote and notes

  24. To fear himself, and love all human kind l. 84

    The poem closes by defining its worshipper as one bound by the Spirit's 'spells' to 'fear himself, and love all human kind'. 'Fear himself' probably means to be wary of one's own capacity for selfishness and evil, and perhaps to revere the divinity potentially within oneself; 'love all human kind' recalls the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39) without its first, the love of God. Many readers take the ending as the humanist core of Shelley's faith in beauty as a moral force.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 22:37-39; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Shelley; Shelley's Poetry and Prose, ed. Donald H. Reiman and Neil Fraistat (Norton Critical Edition, 2nd ed. 2002), notes on this poem
