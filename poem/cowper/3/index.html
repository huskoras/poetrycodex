Death & Dying

The Castaway

by William Cowper 1731–1800

Death & Dying

Obscurest night involved the sky,
The Atlantic billows roar'd,
When such a destined wretch as I,
Wash'd headlong from on board,
Of friends, of hope, of all bereft,
His floating home for ever left.

No braver chief could Albion boast
Than he with whom he went,
Nor ever ship left Albion's coast
With warmer wishes sent.
He loved them both, but both in vain,
Nor him beheld, nor her again.

Not long beneath the whelming brine,
Expert to swim, he lay;
Nor soon he felt his strength decline,
Or courage die away:
But waged with death a lasting strife,
Supported by despair of life.

He shouted; nor his friends had fail'd
To check the vessel's course,
But so the furious blast prevail'd,
That, pitiless perforce,
They left their outcast mate behind,
And scudded still before the wind.

Some succour yet they could afford;
And, such as storms allow,
The cask, the coop, the floated cord,
Delay'd not to bestow:
But he, they knew, nor ship nor shore,
Whate'er they gave, should visit more.

Nor, cruel as it seem'd, could he
Their haste himself condemn,
Aware that flight, in such a sea,
Alone could rescue them;
Yet bitter felt it still to die
Deserted, and his friends so nigh.

He long survives, who lives an hour
In ocean, self-upheld:
And so long he, with unspent power,
His destiny repell'd:
And ever, as the minutes flew,
Entreated help, or cried--"Adieu!"

At length, his transient respite past,
His comrades, who before
Had heard his voice in every blast,
Could catch the sound no more:
For then, by toil subdued, he drank
The stifling wave, and then he sank.

No poet wept him; but the page
Of narrative sincere,
That tells his name, his worth, his age,
Is wet with Anson's tear;
And tears by bards or heroes shed
Alike immortalize the dead.

I therefore purpose not, or dream,
Descanting on his fate,
To give the melancholy theme
A more enduring date:
But misery still delights to trace
Its semblance in another's case.

No voice divine the storm allay'd,
No light propitious shone;
When, snatch'd from all effectual aid,
We perish'd, each alone:
But I beneath a rougher sea,
And whelm'd in deeper gulfs than he.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Castaway title; st. 1

    Cowper wrote the poem in 1799, a year before his death, during his final depression at East Dereham, Norfolk; the exact day is variously given, March or April 1799. He had reread A Voyage Round the World (1748), the official account of Commodore George Anson's circumnavigation of 1740-44, in which a seaman is washed overboard in a storm and watched by his shipmates as he swims until he drowns. William Hayley printed the poem in his Life and Posthumous Writings of William Cowper (1803-04). 'Involved' keeps its Latin sense, wrapped or enveloped (involvere). The poem turns a seaman's death into an image of Cowper's own conviction that he was damned.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018); Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes

  2. castaway (title) title; l. 23

    In the King James Bible Paul says he keeps his body under 'lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway' (1 Cor. 9:27; Greek adokimos, 'disapproved, rejected'). For an evangelical reader like Cowper the word meant one finally rejected from salvation. The seaman is literally cast away, an 'outcast' left behind by the ship; Cowper is the spiritual castaway. Cowper had described himself as such years before, and the double sense governs the turn in the last stanza.

    1 Corinthians 9:27 (KJV); Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018)

  3. a destined wretch as I l. 3

    Cowper, an evangelical Calvinist, had a dream in 1773 after which he believed he was singled out for damnation, and in 'Lines Written during a Period of Insanity' (c. 1774) he called himself damned below Judas. The comparison 'such ... as I' is placed early, though the poem only explains it in the last stanza; the third line thus quietly frames the narrative as autobiography. Critics debate whether the poem is pure despair or achieves, through the control of its art, a measure of consolation; the startling candour of 'destined' supports the darker reading.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Duke University Press, 1986)

  4. stanza form sts. 1-11

    The first four lines alternate eight and six syllables (common metre, the measure of hymns, including Cowper's own Olney Hymns) and the couplet of two tetrameters gives each stanza a firm, often aphoristic close ('But waged with death a lasting strife, / Supported by despair of life'). The plain hymn metre is part of the poem's terrible irony: the form of congregational faith carries the experience of abandonment by God.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; John Newton and William Cowper, Olney Hymns (1779)

  5. chief ... Albion ll. 7-10

    Anson's expedition (1740-44), sent out in the War of Jenkins' Ear, lost most of its men to scurvy and storms but captured a Spanish treasure galleon and completed a circumnavigation in the Centurion; Anson later became First Lord of the Admiralty. 'Albion' is the ancient poetic name for Britain. 'He loved them both' means the seaman loved his commander and his country, and 'both in vain' because he never saw either again.

    Wikipedia, 'George Anson's voyage around the world'; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018)

  6. whelming brine l. 13

    'Whelm' (to turn a vessel upside down over something, to engulf) is older than 'overwhelm'. 'Brine' is poetic diction for the sea. The word returns in the last line ('whelm'd in deeper gulfs'), binding the seaman's fate to the poet's. The stanza praises the man's skill and courage ('Expert to swim'), which only prolongs his ordeal. The negatives ('Not long ... Nor soon') stress how slowly his strength fails, so that his drowning is drawn out, not sudden.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'whelm, v.'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'

  7. Supported by despair of life l. 18

    Literally, knowing he must die, the seaman fights all the harder, a desperate courage. Vincent Newey reads the drowned mariner as a dark double for Cowper's own affliction, and the phrase fits Cowper's own long life sustained in spite of, or by, despair. The antithesis of 'waged with death a lasting strife' and 'despair' sets up the poem's movement from heroic struggle to submersion.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; Vincent Newey, Cowper's Poetry: A Critical Study and Reassessment (Liverpool University Press, 1982)

  8. pitiless perforce l. 22

    'Perforce' means of necessity, by force of circumstances. In Anson's narrative the ship could not be brought round in the storm. Cowper is fair to the crew: they are 'pitiless' only in effect, not in feeling. 'Scudded' is a sailor's term for running before a gale with little or no sail; Anson's account describes the ship lying-to and then driven. The stanza introduces the poem's ethical complexity: abandonment that no one could prevent.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'perforce, adv.'; 'scud, v.1'; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; [Richard Walter and Benjamin Robins], A Voyage Round the World ... by George Anson (London, 1748), Book I, ch. 8

  9. The cask, the coop, the floated cord l. 27

    Ships carried live poultry in wooden coops on deck; coops and empty casks were standard improvised life-buoys. A 'floated cord' is a line let out in the hope the swimmer could reach it. The alliterating list (cask, coop, cord) is precise, in keeping with Cowper's documentary source. The couplet sets the futility plainly: 'nor ship nor shore ... should visit more'.

    [Richard Walter and Benjamin Robins], A Voyage Round the World ... by George Anson (London, 1748), Book I, ch. 8; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'

  10. Nor ... could he / Their haste himself condemn ll. 31-36

    This stanza, Cowper's addition to Anson, gives the seaman an inner life of fairness and grief. Read theologically, it suggests Cowper's refusal to accuse God or the community of believers who could not save him. The final couplet ('bitter ... to die / Deserted, and his friends so nigh') makes nearness the cruelty: the companions are visible but cannot help.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018)

  11. self-upheld ll. 37-40

    The aphorism compresses Anson's observation that the man swam strongly and long. 'Self-upheld' stresses lonely effort: no other support is available, natural or divine. The stanza's last couplet gives the man's two cries, an appeal and a farewell ('Adieu!'), as if he passes from hope to acceptance. 'Destiny repell'd' returns to the poem's language of fate. For a moment the swimmer seems to hold off what is 'destined', the word Cowper had applied to himself in l. 3.

    [Richard Walter and Benjamin Robins], A Voyage Round the World ... by George Anson (London, 1748), Book I, ch. 8; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes

  12. he drank / The stifling wave, and then he sank ll. 47-48

    The plainness is deliberate: no image or consolation, only physical fact ('toil subdued' him). The comrades 'could catch the sound no more': the sound of his voice in 'every blast' has stopped. The repetition of 'and then' gives the couplet the bare sequence of a log entry, close to the source's matter-of-fact prose. After this point the poem leaves narrative for reflection, so the sinking marks the hinge of its structure.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018)

  13. the page / Of narrative sincere ... Anson's tear ll. 49-54

    The Voyage, though issued under Anson's authority, was compiled by the chaplain Richard Walter and, largely, the mathematician Benjamin Robins; Cowper treats it as Anson's 'narrative sincere', and imagines its page wet with the commander's tear. The stanza's maxim ('tears by bards or heroes shed / Alike immortalize the dead') places the prose of a hero beside poetry as memorial. Kinsley locates the poem within the eighteenth-century voyage-account tradition of maritime suffering.

    Zoe Kinsley, "'He swam very strong': Cowper's 'The Cast-away' and the Voyage Account Tradition of Maritime Suffering", The Cowper and Newton Journal (2018); [Richard Walter and Benjamin Robins], A Voyage Round the World ... by George Anson (London, 1748), Book I, ch. 8; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes

  14. Descanting ll. 55-58

    'Descant' meant first a melodic counterpoint and then, by extension, to comment or enlarge upon a theme (OED). The stanza is a formal disclaimer: the poet does not purpose (intend) to immortalise the man ('A more enduring date'), which the Voyage has done. Instead the motive is psychological: misery likes to see its likeness in another's case. This inverts Lucretius' famous claim (De Rerum Natura 2.1-4) that it is sweet to watch from shore another's struggles at sea: here the watcher is himself drowning.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'descant, v.'; Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 2.1-4 (Latin text); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'

  15. No voice divine the storm allay'd l. 61

    In the Gospels, when the disciples fear drowning Jesus rebukes the wind and sea, 'and there was a great calm'. Cowper denies the miracle twice: no voice, no 'light propitious' (a favouring light, perhaps a guiding star or the light of grace). The first-person plural 'We perish'd, each alone' joins seaman and poet, and the paradox of a shared solitude is the poem's most quoted line. Virginia Woolf's Mr Ramsay recites it repeatedly in To the Lighthouse (1927), a sign of its later fame.

    Matthew 8:23-26 (KJV); Mark 4:37-39 (KJV); Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; Virginia Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927), Part III

  16. a rougher sea ... deeper gulfs ll. 65-66

    The closing couplet makes explicit the identity hinted at in 'such a destined wretch as I'. The language recalls the Psalms of the drowning soul ('Deep calleth unto deep ... all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me', Psalm 42:7; 'I am come into deep waters', Psalm 69:2) and Jonah's cry from the deep (Jonah 2:3). 'Gulfs' means abysses. RPO's note recalls that Cowper had described himself in 1773 as a castaway, condemned. Critics differ over whether the final assertion is unrelieved despair or a last act of articulate self-possession; the poem's formal control is often adduced for the second view.

    Psalm 42:7; Psalm 69:2 (KJV); Jonah 2:3 (KJV); Representative Poetry Online, 'The Castaway', ed. G. G. Falle, with the Anson passage and line notes; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp (eds.), The Poems of William Cowper, vol. 3: 1785-1800 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), text and notes to 'The Cast-away'; Vincent Newey, Cowper's Poetry: A Critical Study and Reassessment (Liverpool University Press, 1982)
