On the Receipt of My Mother's Picture out of Norfolk, the Gift of My Cousin, Ann Bodham

by William Cowper 1731–1800

From Poems

Arts & Sciences

O that those lips had language! Life has pass'd
With me but roughly since I heard thee last.
Those lips are thine--thy own sweet smile I see,
The same that oft in childhood solaced me;
Voice only fails, else how distinct they say,
"Grieve not, my child, chase all thy fears away!"
The meek intelligence of those dear eyes
(Blest be the art that can immortalize,
The art that baffles Time's tyrannic claim
To quench it) here shines on me still the same.
Faithful remembrancer of one so dear,
O welcome guest, though unexpected here:
Who bidst me honour with an artless song,
Affectionate, a mother lost so long.
I will obey, not willingly alone,
But gladly, as the precept were her own:
And, while that face renews my filial grief,
Fancy shall weave a charm for my relief,
Shall steep me in Elysian reverie,
A momentary dream, that thou art she.
My mother! when I learn'd that thou wast dead,
Say, wast thou conscious of the tears I shed?
Hover'd thy spirit o'er thy sorrowing son,
Wretch even then, life's journey just begun?
Perhaps thou gavest me, though unfelt, a kiss;
Perhaps a tear, if souls can weep in bliss--
Ah, that maternal smile! it answers--Yes.
I heard the bell toll'd on thy burial day,
I saw the hearse that bore thee slow away,
And turning from my nursery window, drew
A long, long sigh, and wept a last adieu!
But was it such?--It was.--Where thou art gone
Adieus and farewells are a sound unknown.
May I but meet thee on that peaceful shore,
The parting word shall pass my lips no more!
Thy maidens, grieved themselves at my concern,
Oft gave me promise of thy quick return.
What ardently I wish'd, I long believed,
And, disappointed still, was still deceived.
By expectation every day beguiled,
Dupe of to-morrow even from a child.
Thus many a sad to-morrow came and went,
Till, all my stock of infant sorrows spent,
I learn'd at last submission to my lot,
But, though I less deplored thee, ne'er forgot.
Where once we dwelt our name is heard no more,
Children not thine have trod my nursery floor;
And where the gardener Robin, day by day,
Drew me to school along the public way,
Delighted with my bauble coach, and wrapp'd
In scarlet mantle warm, and velvet capp'd,
'Tis now become a history little known,
That once we call'd the pastoral house our own.
Short-lived possession! but the record fair,
That memory keeps of all thy kindness there,
Still outlives many a storm, that has effaced
A thousand other themes less deeply traced.
Thy nightly visits to my chamber made,
That thou mightst know me safe and warmly laid;
Thy morning bounties ere I left my home,
The biscuit or confectionary plum;
The fragrant waters on my cheeks bestow'd
By thy own hand, till fresh they shone and glow'd:
All this, and more endearing still than all,
Thy constant flow of love that knew no fall,
Ne'er roughen'd by those cataracts and breaks
That humour interposed too often makes;
All this still legible in memory's page,
And still to be so to my latest age,
Adds joy to duty, makes me glad to pay
Such honours to thee as my numbers may;
Perhaps a frail memorial, but sincere,
Not scorn'd in heaven, though little noticed here.
Could Time, his flight reversed, restore the hours,
When, playing with thy vesture's tissued flowers,
The violet, the pink, and jessamine,
I prick'd them into paper with a pin,
(And thou wast happier than myself the while,
Wouldst softly speak, and stroke my head, and smile,)
Could those few pleasant days again appear,
Might one wish bring them, would I wish them here?
I would not trust my heart--the dear delight
Seems so to be desired, perhaps I might.--
But no--what here we call our life is such,
So little to be loved, and thou so much,
That I should ill requite thee to constrain
Thy unbound spirit into bonds again.
Thou, as a gallant bark from Albion's coast
(The storms all weather'd and the ocean cross'd)
Shoots into port at some well-haven'd isle
Where spices breathe, and brighter seasons smile,
There sits quiescent on the floods, that show
Her beauteous form reflected clear below,
While airs impregnated with incense play
Around her, fanning light her streamers gay:
So thou, with sails how swift! hast reach'd the shore,
"Where tempests never beat nor billows roar;"
And thy loved consort on the dangerous tide
Of life long since has anchor'd by thy side.
But me, scarce hoping to attain that rest,
Always from port withheld, always distress'd--
Me howling blasts drive devious, tempest-toss'd
Sails ripp'd, seams opening wide, and compass lost,
And day by day some current's thwarting force
Sets me more distant from a prosperous course.
But oh, the thought, that thou art safe, and he!
That thought is joy, arrive what may to me.
My boast is not that I deduce my birth
From loins enthroned, and rulers of the earth;
But higher far my proud pretensions rise--
The son of parents pass'd into the skies.
And now, farewell--Time unrevoked has run
His wonted course, yet what I wish'd is done.
By contemplation's help, not sought in vain,
I seem to have lived my childhood o'er again;
To have renew'd the joys that once were mine,
Without the sin of violating thine;
And, while the wings of fancy still are free,
And I can view this mimic show of thee,
Time has but half succeeded in his theft--
Thyself removed, thy power to soothe me left.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date title; l. 1

    Ann Bodham, née Donne, of Mattishall in Norfolk, was Cowper's cousin on his mother's side. In February 1790 she sent him a miniature of his mother, and on 27 February he wrote thanking her, saying he had kissed the picture and hung it where it would be the last thing he saw at night and the first in the morning. He wrote the poem soon after. It was published in 1798 and reprinted with other poems in 1799. The opening apostrophe, wishing the painted lips could speak, establishes the poem's theme: art preserves the face but not the voice.

    James King and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Letters and Prose Writings of William Cowper, vol. III (Oxford, 1982), letter to Mrs Anne Bodham, 27 February 1790; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; 'On the Receipt of My Mother's Picture', Wikipedia (dates of the picture and of publication); Literary Norfolk: Mattishall (Ann Bodham and the Donne family)

  2. Art against time ll. 8-10

    The idea that art preserves what time destroys is ancient (Horace's 'exegi monumentum', Odes III.30) and was central to Shakespeare's Sonnets. Cowper applies it to portrait painting: the 'meek intelligence' of the eyes survives Time's 'tyrannic claim'. The parenthesis shapes the syntax so that the poem's first long sentence ends in the present: 'here shines on me still the same'. The theme is taken up in the last lines, where Time 'has but half succeeded in his theft'.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Horace, Odes III.30; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tyrannic'

  3. remembrancer l. 11

    The OED records 'remembrancer' in the sense of one who reminds, or a memento; it was also the title of officials of the Exchequer charged with recording debts, which faintly colours the idea of a debt to be paid. The portrait is addressed as a 'welcome guest' who 'bidst' (commands) the poet to write. The command is accepted 'not willingly alone, / But gladly', as if it came from the mother herself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'remembrancer'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  4. artless song l. 13

    'Artless' means free from artifice, natural and sincere; it was a term of praise in the age of sensibility. Cowper's modesty is conventional but also characteristic of the poem's plain conversational couplets, quite unlike the elevated diction of most eighteenth-century elegy. The word stands in pointed contrast to the 'art' of the painter just praised (l. 8): painting can immortalise, and the poem only offers a 'frail memorial' (l. 72).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'artless'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  5. Elysian l. 19

    In Homer (Odyssey IV.563) and Virgil (Aeneid VI) Elysium is the abode of the virtuous dead. Cowper, an Evangelical Christian, uses the classical word as a figure for a dreamlike bliss in which the dead seem present. 'Steep me' suggests immersion, as in a drug or a bath. The reverie is admitted to be 'momentary', and 'Fancy', not faith, produces it, so the poem is honest about its consolation.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Homer, Odyssey IV.563-8; Virgil, Aeneid VI.637-65

  6. The mother's death ll. 21-31

    Cowper was born on 26 November 1731 at the rectory of Great Berkhamsted, Hertfordshire, where his father John Cowper was rector. His mother died at about 34-36 (sources differ) a few days after giving birth. The section recalls the child's experience: being told she was dead, hearing the bell tolled for her burial and seeing the hearse from the nursery window. The questions to her spirit ('wast thou conscious ...?') and the reply from the painted smile, 'Yes', are the emotional centre of the poem's first half.

    James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; 'On the Receipt of My Mother's Picture', Wikipedia (dates of the picture and of publication)

  7. Wretch even then l. 24

    Cowper suffered severe depressive breakdowns in 1763 and 1773, and after a dream in 1773 he believed himself irrevocably damned. The phrase 'Wretch even then, life's journey just begun' projects that sense of lifelong misery back to infancy. It prepares for the poem's later contrast of the mother who has reached harbour and the son 'tempest-toss'd'. Biographers have linked the early loss of his mother to his later melancholy, though this is an interpretation, not Cowper's own claim.

    James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  8. The funeral bell ll. 28-31

    The details are concrete and plainly told, which is the source of their power. The 'long, long sigh' and 'a last adieu' are then questioned in the next line: 'But was it such?--It was.' The broken exchange, question and answer, shows the poet correcting the child's hope that she would return with the adult's knowledge. The repeated 'adieu ... Adieus and farewells' turn on the idea of heaven as a place where partings are unknown.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986)

  9. The servants' promise ll. 36-41

    Eighteenth-century households often concealed death from small children. 'What ardently I wish'd, I long believed' is a moral generalisation in the Johnsonian manner, about the will's power over belief. 'Dupe of to-morrow even from a child' compresses this into an epigram: the child is deceived by hope as the adult is (compare Johnson's Vanity of Human Wishes and Rasselas, which also treat the credulity of hope).

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dupe'

  10. infant sorrows l. 43

    'Stock' is a commercial figure: grief is a store spent day by day. 'Infant' here means childish, of early childhood. 'Submission to my lot' is a Christian virtue, but the next line keeps the emotional truth: 'though I less deplored thee, ne'er forgot'. ('Deplored' meant lamented, wept for.) The couplet ends the paragraph of childhood grief with the theme of memory that governs the rest of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'deplore'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stock'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  11. the pastoral house l. 53

    'Pastoral' plays on two senses: belonging to a pastor (the parsonage), and idyllic, as in pastoral poetry. On John Cowper's death in 1756 the living passed to another clergyman and the family left; 'Children not thine have trod my nursery floor'. 'Short-lived possession!' sets the parsonage, held only by right of office, against the permanent 'record' kept by memory, an idea extended in 'memory's page' (l. 68).

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pastoral'

  12. Robin; bauble coach ll. 48-51

    'Bauble' means a toy or showy trifle, so the 'bauble coach' is a child's small carriage, drawn by hand. Cowper went as a small child to a local school in Berkhamsted; after his mother's death he was sent away to a boarding school at Markyate, an unhappy experience he later recalled. The detail of clothing ('scarlet mantle warm, and velvet capp'd') shows the mother's care for the child's comfort and appearance, preparing for the catalogue of her kindnesses that follows.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bauble'

  13. confectionary plum l. 61

    'Confectionary' (more often 'confectionery' in modern spelling) is here an adjective, 'of the nature of a confection'. With 'the biscuit' it forms part of the mother's 'morning bounties', tiny gifts. The 'fragrant waters' of the next line are scented waters (such as rose or lavender water) used to wash the child's face. Critics have praised this catalogue of domestic particulars as one of the most moving uses of ordinary detail in eighteenth-century verse.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'confectionary'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; 'On the Receipt of My Mother's Picture', Wikipedia (dates of the picture and of publication)

  14. cataracts and breaks ll. 66-7

    'Humour' means temper or mood, a sense derived from the old physiology of the four humours. The image of love as a river is sustained: 'flow', 'fall', 'roughen'd', 'cataracts' (waterfalls, rapids) and 'breaks' (broken water). The tribute is to an unvarying temper; it may also glance, by contrast, at Cowper's own fluctuating moods. 'Legible in memory's page' (l. 68) then shifts the metaphor from river to book.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cataract'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'humour'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  15. numbers l. 71

    The use of 'numbers' for verse was standard in eighteenth-century poetic diction (Pope's 'I lisp'd in numbers'). 'Such honours ... as my numbers may' (supply 'pay') is a modest tribute: the poet's verse can only do what it can. The phrase links back to the 'artless song' of l. 13 and forward to the 'frail memorial' of l. 72, a sequence of modest self-descriptions running through the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'number'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  16. a frail memorial l. 72

    Gray's Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), l. 78, speaks of 'Some frail memorial still erected nigh' to protect the bones of the poor. Cowper's adoption of the phrase sets his poem among elegies for the obscure: his mother, 'little noticed here', is honoured in heaven. The contrast of heaven and earth ('Not scorn'd in heaven') is a Christian turn on the Elegy's theme of unrecognised worth.

    Thomas Gray, Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), l. 78 (Thomas Gray Archive); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  17. tissued l. 75

    The OED gives 'tissue' in its early sense of a rich cloth interwoven with gold or silver, and 'tissued' as woven or decorated in this way. The child pricked out the outlines of the dress's flowers ('The violet, the pink, and jessamine') onto paper with a pin, a pastime of tracing patterns by pinpricks. Jessamine is jasmine. The scene is the last and most intimate of the remembered moments, with the mother's smile and touch.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tissue'; John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  18. The refusal to wish her back ll. 80-87

    The passage dramatises an inner debate in broken syntax and dashes ('I would not trust my heart--the dear delight / Seems so to be desired, perhaps I might.--'). The resolution is Christian: life on earth is 'So little to be loved', and the soul freed from the body would be constrained 'into bonds again'. The idea of the body as the soul's prison is Platonic and Pauline (Romans 7:24; 2 Corinthians 5:1-8). It is the poem's ethical turn, from desire for the past to acceptance of loss.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Bible, King James Version, 2 Corinthians 5:1-8

  19. The voyage simile ll. 88-95

    A 'bark' is a ship; 'Albion' is the ancient poetic name for Britain. The simile of life as a voyage and death as reaching harbour is a commonplace of Christian and classical writing. The ship rests 'quiescent' in a 'well-haven'd isle / Where spices breathe', an exotic picture recalling the Spice Islands of the East Indies, with 'airs impregnated with incense' suggesting the incense of worship as well as fragrant winds. The simile sets up the contrast with the poet's own wrecked voyage that follows.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bark'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Albion'

  20. A quoted line from Garth l. 97

    Samuel Garth's mock-heroic poem The Dispensary (1699) has, in canto III, the line 'Where billows never break, nor tempests roar'. Cowper reverses the order of the two clauses ('Where tempests never beat nor billows roar') but marks it as a quotation. The borrowing gives his image of heaven as a harbour a familiar literary authority; the line was much quoted in the eighteenth century and Bartlett's Familiar Quotations records it.

    Samuel Garth, The Dispensary (1699), canto III, l. 225 (Wikiquote, with Bartlett's attribution); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  21. thy loved consort ll. 98-9

    John Cowper, rector of Great Berkhamsted from 1722 and a chaplain to George II, remarried in 1741 and died in 1756. 'Long since has anchor'd by thy side' completes the ship image and reunites the parents in heaven. The thought becomes the son's consolation in l. 106: 'But oh, the thought, that thou art safe, and he!' That the father is placed beside the mother, though he had married again, reflects the conventional Christian hope of the reunion of the faithful in heaven rather than biographical detail; the father plays a small part in the poem as a whole.

    James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem

  22. The poet's shipwreck ll. 100-104

    'Devious' means straying from the path (Latin de via). 'Sails ripp'd, seams opening wide, and compass lost' is a precise picture of shipwreck; the 'current's thwarting force' drives him further from 'a prosperous course'. After 1773 Cowper believed himself irrevocably rejected by God, which gives the image its theological sense: he is 'scarce hoping to attain that rest'. The same imagery returns, still more desolately, in 'The Castaway' (1799), his last original poem, about a sailor washed overboard.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); William Cowper, 'The Castaway' (written 1799), in Baird and Ryskamp, Poems, vol. III

  23. Noble and heavenly descent ll. 108-111

    Cowper's maternal family, the Donnes of Norfolk, claimed descent from Henry III and other medieval nobility, a lineage biographers record and Cowper knew. 'Deduce my birth' means trace my descent. He disclaims that worldly pride in favour of a higher one: descent from parents now in heaven. The couplet adapts an old moral commonplace (true nobility is virtue, not blood) to the Evangelical language of salvation.

    James King, William Cowper: A Biography (Durham, NC: Duke UP, 1986); John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; Literary Norfolk: Mattishall (Ann Bodham and the Donne family)

  24. Fancy's half-victory ll. 112-121

    'Time unrevoked has run / His wonted course' answers the earlier wish ('Could Time, his flight reversed, restore the hours'). 'Without the sin of violating thine' means without wronging her heavenly rest by recalling her. The 'mimic show' is the portrait. The final antithesis, 'Thyself removed, thy power to soothe me left', resolves the poem's opening complaint that the lips lack language: the picture cannot speak, but it consoles. Critics have connected the poem's reliving of childhood with the Romantic poetry of memory that followed it.

    John D. Baird and Charles Ryskamp, eds., The Poems of William Cowper, vol. III: 1785-1799 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), commentary on the poem; 'On the Receipt of My Mother's Picture', Wikipedia (dates of the picture and of publication)
