Love

Fragment 1

by Sappho c. 630 – c. 570 BC

Translated by Henry Thornton Wharton (1885) · From Fragments

Love

Immortal Aphrodite of the broidered throne, daughter of Zeus, weaver of wiles, I pray thee break not my spirit with anguish and distress, O Queen. But come hither, if ever before thou didst hear my voice afar, and listen, and leaving thy father's golden house camest with chariot yoked, and fair fleet sparrows drew thee, flapping fast their wings around the dark earth, from heaven through mid sky. Quickly arrived they; and thou, blessed one, smiling with immortal countenance, didst ask What now is befallen me, and Why now I call, and What I in my mad heart most desire to see. 'What Beauty now wouldst thou draw to love thee? Who wrongs thee, Sappho? For even if she flies she shall soon follow, and if she rejects gifts shall yet give, and if she loves not shall soon love, however loth.' Come, I pray thee, now too, and release me from cruel cares; and all that my heart desires to accomplish, accomplish thou, and be thyself my ally.

This translation is printed as prose in its source edition.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Hymn to Aphrodite and Wharton's translation opening

    This seven-stanza prayer survives whole because the critic Dionysius of Halicarnassus, writing at Rome around 25 BC, quoted it in full as his model of the smooth, finished style; a papyrus later confirmed parts of it. The archive's text is Henry Thornton Wharton's literal prose translation from his Sappho of 1885, a book that did much to bring Sappho to late-Victorian readers. Wharton printed the Greek beside his version, together with verse renderings by earlier poets (Ambrose Philips, Swinburne and others). His prose gives the sense clause by clause but not the metre: the Greek is in Sapphic stanzas (see the note on the form). Every English word here is a translator's choice from a Greek text with a few disputed readings, two of which are glossed below.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, On the Arrangement of Words (De compositione verborum) 23, which quotes the whole poem; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1

  2. broidered throne st. 1, l. 1

    The poem opens with a compound epithet whose text is uncertain. Wharton's Greek reads poikilothron', 'with intricately worked (many-coloured) throne', hence his 'broidered throne' (broider is an old form of embroider). Part of the manuscript tradition has poikilophron, 'of the shifting, intricate mind', which would chime with 'weaver of wiles' that follows; Swinburne, whose versions Wharton quotes, used both ('O thou of divers-coloured mind', 'Thou of the divers-coloured seat'). Some scholars connect the -thron- element instead with throna, the flower-patterns Andromache weaves into her cloth at Iliad 22.441, so that the goddess would be 'of the many-flowered robe'. Most editors (Page, Campbell) print poikilothron', but each alternative gives a different first picture of the goddess: splendid, crafty or flowered.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 1; Homer, Iliad 22.441 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'broider'

  3. weaver of wiles st. 1, l. 2

    Doloploke combines dolos, 'trick, snare', with plekein, 'to plait or weave'. It belongs to a family of Greek ideas in which cunning is a kind of weaving (Odysseus and Penelope are the great epic examples), and in love poetry Aphrodite's power is regularly described as deceit. Sappho praises the goddess, as hymns must, while hinting at the danger of asking for her help: the same craft that may win the beloved is what has caused the speaker's distress. Wharton keeps an alliteration ('weaver of wiles'), and his 'daughter of Zeus' preserves the hymn habit of naming a god's parentage so that the right deity is addressed.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 1

  4. Prayer form and the Sapphic stanza st. 1-2

    Greek prayers commonly have three parts: the god is named with titles; the worshipper recalls earlier favours ('if ever before thou didst hear') to oblige the god to help again; then comes the request. Sappho fills the middle part with a long narrative of a past epiphany, so that the hymn becomes a story about prayer. The 'if ever before' pattern is old: Chryses uses it to Apollo at Iliad 1.37-42 and Diomedes to Athena at Iliad 5.115-120. The metre is the Sapphic stanza, three eleven-syllable lines and a short closing line (the 'adonic'). Wharton prints the Greek in stanzas but translates into continuous prose, so his sentences run across the stanza breaks. Compare Arthur Way's verse version in this archive ('To the Queen of Love'), which imitates the stanza.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; Homer, Iliad 1.37-42 and 5.115-120 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25); G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 1; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1

  5. thy father's golden house st. 2

    In the Greek, 'golden' (chrysion) stands at the end of a stanza and could go either with the house or, running on into the next stanza, with the chariot that is yoked; editors differ, and Wharton chooses the house. Either way the gold marks the divine world as radiant and imperishable, in contrast with the 'dark earth' the goddess crosses. The journey from Olympus to Lesbos dramatises the distance between god and mortal that prayer has to bridge, and the verbs of leaving, yoking and driving give the epiphany the formality of a Homeric divine descent.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 1; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 1

  6. sparrows st. 3

    Homeric gods drive horses; Sappho gives her goddess a team of small birds whirring their wings, an image at once charming and faintly comic. Ancient writers on natural history record that sparrows were thought lustful, which explains their association with Aphrodite (Athenaeus collects such lore). Wharton's 'flapping fast their wings' renders pukna dinnentes, 'whirling their wings thick and fast'. Several of the English versions Wharton reprints keep the sparrows (Philips: 'The car thy wanton sparrows drew'). Some critics have wondered whether Catullus's poems on Lesbia's sparrow (Carmina 2 and 3) glance at Sappho; that link is debated.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; Athenaeus, Deipnosophists 9.391

  7. the dark earth st. 3

    'Dark' (melainas) is a fixed epithet of earth in epic (for example Iliad 2.699), and Sappho uses it with Homeric dignity as the goddess flies 'from heaven through mid sky'. The phrase recurs at the opening of fr. 16 ('the most beautiful thing on the black earth'), where it means 'the whole world below'. In both poems the epic diction helps Sappho set personal love in a large, almost cosmic frame.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; Homer, Iliad 2.699 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25)

  8. smiling st. 4

    Aphrodite is 'laughter-loving' in Homer and Hesiod, and Sappho's participle meidiaisaisa recalls the title. In context the smile carries tone: the goddess has heard this kind of complaint before (see the repeated 'now' that follows) and treats it with affectionate irony. Critics differ on how far the humour goes: some stress the tenderness of the scene, others the gap between the speaker's agony and the goddess's calm. Either way the smile is 'immortal', a reminder that gods do not suffer love as mortals do.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 1; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 1

  9. What now ... Why now st. 4

    Deute (de aute) means 'this time again'. The goddess asks what Sappho is suffering this time, why she calls this time, and whom she must persuade this time. The repetition makes the epiphany a gently comic scene of a recurring pattern, and also generalises the poem: love is not a single event but a cycle of desire, pain and prayer. Wharton's plain 'now' loses the sense of repetition. His capitals (What, Why) mark reported questions, which turn into direct speech in the next stanza, a shift Sappho makes in the Greek too.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 1

  10. mad heart st. 5

    The thumos is the seat of feeling and impulse; the adjective mainolas, 'raving', links erotic desire with madness. Sappho returns to this territory in fr. 31, where love produces symptoms close to death. Plato's Phaedrus later counts erotic madness among the divine manias. Here the phrase is reported in the goddess's questions, which lets the speaker see her own state from outside.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; Plato, Phaedrus 244a-245c, 265b

  11. What Beauty now wouldst thou draw to love thee? st. 5

    Wharton's Greek reads tina deute Peitho / mais agen es san philotata, in which Peitho ('Persuasion', a goddess of Aphrodite's train) seems to be named; his 'What Beauty ... wouldst thou draw' is a loose attempt to make sense of it. The manuscripts are damaged at this point. Modern editions (Lobel-Page, Voigt, Campbell) print a reading in which the goddess asks whom she must 'persuade' (peitho as a verb, 'I persuade') to bring back to Sappho's love. The difference matters: in the modern text Aphrodite acts as an agent of persuasion, which prepares the promises of the next stanza. Students quoting the archive's line should note that it is the 1885 reading.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 1

  12. Who wrongs thee, Sappho? st. 5

    Self-naming is rare in Sappho and gives the poem the feel of a real conversation between poet and goddess; Swinburne's 'Anactoria', which Wharton quotes, builds on this line. The verb adikein, 'to do wrong', is moral and legal language. Anne Carson argued that the next stanza describes a kind of erotic justice: the beloved who now flees, refuses gifts and does not love will be made to pursue, give and love, so that the roles are reversed. Others read the promise more simply as the standard reassurance a god gives a suppliant. The question also makes clear that the speaker's pain is caused by another person rather than by the goddess.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; Anne Carson, 'The Justice of Aphrodite in Sappho Fr. 1', Transactions of the American Philological Association 110 (1980); D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1

  13. however loth st. 6

    The goddess promises that the beloved will love 'even against her will'. The Greek participle etheloisa is feminine; it is the single grammatical marker of the beloved's sex in the poem, and Wharton's 'she' throughout depends on it. Some earlier translators blurred or altered it. The phrase has also troubled readers morally, since it seems to promise love by compulsion; others take it as a statement of how love works (it overrides the will) rather than a wish to coerce. Wharton's archaic 'loth' (unwilling) keeps the sense without settling the question.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 1; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'loath, loth'

  14. ally st. 7

    Symmachos is a military term, 'one who fights alongside'. By ending on it Sappho turns love into a campaign in which the goddess is asked to take her side. Scholars have connected the poem with Diomedes' prayer to Athena in Iliad 5, where the goddess comes to stand beside the hero; J. J. Winkler read Sappho's hymn as a deliberate reworking of that heroic scene from a woman's point of view. The ring-composition is also notable: the final stanza repeats the opening request ('come', 'release me', 'accomplish'), so the poem closes on the prayer it began with, still unanswered.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 1 with notes; J. J. Winkler, 'Gardens of Nymphs: Public and Private in Sappho's Lyrics', in H. P. Foley, ed., Reflections of Women in Antiquity (1981); Homer, Iliad 5.115-132 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25)
