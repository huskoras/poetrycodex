Religion

Fragment 2

by Sappho c. 630 – c. 570 BC

Translated by Henry Thornton Wharton (1885) · From Fragments

Religion

That man seems to me peer of gods, who sits in thy presence, and hears close to him thy sweet speech and lovely laughter; that indeed makes my heart flutter in my bosom. For when I see thee but a little, I have no utterance left, my tongue is broken down, and straightway a subtle fire has run under my skin, with my eyes I have no sight, my ears ring, sweat pours down, and a trembling seizes all my body; I am paler than grass, and seem in my madness little better than one dead. But I must dare all, since one so poor

This translation is printed as prose in its source edition.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Fragment 31 and Wharton's numbering opening

    Wharton numbered the fragments after Bergk's edition, so this poem appears as fr. 2; modern editions (Lobel-Page, Voigt) call it fr. 31, the number used in most scholarship. It survives as a quotation in the essay On the Sublime ascribed to Longinus, whose author admired how Sappho gathers the symptoms of passion into one design, 'not one passion, but a congress of passions' in Wharton's paraphrase. The quotation breaks off after the first line of a fifth stanza, which is why Wharton's version ends in mid-sentence. The translation is Wharton's literal prose of 1885; Catullus 51 and many English versions (Philips, Smollett, Symonds) are printed in his book.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Longinus, On the Sublime 10 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts, 1899), which quotes the poem; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31

  2. peer of gods l. 1

    The phrase is Homeric (heroes are often 'equal to a god'), and Catullus kept it ('par esse deo'). Readers have argued about why the man is godlike. One view: he is blessed because he is close to the girl. Another: he is godlike because he can sit beside her unmoved, while the speaker collapses; gods, like this man, do not suffer. Wharton's 'peer' (an equal) is an old literary word. 'Seems to me' (phainetai moi) matters too: the poem is about how things appear to the speaker, and the man drops out of view once her own symptoms begin.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur'); G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 31

  3. Who is 'that man'? l. 1

    Wilamowitz (Sappho und Simonides, 1913) read the poem as a wedding song in praise of a bride and groom; Page (1955) rejected this, finding no sign of a wedding. Many earlier readers took it as a song of jealousy. Some recent critics treat the man as hypothetical ('whoever sits opposite you'), since the Greek relative (ostis) can be generalising. The question is still open, but all readings agree that the poem's centre is not the man but the speaker's experience of seeing and hearing the beloved.

    U. von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff, Sappho und Simonides (Berlin, 1913); D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 31; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 31

  4. sweet speech and lovely laughter l. 2

    Imeroen, 'lovely', comes from himeros, 'desire', so the laughter is literally desire-awakening. Catullus rendered the phrase 'dulce ridentem', and Horace echoed it at the end of Odes 1.22 ('dulce ridentem Lalagen amabo, / dulce loquentem'). The order of the poem is carefully built: hearing (voice, laughter), then seeing ('when I see thee'), then the failure of each sense in turn.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur'); Horace, Odes 1.22.23-24

  5. flutter l. 3

    The verb ptoein describes being startled into agitation; it is used in Homer of panic. Placed at the turn from the man to the speaker ('that indeed makes my heart flutter'), it opens the catalogue of symptoms. Wharton's 'flutter' keeps a bird-like image. What 'that' (to) refers to is debated: the girl's laughter, the whole scene, or the man's privileged closeness.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 31

  6. the catalogue of symptoms ll. 4-8

    The symptoms move deliberately through the body's senses, as Longinus noticed: soul and body, hearing and tongue, eyes and skin are all affected at once, as though they belonged to someone else (On the Sublime 10). Several symptoms recall Homeric descriptions of fear in battle, so Sappho takes the language of the warrior's terror and applies it to love. George Devereux (1970) read the passage as a clinical anxiety attack; most critics see it as a literary construction. Catullus 51 keeps the sequence but drops the sweat and pallor and ends with a rebuke to himself.

    Longinus, On the Sublime 10 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts, 1899), which quotes the poem; H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; G. Devereux, 'The Nature of Sappho's Seizure in Fr. 31 LP as Evidence of her Inversion', Classical Quarterly n.s. 20 (1970); Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur')

  7. my tongue is broken l. 5

    The manuscript of Longinus gives words usually printed as alla kam men glossa eage, 'but my tongue breaks'. The hiatus between glossa and eage is irregular, and editors have proposed emendations (for example a verb meaning 'is frozen' or 'is silent'); others defend the transmitted text, suggesting that the broken sound enacts the broken tongue. Wharton prints and translates the transmitted reading. The image itself, a tongue that snaps like something brittle, is striking and has no close parallel in earlier Greek.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 31

  8. a subtle fire has run under my skin l. 6

    Lepton means 'fine, thin, light'; Wharton's 'subtle' keeps the sense of something that penetrates unseen. Catullus has 'tenuis sub artus / flamma demanat' ('a thin flame runs down beneath my limbs'). Love as fire is a commonplace, but Sappho's emphasis is physical and precise: the fire 'has run under' (hupadedromaken) the skin like a current. Wharton notes that Plutarch called the poem 'mixed with fire'.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur'); D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31

  9. my ears ring l. 7

    The verb is connected with rhombos, a whirling ritual instrument that made a humming sound. The image suggests a buzzing that fills the ears, so that hearing, which began the crisis with the girl's voice and laughter, is now blotted out. Catullus: 'sonitu suopte / tintinant aures' ('my ears ring with their own sound'). The detail is one of the most physical in the catalogue, and Wharton's plain 'ring' is accurate.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur'); D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31

  10. paler than grass l. 8

    Chloros, the root of English 'chlorophyll', is the colour of fresh vegetation, of honey, or of sickly skin; Homer speaks of chloron deos, 'green fear' (Iliad 7.479). Wharton's 'paler' is the usual English compromise; some modern translators (Anne Carson among them) keep 'greener than grass'. The comparison ties the body to the natural world and is one of the poem's most-discussed details.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31; Homer, Iliad 7.479 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25)

  11. little better than one dead l. 9

    The speaker sees herself as close to death. Wharton's 'in my madness' has no clear basis in the Greek he prints, which ends the clause with phainomai ('I seem'); modern editions read phainom' em' autai, 'I seem to myself'. That reading stresses that the speaker observes her own collapse: the poem is both an experience of losing control and a cool, exact account of it, a double perspective many critics see as central to its power.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 31; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 31

  12. But I must dare all, since one so poor l. 10

    The last line (alla pan tolmaton, epei kai peneta) begins a fifth stanza, but Longinus stops quoting. Tolmaton can mean 'must be dared' or 'must be endured', and peneta, 'a poor man', suggests a general saying, perhaps that even the poor may change their fortune. Some scholars think the poem went on to a reflection that turned the speaker towards endurance or self-command; Catullus ends his version with a stanza rebuking himself for idleness (otium), which may or may not reflect Sappho's lost lines. Wharton's dots mark the gap honestly.

    H. T. Wharton, Sappho: Memoir, Text, Selected Renderings and a Literal Translation (1885; Gutenberg text of the 5th ed., 1908), fr. 2 (= fr. 31 Lobel-Page/Voigt) with notes with notes; Longinus, On the Sublime 10 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts, 1899), which quotes the poem; Catullus, Carmina 51 ('Ille mi par esse deo videtur'); D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 31
