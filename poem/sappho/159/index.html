Love

To Anactoria (Way)

by Sappho c. 630 – c. 570 BC

Translated by Arthur S. Way (1920) · From Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (1920)

Love

Rushing war-hosts, horsemen or foot or galleys—
These doth one call, those doth another, fairest
Sights on earth: I say that my love of all is
Sweetest and rarest.
Hear the proof, which lightly, I wot, convinces:—
'Mid the comely, Helen would fain discover
One without peer, and of the goodly princes
Chose for her lover
Him who brought the glory of Troy to ruin!
Reckless all of parent and child, she lavished
On the alien love for her own undoing;
Troyward was ravished.
Anactoria—she who contemns the blessing
Near at hand, is like to a reed wind-shaken.
Such are you!—love held in secure possessing
You have forsaken,
Her whose footfall's music myself had rather
Hear, and see her face in its beauty beaming,
Than to gaze where horsemen and footmen gather
Panoply-gleaming.
What is best is set above man's attaining;
Yet, if Fortune smiled on us once, 'tis better
To recall with prayer and with upward-straining
Than to forget her.

Codex Note

Way weaves several fragments in the same metre into one connected poem (his preface); the joins are his.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Fragment 16 in Way's version opening

    Fragment 16 was unknown until Grenfell and Hunt published papyrus P.Oxy. 1231 in 1914; it was still new when Arthur Sanders Way (1847-1930), translator of Homer and Euripides, included it in Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (1920). Way says in his preface that he wove together fragments in the same metre that might belong to one poem, and the archive's note warns that the joins are his. His heading describes Anactoria as having abandoned a girl-friend once dear to Sappho, which is his interpretation, not the papyrus. He keeps the Sapphic stanza and adds rhyme.

    A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface; B. P. Grenfell and A. S. Hunt, The Oxyrhynchus Papyri X (1914), no. 1231 (first edition of fr. 16); summary at Wikipedia, 'Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 1231'; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16

  2. Priamel: horsemen, foot, ships st. 1

    The Greek begins: some say an army of horsemen, some of foot-soldiers, some of ships is the most beautiful thing on the black earth, but I say it is whatever one loves. This rhetorical pattern (the 'priamel') is common in Greek lyric. Sappho's version is pointed because the rejected options are military, the values of epic; she sets eros up as the measure of beauty instead. Way keeps the three-part list ('horsemen or foot or galleys', galleys being oared warships) but turns 'whatever one loves' into 'my love', narrowing the general claim.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16

  3. I say that my love of all is sweetest st. 1

    In the papyrus Sappho says the finest thing is 'that which a person desires' (ken' otto tis eratai). That is a claim about value, often read as an early statement that beauty depends on the lover. Way's 'my love of all is / Sweetest and rarest' turns the maxim into a declaration about one beloved. The change suits his reading of the poem as a personal reproach, but it weakens the general thesis that the Helen example is about to 'prove'.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface

  4. Hear the proof st. 2

    Using a myth as evidence (a paradeigma) is a standard move in archaic poetry, from Phoenix's speech in Iliad 9 onwards. Sappho presents her lyric claim as something that can be demonstrated, giving the poem the outline of an argument: thesis, proof, application to the present (Anactoria). Way's 'I wot' ('I know') and 'fain' ('gladly') are deliberate archaisms, part of the high Victorian diction he also used for Homer.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wot'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fain'

  5. Helen st. 2

    The example is surprising. In epic Helen is the object of everyone's desire, but Sappho makes her the one who chooses: the woman who far surpassed all mortals in beauty herself judged the finest thing to be the man she loved, and left everything for him. Critics have noted that Sappho neither blames nor excuses Helen here, unlike much Greek tradition; her contemporary Alcaeus condemns Helen as the ruin of Troy (frr. 42 and 283). Compare also the old men on the walls of Troy, who find Helen's beauty a sufficient reason for the war (Iliad 3.156-160). Way's lines fit the general sense, but see the next notes on what he adds.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16; Homer, Iliad 3.156-160 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25)

  6. Him who brought the glory of Troy to ruin st. 3

    The Greek of lines 8-9 is broken, and editors after 1914 filled the gaps in different ways. Way's line reflects a restoration in which Helen chooses the man who destroyed all Troy's glory, that is, Paris. Modern editions (Lobel-Page, Voigt, Campbell) print a text in which Helen 'left her husband, the best of men, and went sailing to Troy'. The difference matters: in the modern reading Helen's choice is defined by what she left behind, which suits the poem's theme of absence and longing, whereas Way inserts a moral consequence (Troy's ruin) that the surviving Greek does not state.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16; B. P. Grenfell and A. S. Hunt, The Oxyrhynchus Papyri X (1914), no. 1231 (first edition of fr. 16); summary at Wikipedia, 'Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 1231'

  7. parent and child st. 3

    This detail is secure in the papyrus. Helen's daughter Hermione appears in the Odyssey (4.12-14) being given in marriage to the son of Akhilleus. Sappho's point is the overwhelming force of eros, which overrides every other tie. Way's 'Reckless all of parent and child, she lavished / On the alien love for her own undoing' adds a judgement ('for her own undoing') and a note of foreignness ('alien') that the Greek does not supply.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; Homer, Odyssey 4.3-14; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16

  8. Troyward was ravished st. 3

    Ravish here keeps its older sense, 'to carry off by force' (OED), and Way's word brings the poem closer to the tradition of Helen's abduction. The surviving Greek uses active verbs for Helen's departure. A following line is fragmentary ('led her astray'), and editors supply different subjects, often Kypris (Aphrodite). Scholars therefore still debate how far Sappho shows Helen choosing freely and how far driven by a god, a question as old as Gorgias' Encomium of Helen.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ravish'

  9. Anactoria st. 4

    In the papyrus the Helen example 'has reminded me now of Anactoria, who is not here', and Sappho says she would rather see Anactoria's walk and the bright sparkle of her face than Lydian chariots. Nothing in the Greek says that Anactoria has been unfaithful. Way, reading the fragment as a reproach, gives her a guilt the Greek lacks ('Such are you!', 'You have forsaken'), and his heading names the injured party as a girl-friend of Sappho's. Some scholars have suggested the name is a pseudonym or have identified her with an Anagora of Miletus named in ancient testimony; both ideas are speculative.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16

  10. contemns st. 4

    Way's line voices a familiar moral idea, that people undervalue what they have and want what is far away, which suits his reading of Anactoria as a forsaker. In Sappho's poem the logic runs the other way: it is the absent Anactoria whom the speaker longs for, so the emotion lies in absence rather than reproach. The verb is formal literary English, typical of Way's high style.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'contemn'; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16

  11. a reed wind-shaken st. 4

    Jesus asks the crowds what they went into the wilderness to see: 'A reed shaken with the wind?' (Matthew 11:7; Luke 7:24), meaning someone weak and changeable, which John the Baptist was not. Way borrows the biblical phrase for Anactoria's inconstancy. The papyrus here is damaged (a few letters may suggest something bent or light), and Way's simile is best taken as one of the joins he admits to in his preface. Students should not quote it as Sappho's image.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 11:7; Luke 7:24; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface; D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16

  12. footfall ... face in its beauty beaming st. 5

    This is the emotional centre of the poem: Sappho would rather see Anactoria's way of walking and the radiance of her face than Lydian chariots and armed infantry. The words amarugma ('flashing, sparkle') and lampron ('bright') give the face a brilliance that outshines the gleam of armour. Way turns the step into 'music' (an added image) and, more importantly, attaches it to the forsaken friend rather than to Anactoria, so the speaker's longing is redirected. In the Greek the woman whose step and face are desired is Anactoria herself.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface

  13. horsemen and footmen ... panoply st. 5

    Lydia, the rich kingdom of western Asia Minor with its capital at Sardis, was the great power on Lesbos's horizon in Sappho's time; Lydian luxury and display appear elsewhere in her poems (fr. 96 compares a woman now living in Lydia to the moon). The ring-composition is clear: the poem returns at its close to the armies of its opening, now decisively outweighed. Way's 'panoply' (a full suit of armour, from Greek panoplia) keeps the military glitter but loses the concrete place-name.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; D. L. Page, Sappho and Alcaeus: An Introduction to the Study of Ancient Lesbian Poetry (Oxford, 1955), on fr. 16; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'panoply'

  14. Sapphic stanza and the uncertain ending st. 6

    Scholars disagree whether the poem ended with the Lydian stanza (a neat ring) or went on; the papyrus has traces of further lines that seem to speak of what is impossible for human beings and of 'praying to share'. Way builds these traces into a consoling close, in which remembering past happiness with prayer is better than forgetting, and he personifies Fortune. This ending is his reconstruction. Throughout he keeps Sappho's stanza shape, three long lines and a short final line ('Sweetest and rarest', 'Chose for her lover'), and adds a rhyme scheme Sappho did not use.

    D. A. Campbell, ed. and trans., Greek Lyric I: Sappho and Alcaeus (Loeb Classical Library, 1982), Sappho fr. 16; G. O. Hutchinson, Greek Lyric Poetry: A Commentary on Selected Larger Pieces (Oxford, 2001), on Sappho 16; A. S. Way, trans., Sappho and the Vigil of Venus (London: Macmillan, 1920), poem XXX 'To Anactoria' and preface
