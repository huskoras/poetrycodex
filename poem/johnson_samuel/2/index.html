Death & Dying

On the Death of Mr. Robert Levet

by Samuel Johnson 1709–1784

Death & Dying

Condemn'd to hope's delusive mine,
As on we toil, from day to day,
By sudden blasts, or slow decline,
Our social comforts drop away.

Well try'd, through many a varying year,
See Levet to the grave descend,
Officious, innocent, sincere,
Of ev'ry friendless name the friend.

Yet still he fills affection's eye,
Obscurely wise, and coarsely kind;
Nor, letter'd arrogance, deny
Thy praise to merit unrefined.

When fainting nature call'd for aid,
And hov'ring death prepar'd the blow,
His vig'rous remedy display'd
The pow'r of art, without the show.

In mis'ry's darkest cavern known,
His useful care was ever nigh,
Where hopeless anguish pour'd his groan,
And lonely want retir'd to die.

No summons, mock'd by chill delay,
No petty gain, disdain'd by pride;
The modest wants of ev'ry day
The toil of ev'ry day supply'd.

His virtues walk'd their narrow round,
Nor made a pause, nor left a void;
And sure the eternal master found
The single talent well-employ'd.

The busy day--the peaceful night,
Unfelt, uncounted, glided by;
His frame was firm--his pow'rs were bright,
Though now his eightieth year was nigh.

Then, with no fiery throbbing pain,
No cold gradations of decay,
Death broke, at once, the vital chain,
And freed his soul the nearest way.

Study notes by the editors

  1. On the Death of Dr Robert Levet title; st. 1

    Levet, born near Hull, had worked as a waiter in Paris, where he picked up medical knowledge by attending lectures, and then practised cheaply among the London poor; he lodged in Johnson's household for many years. Johnson called him 'an obscure practiser in physick amongst the lower people'. He died suddenly in Johnson's house at Bolt Court on 17 January 1782. The poem was published in the Gentleman's Magazine (August 1783), apparently without Johnson's intention to print it. It is in nine quatrains of iambic tetrameter rhymed ABAB, plain in diction, a poem in praise of a plain man.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'; Jack Lynch (ed.), annotated text of 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; University of Virginia, Anthology of Poetry, annotated 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; Wikipedia, 'Robert Levet'

  2. hope's delusive mine l. 1

    Roman law punished criminals 'ad metalla', with labour in the mines, and later European penal practice kept the idea; 'condemn'd' points that way. The miners dig for the ore hope promises, and the mine is 'delusive' because it does not yield it. 'Sudden blasts' in l. 3 extends the image, since blasts and explosions of firedamp were mine disasters; 'slow decline' is the other way the miners die. The opening states the gloomy general truth of The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749) before turning to a particular good man.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'; Samuel Johnson, The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749)

  3. social comforts l. 4

    'Social' means belonging to companionship. In 1781-82 Johnson lost Henry Thrale (April 1781) as well as Levet, and he was himself ill. The first stanza generalises in the first-person plural ('we toil', 'Our social comforts'), so the elegy for Levet is also a meditation on the survivor's loneliness. The verb 'drop' quietly continues the mine image (things falling) and suggests fruit dropping, a natural, inevitable loss.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; James Boswell, The Life of Samuel Johnson (1791)

  4. Officious l. 7

    Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines 'officious' first as 'Kind; doing good offices', and only second as 'Importunately forward'. Latin officiosus means dutiful, obliging. The line is a sequence of three adjectives, each a moral portrait in one word: obliging, harmless (innocent, Latin in-nocens, not hurting), and without pretence. Modern readers can easily misread 'officious' as an insult, and editors gloss it for that reason.

    Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), 'officious'; Jack Lynch (ed.), annotated text of 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy

  5. Of ev'ry friendless name the friend l. 8

    The chiasmus-like turn on 'friendless ... friend' compresses Levet's vocation. 'Name' here means person, as in a list or register. Boswell records Johnson's defence of the uncouth Levet: his brutality was in his manners, not his mind. Levet's practice among the poor was rewarded, when at all, in food or drink, and Johnson admired his charity. The line also recalls the biblical charge to care for the fatherless and the stranger, whom no one else befriends.

    James Boswell, The Life of Samuel Johnson (1791); E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; Jack Lynch (ed.), annotated text of 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'

  6. Obscurely wise, and coarsely kind l. 10

    'Obscurely' means without public distinction, not unclearly. 'Coarsely' admits Levet's uncouthness. Johnson's balanced antitheses, characteristic of his prose, here hold praise and candour together, so that the elegy avoids the flattery Johnson criticised in conventional epitaphs (see his 'Essay on Epitaphs', 1740). Critics often cite the line as an example of Johnson's concern with truthful praise. The adverbs qualify without cancelling: the wisdom and kindness are real, and the obscurity and coarseness are honestly admitted.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'; Samuel Johnson, 'An Essay on Epitaphs' (1740)

  7. letter'd arrogance ll. 11-12

    'Letter'd' means learned, educated. The phrase may point at university-trained physicians, who looked down on irregular practitioners like Levet, and more broadly at any learned snob. 'Merit unrefined' is the counterpart of 'coarsely kind'. The apostrophe is a classical device (addressing an abstraction), but its target is social: Johnson, who had left Oxford without a degree and knew poverty at first hand, defends worth without status.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'; University of Virginia, Anthology of Poetry, annotated 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'

  8. art, without the show l. 16

    Johnson's Dictionary gives 'art' as 'the power of doing something not taught by nature', a skill; medicine was 'the art' par excellence. 'Show' glances at the wig, cane and carriage of eighteenth-century physicians, who were often satirised for display. 'Fainting nature' and 'hov'ring death' are personifications in the period's elegiac style, but the stanza's point is practical efficacy: 'vig'rous remedy'.

    Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), 'art'; E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy

  9. mis'ry's darkest cavern ll. 17-20

    The 'cavern' continues, faintly, the mine image of stanza 1, and turns it into the dwellings of London's poor. The stanza's personifications ('hopeless anguish', 'lonely want') stand for particular people: the suffering patient and the destitute dying. 'Want' means poverty. The careful balance of the quatrain (known/nigh, groan/die) gives a sombre dignity to scenes that were plain fact for Levet's practice.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'

  10. No summons ... No petty gain ll. 21-24

    'Summons' is a call to a patient. The two negatives deny two failings of doctors: delay and contempt for small fees. The second couplet is a chiasmus of sorts: 'The modest wants of ev'ry day / The toil of ev'ry day supply'd', where daily work and daily needs balance exactly, so Levet lived without accumulation, like the man in Proverbs who asks for neither poverty nor riches (Proverbs 30:8).

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; Proverbs 30:8 (KJV)

  11. narrow round l. 25

    A 'round' is a regular circuit, as of a watchman or a doctor on visits. The image honours constancy over greatness: his virtues never paused and left no gap. The line is often quoted as one of Johnson's finest, because it makes smallness itself the measure of a life well lived, preparing the parable of the single talent. The two 'nor' clauses insist on continuity: Levet's goodness had no intervals and, now that he is dead, its absence leaves the void it never left in life.

    E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'

  12. The single talent ll. 27-28

    In the parable a master gives five, two and one talents (sums of money) to servants; the servant with one buries it and is condemned. Johnson reverses the case: Levet had one talent and employed it, so 'the eternal master' finds him faithful ('well done, thou good and faithful servant', Matthew 25:21). The parable mattered intensely to Johnson, who feared he had wasted his own gifts and recorded that fear in his private prayers and meditations. The 'sure' is a confident hope, unusual in Johnson's writing about salvation.

    Matthew 25:14-30 (KJV); Jack Lynch (ed.), annotated text of 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy

  13. eightieth year l. 32

    Levet is usually said to have been born in 1705, which would make him seventy-six or seventy-seven in January 1782 (some sources give a slightly different age). Johnson's 'nigh' allows approximation. The stanza praises a vigorous old age: 'His frame was firm' (frame means bodily constitution), 'his pow'rs were bright', and the days and nights 'glided by' uncounted, a happiness of not noticing time. The dashes in the archive text mark pauses in a balanced line, the day set against the night and the body against the mind.

    University of Virginia, Anthology of Poetry, annotated 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; Wikipedia, 'Robert Levet'; E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy

  14. Death broke, at once, the vital chain ll. 33-36

    Levet died suddenly, apparently of a heart attack, at Johnson's house. The 'vital chain' is the bond of life linking soul and body; breaking it is release, so that death here is liberating rather than terrifying. Johnson, who dreaded death, describes the kind of death he wished for. The final stanza answers the first: the 'sudden blasts, or slow decline' of l. 3 become a blessing ('no cold gradations of decay'), and the miner of hope's mine is freed.

    University of Virginia, Anthology of Poetry, annotated 'On the Death of Dr. Robert Levet'; E. L. McAdam, Jr. with George Milne (eds.), Samuel Johnson: Poems, Yale Edition of the Works of Samuel Johnson, vol. 6 (Yale University Press, 1964), notes to the Levet elegy; J. D. Fleeman (ed.), Samuel Johnson: The Complete English Poems (Penguin, 1971), notes to 'On the Death of Dr Robert Levet'
