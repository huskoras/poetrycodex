Ode to Duty

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

LivingReligion

Stern Daughter of the Voice of God!
O Duty! if that name thou love
Who art a Light to guide, a Rod
To check the erring, and reprove;
Thou who art victory and law
When empty terrors overawe;
From vain temptations dost set free;
From strife and from despair; a glorious ministry.

There are who ask not if thine eye
Be on them; who, in love and truth,
Where no misgiving is, rely
Upon the genial sense of youth:
Glad Hearts! without reproach or blot;
Who do thy work, and know it not:
May joy be theirs while life shall last!
And Thou, if they should totter, teach them to stand fast!

Serene will be our days and bright,
And happy will our nature be,
When love is an unerring light,
And joy its own security.
And bless'd are they who in the main
This faith, even now, do entertain:
Live in the spirit of this creed;
Yet find that other strength, according to their need.

I, loving freedom, and untried;
No sport of every random gust,
Yet being to myself a guide,
Too blindly have reposed my trust:
Resolved that nothing e'er should press
Upon my present happiness,
I shoved unwelcome tasks away;
But thee I now would serve more strictly, if I may.

Through no disturbance of my soul,
Or strong compunction in me wrought,
I supplicate for thy controul;
But in the quietness of thought:
Me this uncharter'd freedom tires;
I feel the weight of chance desires:
My hopes no more must change their name,
I long for a repose which ever is the same.

Yet not the less would I throughout
Still act according to the voice
Of my own wish; and feel past doubt
That my submissiveness was choice:
Not seeking in the school of pride
For "precepts over dignified,"
Denial and restraint I prize
No farther than they breed a second Will more wise.

Stern Lawgiver! yet thou dost wear
The Godhead's most benignant grace;
Nor know we any thing so fair
As is the smile upon thy face;
Flowers laugh before thee on their beds;
And Fragrance in thy footing treads;
Thou dost preserve the Stars from wrong;
And the most ancient Heavens through Thee are fresh and strong.

To humbler functions, awful Power!
I call thee: I myself commend
Unto thy guidance from this hour;
Oh! let my weakness have an end!
Give unto me, made lowly wise,
The spirit of self-sacrifice;
The confidence of reason give;
And in the light of truth thy Bondman let me live!

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'Stern Daughter of the Voice of God' l. 1

    In Paradise Lost IX.652-653 Eve says that God left his command 'Sole Daughter of his voice': the prohibition is the child of the divine word. Wordsworth personifies Duty in the same way. The phrase also corresponds to the rabbinic bat qôl, literally 'daughter of the voice', the heavenly voice that conveyed God's will after prophecy ceased. The opening 'Stern' and the ode's apostrophic form also follow Gray's 'Hymn to Adversity', which begins 'Daughter of Jove, relentless Power'.

    Milton, Paradise Lost IX.651-654 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); 'Bat Kol', Jewish Encyclopedia (1906); Thomas Gray, 'Hymn to Adversity' (1753), Thomas Gray Archive

  2. date, model and purpose st. 1

    An early form of the poem, lacking the title and first stanza, was finished before April 1804, so it predates the death at sea of the poet's brother John in February 1805; a link between the two is therefore chronologically impossible for that early version. The Fenwick note says that the ode 'is on the model of Gray's Ode to Adversity, which is copied from Horace's Ode to Fortune' (Odes I.35), and admits that his wife and sister often teased him for forgetting this 'dedication of myself to the stern law-giver'. The poem marks a turn from the faith in spontaneous impulse of 1798. In 1837 Wordsworth added a Latin motto from Seneca (Epistles 120), not printed here, praising a man good by habit who cannot act except rightly.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; Thomas Gray, 'Hymn to Adversity' (1753), Thomas Gray Archive; Horace, Odes I.35 (Perseus)

  3. Light and Rod ll. 3-4

    'Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path' (Psalm 119:105); 'thy rod and thy staff they comfort me' (Psalm 23:4); and the rod of correction in Proverbs 13:24 and 22:15. Duty is 'victory and law' when 'empty terrors overawe', that is, it gives firmness against groundless fears. The stanza ends in a line of six stresses, an alexandrine ('a glorious ministry'), the closing feature of every stanza, which slows and solemnizes the end.

    The Bible (KJV), Psalm 119:105; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 23:4; The Bible (KJV), Proverbs 13:24

  4. stanza form st. 1, l. 8

    Gray's 'Hymn to Adversity' used an eight-line stanza closed by a longer line; Wordsworth adapts it with a hexameter close, a feature also found in the Spenserian stanza. The weighty final line recurs as a moral summation in each stanza ('teach them to stand fast!', 'according to their need'). The 1807 text of the first stanza ended 'And calm'st the weary strife of frail humanity!'; the reading here ('From strife and from despair; a glorious ministry') is a later revision.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; Thomas Gray, 'Hymn to Adversity' (1753), Thomas Gray Archive

  5. genial l. 12

    'Genial' in this older sense means belonging to one's natural disposition, productive and life-giving (OED), not merely friendly. Wordsworth's 'Tintern Abbey' speaks of 'my genial spirits', and Coleridge's 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802) laments 'My genial spirits fail'. The second stanza describes those who do good by instinct, 'Who do thy work, and know it not', and blesses them; but the poet asks Duty to support them if they 'totter'. In 1807 this stanza closed differently, with a prayer that if they fail Duty's 'saving arms' be cast around them; the ending here is a later revision.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genial, adj.'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  6. 'Who do thy work, and know it not' l. 14

    The line captures the poem's starting position: the ideal of 1798, that a heart in harmony with nature does right without rules. The poem does not reject that ideal but questions whether it is reliable; the speaker counts himself among those who trusted it 'Too blindly'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  7. 'When love is an unerring light' ll. 17-20

    The stanza looks forward to a condition where duty and desire coincide. The idea recalls Augustine's precept 'love, and do what you will' (dilige, et quod vis fac, Homilies on the First Epistle of John 7.8): perfected love needs no external law. Those who 'in the main' (for the most part) live by this faith are 'bless'd', yet even they need 'that other strength' of duty when love falters. 'Even now' implies that this state belongs fully only to an ideal future.

    Augustine, In epistulam Ioannis ad Parthos tractatus 7.8; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  8. the poet's confession ll. 25-32

    'Untried' means not yet tested by hardship. 'No sport of every random gust' insists that he was not merely unstable, but he has 'Too blindly' trusted his own guidance. The confession matches the Fenwick note, where Wordsworth admits he has often been a 'transgressor'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  9. 'Me this uncharter'd freedom tires' l. 37

    A charter grants specified rights or privileges; 'unchartered' freedom is liberty without definition or bounds. Blake's 'London' (1794) had used 'charter'd' bitterly of streets and river, owned and regulated. Wordsworth's paradox, that boundless liberty is a burden, anticipates his sonnet 'Nuns fret not at their convent's narrow room' (written 1802, published 1807), where the sonnet's 'scanty plot of ground' is a relief from 'too much liberty'. Some readers see a political as well as a personal retreat here, from revolutionary freedom to order.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; William Blake, 'London', Songs of Experience (1794); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chartered, adj.'

  10. 'My hopes no more must change their name' ll. 39-40

    The line is compressed. Most readers take it to mean that hopes subject to 'chance desires' constantly turn into something else, disappointment, fear or a new wish, and so 'change their name'. The longing for unchanging repose answers this restlessness. The paradox is that the repose sought is not inactivity but stable obedience. Critics have found in the stanza a candid portrait of the strain of living by impulse.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (Oxford, 1940-49), notes to the poem

  11. the suppressed stanza st. 6

    The 1807 first edition included this stanza; Wordsworth later omitted it. It qualifies the submission of the poem: the speaker will still act according to his 'own wish' and wants to feel that his 'submissiveness was choice'. He values denial and restraint only as far as they 'breed a second Will more wise', a self chosen rather than imposed. The quotation 'precepts over dignified' is presented as a citation but its source has not been securely identified; it mocks pompous moralizing ('the school of pride'). Its removal makes the later poem more simply devotional.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (Oxford, 1940-49), notes to the poem

  12. 'Stern Lawgiver!' and Duty's smile ll. 49-52

    'Lawgiver' recalls Moses and the law of Sinai, and the epithet 'Stern' recalls Gray's 'Stern rugged Nurse' in the 'Hymn to Adversity', where the goddess is asked to wear a gentler form. The shift from severity to grace is the turn of the ode: Duty's smile is the fairest thing known. 'Benignant' means kindly, gracious. The poem here moves from moral psychology to a cosmic vision.

    Thomas Gray, 'Hymn to Adversity' (1753), Thomas Gray Archive; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  13. 'Flowers laugh before thee' ll. 53-54

    Nature rejoices in Duty's presence and fragrance follows her steps. Classical poetry gives similar attributes to goddesses of love: in Hesiod's Theogony (194ff.) grass springs up beneath Aphrodite's feet. The biblical image of the hills breaking into singing and the trees clapping their hands (Isaiah 55:12) is also close. Wordsworth transfers to Duty attributes traditionally given to love and beauty, uniting the moral law with natural joy.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem; The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 55:12

  14. Duty and the stars ll. 55-56

    The ancient heavens are 'fresh and strong' through Duty: the order of the universe is itself a kind of obedience. Readers often compare Kant's conclusion to the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), which joins 'the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me' as twin objects of awe; Wordsworth probably knew Kant's ideas, if at all, through Coleridge, so the parallel is one of thought rather than a direct source. The idea also has biblical roots in Psalm 19, where the heavens declaring God's glory are followed by praise of his law.

    Kant, Critique of Practical Reason (1788), Conclusion; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:1-8; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  15. awful l. 57

    In older English 'awful' means inspiring awe (OED). The address echoes the invocation of Gray's 'Hymn to Adversity' and Horace's Ode to Fortune, both prayers to a dreaded power to be gentle. The final stanza moves 'To humbler functions': having seen Duty in the cosmos, the speaker commends himself to her guidance in ordinary life. The movement from cosmic to personal reverses the poem's earlier ascent and makes the closing prayer an act of humility.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'awful, adj.'; Thomas Gray, 'Hymn to Adversity' (1753), Thomas Gray Archive

  16. 'made lowly wise' l. 61

    In Paradise Lost VIII.173 Raphael tells Adam to restrain his curiosity about the heavens: 'be lowly wise'. Wordsworth's echo makes humility a form of wisdom and links his ode with Milton's argument for obedience. The speaker prays for 'The spirit of self-sacrifice' and 'The confidence of reason', a balanced pairing of feeling and judgment. The tone is that of a collect or private prayer, and the stanza moves away from the self-analysis of the middle stanzas toward simple petition.

    Milton, Paradise Lost VIII.172-174 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 3 (1896), Fenwick note, variants and notes to the poem

  17. 'thy Bondman' l. 64

    A bondman is a slave or serf. The paradox of free bondage is Pauline: 'Being then made free from sin, ye became the servants of righteousness' (Romans 6:18), and the Book of Common Prayer's Collect for Peace addresses God, 'whose service is perfect freedom'. The ode thus ends by resolving its opening contrast between freedom and law: true freedom is found in chosen obedience, the 'second Will more wise' of the suppressed stanza.

    The Bible (KJV), Romans 6:18; Book of Common Prayer (1662), Morning Prayer, Second Collect, for Peace
