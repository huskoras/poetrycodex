Death & Dying

A slumber did my spirit seal

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Death & Dying

A slumber did my spirit seal;
I had no human fears:
She seem'd a thing that could not feel
The touch of earthly years.

No motion has she now, no force;
She neither hears nor sees;
Roll'd round in earth's diurnal course
With rocks, and stones, and trees!

Study notes by the editors

  1. A 'Lucy' poem (Goslar, 1798-99) l. 1

    Wordsworth's note to Isabella Fenwick says only 'Written in Germany'. He and Dorothy spent the bitter winter of 1798-99 at Goslar, where he also wrote 'Strange fits of passion' and 'She dwelt among the untrodden ways'. He sent the poem to Coleridge, who in April 1799 called it 'a most sublime Epitaph' and added that whether it had any reality he could not say; perhaps, in some gloomier moment, Wordsworth had imagined the moment when his sister might die. The poem appeared without title in volume II of Lyrical Ballads (1800). Grouping it with the Lucy poems is an editorial tradition (Wordsworth never published the poems as a sequence). Who 'she' is remains unknown, and many critics think the question misguided.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote and notes; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'A Slumber did my Spirit Seal', notes (quoting Coleridge's letter of April 1799); Wordsworth, Lyrical Ballads, with Other Poems (1800), vol. 2; Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others)

  2. my spirit seal l. 1

    To 'seal' is to close up, as with wax, or to close the eyes (OED). The line inverts the usual order (subject 'A slumber', object 'my spirit'), so the slumber acts upon him. The slumber has been read as a lover's blissful illusion (Mary Moorman calls it a 'creative sleep of the senses') or as a culpable blindness to mortality. The heavy alliteration of s-sounds ('slumber', 'spirit', 'seal', later 'She seem'd') gives the opening its hushed, soporific movement.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'seal'; Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others)

  3. no human fears l. 2

    'Human' here means proper to mortals, as against the timeless state the lover imagined she enjoyed. The lack of fear is the error the second stanza punishes, but the word also hints that his state was not quite human: a slumber of the spirit that shut out mortal knowledge. Commentators note that the line can be read as either innocence or complacency, and the poem does not decide.

    Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote and notes

  4. She seem'd a thing that could not feel ll. 3-4

    In stanza one 'a thing that could not feel / The touch of earthly years' means a being exempt from ageing, almost divine. Stanza two makes the phrase literally true in the worst sense: she is now a thing, insensible, among rocks and stones. The word 'thing', which can be tender (as in 'sweet thing') or merely physical, carries both senses. Cleanth Brooks's discussion of irony in poetic structure drew on this kind of reversal. 'The touch of earthly years' personifies time as a hand laid on the body.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Irony as a Principle of Structure' (1949); Spencer Hall, essay on Wordsworth's Lucy poems, summarizing the Bateson-Brooks dispute and E. D. Hirsch, Validity in Interpretation (Yale, 1967) (Rhode Island College Digital Collections)

  5. the gap between the stanzas ll. 5-6

    Stanza one is in the past tense ('did', 'had', 'seem'd'); stanza two in the present ('has', 'hears', 'sees'). The death itself falls in the white space. The form is two quatrains in common (ballad or hymn) metre, alternating four- and three-stress lines rhyming abab, with almost entirely monosyllabic words. 'No motion ... no force' uses terms from Newtonian physics: she has neither motion of her own nor power to act, though, as the last lines say, she is moved.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote and notes; Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others)

  6. She neither hears nor sees l. 6

    Psalm 115:5-6 (and 135:16-17) mocks idols of silver and gold that have the organs of sense without the use of them. The echo, if intended, makes the dead girl an image or statue of herself, and may glance ironically at the lover's earlier idolizing of her as a being above time. The flat statement of loss of the senses also completes the first stanza's 'could not feel'.

    Bible, King James Version, Psalm 115:5-6; Spencer Hall, essay on Wordsworth's Lucy poems, summarizing the Bateson-Brooks dispute and E. D. Hirsch, Validity in Interpretation (Yale, 1967) (Rhode Island College Digital Collections)

  7. diurnal l. 7

    From Latin diurnus, 'daily' (OED). In a poem of monosyllables the three-syllable scientific word stands out, and it widens the view to the scale of the planet: her body is carried round with the earth each day. Critics note that the word is both majestic and impersonal; it gives the dead girl a place in the cosmos and also reduces her to an object in mechanical motion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'diurnal'; Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others)

  8. Roll'd round in earth's diurnal course ll. 7-8

    F. W. Bateson read the last two lines as a climax of 'pantheistic magnificence': Lucy, dead, is now part of the living motion of nature. Cleanth Brooks heard instead the dead lifelessness of a body tumbled about like stones. E. D. Hirsch, in Validity in Interpretation (1967), used the dispute as a test case, arguing that both readings are linguistically possible and that only a probable context (here, Wordsworth's pantheistic tendencies in 1798-99) can decide between them; Spencer Hall argued for a humanist reading that is neither consolation nor despair. Geoffrey Hartman stressed how the poem merges the human into nature. Many readers find the power of the lines in holding both responses at once.

    Spencer Hall, essay on Wordsworth's Lucy poems, summarizing the Bateson-Brooks dispute and E. D. Hirsch, Validity in Interpretation (Yale, 1967) (Rhode Island College Digital Collections); E. D. Hirsch, Jr., Validity in Interpretation (New Haven: Yale UP, 1967), appendix on the poem's interpretation; Cleanth Brooks, 'Irony as a Principle of Structure' (1949); Wikipedia, 'A slumber did my spirit seal' (summarizing Hartman, Ford, Moorman and others)

  9. rocks, and stones, and trees l. 8

    The list moves from the least living (rocks, stones) to the living but unconscious (trees). The exclamation mark in this text (later editions print a full stop) adds a cry that the bare statement otherwise refuses. Compare the companion poem 'Three years she grew', where Nature takes Lucy to herself, and the more consoling tone of that ending.

    Francis T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861)
