The Mind

Nutting

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poetical Works, ed. Knight (1896), vol. II

The Mind

--It seems a day
(I speak of one from many singled out)
One of those heavenly days that cannot die;
When, in the eagerness of boyish hope,
I left our cottage-threshold, sallying forth
With a huge wallet o'er my shoulders slung,
A nutting-crook in hand; and turned my steps
Tow'rd some far-distant wood, a Figure quaint,
Tricked out in proud disguise of cast-off weeds
Which for that service had been husbanded,
By exhortation of my frugal Dame--
Motley accoutrement, of power to smile
At thorns, and brakes, and brambles,--and, in truth,
More ragged than need was! O'er pathless rocks,
Through beds of matted fern, and tangled thickets,
Forcing my way, I came to one dear nook
Unvisited, where not a broken bough
Drooped with its withered leaves, ungracious sign
Of devastation; but the hazels rose
Tall and erect, with tempting clusters hung,
A virgin scene!--A little while I stood,
Breathing with such suppression of the heart
As joy delights in; and, with wise restraint
Voluptuous, fearless of a rival, eyed
The banquet;--or beneath the trees I sate
Among the flowers, and with the flowers I played;
A temper known to those, who, after long
And weary expectation, have been blest
With sudden happiness beyond all hope.
Perhaps it was a bower beneath whose leaves
The violets of five seasons re-appear
And fade, unseen by any human eye;
Where fairy water-breaks do murmur on
For ever; and I saw the sparkling foam,
And--with my cheek on one of those green stones
That, fleeced with moss, under the shady trees,
Lay round me, scattered like a flock of sheep--
I heard the murmur and the murmuring sound,
In that sweet mood when pleasure loves to pay
Tribute to ease; and, of its joy secure,
The heart luxuriates with indifferent things,
Wasting its kindliness on stocks and stones,
And on the vacant air. Then up I rose,
And dragged to earth both branch and bough, with crash
And merciless ravage: and the shady nook
Of hazels, and the green and mossy bower,
Deformed and sullied, patiently gave up
Their quiet being: and, unless I now
Confound my present feelings with the past;
Ere from the mutilated bower I turned
Exulting, rich beyond the wealth of kings,
I felt a sense of pain when I beheld
The silent trees, and saw the intruding sky.--
Then, dearest Maiden, move along these shades
In gentleness of heart; with gentle hand
Touch--for there is a spirit in the woods.

Codex Note

Composed 1799; published 1800.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening and origin in The Prelude ll. 1-3

    'Nutting' was written at Goslar in late 1798 (Dorothy quoted it in a letter of December that year) and published in the second edition of Lyrical Ballads (1800). Wordsworth later told Isabella Fenwick it was intended for 'a poem on my own life', the work that became The Prelude, but struck out as not wanted there. The opening dash and the short first line keep the mark of excision: we enter mid-thought. The parenthesis 'I speak of one from many singled out' presents the day as representative, like one of Wordsworth's 'spots of time', and 'heavenly days that cannot die' sets up the paradox that a remembered day of destruction lives on as moral memory.

    Representative Poetry Online, editorial notes to 'Nutting' (Lyrical Ballads, 1800); Jared Curtis, ed., The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth (1993), note on 'Nutting'; E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'

  2. wallet l. 6

    In older English a wallet is a bag for food, clothes or other goods carried on a journey, often slung over the shoulder, like a pedlar's pack or pilgrim's scrip. The modern sense of a pocket-book for money is later. The 'huge' size signals the boy's greedy hopes for his haul and adds to the comic self-portrait that follows; the grown narrator watches his younger self with affectionate irony before the tone darkens.

    OED, s.v. 'wallet', n., sense 'a bag for holding provisions, clothing, etc., esp. on a journey'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  3. nutting-crook l. 7

    The crook lets the gatherer hook high boughs and bend them within reach; it is the instrument that later becomes violent when the boy drags 'to earth both branch and bough' (l. 44). Nutting was a traditional autumn pastime in the Lake District; Wordsworth told Isabella Fenwick in 1843 that the poem grew from boyhood feelings, and the coppice woods around Hawkshead and Esthwaite are the setting usually imagined for it.

    Jared Curtis, ed., The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth (1993), note on 'Nutting'; OED, s.v. 'crook', n., sense 'a hooked staff'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  4. a Figure quaint ... proud disguise ll. 8-12

    'Sallying forth' and the capitalised 'Figure' set the boy up as a knight in a romance setting out on adventure, but his armour is cast-off clothing. 'Quaint' here means odd, curiously dressed. 'Proud disguise' is oxymoronic: the rags are worn with pride, as if they were knightly gear, and 'Motley accoutrement' joins the jester's parti-coloured motley with 'accoutrement', a soldier's equipment. The mock-heroic tone matters: it marks the distance between the narrator and the boy, and it makes the shift to 'merciless ravage' more disturbing.

    OED, s.v. 'quaint', adj.; 'motley', n. and adj.; 'accoutrement', n.; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  5. weeds l. 9

    'Weeds' in this sense is a different word from the garden weed, descending from Old English wǣd, 'garment'. By 1800 it was poetic or archaic, surviving mainly in 'widow's weeds'. The pun is latent but apt in a poem about plants: the boy goes into the wood dressed in 'weeds'. 'Tricked out' means dressed up or adorned, often showily, which adds to the irony of rags worn as finery.

    OED, s.v. 'weed', n.2, 'an article of apparel; clothing'; OED, s.v. 'trick', v., 'trick out'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  6. husbanded l. 10

    To husband is to manage economically, to conserve. The old clothes had been kept back for just this purpose, a detail that characterises the 'frugal Dame' and the cottage economy of Hawkshead. The word belongs to a vocabulary of thrift and stewardship that the poem sets against waste: the boy, so carefully provided for, goes on to squander the wood's 'quiet being', and his heart is said to be 'Wasting its kindliness' (l. 42).

    OED, s.v. 'husband', v., sense 'to manage thriftily; to save'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  7. my frugal Dame l. 11

    'Dame' is used in its older sense of the mistress of a household and, specifically, a woman who boarded schoolboys. Editors identify her as Ann Tyson, in whose cottage Wordsworth and his brothers lived while at Hawkshead Grammar School; she reappears with affection in The Prelude. Without that context the figure can be misread: Thomas Kinsella's close reading of the poem describes the person who dresses the boy as the speaker's wife, which shows how detaching the poem from its Prelude context can mislead.

    Representative Poetry Online, note to l. 11; E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'; Thomas Kinsella, 'Reading Wordsworth: Two Poems from Lyrical Ballads', PN Review 232 (2016)

  8. brakes l. 13

    A brake is a thicket, or a patch of bracken or fern; the alliterating triad 'thorns, and brakes, and brambles' imitates the boy's struggle through undergrowth. The clothing is 'of power to smile / At' these obstacles, a playful personification: his rags can laugh at the thorns because there is nothing left to spoil. The difficulty of the approach ('pathless rocks', 'matted fern', 'tangled thickets', 'Forcing my way') also sets up the sense of a hidden, protected place reached by intrusion.

    OED, s.v. 'brake', n.; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  9. tempting clusters l. 20

    'Tempting' introduces a moral vocabulary into a description of nuts. In Genesis 3:6 the tree is 'pleasant to the eyes' and 'to be desired'; in Paradise Lost the fruit tempts Eve, and the Fall leaves nature wounded. Stephen Fallon argues that 'Nutting' is woven from Paradise Lost: the boy plays Satan intruding on the bower, then Adam and Eve in their fall, and finally gains a chastened knowledge that he passes to the Maiden. The 'intruding sky' (l. 53) and the pain that follows triumph fit the Miltonic pattern of exultation followed by remorse.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:6; Stephen M. Fallon, 'Wordsworth after Milton: Paradise Lost and Regained in "Nutting"', Partial Answers 13.2 (2015), 193-213

  10. A virgin scene! l. 21

    The nook is 'Unvisited', the hazels 'Tall and erect, with tempting clusters hung', and the boy eyes the 'banquet' with 'wise restraint / Voluptuous'. Many readers take these terms, and the later 'Deformed and sullied' bower, as a figurative sexual violation, so that the poem becomes an allegory of masculine desire possessing a feminised nature. Others resist a simply sexual reading, and Kinsella's close reading stays with the boy's breathless response to the 'virgin' scene without framing it as erotic. Fallon reads the same language through Paradise Lost, with the boy as Satan entering Eden. The sexual reading is now common but not uncontested.

    Stephen M. Fallon, Partial Answers 13.2 (2015); Robert Burns Neveldine, 'Wordsworth's "Nutting" and the Violent End of Reading' (Project MUSE); Thomas Kinsella, 'Reading Wordsworth: Two Poems from Lyrical Ballads', PN Review 232 (Nov-Dec 2016)

  11. wise restraint / Voluptuous ll. 23-25

    The enjambment holds 'wise restraint' apart from 'Voluptuous', so that restraint itself becomes a sensual luxury. The boy is 'fearless of a rival': no one else has found the nook, so he may defer gratification. 'Banquet' frames the hazels as a feast laid out for him. Readers attentive to Milton hear Eden behind the passage, including Eve's 'sweet reluctant amorous delay' in Paradise Lost IV.311; for Fallon the Miltonic texture is enabling rather than a sign of anxious influence in Harold Bloom's sense. 'Sate' (l. 25) is an old past tense of 'sit'.

    Stephen M. Fallon, Partial Answers 13.2 (2015); John Milton, Paradise Lost IV.311, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; OED, s.v. 'voluptuous', adj.

  12. The violets of five seasons ll. 30-32

    'Perhaps' marks a shift into conjecture: the boy, or the remembering narrator, imagines that this bower has lain undiscovered for years. The violets that 're-appear / And fade, unseen by any human eye' recall the eighteenth-century commonplace of flowers born to blush unseen, made famous by Gray's Elegy, and they also suggest the hidden, short-lived flower of the Lucy poems written in the same Goslar winter. Five years is also the interval in 'Tintern Abbey' ('Five years have passed'), written months earlier, though editors do not claim a direct link.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'; E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'; Thomas Gray, Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), st. 14

  13. water-breaks l. 33

    A water-break is a place where running water is broken into ripples or small cascades by stones. 'Fairy' suggests both their smallness and a touch of enchantment, keeping the bower in the realm of romance established by 'sallying forth'. The murmur that goes on 'For ever' gives the place a timeless life independent of human witness, the very life that the boy will shortly interrupt.

    OED, s.v. 'water-break', n.; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  14. fleeced with moss ... like a flock of sheep ll. 36-37

    The metaphor 'fleeced with moss' prepares the simile 'scattered like a flock of sheep', a recognisably Lake District image of grey stones on a hillside. It animates the stones, making them a peaceful flock among which the boy lies with his cheek against one, in intimate contact with the place. The pastoral calm of the image heightens the shock of the 'merciless ravage' to come, and it also resists the idea that the wood is mere dead matter: its stones, too, are given a quasi-living being.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'; Jonathan Bate, Romantic Ecology: Wordsworth and the Environmental Tradition (1991)

  15. the murmur and the murmuring sound l. 38

    The line repeats 'murmur' from l. 33 and then doubles it within itself, an apparent tautology that imitates the lulling, continuous sound of water and the half-sleeping state in which it is heard. The blank verse slows almost to stasis; the long sentence from l. 30 to l. 43 suspends action in a mood of 'sweet' ease before the abrupt 'Then up I rose'. Such repetition, which Wordsworth's critics have often found odd or naive, is characteristic of Wordsworth's attempt to render states of mind through sound.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'; E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'

  16. luxuriates with indifferent things ll. 39-43

    'Pleasure loves to pay / Tribute to ease': delight, sure of itself, relaxes into idleness. 'Luxuriates' means both to grow profusely and to indulge oneself, and 'indifferent' means neutral, things neither good nor bad in themselves. The heart is 'Wasting its kindliness', spending feeling without discrimination. The psychology is precise and unsettling: the same untroubled security that lets the boy love everything also lets him destroy without thought. 'Then up I rose' follows with no stated motive, which many critics find the poem's most disturbing feature.

    OED, s.v. 'luxuriate', v.; 'indifferent', adj.; Robert Burns Neveldine, 'Wordsworth's "Nutting" and the Violent End of Reading' (Project MUSE); R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  17. stocks and stones l. 42

    'Stocks and stones' is a proverbial pair for senseless objects, especially idols: in Jeremiah 2:27 the faithless say to a stock, 'Thou art my father', and to a stone, 'Thou hast brought me forth'; Milton's sonnet 'On the Late Massacre in Piedmont' speaks of fathers who 'worship't Stocks and Stones'. Wordsworth's usage is ironic. The boy squanders his kindliness on what he takes for mere things, yet the poem's last line insists that 'there is a spirit in the woods'. The phrase raises the question whether nature is dead matter or alive, which the poem answers against the boy.

    Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 2:27; John Milton, 'On the Late Massacre in Piedmont', l. 4, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room

  18. crash / And merciless ravage ll. 43-48

    The verse turns abrupt: 'Then up I rose, / And dragged to earth both branch and bough, with crash / And merciless ravage'. The bower is 'Deformed and sullied', words with moral and sexual overtones, and it 'patiently gave up / Their quiet being', personifying the trees as passive victims. Readers often compare Guyon's destruction of the Bower of Bliss in Spenser's Faerie Queene II.xii, though there the bower is evil and here it is innocent. Ecocritics such as Jonathan Bate read the episode as an early lesson in respecting non-human life; Lisa Ottum questions how far the poem works as a straightforwardly 'green' text, and Neveldine connects it to a Romantic tradition of trespass and violence.

    Jonathan Bate, Romantic Ecology (1991); Lisa Ottum, 'On (Not) Hugging Trees: Affect, Emotion, and Ecology in Wordsworth's "Nutting"', Interdisciplinary Literary Studies 18.2 (2016), 257-281; Robert Burns Neveldine, 'Wordsworth's "Nutting" and the Violent End of Reading' (Project MUSE); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene II.xii

  19. unless I now / Confound my present feelings with the past ll. 48-49

    This qualification is one of the poem's most important moments. The boy left 'Exulting, rich beyond the wealth of kings', yet the narrator believes he also felt 'a sense of pain'. By conceding that he may be confusing present feeling with past, Wordsworth exposes the double consciousness of The Prelude, where the adult self reinterprets the child's experience. Readers divide on whether the pain was really felt by the boy or is the retrospective moral the man needs; the poem deliberately leaves the question open.

    E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'; Stephen M. Fallon, Partial Answers 13.2 (2015)

  20. the intruding sky l. 53

    The phrase transfers the boy's guilt onto the sky. Literally, breaking the canopy lets light into the shady nook; figuratively, the private sanctuary has been opened and exposed, and the boy now sees what he has done. The 'silent trees' are a reproach. The moment works as a small fall from innocence, with a changed world visible after the transgression, which is how Fallon reads it in relation to Paradise Lost. It is also the point at which the poem's sympathy decisively shifts from the boy to the wood.

    Stephen M. Fallon, Partial Answers 13.2 (2015); R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'

  21. dearest Maiden l. 54

    The sudden address turns memory into instruction. An early manuscript, probably of 1799, opened with a passage addressed to a 'beloved Friend' called Lucy, reproaching her as a ravager of the autumn woods, as the poet recalls himself to have been in boyhood; this was dropped on publication, and editors take the 'Maiden' to be that Lucy. Because 'Nutting' was written in the same Goslar winter as the Lucy poems, and because Dorothy was Wordsworth's companion there and the listener in 'Tintern Abbey', many readers identify the Maiden with Dorothy. The manuscript supports the Lucy figure; the Dorothy link is biographical inference.

    Representative Poetry Online, notes to ll. 1 and 54; James Butler and Karen Green, eds., Lyrical Ballads, and Other Poems, 1797-1800 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1992), early manuscript versions of 'Nutting'; E. de Selincourt, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. II (Oxford, 1944), notes to 'Nutting'

  22. there is a spirit in the woods l. 56

    'Touch--for there is a spirit in the woods' breaks the line at the very act of touching, a pause that enacts restraint. The claim is close to the pantheistic language of 'Tintern Abbey' ('a motion and a spirit, that impels / All thinking things'), written a few months earlier. It answers the boy's treatment of the wood as 'stocks and stones' and turns a story of violation into an ethic of 'gentleness'. Ecocritics such as Bate have read the line as a founding moment of Romantic environmental feeling, though Ottum and others question that use of the poem; others note that the lesson is handed to a young woman, so that the male poet's remorse becomes a rule for female conduct.

    Jonathan Bate, Romantic Ecology (1991); Lisa Ottum, Interdisciplinary Literary Studies (2016); R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads (1963; 2nd edn 1991), notes to 'Nutting'
