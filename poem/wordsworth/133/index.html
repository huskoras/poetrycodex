The Mind

Ode: Intimations of Immortality

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

The MindTime & BrevityNature

There was a time when meadow, grove, and stream,
The earth, and every common sight,
To me did seem
Apparell'd in celestial light,
The glory and the freshness of a dream.
It is not now as it has been of yore;--
Turn wheresoe'er I may,
By night or day,
The things which I have seen I now can see no more.

The Rainbow comes and goes,
And lovely is the Rose,
The Moon doth with delight
Look round her when the heavens are bare;
Waters on a starry night
Are beautiful and fair;
The sunshine is a glorious birth;
But yet I know, where'er I go,
That there hath pass'd away a glory from the earth.

Now, while the Birds thus sing a joyous song,
And while the young Lambs bound
As to the tabor's sound,
To me alone there came a thought of grief:
A timely utterance gave that thought relief,
And I again am strong.
The Cataracts blow their trumpets from the steep,
No more shall grief of mine the season wrong;
I hear the Echoes through the mountains throng,
The Winds come to me from the fields of sleep,
And all the earth is gay,
Land and sea
Give themselves up to jollity,
And with the heart of May
Doth every Beast keep holiday,
Thou Child of Joy
Shout round me, let me hear thy shouts, thou happy Shepherd Boy!

Ye blessed Creatures, I have heard the call
Ye to each other make; I see
The heavens laugh with you in your jubilee;
My heart is at your festival,
My head hath its coronal,
The fullness of your bliss, I feel--I feel it all.
Oh evil day! if I were sullen
While the Earth herself is adorning,
This sweet May-morning,
And the Children are pulling,
On every side,
In a thousand vallies far and wide,
Fresh flowers; while the sun shines warm,
And the Babe leaps up on his mother's arm:--
I hear, I hear, with joy I hear!
--But there's a Tree, of many one,
A single Field which I have look'd upon,
Both of them speak of something that is gone:
The Pansy at my feet
Doth the same tale repeat:
Whither is fled the visionary gleam?
Where is it now, the glory and the dream?

Our birth is but a sleep and a forgetting:
The Soul that rises with us, our life's Star,
Hath had elsewhere its setting,
And cometh from afar:
Not in entire forgetfulness,
And not in utter nakedness,
But trailing clouds of glory do we come
From God, who is our home;
Heaven lies about us in our infancy!
Shades of the prison-house begin to close
Upon the growing Boy,
But He beholds the light, and whence it flows,
He sees it in his joy;
The Youth, who daily farther from the East
Must travel, still is Nature's Priest,
And by the vision splendid
Is on his way attended;
At length the Man perceives it die away,
And fade into the light of common day.

Earth fills her lap with pleasures of her own;
Yearnings she hath in her own natural kind,
And, even with something of a Mother's mind,
And no unworthy aim,
The homely Nurse doth all she can
To make her Foster-child, her Inmate Man,
Forget the glories he hath known,
And that imperial palace whence he came.

Behold the Child among his new-born blisses,
A four year's Darling of a pigmy size!
See, where mid work of his own hand he lies,
Fretted by sallies of his Mother's kisses,
With light upon him from his Father's eyes!
See, at his feet, some little plan or chart,
Some fragment from his dream of human life,
Shap'd by himself with newly-learned art;
A wedding or a festival,
A mourning or a funeral;
And this hath now his heart,
And unto this he frames his song:
Then will he fit his tongue
To dialogues of business, love, or strife;
But it will not be long
Ere this be thrown aside,
And with new joy and pride
The little Actor cons another part,
Filling from time to time his "humourous stage"
With all the Persons, down to palsied Age,
That Life brings with her in her Equipage;
As if his whole vocation
Were endless imitation.

Thou, whose exterior semblance doth belie
Thy Soul's immensity;
Thou best Philosopher, who yet dost keep
Thy heritage, thou Eye among the blind,
That, deaf and silent, read'st the eternal deep,
Haunted for ever by the eternal mind,--
Mighty Prophet! Seer blest!
On whom those truths do rest,
Which we are toiling all our lives to find;
Thou, over whom thy Immortality
Broods like the Day, a Master o'er a Slave,
A Presence which is not to be put by;
To whom the grave
Is but a lonely bed without the sense or sight
Of day or the warm light,
A place of thought where we in waiting lie;
Thou little Child, yet glorious in the might
Of untam'd pleasures, on thy Being's height,
Why with such earnest pains dost thou provoke
The Years to bring the inevitable yoke,
Thus blindly with thy blessedness at strife?
Full soon thy Soul shall have her earthly freight,
And custom lie upon thee with a weight,
Heavy as frost, and deep almost as life!

O joy! that in our embers
Is something that doth live,
That nature yet remembers
What was so fugitive!
The thought of our past years in me doth breed
Perpetual benedictions: not indeed
For that which is most worthy to be blest;
Delight and liberty, the simple creed
Of Childhood, whether fluttering or at rest,
With new-born hope for ever in his breast:--
Not for these I raise
The song of thanks and praise;
But for those obstinate questionings
Of sense and outward things,
Fallings from us, vanishings;
Blank misgivings of a Creature
Moving about in worlds not realiz'd,
High instincts, before which our mortal Nature
Did tremble like a guilty Thing surpriz'd:
But for those first affections,
Those shadowy recollections,
Which, be they what they may,
Are yet the fountain light of all our day,
Are yet a master light of all our seeing;
Uphold us, cherish us, and make
Our noisy years seem moments in the being
Of the eternal Silence: truths that wake,
To perish never;
Which neither listlessness, nor mad endeavour,
Nor Man nor Boy,
Nor all that is at enmity with joy,
Can utterly abolish or destroy!
Hence, in a season of calm weather,
Though inland far we be,
Our Souls have sight of that immortal sea
Which brought us hither,
Can in a moment travel thither,
And see the Children sport upon the shore,
And hear the mighty waters rolling evermore.

Then, sing ye Birds, sing, sing a joyous song!
And let the young Lambs bound
As to the tabor's sound!
We in thought will join your throng,
Ye that pipe and ye that play,
Ye that through your hearts to day
Feel the gladness of the May!
What though the radiance which was once so bright
Be now for ever taken from my sight,
Though nothing can bring back the hour
Of splendour in the grass, of glory in the flower;
We will grieve not, rather find
Strength in what remains behind,
In the primal sympathy
Which having been must ever be,
In the soothing thoughts that spring
Out of human suffering,
In the faith that looks through death,
In years that bring the philosophic mind.

And oh ye Fountains, Meadows, Hills, and Groves,
Think not of any severing of our loves!
Yet in my heart of hearts I feel your might;
I only have relinquish'd one delight
To live beneath your more habitual sway.
I love the Brooks which down their channels fret,
Even more than when I tripp'd lightly as they;
The innocent brightness of a new-born Day
Is lovely yet;
The Clouds that gather round the setting sun
Do take a sober colouring from an eye
That hath kept watch o'er man's mortality;
Another race hath been, and other palms are won.
Thanks to the human heart by which we live,
Thanks to its tenderness, its joys, and fears,
To me the meanest flower that blows can give
Thoughts that do often lie too deep for tears.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, epigraphs and composition st. 1

    Dorothy Wordsworth's journal for 27 March 1802 records that William wrote part of an ode; he reached the question that closes stanza 4 and then stopped, finishing stanzas 5-11 in early 1804. In Poems, in Two Volumes (1807) it closed the second volume under the title 'Ode' with the epigraph 'Paulo majora canamus' ('let us sing of somewhat greater things', Virgil, Eclogue 4.1), marking a rise in style. From 1815 it was called 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood', with an epigraph from 'My heart leaps up': 'The Child is Father of the Man'. The archive's text is the 1807 version (note the four lines on the grave in stanza 8, later cut).

    Dorothy Wordsworth, The Grasmere Journals, ed. Pamela Woof (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1991); 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history); Virgil, Eclogue 4.1 ('Sicelides Musae, paulo maiora canamus'), Latin text, The Latin Library; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  2. Form: the irregular (Pindaric) ode ll. 1-9

    The English irregular ode, developed by Abraham Cowley from a misunderstanding of Pindar's metrics, allowed long and short lines to vary with the feeling. Wordsworth's line lengths swing from the dimeter 'To me did seem' to alexandrines such as 'The things which I have seen I now can see no more'. The eleven stanzas fall into three movements (1-4, 5-8, 9-11): loss, explanation, and consolation. The variety suits a poem that moves between lament, myth and argument.

    'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem; Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  3. Apparell'd in celestial light l. 4

    'Apparelled' (clothed, dressed) makes light into a garment laid over things, so that the radiance appears to be added to them rather than seen in them. The poem's question is where that radiance came from: from the things, from the child's eye, or from a heavenly origin. Coleridge's 'Dejection', written in reply to these opening stanzas in April 1802, answers that the 'glory' comes from the soul itself.

    S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (Oxford, 1912); Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'apparel'

  4. The glory and the freshness of a dream l. 5

    The comparison to a dream is double-edged: it conveys vividness and also unreality. Cleanth Brooks's reading of the ode in The Well Wrought Urn (1947) stressed its paradoxes, and 'glory' and light do shift sense across the poem. The word recurs as 'a glory from the earth' (st. 2), 'the glory and the dream' (st. 4), 'clouds of glory' (st. 5) and 'glory in the flower' (st. 10).

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  5. The Rainbow comes and goes l. 10

    In 'My heart leaps up' the sight of the rainbow still moves the adult as it moved the child, and he hopes his days may be 'bound each to each by natural piety'. The ode, begun the next day, admits that the rainbow is still lovely but that something has gone from the seeing. Behind both lies the rainbow as God's covenant sign in Genesis 9:13. The 1815 epigraph made the connection explicit.

    William Wordsworth, 'My heart leaps up' (written 26 March 1802), Poems, in Two Volumes (1807); The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 9:13; 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history)

  6. A timely utterance gave that thought relief l. 23

    Wordsworth never identified it. Many editors suggest 'My heart leaps up' (26 March 1802), written the day before the ode was begun; others have proposed 'Resolution and Independence' (May 1802) or some other poem; still others read the phrase generally, as any expression that relieved the grief, perhaps this poem itself. The uncertainty matters because the relief is short-lived: by the end of stanza 4 the grief returns.

    'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem; Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  7. the fields of sleep l. 28

    Readers have proposed several meanings. Literally, the fields where people or flocks are still asleep in the early morning; or the quiet, distant uplands from which the wind comes; or, looking ahead to stanza 5 ('Our birth is but a sleep'), a mysterious region associated with the pre-existent life. The phrase is suggestive rather than precise, and the ambiguity is part of its effect.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem; Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  8. a Tree, of many one, / A single Field ll. 51-52

    'Of many one' means one among many. The specificity is striking: not nature in general but a single tree and field 'speak of something that is gone'. Trilling stressed that the ode describes a change in perception that every adult experiences, not simply a failure of poetic power. The pansy continues the theme of particular objects bearing loss. Brooks emphasised the paradoxes of the ode's language; this passage turns the familiar Romantic delight in particular objects into a source of grief.

    Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950); Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  9. The Pansy l. 54

    Wordsworth's choice of flower brings with it its traditional meaning of thought or remembrance (Hamlet 4.5). The small flower 'doth the same tale repeat' of loss. It may be compared with the 'meanest flower that blows' of the last lines, where a flower again gives rise to thoughts, but now consoling ones. The flower is small and close at hand, 'at my feet', in contrast with the grand features of the May landscape, and it is the humble object rather than the grand one that speaks. This contrast between small and great objects runs through the poem.

    William Shakespeare, Hamlet, Folger Shakespeare Library text; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pansy'

  10. Whither is fled the visionary gleam? ll. 56-57

    The two questions ('Whither ...? / Where is it now ...?') end the first movement. Wordsworth's later note told Isabella Fenwick that at least two years passed before he wrote the rest. Coleridge's 'Dejection' was in part an answer to the question. The rest of the ode offers two answers: a myth of pre-existence (stanzas 5-8) and a philosophical consolation (stanzas 9-11).

    The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (London: Bristol Classical Press, 1993; rev. Humanities-Ebooks, 2007), note on the Ode; S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (Oxford, 1912); 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history)

  11. Our birth is but a sleep and a forgetting l. 58

    In Plato's Phaedo and Meno, learning is recollection (anamnesis) of what the soul knew before birth; in the Republic's myth of Er souls drink from the river of forgetfulness before rebirth. Henry Vaughan's 'The Retreate' (1650) also looks back to an 'angel-infancy'. In the Fenwick note (1843) Wordsworth said he took up the notion of pre-existence as having enough foundation in human feeling to be used poetically, not as a doctrine he asserted. The inversion, birth as sleep, makes ordinary life a dream from which death might wake us.

    Plato, Phaedo 72e-77a and Meno 81a-86b (the doctrine of recollection), trans. Benjamin Jowett, Project Gutenberg; Henry Vaughan, 'The Retreate', Silex Scintillans (1650); The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (London: Bristol Classical Press, 1993; rev. Humanities-Ebooks, 2007), note on the Ode; Victorian Web, study questions on the Ode, quoting Wordsworth's own note on the poem ('abyss of idealism')

  12. trailing clouds of glory l. 64

    'Glory' in the biblical sense is the visible radiance of God's presence, often shown as cloud (e.g. Exodus 16:10, 'the glory of the LORD appeared in the cloud'). The children come 'not in utter nakedness': they carry something of heaven with them. The phrase has become one of the most quoted in English. The image of clouds trailing behind the newborn suggests both the light it brings and its gradual dispersal. It also connects with the closing stanza's clouds that 'gather round the setting sun' and take 'a sober colouring', so that the poem's arc runs from dawn clouds to sunset clouds.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Exodus 16:10; Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  13. Shades of the prison-house l. 67

    The image of the body or this world as a prison is Platonic (Phaedo 62b, 82e) and was a commonplace of Christian devotion. 'Shades' means shadows, as the prison walls darken the light; there may also be a sense of 'ghosts'. The growing child still 'beholds the light'; the youth is 'Nature's Priest'; but the man sees it fade 'into the light of common day'. Critics note the inverted value: adulthood, ordinarily seen as growth, is pictured as imprisonment.

    Plato, Phaedo 72e-77a and Meno 81a-86b (the doctrine of recollection), trans. Benjamin Jowett, Project Gutenberg; Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  14. The Youth ... farther from the East ... Nature's Priest ll. 71-72

    The East is where the sun rises, and in Christian tradition the direction of Eden and of Christ's coming. The youth's journey west is the sun's course through the day toward evening. 'Nature's Priest' gives him a mediating, quasi-sacred role: he still ministers to the vision. Vaughan's 'The Retreate' similarly imagines the soul moving away from its first light. The structure of the stanza thus maps the ages of life onto the day: dawn, morning and the plain daylight of adulthood.

    Henry Vaughan, 'The Retreate', Silex Scintillans (1650); The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:8; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem

  15. The homely Nurse ... her Foster-child, her Inmate Man ll. 81-82

    'Homely' means simple, domestic; 'inmate' an inhabitant, a lodger in someone else's house (not yet the modern sense of a prisoner, though it may be felt). The figure is of a royal child put out to nurse with a humble woman, a romance motif. 'That imperial palace whence he came' completes it. The stanza is notably gentle towards earth: her aim is 'no unworthy' one.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'inmate'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'homely'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem

  16. A four year's Darling of a pigmy size l. 86

    Coleridge, quoting the ode in Biographia Literaria (1817), reads 'a six years' Darling of a pigmy size', showing the later reading; the archive preserves the earlier 'four year's'. Commentators have often connected the child with Hartley Coleridge (born 1796), to whom Wordsworth addressed 'To H. C., Six Years Old', though the poem does not name him. 'Fretted' here means touched and teased, not worried.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 22, on the 'characteristic defects' of Wordsworth's poetry (Project Gutenberg text); 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality', Wikipedia (composition, titles, epigraphs, critical history); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem

  17. his "humourous stage" l. 103

    The quotation marks signal a borrowing: Daniel's dedication opens 'I do not here upon this hum'rous stage'. 'Humorous' refers to the 'humours', the temperaments and moods of characters, as in comedies of humours. The child as 'little Actor' rehearsing roles recalls Jaques's 'All the world's a stage' in As You Like It (2.7), where life's ages run down to 'second childishness'; Wordsworth's 'palsied Age' sketches a similar sequence. 'Equipage' is a retinue or train of followers.

    Samuel Daniel, dedicatory sonnet to Fulke Greville prefixed to Musophilus (1599), Renascence Editions text; William Shakespeare, As You Like It 2.7, Folger Shakespeare Library; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'equipage'

  18. Thou best Philosopher ... Mighty Prophet! Seer blest! st. 8

    In chapter 22 of Biographia Literaria Coleridge cited this stanza as an example of 'thoughts and images too great for the subject'. He asked in what sense a child could be a philosopher or read 'the eternal deep', since the child is unconscious of these truths; such terms, he argued, would apply equally to a bee, a dog or a field of corn. Defenders reply that the child is 'Eye among the blind' because his way of being, not his thinking, embodies truths adults seek. 'Deaf and silent' is one of the obscurities Coleridge noted, since it is unclear whether it modifies 'Eye' or 'Philosopher'.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 22, on the 'characteristic defects' of Wordsworth's poetry (Project Gutenberg text); Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  19. To whom the grave / Is but a lonely bed ll. 121-124

    The child is said to imagine the grave as a bed where we lie awake, thinking, waiting. Coleridge objected that this made the passage only a comment on 'We are Seven', and protested at the frightful notion of lying awake in the grave, adding that Christian children learn to call death sleep. The lines did not appear in later collected editions; the archive's 1807 text preserves them.

    S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 22, on the 'characteristic defects' of Wordsworth's poetry (Project Gutenberg text); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds, The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, 5 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1940-49), notes on the poem

  20. obstinate questionings ... Fallings from us, vanishings ll. 144-146

    In the Fenwick note Wordsworth said that as a child he was often unable to think of external things as having external existence, and on the way to school would grasp at a wall or tree to recall himself 'from this abyss of idealism to the reality'. The 'blank misgivings of a Creature / Moving about in worlds not realiz'd' are these experiences, when the physical world seemed to fall away. The thanksgiving is for this sense of the mind's independence of sense, which he took as an intimation of immortality.

    The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (London: Bristol Classical Press, 1993; rev. Humanities-Ebooks, 2007), note on the Ode; Victorian Web, study questions on the Ode, quoting Wordsworth's own note on the poem ('abyss of idealism'); Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  21. like a guilty Thing surpriz'd l. 150

    Horatio's lines describe the ghost fleeing the dawn. Wordsworth's 'mortal Nature' trembles before 'High instincts' as the ghost before day: the comparison reverses expectation by making our natural self the guilty spirit before a higher reality. The Shakespearean echo brings with it the supernatural atmosphere of the opening scene of Hamlet, where a ghost from another world must fade before the ordinary light of day. Wordsworth inverts the moral weight, since here it is the earthly self, not the visitant from another world, that is guilty and surprised.

    William Shakespeare, Hamlet, Folger Shakespeare Library text; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem

  22. that immortal sea / Which brought us hither ll. 166-167

    The seascape completes the myth: our origin is an ocean and we live 'inland far'. In moments of calm the soul can 'travel thither' and see 'the Children sport upon the shore', an image of the innocent beginning. Readers have noted that the children are seen from a distance, not rejoined. The sea image joins the poem's other waters: the cataracts of stanza 3, the 'fountain light' of stanza 9, and the 'Brooks which down their channels fret' in the last stanza.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950)

  23. years that bring the philosophic mind l. 189

    'Primal sympathy' is the bond of feeling with nature and with others that existed from the beginning and 'must ever be'. The list of consolations ends in a 'philosophic mind', a reflective calm that, Trilling argued, is a real gain and not just a substitute. Critics differ on whether the consolation is convincing or a resigned acceptance of loss. The phrase recalls the classical ideal of philosophy as consolation (Boethius) and the Stoic acceptance of loss, but Wordsworth's version is grounded in feeling ('soothing thoughts that spring / Out of human suffering') rather than reason alone.

    Lionel Trilling, 'The Immortality Ode' (English Institute, 1941), repr. in The Liberal Imagination (1950); Cleanth Brooks, 'Wordsworth and the Paradox of the Imagination', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  24. Another race hath been, and other palms are won l. 202

    Paul compares the Christian life to a race in which one wins the prize (1 Corinthians 9:24), and in Revelation 7:9 the redeemed hold palms. The palm branch was the victor's token in ancient games. 'Another race' may also mean another generation. The line follows the 'sober colouring' given to the sunset by 'an eye / That hath kept watch o'er man's mortality', which revises the 'celestial light' of the opening.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 9:24; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 7:9; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem

  25. the meanest flower that blows l. 205

    In the older sense 'mean' is lowly, common; 'blow' (Old English blowan) means to bloom, a verb distinct from 'blow' of wind. The last lines give the consolation its final form: the mature heart, through its tenderness, joys and fears, finds in the humblest flower thoughts that are beyond weeping. Compare the pansy of stanza 4, which spoke only of loss.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mean'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blow'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth, Oxford Authors / 21st-Century Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1984; 2010), notes on the poem
