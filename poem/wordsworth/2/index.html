Nature

The World Is Too Much with Us

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

NatureSocial Commentaries

The world is too much with us; late and soon,
Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers;—
Little we see in Nature that is ours;
We have given our hearts away, a sordid boon!
This Sea that bares her bosom to the moon;
The winds that will be howling at all hours,
And are up-gathered now like sleeping flowers;
For this, for everything, we are out of tune;
It moves us not.—Great God! I’d rather be
A Pagan suckled in a creed outworn;
So might I, standing on this pleasant lea,
Have glimpses that would make me less forlorn;
Have sight of Proteus rising from the sea;
Or hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A sonnet against getting and spending. Wordsworth would rather be a pagan who sees Proteus rise from the sea than a modern soul deaf to nature — a furious defence of wonder over commerce.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening and context l. 1

    The sonnet belongs to the outburst of sonnets Wordsworth wrote from 1802, when he took up Milton's Petrarchan model. It appeared among the 'Miscellaneous Sonnets' in 1807. 'The world' has the moral sense of the New Testament: 'Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world' (1 John 2:15), meaning the realm of material interests set against spiritual life. 'Too much with us' means it occupies us excessively. 'Late and soon' (late and early) means at all times, an older idiom.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Bible (KJV), 1 John 2:15; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'The World is too much with us', editorial note to l. 11 (from J. R. MacGillivray's edition)

  2. Getting and spending l. 2

    The participles describe a cycle of acquisition and consumption that critics often relate to Britain's expanding commercial and industrial economy around 1800. 'Lay waste our powers' uses a military idiom (to lay waste a country) for self-destruction; 'powers' means the faculties of feeling and imagination. The thought parallels the Preface to Lyrical Ballads, which complains that city life and the craving for stimulation blunt 'the discriminating powers of the mind'.

    Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1802); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  3. Little we see in Nature that is ours l. 3

    The line inverts the economic language of possession: in the age of getting, we own much and yet cannot claim nature, which requires a different kind of relationship. The capital N personifies Nature, as in 'Tintern Abbey'. The line anticipates the speaker's turn to paganism, a religion in which nature was felt to be full of gods, so that every sea and meadow had its presiding deity.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  4. a sordid boon l. 4

    A 'boon' is a gift or favour, often one granted on request; 'sordid' (Latin sordidus, dirty) means mean, mercenary or degraded. Editors debate the syntax: the phrase can describe the act of giving our hearts away (a squalid bargain) or what we received in exchange (worldly goods, a contemptible reward). Either way, the oxymoron sums up the bad bargain of a commercial society. The semicolon after it closes the octave's first half, the diagnosis.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'boon, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sordid, adj.'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  5. This Sea that bares her bosom to the moon l. 5

    The image combines erotic personification with natural fact, the moon's pull on the tides. The winds that 'will be howling at all hours' are now 'up-gathered... like sleeping flowers', folded in calm, a simile that domesticates wildness. These are examples of the natural grandeur we fail to feel. The capital in 'Sea' marks a further personification, preparing for the sea-gods of the sestet.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  6. out of tune l. 8

    The metaphor of tuning suggests an instrument that no longer responds to the world, compare the Aeolian harp, a favourite Romantic image for a mind responsive to natural influences (Coleridge, 'The Eolian Harp'). 'For this, for everything' broadens the charge from these sights to all of nature. The line ends the octave, in which the speaker speaks for 'us'; in the sestet he speaks as 'I'.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; S. T. Coleridge, 'The Eolian Harp' (composed 1795, printed 1796)

  7. Petrarchan sonnet with displaced volta l. 9

    The octave runs on into line 9 with a short, flat sentence that sums up the complaint; the volta then arrives in mid-line with the exclamation 'Great God!' This displaced turn imitates Milton's sonnets, which often break the octave-sestet division, and gives a sense of rising passion. The rhymes in the octave (soon/boon/moon/... powers/hours/flowers) are tight; the sestet alternates (be/lea/sea; outworn/forlorn/horn).

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (1993), note to the poem

  8. A Pagan suckled in a creed outworn l. 10

    A 'Pagan' (Latin paganus, a country-dweller, later a non-Christian) is one outside the Christian faith. 'Suckled' means nursed from infancy, so the belief would be bred in the bone. 'Creed outworn' admits the pagan faith is dead. The outburst is shocking from a Christian poet, though the conditional 'I'd rather be' marks it as a rhetorical preference rather than a statement of belief. Comparable nostalgia for Greek religion appears in Schiller's 'Die Götter Griechenlandes' (1788) and later in Keats.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pagan, n. and adj.'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Friedrich Schiller, 'Die Götter Griechenlandes' (1788)

  9. pleasant lea l. 11

    'Lea' (Old English leah, a clearing or meadow) is a poetic word for open grassland, here near the sea. Wordsworth's editors note the echo of Spenser's Colin Clouts Come Home Againe (1595), l. 283, 'Yet seemed to be a goodly pleasant lea'. Spenser's poem also supplies the image of Triton in the final line, so the sestet has a Spenserian frame: it imagines the pagan world through the lens of Elizabethan mythological pastoral.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'The World is too much with us', editorial note to l. 11 (from J. R. MacGillivray's edition); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lea, n.1'; Edmund Spenser, Colin Clouts Come Home Againe (1595), l. 283

  10. Proteus l. 13

    In Odyssey Book 4 Menelaus seizes Proteus on the shore of Pharos and holds him through all his transformations until he prophesies; Virgil retells the story with Aristaeus in Georgics 4. As a god of the sea's changing forms and of prophecy, Proteus embodies the living, divine nature the modern world cannot see. 'Have sight of' answers 'Little we see' in line 3. Spenser's Colin Clout passage also pairs Proteus with Triton among the sea-gods.

    Homer, Odyssey 4.351-570; Virgil, Georgics 4.387-529; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'The World is too much with us', editorial note to l. 11 (from J. R. MacGillivray's edition)

  11. Triton and his wreathèd horn l. 14

    In Ovid's Metamorphoses (1.330-42) Neptune orders Triton to blow his shell-trumpet to call back the flood waters after the deluge. Spenser's Colin Clout (l. 245) has 'Triton blowing loud his wreathed horne', which Wordsworth's line closely follows. 'Wreathèd' means spirally twisted, like a conch; the accent tells the reader to pronounce it as two syllables, keeping the pentameter. The poem ends on a sound, so the speaker's wish moves from sight to hearing, a full sensory renewal.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.330-42; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'The World is too much with us', editorial note to l. 11 (from J. R. MacGillivray's edition); Edmund Spenser, Colin Clouts Come Home Againe (1595), l. 245
