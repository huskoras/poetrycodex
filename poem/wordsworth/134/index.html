Death & Dying

The Lost Love

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Death & Dying

She dwelt among the untrodden ways
Beside the springs of Dove;
A maid whom there were none to praise,
And very few to love.

A violet by a mossy stone
Half hidden from the eye!
--Fair as a star, when only one
Is shining in the sky.

She lived unknown, and few could know
When Lucy ceased to be;
But she is in her grave, and O!
The difference to me!

Study notes by the editors

  1. date, title and the 'Lucy poems' title; st. 1

    Wordsworth sent this poem and 'Strange fits of passion' to Coleridge in a letter of December 1798, calling them little rhyme poems. With 'Strange fits', 'A slumber did my spirit seal', 'Three years she grew' and the later 'I travelled among unknown men', it forms the group known as the 'Lucy poems', though Wordsworth never grouped them himself; the grouping comes from nineteenth-century editors and critics. The archive text follows Palgrave, who added the title 'The Lost Love'. The stanza is the ballad or common measure (alternating four- and three-beat lines, rhyming abab), and the diction is almost entirely monosyllabic.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones); Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995)

  2. the untrodden ways l. 1

    'Untrodden' means not trampled by feet, hence remote and solitary. For the metre the line must be read with elision, 'th'untrodden', to give eight syllables. Critics have read the phrase both literally, of an isolated rural life, and figuratively, of a mind and life unknown to others; the second stanza's hidden violet develops the same idea.

    'She dwelt among the untrodden ways', Wikipedia (summarizing Brooks, Woolford, Butler); Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'untrodden'

  3. the springs of Dove l. 2

    Wordsworth knew rivers of this name: the Derbyshire and Staffordshire Dove of Dovedale, a Dove in Yorkshire, and Dove beck near Patterdale in Westmorland. Critics agree that the exact location adds little; the name is pastoral and gentle (the dove being a bird of peace and love), and 'springs' implies a source, a place of beginnings far from towns. The plural 'springs' and the stream's obscurity reinforce the sense of a hidden life at the margins of the known world.

    'She dwelt among the untrodden ways', Wikipedia (summarizing Brooks, Woolford, Butler); 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones)

  4. 'none to praise, / And very few to love' ll. 3-4

    The lines turn on litotes and quiet paradox: a maid unpraised is the subject of a poem of praise. Hartley Coleridge's parody 'He lived amidst th'untrodden ways' (printed posthumously) turned the stanza against Wordsworth himself, making him a bard whom 'there were none to praise, / And very few to read', a joke on the poor sales of his early volumes. The parody shows how recognizable the poem's manner already was.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones)

  5. the violet ll. 5-6

    The violet was a traditional emblem of modesty and humility because it grows low and hidden. The 'mossy stone' adds age, stillness and perhaps a hint of the grave. Critics have debated how the violet and the star relate: some read them as a contrast between the hidden flower and the star visible to all, others as two images of the same singularity. The images are juxtaposed without a connecting word, a striking leap from earth to sky.

    'She dwelt among the untrodden ways', Wikipedia (summarizing Brooks, Woolford, Butler); 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones)

  6. the single star ll. 7-8

    The image complements the violet: one is hidden, the other conspicuous, yet both are singular and solitary. A star that is 'only one' is beautiful because there is nothing to rival it, so the line also implies that for the speaker Lucy was unique in his world. The first two stanzas are thus a miniature of the poem's argument: unseen by the world, she was everything to one person.

    'She dwelt among the untrodden ways', Wikipedia (summarizing Brooks, Woolford, Butler); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'evening star'

  7. 'She lived unknown' l. 9

    The stanza moves from public obscurity ('few could know') to private devastation in the space of four lines; the syntax is plain statement followed by exclamation. The ballad metre's short lines make the end abrupt. The word 'lived' stresses the contrast between her life (unknown) and her death (a fact only for the speaker).

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  8. Lucy l. 10

    Lucy was a common name in pastoral and ballad verse. Readers since Coleridge's day have guessed that she stands for Dorothy Wordsworth; others have proposed Mary Hutchinson or her sister Margaret (who died in 1796), a composite, or no one at all. Most modern critics treat Lucy as an imagined figure, part girl, part spirit of nature. The name derives from Latin lux, light, which some connect with the star image.

    'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones); Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995); Mary Moorman, William Wordsworth: A Biography. The Early Years, 1770-1803 (Oxford, 1957)

  9. ceased to be l. 10

    The phrase avoids 'died' and avoids any religious consolation; Lucy is not said to have gone to heaven but simply to have stopped existing. The same restraint marks 'A slumber did my spirit seal', where the dead girl is 'Rolled round in earth's diurnal course'. The plain, almost legal phrase was used by later poets too, most famously by Keats ('When I have fears that I may cease to be', written 1818).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cease, v.'; Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995)

  10. 'The difference to me!' ll. 11-12

    The exclamation is famous for its restraint. The poem never says what the difference is; readers take the reticence as grief too great for description. The final lines balance 'her grave' against 'me', and the irony of the whole poem rests on the gap between the world's indifference and the speaker's loss. The 'O!' at the line break is a rare cry in an otherwise plain poem.

    'She dwelt among the untrodden ways', Wikipedia (summarizing Brooks, Woolford, Butler); 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones)
