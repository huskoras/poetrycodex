Death & Dying

Strange fits of passion have I known

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poetical Works, ed. Knight (1896), vol. II

Death & Dying

Strange fits of passion have I known:
And I will dare to tell,
But in the Lover's ear alone,
What once to me befel.

When she I loved looked every day
Fresh as a rose in June,
I to her cottage bent my way,
Beneath an evening moon.

Upon the moon I fixed my eye,
All over the wide lea;
With quickening pace my horse drew nigh
Those paths so dear to me.

And now we reached the orchard-plot;
And, as we climbed the hill,
The sinking moon to Lucy's cot
Came near, and nearer still.

In one of those sweet dreams I slept,
Kind Nature's gentlest boon!
And all the while my eyes I kept
On the descending moon.

My horse moved on; hoof after hoof
He raised, and never stopped:
When down behind the cottage roof,
At once, the bright moon dropped.

What fond and wayward thoughts will slide
Into a Lover's head!
"O mercy!" to myself I cried,
"If Lucy should be dead!"

Codex Note

Composed 1799; published 1800.

Study notes by the editors

  1. date and draft history st. 1

    The Fenwick note says only 'Written in Germany, 1799', but the poem was in a letter to Coleridge in December 1798, where it is one of 'little Rhyme poems'. The draft had a final stanza in which the speaker tells Lucy of his fear, she laughs, and he now recalls the night with tears; Wordsworth cut it, ending on the unanswered fear. The text here is a later revision; the 1800 version read, for example, 'When she I lov'd, was strong and gay / And like a rose in June'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones); Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995)

  2. fits of passion l. 1

    A 'fit' (OED) is a sudden seizure, used of illness, emotion or madness; 'passion' is strong emotion, not only erotic love. The phrase presents the experience as involuntary and strange, a kind of mental episode. 'Fit' also had an old sense of a section of a ballad or song, and some readers hear a faint play on it at the opening of a ballad-like poem. The speaker addresses only 'the Lover's ear', claiming that only those in love will understand.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fit, n.2'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fit, n.1 (canto)'

  3. the lover as audience ll. 3-4

    The device creates intimacy and implies that the irrational fear he describes is common to lovers. It is also faintly self-ironic: 'dare to tell' makes a small confession sound like a perilous disclosure. 'Befel' (befell) means happened, a ballad word. The final stanza returns to the 'Lover's head', framing the poem. The convention of a tale told only to initiates also belongs to love-lyric tradition, in which lovers form a private community of understanding.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'befall, v.'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  4. 'Fresh as a rose in June' l. 6

    The simile is a folk-song commonplace (compare Burns, 'O my Luve's like a red, red rose, / That's newly sprung in June', composed 1794). Wordsworth's 1800 text described Lucy as 'strong and gay', emphasizing her vitality, before the revision of 1836. The emphasis on her health 'every day' makes the speaker's sudden fear of her death irrational, the point of the poem.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Robert Burns, 'A Red, Red Rose' (1794)

  5. the moon ll. 8-9

    The moon is introduced as setting, then becomes the object of the rider's fixed gaze ('Upon the moon I fixed my eye'). Its gradual descent as the rider climbs the hill is an optical effect of perspective, but in the speaker's trance it acquires the force of an omen.

    Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964); 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones)

  6. lea l. 10

    'Lea' (OED) is a poetic word for meadow or pasture land, common in ballads and pastoral. With 'cot' (cottage), 'orchard-plot' and 'paths so dear' the vocabulary is deliberately simple and rustic, suited to Lyrical Ballads. The 'wide' lea contrasts with the narrowing focus on the cottage roof. The ride across open country towards a single point is the poem's whole structure: space contracts as the moon sinks, until both meet at the cottage.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lea, n.1'

  7. cot l. 15

    'Cot' is a short form of cottage, frequent in eighteenth-century pastoral verse (OED). In 1800 the lines read 'Towards the roof of Lucy's cot / The moon descended still'; the revision 'Came near, and nearer still' makes the moon seem to approach the house, intensifying the sense of fatal convergence. Naming Lucy at the moment the moon nears her home links the girl and the moon for the rest of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cot, n.1'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  8. 'In one of those sweet dreams I slept' ll. 17-20

    The speaker is not literally asleep, for he keeps his eyes on the descending moon; he is in a reverie, a reverie. Sleep is 'Kind Nature's gentlest boon' (boon means gift or favour), an echo of the poetic tradition praising sleep. Critics have seen the passage as a study of how a mind absorbed in a monotonous rhythm and a single object can slip into associative fears. The motion of the horse ('hoof after hoof') prolongs the hypnotic state.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'boon, n.1'; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964)

  9. 'hoof after hoof' ll. 21-22

    The repetition 'hoof after hoof / He raised, and never stopped' enacts the monotony of the ride; the enjambment carries the motion across the line break. The steady motion is the precondition of the sudden shock in the next lines, where the moon drops 'At once'. The pattern of long continuity broken by a sudden event is typical of Wordsworth's 'spots of time', moments in which a small perception produces a disproportionate shock.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  10. 'At once, the bright moon dropped' ll. 23-24

    As the rider nears the cottage, the roof hides the moon; a natural effect of perspective is felt as a blow. The pause after 'At once' and the plain verb 'dropped' make the moment abrupt. The moon's disappearance becomes an unspoken symbol of Lucy's life going out.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'planet, n.'

  11. fond and wayward l. 25

    In older English 'fond' meant foolish or credulous (OED), and Wordsworth uses both senses: the thought is foolish because irrational, and loving because born of affection. 'Wayward' thoughts are those that turn aside from the expected path, as the mind does in reverie. The general reflection ('will slide / Into a Lover's head') returns to the audience of lovers addressed in the first stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fond, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wayward, adj.'

  12. 'If Lucy should be dead!' ll. 27-28

    Wordsworth deleted the draft's final stanza, in which Lucy laughed at his fear and the speaker remembered the night with tears. Without it, the poem ends abruptly in the fear itself. Read alongside the other Lucy poems, where she does die, the cry seems a premonition; read alone, it is a psychological study of how love creates fear. Mark Jones's study of the Lucy poems shows how much the reading of each poem depends on the group into which editors have placed it.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; 'The Lucy poems', Wikipedia (summary of the critical tradition: Hartman, Bateson, Ferry, Moorman, Jones); Mark Jones, The 'Lucy Poems': A Case Study in Literary Knowledge (Toronto, 1995)
