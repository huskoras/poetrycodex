Nature

Lines Written in Early Spring

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Lyrical Ballads (1798)

NatureSocial Commentaries

I heard a thousand blended notes,
While in a grove I sate reclined,
In that sweet mood when pleasant thoughts
Bring sad thoughts to the mind.

To her fair works did nature link
The human soul that through me ran;
And much it griev’d my heart to think
What man has made of man.

Through primrose-tufts, in that sweet bower,
The periwinkle trail’d its wreathes;
And ’tis my faith that every flower
Enjoys the air it breathes.

The birds around me hopp’d and play’d:
Their thoughts I cannot measure,
But the least motion which they made,
It seem’d a thrill of pleasure.

The budding twigs spread out their fan,
To catch the breezy air;
And I must think, do all I can,
That there was pleasure there.

If I these thoughts may not prevent,
If such be of my creed the plan,
Have I not reason to lament
What man has made of man?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, place and volume title; l. 1

    The poem belongs to the spring of 1798, when William and Dorothy Wordsworth were living at Alfoxden House near Nether Stowey, close to Coleridge, and the two poets were preparing Lyrical Ballads (published anonymously, autumn 1798). In the Fenwick note of 1843 Wordsworth recalled that it was actually composed while he sat beside the brook that runs down the combe and through the Alfoxden grounds, a favourite resort of his. (Editors warn that the Fenwick notes, dictated decades later, are not always accurate.) The title is typical of the volume's 'lines written' poems that claim to record a precise moment. 'A thousand blended notes' introduces the poem's central idea of harmony between nature and the listening mind.

    Isabella Fenwick notes (dictated by Wordsworth, 1843), as printed in William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (1896); R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford, 1989), on Alfoxden, 1797-98

  2. Sate l. 2

    'Sate' (rhyming with 'gate' or pronounced like 'sat') was a common literary form of 'sat' in the eighteenth century. The posture, 'reclined' in a grove, is the conventional pose of the pastoral poet or melancholy contemplative, and sets up the poem's mixture of pleasure and sadness. Wordsworth's Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800) attacked 'poetic diction', yet the 1798 poems still keep such traditional forms where they suit metre and rhyme, and 'sate' here also supplies the stressed syllable the tetrameter needs.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sit'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'

  3. Pleasure turning to sadness ll. 3-4

    The stanza ends on a short trimeter line that turns the pleasure of the scene into sadness. The sadness is not personal grief but moral reflection, explained in the next stanza: human beings, unlike the rest of nature, seem to have spoiled their own condition. The idea that sweetness and sadness are allied draws on eighteenth-century 'pleasing melancholy' (compare Gray and the poetry of sensibility), which Wordsworth deepens into a social and philosophical concern.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1947), poem and apparatus

  4. Nature linking the soul ll. 5-6

    The inversion 'To her fair works did nature link / The human soul' presents the human spirit as part of the same chain as flowers and birds. The phrase 'that through me ran' imagines the soul as something that flows through the individual rather than belonging to him, close to Coleridge's 'one Life within us and abroad' ('The Eolian Harp', lines added 1817) and to 'Tintern Abbey' (also 1798), where a 'motion and a spirit' rolls 'through all things'. The passage belongs with the 'one life' strain of thought in the Alfoxden period.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; William Wordsworth, 'Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey' (1798), ll. 101-03

  5. 'What man has made of man' l. 8; l. 24

    The line closes stanzas 2 and 6, giving the poem a frame. It refers to what humans have done to their own kind: war, poverty, and the injustices that troubled Wordsworth after his disillusionment with the French Revolution, rather than to damage to the natural environment. Commentators often hear an echo of Robert Burns's 'Man was made to Mourn' (1786): 'Man's inhumanity to man / Makes countless thousands mourn!' The phrasing is also a chiasmus in miniature, man acting upon man, which makes humanity both agent and victim. The repetition at the end turns the statement into a question.

    Robert Burns, 'Man was made to Mourn: A Dirge' (1786), st. 7; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford, 1989), on Alfoxden, 1797-98

  6. Periwinkle l. 10

    The periwinkle (Vinca minor) is a low, creeping plant whose blue flowers appear in early spring, alongside the primrose, so the details fit the season named in the title. 'Wreathes' (the archive's spelling of 'wreaths') describes its twining stems. The 'bower' is a shady recess formed by plants, a word with pastoral and romance associations. The stanza offers the first piece of evidence for the speaker's 'faith' in the joy of flowers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'periwinkle'; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'

  7. 'my faith that every flower / Enjoys the air' ll. 11-12

    Wordsworth carefully presents the idea as belief: 'tis my faith'. The notion that plants have sensation had some currency in the period; Erasmus Darwin's The Botanic Garden (1789-91) had popularised the sexual and quasi-animate life of plants, and Wordsworth knew Darwin's work. The repeated terms 'pleasure' and 'thrill of pleasure' (stanzas 4-5) build a case by analogy: birds and twigs seem to feel joy, so the speaker 'must think' it is so. The tentative verbs ('I cannot measure', 'It seem'd', 'I must think, do all I can') show a mind resisting and yet unable to resist the conviction.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1947), poem and apparatus; Erasmus Darwin, The Botanic Garden (1789-91)

  8. Limits of knowledge l. 14

    The stanza is a small exercise in humility. The speaker grants that animal consciousness is beyond measurement, yet the 'least motion' of the birds looks like pleasure. The shift from claim to appearance ('It seem'd') is typical of the 1798 poems, which test the boundary between perception and projection. Coleridge would later object, in Biographia Literaria (1817), to some of Wordsworth's theories and diction in the Lyrical Ballads, though not specifically to this poem.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 22

  9. Twigs spreading a fan ll. 17-18

    The fan was a familiar fashionable object, and the comparison briefly domesticates the twigs while giving them intention: they spread out 'to catch the breezy air'. The breeze recalls the Romantic figure of the wind as a life-giving breath, and the stanza completes the series of flower, bird and twig as evidence for the speaker's belief. The verb 'must think, do all I can' shows the speaker almost compelled into his belief.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'

  10. 'If such be of my creed the plan' (1798 reading) ll. 21-22

    The archive prints the original Lyrical Ballads reading: 'If I these thoughts may not prevent, / If such be of my creed the plan'. That is, if he cannot stop himself thinking this way, and if such is the shape of his beliefs, then he has reason to grieve over humanity. In later editions Wordsworth revised the lines to 'If this belief from heaven be sent, / If such be Nature's holy plan', which makes the belief a divine revelation and nature's plan sacred. Critics read the revision as part of the older Wordsworth's movement toward orthodox piety; the 1798 version is more personal and more tentative.

    E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1947), poem and apparatus; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'

  11. The closing question ll. 23-24

    The poem is in six quatrains rhymed abab, mostly iambic tetrameter with shorter trimeter lines, which often fall last and carry the emotional weight. By repeating 'What man has made of man' at the end, now as a question, Wordsworth closes the circle opened in stanza 2. The interrogative form invites readers to test their own experience against the speaker's: if nature is joyful, the misery of human society is a human creation.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Wordsworth and Coleridge: Lyrical Ballads (Methuen, 1963; 2nd edn. 1991), notes to 'Lines written in early spring'; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1947), poem and apparatus
