Nature

Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Lyrical Ballads (1798)

NatureTime & BrevityThe Mind

Five years have passed; five summers, with the length
Of five long winters! and again I hear
These waters, rolling from their mountain-springs
With a sweet inland murmur.--Once again
Do I behold these steep and lofty cliffs,
Which on a wild secluded scene impress
Thoughts of more deep seclusion; and connect
The landscape with the quiet of the sky.
The day is come when I again repose
Here, under this dark sycamore, and view
These plots of cottage-ground, these orchard-tufts,
Which, at this season, with their unripe fruits,
Among the woods and copses lose themselves,
Nor, with their green and simple hue, disturb
The wild green landscape. Once again I see
These hedge-rows, hardly hedge-rows, little lines
Of sportive wood run wild; these pastoral farms
Green to the very door; and wreathes of smoke
Sent up, in silence, from among the trees,
With some uncertain notice, as might seem,
Of vagrant dwellers in the houseless woods,
Or of some hermit’s cave, where by his fire
The hermit sits alone.

Though absent long,
These forms of beauty have not been to me,
As is a landscape to a blind man’s eye:
But oft, in lonely rooms, and mid the din
Of towns and cities, I have owed to them,
In hours of weariness, sensations sweet,
Felt in the blood, and felt along the heart,
And passing even into my purer mind
With tranquil restoration:--feelings too
Of unremembered pleasure; such, perhaps,
As may have had no trivial influence
On that best portion of a good man’s life;
His little, nameless, unremembered acts
Of kindness and of love. Nor less, I trust,
To them I may have owed another gift,
Of aspect more sublime; that blessed mood,
In which the burthen of the mystery,
In which the heavy and the weary weight
Of all this unintelligible world
Is lighten’d:--that serene and blessed mood,
In which the affections gently lead us on,
Until, the breath of this corporeal frame,
And even the motion of our human blood
Almost suspended, we are laid asleep
In body, and become a living soul:
While with an eye made quiet by the power
Of harmony, and the deep power of joy,
We see into the life of things.

If this
Be but a vain belief, yet, oh! how oft,
In darkness, and amid the many shapes
Of joyless day-light; when the fretful stir
Unprofitable, and the fever of the world,
Have hung upon the beatings of my heart,
How oft, in spirit, have I turned to thee
O sylvan Wye! Thou wanderer through the woods,
How often has my spirit turned to thee!

And now, with gleams of half-extinguish’d thought,
With many recognitions dim and faint,
And somewhat of a sad perplexity,
The picture of the mind revives again:
While here I stand, not only with the sense
Of present pleasure, but with pleasing thoughts
That in this moment there is life and food
For future years. And so I dare to hope
Though changed, no doubt, from what I was, when first
I came among these hills; when like a roe
I bounded o’er the mountains, by the sides
Of the deep rivers, and the lonely streams,
Wherever nature led; more like a man
Flying from something that he dreads, than one
Who sought the thing he loved. For nature then
(The coarser pleasures of my boyish days,
And their glad animal movements all gone by,)
To me was all in all.--I cannot paint
What then I was. The sounding cataract
Haunted me like a passion: the tall rock,
The mountain, and the deep and gloomy wood,
Their colours and their forms, were then to me
An appetite: a feeling and a love,
That had no need of a remoter charm,
By thought supplied, or any interest
Unborrowed from the eye.--That time is past,
And all its aching joys are now no more,
And all its dizzy raptures. Not for this
Faint I, nor mourn nor murmur: other gifts
Have followed, for such loss, I would believe,
Abundant recompence. For I have learned
To look on nature, not as in the hour
Of thoughtless youth, but hearing oftentimes
The still, sad music of humanity,
Not harsh nor grating, though of ample power
To chasten and subdue. And I have felt
A presence that disturbs me with the joy
Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime
Of something far more deeply interfused,
Whose dwelling is the light of setting suns,
And the round ocean, and the living air,
And the blue sky, and in the mind of man,
A motion and a spirit, that impels
All thinking things, all objects of all thought,
And rolls through all things. Therefore am I still
A lover of the meadows and the woods,
And mountains; and of all that we behold
From this green earth; of all the mighty world
Of eye and ear, both what they half-create,
And what perceive; well pleased to recognize
In nature and the language of the sense,
The anchor of my purest thoughts, the nurse,
The guide, the guardian of my heart, and soul
Of all my moral being.

Nor, perchance,
If I were not thus taught, should I the more
Suffer my genial spirits to decay:
For thou art with me, here, upon the banks
Of this fair river; thou, my dearest Friend,
My dear, dear Friend, and in thy voice I catch
The language of my former heart, and read
My former pleasures in the shooting lights
Of thy wild eyes. Oh! yet a little while
May I behold in thee what I was once,
My dear, dear Sister! And this prayer I make,
Knowing that Nature never did betray
The heart that loved her; ’tis her privilege,
Through all the years of this our life, to lead
From joy to joy: for she can so inform
The mind that is within us, so impress
With quietness and beauty, and so feed
With lofty thoughts, that neither evil tongues,
Rash judgments, nor the sneers of selfish men,
Nor greetings where no kindness is, nor all
The dreary intercourse of daily life,
Shall e’er prevail against us, or disturb
Our chearful faith that all which we behold
Is full of blessings. Therefore let the moon
Shine on thee in thy solitary walk;
And let the misty mountain winds be free
To blow against thee: and in after years,
When these wild ecstasies shall be matured
Into a sober pleasure, when thy mind
Shall be a mansion for all lovely forms,
Thy memory be as a dwelling-place
For all sweet sounds and harmonies; Oh! then,
If solitude, or fear, or pain, or grief,
Should be thy portion, with what healing thoughts
Of tender joy wilt thou remember me,
And these my exhortations! Nor, perchance,
If I should be, where I no more can hear
Thy voice, nor catch from thy wild eyes these gleams
Of past existence, wilt thou then forget
That on the banks of this delightful stream
We stood together; and that I, so long
A worshipper of Nature, hither came,
Unwearied in that service: rather say
With warmer love, oh! with far deeper zeal
Of holier love. Nor wilt thou then forget,
That after many wanderings, many years
Of absence, these steep woods and lofty cliffs,
And this green pastoral landscape, were to me
More dear, both for themselves, and for thy sake.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and occasion ll. 1-2

    The full 1798 title reads 'Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey, on revisiting the banks of the Wye during a tour, July 13, 1798'. Wordsworth later recalled beginning it on leaving Tintern and finishing it as he entered Bristol after a four- or five-day ramble with his sister, adding that not a line was altered or written down until he reached Bristol (Fenwick note). The 1793 visit came when he was a lonely, politically troubled young man after his time in revolutionary France. The poem was placed last in Lyrical Ballads (1798), where it stands apart from the ballads in its meditative blank verse. Marjorie Levinson noted that the date falls on the eve of Bastille Day and argued that the poem's careful framing keeps history, poverty and industry out of view; other critics, such as Hartman, read it chiefly as a drama of memory and consciousness. The insistent counting ('five... five... five') stresses how much time, and inner change, lies between the two visits.

    Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads, with a Few Other Poems (1798), text and Wordsworth's notes; The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (1993), note to the poem; Marjorie Levinson, Wordsworth's Great Period Poems: Four Essays (Cambridge, 1986), ch. 1; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964)

  2. inland murmur l. 4

    Wordsworth glossed this phrase in 1798: the river is 'not affected by the tides a few miles above Tintern'. Lower down, near Chepstow, the Wye is a muddy tidal river; upstream it is a fresh, flowing stream heard rather than seen. The word 'inland' therefore places the speaker exactly and also suggests something interior and sheltered, a sound set apart from the sea and the outside world. 'Murmur' is the first of the poem's many soft sound-words (compare 'the still, sad music of humanity'). Hearing comes before seeing in the opening: 'again I hear' precedes 'Do I behold', so the landscape enters first through the ear.

    Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads, with a Few Other Poems (1798), text and Wordsworth's notes; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  3. hedge-rows, hardly hedge-rows ll. 16-17

    The self-correction makes us watch the mind looking and revising, a typical Wordsworthian move. The lines describe a landscape on the border of farm and wilderness: cottage plots and orchards that 'lose themselves' among the woods, and farms 'green to the very door'. The Wye was famous in the 1780s and 1790s as a picturesque tour, popularised by William Gilpin's Observations on the River Wye (1782), and the lines show Wordsworth's familiarity with that way of seeing, while preferring calm harmony ('Nor... disturb / The wild green landscape') to the picturesque taste for rough contrasts.

    William Gilpin, Observations on the River Wye (1782); R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  4. vagrant dwellers... hermit ll. 18-23

    The 'wreathes of smoke' give only 'uncertain notice' of who is there; the speaker imagines either 'vagrant dwellers in the houseless woods' (vagrants, wanderers without houses) or a solitary hermit. The hermit turns the scene into a contemplative image of the poet himself. Historicist critics, above all Marjorie Levinson, have pointed out that the real Wye valley in the 1790s held charcoal-burners, iron works and many poor people (Gilpin had noticed beggars living about the Abbey ruins) and argued that the poem converts social fact into peaceful landscape. Others reply that the vagrants are in fact acknowledged here, and that the poem's subject is the mind rather than the economy. 'Houseless' is a strong word, recalling King Lear's 'houseless heads' on the heath.

    Marjorie Levinson, Wordsworth's Great Period Poems: Four Essays (Cambridge, 1986), ch. 1; William Gilpin, Observations on the River Wye (1782); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vagrant, adj. and n.'

  5. a landscape to a blind man's eye ll. 23-25

    The negative simile states the key claim of the second paragraph: memory has kept 'these forms of beauty' alive. 'Forms' is a weighty word for Wordsworth, meaning the shapes and outlines of nature that impress themselves on the mind (compare 'Their colours and their forms' later and 'a mansion for all lovely forms' at the end). The lines also introduce the poem's arc of eye imagery, which runs through 'an eye made quiet', 'the mighty world / Of eye and ear', and Dorothy's 'wild eyes'.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964)

  6. sensations sweet, felt in the blood ll. 27-30

    The lines trace a progression from body to mind: sensations are 'felt in the blood, and felt along the heart' and then pass 'even into my purer mind / With tranquil restoration'. Editors relate this to David Hartley's associationist psychology (Observations on Man, 1749), which Coleridge admired in the 1790s and which explained how simple sensations combine into complex ideas and moral feelings. The phrase 'tranquil restoration' looks forward to Wordsworth's 1800 definition of poetry's origin in 'emotion recollected in tranquillity'. The repetition 'felt... felt' imitates the slow pulse it describes.

    R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800/1802)

  7. little, nameless, unremembered acts ll. 34-35

    The claim is carefully hedged ('such, perhaps, / As may have had no trivial influence'), typical of the poem's tentative tone. Unremembered pleasures produce unremembered acts of kindness, so the moral effect of nature lies below conscious memory. The idea that small habitual feelings form character belongs to Wordsworth's programme in Lyrical Ballads and to associationist thinking. The triple adjective slows the line and enacts the modesty it praises. Critics often contrast this quiet 'best portion of a good man's life' with the heroic ethics of earlier poetry.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'

  8. burthen of the mystery ll. 37-41

    'Burthen' (Old English byrthen, from beran, to bear) is the load the 'blessed mood' lightens; the word can also mean a refrain in a song, and some readers hear that second sense too. 'Mystery' carries religious weight (Greek mysterion, a secret rite or revealed truth), but here it is the oppressive puzzle of existence, the 'heavy and the weary weight / Of all this unintelligible world'. The repeated 'In which... In which' builds the passage like a liturgical period. The phrase became famous enough for Keats to adopt it in his letter on the 'Mansion of Many Apartments' (3 May 1818), where he says Wordsworth was exploring the 'burden of the Mystery'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'burden, n.'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; John Keats, letter to J. H. Reynolds, 3 May 1818

  9. a living soul ll. 43-47

    The phrase echoes Genesis 2:7, where God breathes into Adam's nostrils 'the breath of life; and man became a living soul'; Paul repeats it in 1 Corinthians 15:45. Wordsworth reverses the biblical order in a striking way: here it is the suspension of breath ('the breath of this corporeal frame... / Almost suspended') that brings about the new life of the soul. The passage describes a trance-like state close to mystical experience, though the poem avoids explicit Christian doctrine. 'Corporeal frame' (bodily structure) is eighteenth-century diction.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:7; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:45; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  10. the life of things ll. 47-49

    The climax of the second paragraph. Sight here is no longer the eager, physical seeing of youth (the youthful passion for 'colours and their forms', which he calls 'an appetite') but 'an eye made quiet', an inward vision. 'Harmony' and 'joy' are 'powers', not just feelings. 'The life of things' implies that nature is alive throughout, an idea that prepares for the 'motion and a spirit' later. Readers differ on whether this is mystical (Bradley), philosophical (influenced by Coleridge's interest in unitarian and pantheist ideas in 1798), or a psychological account of deep calm. Note that the very next line ('If this / Be but a vain belief') immediately raises a doubt.

    A. C. Bradley, 'Wordsworth', in Oxford Lectures on Poetry (1909); Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  11. If this be but a vain belief ll. 49-50

    This is one of the poem's characteristic hedges (compare 'I trust', 'I would believe', 'Nor, perchance'). Critics read the hesitations in different ways: as honest scepticism about mystical claims, as the rhythm of a mind thinking aloud, or (with Levinson and others) as a sign that the poem's affirmations are under strain. The paragraph then turns to the opposite of the blessed mood: the 'fretful stir / Unprofitable, and the fever of the world', city life and anxiety, against which the Wye remains a refuge. 'Unprofitable' recalls Hamlet's 'weary, stale, flat, and unprofitable' world (Hamlet 1.2).

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Marjorie Levinson, Wordsworth's Great Period Poems: Four Essays (Cambridge, 1986), ch. 1; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.2.133-34

  12. apostrophe to the Wye ll. 55-57

    Apostrophe (direct address to an absent or inanimate thing) marks the emotional peak of the paragraph; 'sylvan' (Latin silva, a wood) means wooded. Wordsworth told readers in a note added in 1800 that he had not ventured to call the poem an ode but hoped its transitions and 'impassioned music' would recall that form. The paragraph structure, with turns and returns, does resemble the 'greater Romantic lyric' that M. H. Abrams described: a speaker in a specific landscape, a meditation that moves away and returns with new understanding. The river as 'wanderer' mirrors the speaker's own wanderings.

    Wordsworth, note to the poem in Lyrical Ballads (1800); M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric' (1965); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sylvan, adj.'

  13. like a roe ll. 67-68

    A roe is a small deer. The Song of Solomon repeatedly compares the lover to 'a roe or a young hart' upon the mountains (2:9, 2:17, 8:14), and the echo gives his youthful energy a Hebraic, lyrical colour. The speaker then complicates the picture: in 1793 he was 'more like a man / Flying from something that he dreads, than one / Who sought the thing he loved'. Biographers connect this flight with his guilt and distress after leaving Annette Vallon and their daughter in France and with his disillusionment over the Revolution and Britain's war with France, though the poem itself names nothing.

    Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:17; Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford, 1989); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  14. the sounding cataract / haunted me like a passion ll. 76-83

    A cataract is a waterfall. The verb 'Haunted' and the simile 'like a passion' make the youthful love of nature obsessive, almost erotic. These were feelings with 'no need of a remoter charm, / By thought supplied', that is, they did not require associations or ideas to make them attractive. This marks the second of the three stages critics often find in the poem's account of growth: the 'glad animal movements' of boyhood, the passionate 'appetite' of youth, and the mature, thoughtful love of nature that follows. The later Prelude develops the same scheme at length.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cataract, n.'

  15. aching joys... dizzy raptures ll. 84-85

    'Aching joys' joins pleasure and pain; 'dizzy raptures' suggests ecstasy that unbalances the mind ('rapture', from Latin rapere, to seize, means being carried away). Their loss leads into a crucial statement: 'Not for this / Faint I, nor mourn nor murmur'. The inversion and alliteration ('mourn nor murmur') give the line a formal, almost biblical firmness. The theme of loss compensated by 'other gifts' is the basis of what critics call Wordsworth's poetry of compensation, later restated in the 'Intimations' Ode ('We will grieve not, rather find / Strength in what remains behind').

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rapture, n.'

  16. recompence ll. 86-88

    'Recompence' is an old spelling of 'recompense', repayment or compensation. The phrase is hedged ('I would believe'), so the poem offers its consolation as a belief rather than a certainty. Critics such as Hartman have stressed how much of Wordsworth's major poetry depends on this exchange of immediate experience for reflective consciousness, and how anxious the exchange can be. The language of profit and loss here (gifts, recompence, 'owed') runs through the poem and contrasts with the 'unprofitable' fever of the world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'recompense, n.'; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  17. the still, sad music of humanity l. 92

    This is the first gain of maturity: nature is no longer seen in isolation but with an awareness of human life and sorrow. 'Still' means quiet and also continuing; 'sad' adds solemnity. The music is 'Not harsh nor grating, though of ample power / To chasten and subdue', so it disciplines the self without disturbing it. Critics of the poem's politics ask whether this abstract 'humanity' stands in for the real poverty of the Wye valley, while others see in it the ethical heart of Lyrical Ballads, a collection about the rural poor. The paradox of a 'still' music (silent yet heard) suggests a sound perceived by the mind rather than the ear.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Marjorie Levinson, Wordsworth's Great Period Poems: Four Essays (Cambridge, 1986), ch. 1; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'

  18. something far more deeply interfused ll. 93-102

    'Interfused' means poured through and mingled with. The passage describes one spirit present in light, ocean, air, sky 'and in the mind of man', which 'impels / All thinking things, all objects of all thought, / And rolls through all things'. It dissolves the line between thinking subject and object thought about. Its closest contemporary parallels are in Coleridge's 1790s poems ('The Eolian Harp', 'Religious Musings'), and editors also mention Spinoza, Newtonian ideas of a pervasive ether, and Virgil's 'spiritus intus alit' (Aeneid 6.726). Early readers, and later Wordsworth himself in revising other poems, worried that such passages were pantheistic rather than Christian; others call the view panentheist or simply Romantic natural piety.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Virgil, Aeneid 6.724-27; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964)

  19. what they half-create, and what perceive ll. 106-107

    Wordsworth's 1798 note says the line closely resembles an admirable line of Young whose exact words he could not recall: Edward Young's Night Thoughts (Night 6) speaks of the senses that 'half create the wondrous world they see'. The idea is central to Romantic theories of imagination: the mind is not a passive mirror but an active partner with nature (compare Coleridge's account of the 'primary imagination' in Biographia Literaria, ch. 13). The phrase 'the language of the sense' makes nature a language read through the senses. The following lines pile up titles for nature (anchor, nurse, guide, guardian, soul), a list critics have called both moving and strained.

    Wordsworth and Coleridge, Lyrical Ballads, with a Few Other Poems (1798), text and Wordsworth's notes; Edward Young, The Complaint, or Night Thoughts (1742-45), Night 6; S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 13

  20. genial spirits l. 113

    In Samson Agonistes Samson laments, 'So much I feel my genial spirits droop' (l. 594). 'Genial' (Latin genialis, from genius, the inborn spirit, also connected with generation) means relating to one's vital, creative nature, not merely 'cheerful'. The fear of their decay was a shared worry in the circle: Coleridge would echo the same phrase in 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802), 'My genial spirits fail'. Here the speaker says that even if he lacked his philosophy of nature, he would not let his spirits fail, because Dorothy is with him.

    Milton, Samson Agonistes, l. 594; S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (1802), l. 39; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genial, adj.'

  21. my dearest Friend... my dear, dear Sister ll. 115-121

    Dorothy Wordsworth (1771-1855), the poet's sister and companion, kept the journals that underlie several of his poems. Her late appearance transforms the poem into a conversation poem like Coleridge's 'Frost at Midnight', addressed to a silent listener. He reads 'my former pleasures in the shooting lights / Of thy wild eyes', so she embodies the earlier stage of his own development. Feminist critics, notably Margaret Homans, have objected that Dorothy is denied a mature mind of her own and serves as a mirror for her brother's growth; others stress the poem's tenderness and its prayer for her future. The repeated 'dear, dear' is unusually intimate for the period's blank verse.

    Margaret Homans, Women Writers and Poetic Identity (Princeton, 1980); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'

  22. Nature never did betray the heart that loved her ll. 122-123

    The poem's most absolute claim, made in the language of prayer ('this prayer I make'). Nature is personified as a faithful female guide who leads 'from joy to joy'. Some readers find the assertion bold beyond what the poem has earned, given the earlier doubts ('If this / Be but a vain belief'); others take it as a deliberate act of faith. The following list of social evils ('evil tongues, / Rash judgments... the sneers of selfish men') reflects the hostility Wordsworth and Coleridge felt as suspected radicals in 1797-98, when they were watched by a government agent at Alfoxden.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964); Juliet Barker, Wordsworth: A Life (2000)

  23. evil tongues ll. 128-129

    At the start of Paradise Lost Book 7, Milton, blind and politically defeated after the Restoration, speaks of himself as 'fall'n on evil days, / On evil days though fall'n, and evil tongues' (7.25-26). Wordsworth borrows the phrase to align his own position as a misjudged, isolated poet with Milton's. 'Greetings where no kindness is' and 'the dreary intercourse of daily life' (intercourse meaning social dealings) widen the list to the hollowness of ordinary society. Milton is a constant presence in Wordsworth's blank verse.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 7.25-26; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'

  24. a mansion... a dwelling-place ll. 139-142

    The architectural metaphor (mansion, from Latin manere, to stay, 'dwelling-place') turns memory into a home in which experience is kept. It closes the circle of the poem: the poet has been describing how his own memory sustained him during five years of absence; now he projects the same process onto Dorothy, whose 'wild ecstasies' will mature 'into a sober pleasure'. The final lines imagine his own absence or death ('where I no more can hear / Thy voice'), so the poem ends by asking to be remembered within that mansion of memory. Keats's well-known 'Mansion of Many Apartments' letter (3 May 1818) uses a different mansion, the house of life, but it is in that letter that he quotes this poem's 'burden of the Mystery'.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; John Keats, letter to J. H. Reynolds, 3 May 1818; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mansion, n.'

  25. blank verse and the closing return ll. 157-159

    The poem is written in blank verse, the metre of Milton's Paradise Lost and of Cowper's The Task, arranged in five verse paragraphs of unequal length. Heavy enjambment and long periodic sentences give it the movement of thought rather than song. The ending deliberately repeats the opening ('these steep woods and lofty cliffs', 'many years / Of absence'), but now the landscape is 'More dear, both for themselves, and for thy sake': loved for its own sake and through Dorothy. Critics call this circular structure, returning to the starting point with deepened understanding, the hallmark of the 'greater Romantic lyric'.

    M. H. Abrams, 'Structure and Style in the Greater Romantic Lyric' (1965); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; R. L. Brett and A. R. Jones, eds., Lyrical Ballads, 2nd edn (Routledge, 1991), notes to 'Tintern Abbey'
