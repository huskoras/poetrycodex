Milton! thou should'st be living at this hour

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

History & Politics

Milton! thou should'st be living at this hour:
England hath need of thee: she is a fen
Of stagnant waters: altar, sword and pen,
Fireside, the heroic wealth of hall and bower,
Have forfeited their ancient English dower
Of inward happiness. We are selfish men;
Oh! raise us up, return to us again;
And give us manners, virtue, freedom, power.
Thy soul was like a Star and dwelt apart:
Thou hadst a voice whose sound was like the sea;
Pure as the naked heavens, majestic, free,
So didst thou travel on life's common way,
In chearful godliness; and yet thy heart
The lowliest duties on itself did lay.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion: 'London, 1802' title; ll. 1-2

    The archive titles the sonnet by its first line; in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807) it is headed 'London, 1802' and stands among the 'Sonnets Dedicated to Liberty'. In August 1802, during the brief Peace of Amiens, Wordsworth and Dorothy crossed to Calais to see Annette Vallon and his daughter Caroline before his marriage to Mary Hutchinson. His later note on the companion sonnet 'Written in London, September, 1802' says it was composed 'immediately after my return from France to London', and that he was struck by the vanity and parade of England, especially its great towns, against the quiet and desolation of France. That mood of indictment governs this poem too: Milton is summoned as a remedy for national corruption, not only as a literary ancestor.

    Wordsworth's 1843 note, as given in Representative Poetry Online; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet; Representative Poetry Online, 'London, 1802' (text of 1807)

  2. Milton and Wordsworth's turn to the sonnet l. 1

    Dorothy Wordsworth's Grasmere Journal for 21 May 1802 records that William wrote two sonnets on Buonaparte after she had read Milton's sonnets to him. In the 1838 advertisement to his collected sonnets Wordsworth said that admiration for some of Milton's sonnets first tempted him to write in that form. The political sonnets of 1802-3 take from Milton not only the Italian structure but the public, admonitory voice of poems such as 'On the Late Massacre in Piedmont' and the sonnets to Cromwell and Fairfax. John Bard McNulty traced these debts in detail; more recently P. J. Connell has read the sequence as Wordsworth's attempt to ground British liberty in the seventeenth-century revolution while his own politics were moving toward Burke.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, Grasmere Journal, 21 May 1802 (ed. Darbishire), via Reading Experience Database; John Bard McNulty, 'Milton's Influence on Wordsworth's Early Sonnets', PMLA 62.3 (1947), 745-751; P. J. Connell, 'Wordsworth's "Sonnets Dedicated to Liberty" and the British Revolutionary Past', ELH 85 (2018)

  3. fen / Of stagnant waters ll. 2-3

    A fen is low, waterlogged ground; the stress here falls on 'stagnant', water that no longer flows and so breeds corruption. The run-on from 'fen' into 'Of stagnant waters' holds the reader in suspension before the image completes. Readers may compare Milton's own figure in Areopagitica, where truth is a 'streaming fountain' whose waters, if they stop moving, 'sick'n into a muddy pool of conformity and tradition'. No edition consulted here claims a deliberate echo, but the parallel suits a poem that invokes Milton as the voice of free, moving thought against national torpor. The image also anticipates the sestet's contrasting water, the sea of Milton's voice (l. 10).

    OED, s.v. 'fen', n.1; John Milton, Areopagitica (1644), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  4. altar, sword and pen, / Fireside ll. 3-4

    Each noun stands for an institution: 'altar' the established Church and its clergy, 'sword' the army and navy, 'pen' writers, scholars and lawyers, and 'Fireside' the private household. The list moves from public to private, so that no sphere escapes the charge. The catalogue is compressed in Milton's manner, and it pointedly includes the 'pen', Wordsworth's own trade; the poet is not exempt from 'We are selfish men' (l. 6). The asyndeton (no linking 'and' before 'Fireside') and the line break give the list the air of an accusation being read out.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet; Representative Poetry Online, 'London, 1802' (text of 1807)

  5. hall and bower l. 4

    'Hall' is the main public room of a lord's dwelling; 'bower' in its older sense is an inner chamber, especially a lady's private apartment, rather than a garden arbour. The pairing is archaic and ballad-like, and it gives the 'heroic wealth' of England an ancient, almost Anglo-Saxon pedigree, matching 'ancient English dower' in the next line. Wordsworth means the inherited moral riches of the great houses and the families who lived in them, not their material wealth, which in 1802 he thought all too abundant.

    OED, s.v. 'bower', n.1, sense 'an inner apartment; a lady's private apartment'; OED, s.v. 'hall', n.1; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  6. dower l. 5

    A dower is strictly a widow's portion of her husband's estate, and by extension any dowry, gift or natural endowment. Wordsworth uses the legal sense figuratively: inward happiness was the inheritance settled on England, and it has been 'forfeited', another legal term for property lost through misconduct. The language of estates and title-deeds sharpens the paradox of a country rich in money and bankrupt in spirit. 'Inward happiness' is held over to l. 6 by enjambment, the key phrase arriving late and stressed.

    OED, s.v. 'dower', n.1; OED, s.v. 'forfeit', v.; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  7. We are selfish men l. 6

    After two and a half lines of formal catalogue, the sentence stops dead and the speaker joins the accused. The charge is selfishness, the private getting and spending that Wordsworth also attacks in 'The world is too much with us' and in the companion sonnet to Coleridge, where 'plain living and high thinking are no more'. His later note on that sonnet defends the severity of the verse by pointing to the corrupting effect of unchecked wealth. The 1802 sonnets thus combine patriotism with a sharp critique of commercial England; Connell reads this as a residue of Wordsworth's republican sympathies coexisting with a new national loyalty.

    Wordsworth's 1843 note on 'Written in London, September, 1802', via Representative Poetry Online; P. J. Connell, ELH 85 (2018); E. de Selincourt and H. Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. III (Oxford, 1946), notes to 'Poems Dedicated to National Independence and Liberty'

  8. manners, virtue, freedom, power l. 8

    In eighteenth-century usage 'manners' often meant the moral habits and customs of a people (Latin mores), close to what we now call public morality. 'Virtue' carries the civic-republican sense of public spirit as well as private goodness. The line rises from inward qualities to political ones, 'freedom' and 'power', so that moral reform is presented as the ground of national strength at a moment when war with Napoleon was expected to resume. The prayer 'raise us up, return to us again' (l. 7) has a quasi-liturgical cadence, treating Milton almost as a saviour figure.

    OED, s.v. 'manner', n.1, sense 'customs, mode of life; moral character'; P. J. Connell, ELH 85 (2018); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  9. Petrarchan form and volta ll. 1-14; volta at l. 9

    The rhymes run hour/fen/pen/bower/dower/men/again/power (abbaabba) and apart/sea/free/way/heart/lay (cddece), a Petrarchan pattern of the kind Milton used. The octave is diagnosis and plea; at l. 9 the poem turns from 'us' to 'thee' and becomes portrait. Within this frame Wordsworth follows Milton in letting sense run across line endings ('a fen / Of stagnant waters', 'thy heart / The lowliest duties'), so that the sonnet moves like argument rather than song. The closing lines also reverse the sestet's climb: from star, sea and heavens the poem descends to 'life's common way' and 'lowliest duties'.

    Representative Poetry Online, rhyme analysis of 'London, 1802'; John Bard McNulty, PMLA 62.3 (1947); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  10. like a Star and dwelt apart l. 9

    The capitalised 'Star' suggests a fixed star, constant and remote, the kind by which navigators set a course. 'Dwelt apart' recalls Milton's isolation in the Restoration years, blind and politically defeated, and his self-image as a poet set apart for a great work. The line also anticipates Wordsworth's own idealisation of the solitary poet. Yet the sestet qualifies this aloofness: the star-like soul walked 'life's common way' (l. 12). The poem thus joins the two halves of Milton's career, visionary poet and public servant.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet; E. de Selincourt and H. Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. III (Oxford, 1946), notes to 'Poems Dedicated to National Independence and Liberty'

  11. a voice whose sound was like the sea l. 10

    The simile evokes the rolling periods of Milton's blank verse, its long verse-paragraphs and organ-like sound. It also borrows biblical grandeur: in Revelation 1:15 the voice of the Son of man is 'as the sound of many waters', and in Ezekiel 43:2 the voice of God is 'like a noise of many waters'. The living, moving sea answers the 'stagnant waters' of England's fen in l. 3, a contrast between dead and vital water that organises the whole poem. 'Pure as the naked heavens' then adds air to water: Milton's voice is as open and unclouded as the sky.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 1:15; Ezekiel 43:2; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet

  12. chearful godliness l. 13

    The spelling 'chearful' was still current in 1807 and is kept here from the first edition. The phrase answers a common view of Milton and the Puritans as severe and joyless: Wordsworth insists on a godliness compatible with cheerfulness. Some readers hear a reconciliation of Milton's own paired poems L'Allegro (the cheerful man) and Il Penseroso (the contemplative), though this is a reader's association rather than an established allusion. The adjective also prepares the humility of the last line.

    OED, s.v. 'cheerful', adj. (variant spellings); Representative Poetry Online, text of 1807

  13. The lowliest duties ll. 13-14

    The inverted syntax ('on itself did lay') makes the duties self-imposed. Readers usually think of Milton's years as schoolmaster, pamphleteer and Latin Secretary to the Council of State, when he set poetry aside; in The Reason of Church Government he said that in prose he had the use 'but of my left hand'. The line may also recall Paradise Lost VIII.192-4, where knowing 'That which before us lies in daily life' is called 'the prime Wisdom'. Critics differ on the weight of the close: some find a quiet anticlimax after star and sea, others the poem's real point, that greatness is proved by humble duty, which is what selfish England lacks.

    John Milton, The Reason of Church Government (1642), Book II, preface; Paradise Lost VIII.192-4, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), notes to the sonnet; P. J. Connell, ELH 85 (2018)
