I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

NatureThe Mind

I wandered lonely as a cloud
That floats on high o’er vales and hills,
When all at once I saw a crowd,
A host, of golden daffodils;
Beside the lake, beneath the trees,
Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Continuous as the stars that shine
And twinkle on the milky way,
They stretched in never-ending line
Along the margin of a bay:
Ten thousand saw I at a glance,
Tossing their heads in sprightly dance.

The waves beside them danced; but they
Out-did the sparkling waves in glee:
A poet could not but be gay,
In such a jocund company:
I gazed—and gazed—but little thought
What wealth the show to me had brought:

For oft, when on my couch I lie
In vacant or in pensive mood,
They flash upon that inward eye
Which is the bliss of solitude;
And then my heart with pleasure fills,
And dances with the daffodils.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Memory, not the flowers, is the poem’s real subject. The crowd of daffodils returns to Wordsworth ‘in vacant or in pensive mood’ — proof of his belief that poetry begins in emotion recollected in tranquillity.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and composition l. 1

    Dorothy's Grasmere Journal for 15 April 1802 describes daffodils by the lake beyond Gowbarrow Park, near Glencoyne Bay on Ullswater: some rested their heads on mossy stones, the rest 'tossed and reeled and danced'. William wrote the poem two years later (1804) and published it in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807). In 1815 he added the second stanza and revised the first, producing the four-stanza text given here. The famous opening simile is telling: Dorothy was with him, so the 'lonely' speaker is a poetic construction. The cloud, drifting high and detached, suggests a solitary consciousness before the encounter.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, ed. William Knight (1897), vol. 1, Grasmere Journal; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); 'I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud', Wikipedia (summary of composition and revision history)

  2. crowd... host ll. 3-4

    'Host' (Latin hostis, enemy, hence army) means a great multitude, especially an army, and in biblical usage the angels, 'the heavenly host' (Luke 2:13). The self-correction turns a mere crowd into something grand and almost sacred. The flowers are treated as a company of beings, setting up the poem's social imagery ('company', 'dance'). The word placement at the start of line 4, followed by 'of golden daffodils', delays the subject for effect.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'host, n.1'; Bible (KJV), Luke 2:13; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  3. golden daffodils l. 4

    The wild daffodil of the Lake District (Narcissus pseudonarcissus, Lent lily) is small and pale yellow. The 1815 change from 'dancing' to 'golden' introduces richness and quietly prepares for the economic metaphor of 'wealth' in stanza 3. The flower's classical name recalls Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection (Ovid, Metamorphoses 3), and some critics connect that myth with the poem's turn from the external scene to the self-reflecting 'inward eye'.

    'I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud', Wikipedia (summary of composition and revision history); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.339-510

  4. Continuous as the stars... milky way ll. 7-10

    This stanza did not exist in 1807. It links the flowers below with the stars above, mirroring the cloud of stanza 1 'on high', and suggests an infinite order ('never-ending line'). The hyperbole of 'Ten thousand saw I at a glance' recalls the biblical ten thousands (e.g. Psalm 91:7), but chiefly expresses abundance seized in one look. 'Margin' of a bay means its edge or shore. Critics who prefer the shorter 1807 text find the addition decorative; others think it gives the poem its sense of the sublime.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); 'I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud', Wikipedia (summary of composition and revision history); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'margin, n.'

  5. gay... jocund company ll. 15-16

    'Jocund' (Latin jocundus, pleasant, associated with jocus, a joke) is a slightly literary word, found in Milton's L'Allegro ('the jocund rebeck') and in Shakespeare. 'Gay' has its older sense of joyful, bright, carefree. The stanza gives the waves and the daffodils a contest in 'glee', and the poet joins the dance by sympathy. Wordsworth in the 1815 Preface classed the poem among those showing the power of the imagination, and the personification here is part of that claim.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'jocund, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gay, adj.'; Milton, L'Allegro, l. 94

  6. I gazed—and gazed l. 17

    The repetition imitates prolonged looking. The line also marks the poem's turn from past experience to later reflection: he 'little thought / What wealth the show to me had brought'. The pleasure of the moment is separate from the deeper gain that only memory reveals, a pattern central to Wordsworth's theory that poetry arises from 'emotion recollected in tranquillity' (Preface, 1800).

    Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800/1802); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  7. wealth... the show l. 18

    The economic word turns memory into a treasury, an idea close to the 'life and food / For future years' of 'Tintern Abbey'. Ironically it borrows the language of gain from the commercial world that Wordsworth attacks in 'The world is too much with us'. 'Show' (spectacle, display) frames nature as a performance witnessed by the poet. Some critics find the transaction too comfortable; others point out that the 'golden' flowers of 1815 make the metaphor deliberate.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  8. vacant or pensive l. 20

    The older sense of 'vacant' is unoccupied or at leisure (OED), so the line describes two states of repose: idle and contemplative. 'Pensive' (French pensif, from Latin pensare, to weigh, consider) carries the melancholy of Milton's Il Penseroso. The couch suggests rest at home, far from the lake. The memory arrives unbidden: 'They flash', a sudden involuntary recollection rather than a willed one.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vacant, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pensive, adj.'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  9. that inward eye which is the bliss of solitude ll. 21-22

    In the Fenwick note Wordsworth said the two best lines in the poem were by Mary (Mary Hutchinson, whom he married in October 1802). In Biographia Literaria ch. 22 Coleridge quoted these lines and the couplet that follows as an instance of what he called 'mental bombast', a thought too great for its occasion: if 'inward eye' and 'bliss of solitude' fit a remembered flash of daffodils, he asked, what words are left for the memory of a whole well-spent life, so that the fall to 'dances with the daffodils' strikes him as bathos. Defenders argue the disproportion is the point: an ordinary sight, recalled in solitude, becomes a source of lasting joy. 'Solitude' answers the 'lonely' of the first line, now transformed from loneliness into bliss.

    The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (1993), note to the poem; S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 22; 'I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud', Wikipedia (summary of composition and revision history)

  10. stanza form and closing ll. 23-24

    The sixain rhymes ababcc, ending each stanza with a couplet that rounds it off, and the lines are iambic tetrameter, a light metre suited to song. The final couplet completes the poem's movement from solitary cloud to shared dance: in stanza 1 the flowers dance, in stanza 3 the waves and the flowers, and finally the heart itself 'dances with the daffodils'. Alliteration on d binds the last line. The plain diction is in line with Wordsworth's aim, in the Preface, to use 'a selection of the language really used by men'.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1802)
