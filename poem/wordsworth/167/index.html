Death & Dying

Lucy Gray; or, Solitude

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poetical Works, ed. Knight (1896), vol. II

Death & Dying

Oft I had heard of Lucy Gray:
And, when I crossed the wild,
I chanced to see at break of day
The solitary child.

No mate, no comrade Lucy knew;
She dwelt on a wide moor,
--The sweetest thing that ever grew
Beside a human door!

You yet may spy the fawn at play,
The hare upon the green;
But the sweet face of Lucy Gray
Will never more be seen.

"To-night will be a stormy night--
You to the town must go;
And take a lantern, Child, to light
Your mother through the snow."

"That, Father! will I gladly do:
'Tis scarcely afternoon--
The minster-clock has just struck two,
And yonder is the moon!"

At this the Father raised his hook,
And snapped a faggot-band;
He plied his work;--and Lucy took
The lantern in her hand.

Not blither is the mountain roe:
With many a wanton stroke
Her feet disperse the powdery snow,
That rises up like smoke.

The storm came on before its time:
She wandered up and down;
And many a hill did Lucy climb
But never reached the town.

The wretched parents all that night
Went shouting far and wide;
But there was neither sound nor sight
To serve them for a guide.

At day-break on a hill they stood
That overlooked the moor;
And thence they saw the bridge of wood,
A furlong from their door.

They wept--and, turning homeward, cried,
"In heaven we all shall meet;"
--When in the snow the mother spied
The print of Lucy's feet.

Then downwards from the steep hill's edge
They tracked the footmarks small;
And through the broken hawthorn hedge,
And by the long stone-wall;

And then an open field they crossed:
The marks were still the same;
They tracked them on, nor ever lost;
And to the bridge they came.

They followed from the snowy bank
Those footmarks, one by one,
Into the middle of the plank;
And further there were none!

--Yet some maintain that to this day
She is a living child;
That you may see sweet Lucy Gray
Upon the lonesome wild.

O'er rough and smooth she trips along,
And never looks behind;
And sings a solitary song
That whistles in the wind.

Codex Note

Composed 1799; published 1800.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Lucy Gray: occasion and source st. 1

    Wordsworth's Fenwick note says the poem 'was founded on a circumstance told me by my sister' of a little girl near Halifax in Yorkshire who was lost in a snowstorm; her footsteps were traced by her parents to the middle of a lock on the canal, and no other vestige of her could be found. He added that the treatment and 'spiritualizing' of the character might be contrasted with George Crabbe's 'matter-of-fact' handling of similar subjects. The subtitle 'or, Solitude' names the theme; Henry Crabb Robinson reports that Wordsworth meant to exhibit 'entire solitude'. Wordsworth later placed the poem among the 'Poems referring to the Period of Childhood'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (Oxford, 1940-49), notes to the poem

  2. ballad stanza passim

    The traditional ballad stanza suits the poem's folk-tale manner: a narrator who has heard the story ('Oft I had heard'), dialogue in direct speech, a stark event, and a closing legend. Lyrical Ballads presented such forms as experiments in using the language of ordinary people. 'The wild' means wild, uncultivated country, a moor; the speaker claims once to have glimpsed the child, so the frame both vouches for her reality and prepares for the ghostly ending.

    Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800; revised 1802); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  3. the solitary child ll. 4-8

    Crabb Robinson reports Wordsworth's saying that his aim was to exhibit 'entire solitude'. The child is described in the language of nature: 'The sweetest thing that ever grew / Beside a human door', as if she were a flower or tree at the edge of the human world. The phrase 'human door' marks that boundary between the household and the moor, which she will cross.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964)

  4. 'Beside a human door' l. 8

    The verb 'grew' and the adjective 'human' are both unexpected: we expect 'the sweetest flower that ever grew' and 'beside a cottage door'. By calling the door 'human', Wordsworth implies that Lucy herself belongs partly outside the human order, to the moor and its creatures. The next stanza confirms this: the fawn and the hare may still be seen, but Lucy 'Will never more be seen', so she is grouped with wild animals rather than with people.

    Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  5. the fawn and the hare ll. 9-12

    This stanza anticipates the ending, telling the reader at once that Lucy 'Will never more be seen', so the narrative that follows is read as a tragedy foretold. The animals also prepare for the comparison of Lucy with the 'mountain roe' (l. 25).

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  6. minster l. 19

    The word comes ultimately from Latin monasterium and is used in the north of England for great churches such as York Minster. The hour matters: it is only two in the afternoon, and Lucy is confident of returning before dark.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'minster'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  7. the afternoon moon l. 20

    Crabb Robinson recorded that Wordsworth meant the child to be seen 'observing the day-moon, which no town or village girl would ever notice'. The moon thus characterizes Lucy as a creature attuned to the moor. It also introduces an ominous note: the moon is already up while she expects to light her mother home through the snow, and the storm comes 'before its time' (l. 29). Moon imagery recurs across the Lucy poems, most strikingly in 'Strange fits of passion'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  8. hook and faggot-band ll. 21-22

    A faggot is a bundle of sticks for fuel, and the faggot-band the twisted withy or cord tying it; the 'hook' is a billhook, a curved woodman's blade. The gesture is brisk and unreflective: the father sends the child and goes on with his work ('He plied his work'). The poem does not blame him, but the everyday task, set against the coming storm, sharpens the pathos.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'faggot, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hook, n. (bill-hook)'

  9. the mountain roe l. 25

    The roe is a small deer. In the Song of Solomon the beloved is 'like a roe or a young hart' leaping on the mountains (2:9, 2:17), and 'swift as a roe' is a scriptural commonplace (2 Samuel 2:18). The comparison continues the identification of Lucy with the creatures of the moor. 'Not blither' (not more cheerful) is an ironic understatement, given what follows.

    The Bible (KJV), Song of Solomon 2:9; The Bible (KJV), 2 Samuel 2:18; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  10. wanton l. 26

    'Wanton' originally meant undisciplined or ungoverned, and in older poetry is often used of children and young animals at play (OED). The image of the snow rising 'like smoke' is precise and visual, and the playful energy of the stanza marks the last moment of ordinary life before 'The storm came on before its time'. The word thus carries, in retrospect, a hint of carelessness or heedlessness, though the poem does not blame the child.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wanton, adj.'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  11. the storm and the search ll. 29-36

    The catastrophe is told with almost no detail: 'She wandered up and down'. The parents' night search is equally bare. The poem's restraint follows the ballad tradition of telling events without commentary. The phrase 'neither sound nor sight / To serve them for a guide' reverses Lucy's mission: she was to carry a light to guide her mother, and now the parents have no guide to find her.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (Oxford, 1940-49), notes to the poem

  12. furlong l. 40

    From the hill at daybreak the parents see the wooden bridge, very close to home: Lucy was lost almost within reach of the house. The measurement increases the pathos. In the Fenwick note the real child's footprints ended in the middle of a canal lock; Wordsworth changes the lock to a 'bridge of wood' and 'plank', a more rural setting.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'furlong'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  13. 'In heaven we all shall meet' l. 42

    The cry expresses the conventional Christian consolation for a lost child; it comes at the very moment when the mother spies 'The print of Lucy's feet', so faith and the physical trace are juxtaposed. The following stanzas, tracking the 'footmarks small' through hedge and field and along the wall, are among the most suspenseful in Wordsworth, a slow repetition ('one by one') that builds towards the empty plank.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  14. 'And further there were none!' ll. 53-56

    Wordsworth's source ended with the footsteps stopping in the middle of a canal lock and no trace of the child afterwards; the poem keeps the bare trace and adds the plank. The footprints simply end, leaving a mystery that the closing stanzas exploit. This is the 'spiritualizing' that Wordsworth contrasted with Crabbe's realism: by never describing the death, he lets the child pass out of the human world rather than die in it. Readers differ on whether the effect is consoling, suggesting her absorption into nature, or uncanny and bleak.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964)

  15. the closing legend ll. 57-64

    The last two stanzas move from narrative to rumour ('some maintain'), reporting a folk belief without endorsing it. The present tense ('she trips along', 'sings') contrasts with the past tense of the story, so Lucy enters a timeless state. Many readers find the ending eerie rather than comforting; others see it as Wordsworth's characteristic transformation of loss into enduring presence in the landscape. The opening frame ('Oft I had heard', 'I chanced to see') suggests that the narrator himself may have seen her spirit.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964)

  16. the solitary song ll. 63-64

    'Never looks behind' recalls her obedient errand and the moving forward of a spirit; her 'solitary song' fulfils the subtitle 'Solitude'. The wind that 'whistles' may be heard as her song, or her song as the wind; the ambiguity completes the poem's movement of Lucy out of the human household into the moor. Readers may compare the Highland reaper's 'solitary' song in 'The Solitary Reaper' (1807).

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem
