The Mind

Resolution and Independence

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

The MindLivingArts & Sciences

There was a roaring in the wind all night;
The rain came heavily and fell in floods;
But now the sun is rising calm and bright;
The birds are singing in the distant woods;
Over his own sweet voice the Stock-dove broods;
The Jay makes answer as the Magpie chatters;
And all the air is fill'd with pleasant noise of waters.

All things that love the sun are out of doors;
The sky rejoices in the morning's birth;
The grass is bright with rain-drops; on the moors
The Hare is running races in her mirth;
And with her feet she from the plashy earth
Raises a mist; which, glittering in the sun,
Runs with her all the way, wherever she doth run.

I was a Traveller then upon the moor;
I saw the Hare that rac'd about with joy;
I heard the woods, and distant waters, roar;
Or heard them not, as happy as a Boy:
The pleasant season did my heart employ:
My old remembrances went from me wholly;
And all the ways of men, so vain and melancholy.

But, as it sometimes chanceth, from the might
Of joy in minds that can no farther go,
As high as we have mounted in delight
In our dejection do we sink as low,
To me that morning did it happen so;
And fears, and fancies, thick upon me came;
Dim sadness, & blind thoughts I knew not nor could name.

I heard the Sky-lark singing in the sky;
And I bethought me of the playful Hare:
Even such a happy Child of earth am I;
Even as these blissful Creatures do I fare;
Far from the world I walk, and from all care;
But there may come another day to me,
Solitude, pain of heart, distress, and poverty.

My whole life I have liv'd in pleasant thought,
As if life's business were a summer mood;
As if all needful things would come unsought
To genial faith, still rich in genial good;
But how can He expect that others should
Build for him, sow for him, and at his call
Love him, who for himself will take no heed at all?

I thought of Chatterton, the marvellous Boy,
The sleepless Soul that perish'd in its pride;
Of Him who walk'd in glory and in joy
Behind his plough, upon the mountain-side:
By our own spirits are we deified;
We Poets in our youth begin in gladness;
But thereof comes in the end despondency and madness.

Now, whether it were by peculiar grace,
A leading from above, a something given,
Yet it befel, that, in this lonely place,
When up and down my fancy thus was driven,
And I with these untoward thoughts had striven,
I saw a Man before me unawares:
The oldest Man he seem'd that ever wore grey hairs.

My course I stopped as soon as I espied
The Old Man in that naked wilderness:
Close by a Pond, upon the further side,
He stood alone: a minute's space I guess
I watch'd him, he continuing motionless:
To the Pool's further margin then I drew;
He being all the while before me full in view.

As a huge Stone is sometimes seen to lie
Couch'd on the bald top of an eminence;
Wonder to all who do the same espy
By what means it could thither come, and whence;
So that it seems a thing endued with sense:
Like a Sea-beast crawl'd forth, which on a shelf
Of rock or sand reposeth, there to sun itself.

Such seem'd this Man, not all alive nor dead,
Nor all asleep; in his extreme old age:
His body was bent double, feet and head
Coming together in their pilgrimage;
As if some dire constraint of pain, or rage
Of sickness felt by him in times long past,
A more than human weight upon his frame had cast.

Himself he propp'd, his body, limbs, and face,
Upon a long grey Staff of shaven wood:
And, still as I drew near with gentle pace,
Beside the little pond or moorish flood
Motionless as a Cloud the Old Man stood;
That heareth not the loud winds when they call;
And moveth altogether, if it move at all.

At length, himself unsettling, he the Pond
Stirred with his Staff, and fixedly did look
Upon the muddy water, which he conn'd,
As if he had been reading in a book:
And now such freedom as I could I took;
And, drawing to his side, to him did say,
"This morning gives us promise of a glorious day."

A gentle answer did the Old Man make,
In courteous speech which forth he slowly drew:
And him with further words I thus bespake,
"What kind of work is that which you pursue?
This is a lonesome place for one like you."
He answer'd me with pleasure and surprize;
And there was, while he spake, a fire about his eyes.

His words came feebly, from a feeble chest,
Yet each in solemn order follow'd each,
With something of a lofty utterance drest;
Choice word, and measured phrase; above the reach
Of ordinary men; a stately speech!
Such as grave Livers do in Scotland use,
Religious men, who give to God and Man their dues.

He told me that he to this pond had come
To gather Leeches, being old and poor:
Employment hazardous and wearisome!
And he had many hardships to endure:
From Pond to Pond he roam'd, from moor to moor,
Housing, with God's good help, by choice or chance:
And in this way he gain'd an honest maintenance.

The Old Man still stood talking by my side;
But now his voice to me was like a stream
Scarce heard; nor word from word could I divide;
And the whole Body of the man did seem
Like one whom I had met with in a dream;
Or like a Man from some far region sent;
To give me human strength, and strong admonishment.

My former thoughts return'd: the fear that kills;
The hope that is unwilling to be fed;
Cold, pain, and labour, and all fleshly ills;
And mighty Poets in their misery dead.
And now, not knowing what the Old Man had said,
My question eagerly did I renew,
"How is it that you live, and what is it you do?"

He with a smile did then his words repeat;
And said, that, gathering Leeches, far and wide
He travelled; stirring thus about his feet
The waters of the Ponds where they abide.
"Once I could meet with them on every side;
But they have dwindled long by slow decay;
Yet still I persevere, and find them where I may."

While he was talking thus, the lonely place,
The Old Man's shape, and speech, all troubled me:
In my mind's eye I seem'd to see him pace
About the weary moors continually,
Wandering about alone and silently.
While I these thoughts within myself pursued,
He, having made a pause, the same discourse renewed.

And soon with this he other matter blended,
Chearfully uttered, with demeanour kind,
But stately in the main; and, when he ended,
I could have laugh'd myself to scorn, to find
In that decrepit Man so firm a mind.
"God," said I, "be my help and stay secure;
I'll think of the Leech-gatherer on the lonely moor."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion, composition and title st. 1

    On 3 October 1800 Dorothy's Grasmere journal describes an old man, bent almost double, born in the army to Scottish parents, who had lost nine of his ten children, had been badly hurt in a cart accident, and gathered leeches, which had grown scarce, and begged. Wordsworth drafted the poem between 3 and 9 May 1802 under the title 'The Leech-Gatherer' and revised it over the following weeks after criticism from Mary and Sara Hutchinson. It appeared in 1807 as 'Resolution and Independence'. The archive prints the 1807 text; later editions altered several lines (e.g. 'their pilgrimage' became 'life's pilgrimage'). The narrative moves the meeting from a Grasmere road to an open moor, and turns a charitable encounter into a crisis in the poet's own mind.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, The Grasmere Journals, ed. Pamela Woof (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1991); 'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989)

  2. Stanza: rhyme royal with an alexandrine st. 1, l. 7

    Wordsworth adapts rhyme royal (the stanza of Chaucer's Troilus) by stretching the final line, as in 'And all the air is fill'd with pleasant noise of waters'. The long line slows each stanza to a halt, a fitting rhythm for a poem about stillness and endurance. The form also links the poem to Chatterton, named in stanza 7, whose 'Excelente Balade of Charitie' (a poem about a poor pilgrim refused alms) may lie behind the choice of stanza, which would then be a tribute to the 'marvellous Boy', though the link is conjectural. The archaic, Spenserian flavour of the stanza suits the poem's later language of grace and admonishment.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Thomas Chatterton, 'An Excelente Balade of Charitie' (written 1770), Literary Ballad Archive text; 'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody)

  3. Over his own sweet voice the Stock-dove broods l. 5

    The stock-dove is a wild pigeon of woods with a soft, repeated coo. In the 1815 Preface Wordsworth cites the line as an instance of the imaginative use of 'broods', which suggests how the bird dwells on and prolongs its note with quiet satisfaction, as if brooding over its own voice as over a nest. The comment shows how carefully he weighed the opening stanza's diction. The bird absorbed in its own music is also a quiet emblem of the self-delighting poet whom the poem will question.

    William Wordsworth, Preface to Poems (1815), in The Prose Works of William Wordsworth, ed. W. J. B. Owen and J. W. Smyser, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1974), vol. 3; Wordsworth's 1815 Preface quoted in the entry for the verb 'coo' (Webster's / WEHD); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brood'

  4. The Hare and her mist ll. 11-14

    'Plashy' means wet, splashy, full of puddles. The hare running 'races in her mirth' is the emblem of unreflecting happiness, recalled in stanza 5 when the speaker compares himself to 'these blissful Creatures'. The glittering mist that accompanies the hare is a precise observation and also a figure of the transient radiance that surrounds joy; it is gone as soon as the hare stops.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'plashy'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  5. I was a Traveller then upon the moor l. 15

    The first two stanzas are in the present ('now the sun is rising'); stanza 3 reveals they are a remembered scene. The 'Traveller' is the poet himself, on foot, a characteristic Wordsworthian figure of the wanderer. The setting is an upland moor; Wordsworth later said the state of mind came to him while crossing Barton Fell near Ullswater, whereas the old man had been met near Dove Cottage. 'My old remembrances went from me wholly' describes a forgetting of sorrows that will not last.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989); 'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody)

  6. From joy to dejection st. 4

    The stanza states a psychological law: 'As high as we have mounted in delight / In our dejection do we sink as low'. Wordsworth was writing in the weeks after Coleridge sent the verse letter that became 'Dejection: An Ode' (April 1802), and the two poems are in dialogue about joy, despondency and the poet's vocation. 'Dim sadness, & blind thoughts I knew not nor could name' describes depression without an object, the 'grief without a pang' of Coleridge's poem.

    S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (Morning Post, 4 October 1802), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (Oxford, 1912); Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  7. a happy Child of earth l. 31

    The skylark and the hare are creatures that live in the present. The comparison is first a boast and then a fear: 'But there may come another day to me, / Solitude, pain of heart, distress, and poverty.' The line of abstract nouns, ending on the alexandrine, sounds like a list of the leech-gatherer's actual conditions, which the poet has not yet met. Compare Burns's ending to 'To a Mouse', where the creature is luckier than the man because 'the present only toucheth' it.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  8. genial faith ... genial good l. 39

    The older sense of 'genial' (Latin genialis, from genius, the inborn spirit, also connected with generation) is 'belonging to one's nature; productive, enlivening'. Milton's Samson says 'So much I feel my genial spirits droop', and Coleridge's 'Dejection' (1802) laments 'My genial spirits fail'. Wordsworth's repetition questions this optimism: the stanza turns to a sharp moral question about a man 'who for himself will take no heed at all', a self-accusation for the poet's carefree life.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genial'; John Milton, Samson Agonistes (1671), l. 594 ('So much I feel my genial spirits droop'), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (Morning Post, 4 October 1802), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (Oxford, 1912)

  9. Chatterton, the marvellous Boy l. 43

    Chatterton's 'Rowley' poems, purportedly by a fifteenth-century priest, were a sensation; he went to London to live by writing and died of arsenic poisoning in August 1770, generally believed to be suicide. For the Romantics he became the type of neglected genius. 'The sleepless Soul that perish'd in its pride' suggests both his restless energy and the pride of a self-made career. Wordsworth's stanza form may recall Chatterton's 'Balade of Charitie'.

    'Thomas Chatterton', Encyclopaedia Britannica; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); 'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody)

  10. Him who walk'd in glory and in joy / Behind his plough ll. 45-46

    Burns was hailed in Henry Mackenzie's review of the Kilmarnock Poems (The Lounger, December 1786) as a 'heaven-taught ploughman', and the image of the poet singing at the plough stuck to him. Wordsworth admired him deeply and later wrote poems at his grave. Here Burns is the second example, after Chatterton, of a poet whose youthful 'glory' ended in misery. The juxtaposition makes the speaker's fear concrete: inspiration does not guarantee a living.

    Henry Mackenzie, review of Burns's Poems, The Lounger 97 (9 December 1786); 'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody); Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989)

  11. By our own spirits are we deified ll. 47-49

    The line can be read in two ways: as a proud claim that poets raise themselves to divinity by their genius, or as a warning that self-deification is the root of the fall ('in the end despondency and madness'). The sequence 'gladness ... despondency and madness' may also glance at William Cowper and Christopher Smart, both poets of famous mental illness, though only Chatterton and Burns are named. Coleridge's crisis of 1802 is part of the context. Many critics have seen here the poem's central anxiety about the self-sufficient imagination.

    Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'; S. T. Coleridge, 'Dejection: An Ode' (Morning Post, 4 October 1802), in E. H. Coleridge, ed., Complete Poetical Works (Oxford, 1912); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  12. peculiar grace ... A leading from above l. 50

    'Peculiar' means particular, singled out (Latin peculiaris, 'one's own'); in Protestant theology 'special' or 'peculiar' grace is God's favour shown to particular persons. Wordsworth hedges ('whether it were ... a something given'), but the poem frames the meeting as providential, and its ending is a prayer. The old man appears 'unawares', i.e. unexpectedly, as if sent. The language of grace and 'leading' prepares the reader to see the old man as a providential messenger, a reading the poem both invites and qualifies, since what he says is plain and practical.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'peculiar'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  13. untoward thoughts l. 54

    The older sense of 'untoward' (literally 'not inclined towards') is 'awkward, perverse, unmanageable', rather than the modern 'unfortunate'. The phrase names the depressive 'fears and fancies' of stanzas 4-7 as something the poet has been struggling with ('had striven'), just before the encounter. The sense survives in phrases such as 'an untoward child', meaning a wayward one. The stanza's long periodic sentence ('Now, whether it were ... Yet it befel ... When ... And ...') delays the sight of the man, so that his sudden appearance ('unawares') has the effect of an apparition.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'untoward'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  14. The stone and the sea-beast st. 10

    In the 1815 Preface Wordsworth analysed this passage as the chief example of how the imagination confers, abstracts and modifies: the stone is given something of the power of life to bring it near the sea-beast, and the sea-beast loses some of its vitality to resemble the stone, and the combined image is then applied to the old man. The boulder 'couch'd on the bald top of an eminence' is a glacial erratic, whose presence prompts wonder 'by what means it could thither come'. The man thus seems half mineral, half animal, poised between life and death, as stanza 11 says outright.

    William Wordsworth, Preface to Poems (1815), in The Prose Works of William Wordsworth, ed. W. J. B. Owen and J. W. Smyser, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1974), vol. 3; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  15. a Sea-beast crawl'd forth ll. 69-70

    The second term of the simile moves from mineral to animal and from moor to shore. The creature that has 'crawl'd forth' from the sea suggests something very ancient, even primordial, at the boundary of land and water; that boundary is echoed in the pond where the leech-gatherer works. Wordsworth's own commentary (1815 Preface) treats the sea-beast as the mediating image between the stone and the man.

    William Wordsworth, Preface to Poems (1815), in The Prose Works of William Wordsworth, ed. W. J. B. Owen and J. W. Smyser, 3 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1974), vol. 3; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  16. not all alive nor dead ... pilgrimage ll. 71-74

    Dorothy's journal recorded an old man 'almost double'. The verse makes his bent posture a figure: head and feet 'coming together in their pilgrimage', as if life's journey had curved back on itself. Later editions read 'life's pilgrimage', making the allegory explicit. The language of pain, 'rage / Of sickness' and 'more than human weight' prepares for the man's role as an image of endurance beyond ordinary strength.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, The Grasmere Journals, ed. Pamela Woof (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1991); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds, The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, 5 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1940-49), notes on the poem

  17. moorish flood l. 81

    Wordsworth's word has nothing to do with the Moors; it is the adjective from 'moor' (marshy upland). 'Flood' in older and poetic usage can mean a stream, pool or lake. The cloud simile that follows ('That heareth not the loud winds when they call; / And moveth altogether, if it move at all') extends the stillness: the old man moves, if at all, as a whole, like a cloud in still air.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'moorish'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flood'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  18. conn'd ... As if he had been reading in a book ll. 87-88

    'Con' (Old English cunnian, 'to try, examine') means to study or learn by heart. The leech-gatherer looking for leeches in the muddy water appears to be reading, and the simile invites a symbolic reading: he reads nature as a text, as the poet would wish to. Critics have often taken the old man as a type of patient interpretation set against the poet's anxious fancy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'con'; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  19. grave Livers ... in Scotland l. 104

    'Livers' are those who live in a certain way: 'grave livers' are people of sober and religious life. The comparison to Scottish speech fits Dorothy's note that the old man was of Scottish parentage, and reflects the English view of Scottish Presbyterians as serious, Bible-reading people with dignified speech. 'Who give to God and Man their dues' recalls Christ's 'Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's' (Matthew 22:21).

    Dorothy Wordsworth, The Grasmere Journals, ed. Pamela Woof (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1991); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'liver'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 22:21

  20. Leech-gathering l. 107

    Medicinal leeches were in heavy demand in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and wild stocks in Britain were depleted, which is why the old man says they 'have dwindled long by slow decay'. Gatherers often waded and let the leeches attach to their bare legs, so the work was 'hazardous and wearisome'. Dorothy's journal records the man's complaint that leeches had become scarce.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, The Grasmere Journals, ed. Pamela Woof (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1991); 'Hirudo medicinalis' (medicinal leech), Wikipedia (history of medical use and decline in Europe); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  21. a Man from some far region sent ll. 115-119

    As the speaker stops hearing his words, the man becomes an apparition 'met with in a dream'. 'Admonishment' means warning or reproof. The figure of a strange messenger whom the speaker does not fully hear has been read as the poem's real crisis: the poet is so absorbed in his own fears that he cannot attend to the man. Lewis Carroll noticed this comedy and parodied it in the White Knight's song in Through the Looking-Glass (1871), where the narrator repeatedly fails to listen to an 'aged aged man'.

    'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983)

  22. mighty Poets in their misery dead l. 123

    The stanza gathers the poem's anxieties into a single line of abstract nouns and then this line about the poets. 'The hope that is unwilling to be fed' is a hope that refuses nourishment, a depression that resists consolation. The repetition of the earlier fear shows that the first meeting did not cure it. The list, without main verbs, imitates the speaker's mind returning helplessly to the same anxieties. The irony is that the man beside him embodies a cure the speaker has not yet taken in.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  23. The repeated question ll. 125-126

    'How is it that you live, and what is it you do?' repeats the question of stanza 14 ('What kind of work is that which you pursue?'). The old man patiently repeats himself 'with a smile'. Critics have discussed whether the poem is aware of its narrator's self-absorption; Carroll's parody fastened on exactly this repetition. The answer, when it comes again, is plain fact: the leeches have dwindled, 'Yet still I persevere'.

    'Resolution and Independence', Wikipedia (composition May 1802 as 'The Leech-Gatherer'; Carroll parody); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'

  24. Yet still I persevere l. 133

    The title's abstract nouns are embodied here: a man who goes on working although his resources are failing. The point of the poem is that the poet's fears ('Solitude, pain of heart, distress, and poverty') are the old man's actual condition, and he bears them with cheerfulness and dignity. The word 'persevere' has a theological ring as well (the 'perseverance of the saints').

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807, Cornell Wordsworth (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1983); Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'persevere'

  25. The ending ll. 143-147

    'I could have laugh'd myself to scorn' is self-mockery at the contrast between the poet's imagined troubles and the old man's 'firm mind'. The prayer, 'God ... be my help and stay secure', echoes the Psalms' language of God as stay and support (e.g. Psalm 18:18, 'the Lord was my stay'). Some readers find the resolution too easy, a moral tacked on; others see the restraint of the ending, which offers only a memory to be held on to, as true to the poem's understated piety.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 18:18; Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven: Yale UP, 1964), on 'Resolution and Independence'; Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989)
