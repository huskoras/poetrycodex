Nature

Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

NatureLiving

Earth has not any thing to show more fair:
Dull would he be of soul who could pass by
A sight so touching in its majesty:
This City now doth, like a garment, wear
The beauty of the morning; silent, bare,
Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples lie
Open unto the fields, and to the sky;
All bright and glittering in the smokeless air.
Never did sun more beautifully steep
In his first splendour, valley, rock, or hill;
Ne’er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep!
The river glideth at his own sweet will:
Dear God! the very houses seem asleep;
And all that mighty heart is lying still!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The great Romantic of nature stops, astonished, before the sleeping city at dawn. London wears the morning ‘like a garment’; even the machine of the metropolis lies still, its mighty heart quiet.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and occasion title; l. 1

    William and Dorothy left London early on 31 July 1802 by the Dover coach to visit Annette Vallon and Caroline, William's daughter, in Calais during the Peace of Amiens. Dorothy's journal describes the city, St Paul's and the river with its little boats as 'a most beautiful sight' from Westminster Bridge, the houses 'not overhung by their cloud of smoke'. The sonnet was published in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807) with the title date 3 September 1802; Dorothy's journal places the crossing on the morning of 31 July, and the Fenwick note says the sonnet was composed on the roof of a coach on the way to France, so the title's September date probably marks when the poem was finished after their return. The opening line is a bold superlative: this city sight outdoes anything the earth can show, an extraordinary claim from the poet of rural nature.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, ed. William Knight (1897), vol. 1, Grasmere Journal; The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (1993), note to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); 'Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802', Wikipedia (dating history)

  2. dull of soul l. 2

    'Dull' here means slow of perception, lacking feeling (OED sense of mental or spiritual dullness), as in Matthew 13:15, 'their ears are dull of hearing'. The inverted syntax ('Dull would he be') gives the line the weight of a proverb. The sight is 'touching in its majesty', a pairing of tenderness and grandeur that runs through the poem. The poem therefore opens by testing the reader's capacity for feeling, a concern central to Wordsworth's Preface to Lyrical Ballads, which complains of a public whose minds are blunted by city life and sensation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dull, adj.'; Bible (KJV), Matthew 13:15; Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1802)

  3. like a garment ll. 4-5

    The Psalmist praises God 'Who coverest thyself with light as with a garment' (Psalm 104:2), and the echo lends the city a sacred radiance. A garment is also something put on and taken off, so the beauty belongs to this hour of morning, not to London itself; the smoke and noise of the working day will soon return. Critics note the ambiguity of 'silent, bare' at the line end: 'bare' attaches both to the city (naked under the garment, exposed) and to the following list of buildings.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 104:2; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  4. Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples l. 6

    The catalogue (asyndeton until the last item) takes in the whole of London: ships of the Thames trade, towers, the domes (above all St Paul's, which Dorothy mentions), the theatres (Drury Lane and Covent Garden were the patent theatres), and 'temples', a classicising word for churches. The heavy stresses slow the line. The list is neutral, without satire; the commercial city that Wordsworth criticises elsewhere ('Getting and spending') appears here only as form and shape, lit by the sun.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, ed. William Knight (1897), vol. 1, Grasmere Journal; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  5. Open unto the fields, and to the sky l. 7

    The line joins city and country, a reconciliation unusual in Wordsworth, whose Prelude Book 7 presents London as a chaotic spectacle. 'Unto' has a biblical ring. The buildings 'lie / Open', which suggests both physical visibility and a kind of vulnerability or receptivity. Editors note that in the early nineteenth century fields still lay close to Westminster and Lambeth, so the line is literal as well as visionary.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Wordsworth, The Prelude (1805), Book 7

  6. smokeless air l. 8

    Coal smoke was a notorious feature of London from the seventeenth century (John Evelyn's Fumifugium of 1661 complained of it). Dorothy's journal observes that the houses were 'not overhung by their cloud of smoke' that morning. The adjective quietly reminds us of what the city normally looks like, so the poem's beauty is a rare and momentary condition. 'Glittering' captures the bright, clear sunlight of a summer dawn.

    Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, ed. William Knight (1897), vol. 1, Grasmere Journal; John Evelyn, Fumifugium (1661); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  7. Petrarchan sonnet and turn ll. 9-14

    Wordsworth revived the Miltonic sonnet in 1802, after Dorothy read Milton's sonnets to him on 21 May 1802 (her journal entry; he later said he already knew them by heart), and wrote many on the Petrarchan model. The octave describes the city's appearance; the sestet compares it to nature ('valley, rock, or hill') and turns inward to feeling ('Ne'er saw I, never felt'). The sestet's run of exclamations raises the emotional pitch. Critics note the poem's strong use of negatives ('not any thing', 'Never', 'Ne'er') to express a superlative.

    The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth, ed. Jared Curtis (1993), note to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, ed. William Knight (1897), vol. 1, Grasmere Journal, entry for 21 May 1802

  8. steep l. 9

    'Steep' as a verb means to soak in liquid (OED). The sun pours its 'first splendour' over valley, rock and hill as though dyeing them. The comparison grants the city the same intensity of light as the natural landscapes Wordsworth knew in the Lake District. The word also faintly echoes 'sleep' later in the sestet, and both suggest immersion in a single, all-embracing condition of light and rest.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'steep, v.1'; Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem

  9. glideth at his own sweet will l. 12

    The river is personified as masculine ('his'), like Old Father Thames. Usually crowded with boats and shaped by commerce, at this hour it seems to move as it pleases. The archaic verb form 'glideth' gives a slow, gliding rhythm. Some readers note that the Thames at Westminster is tidal, so its flow is in fact governed; the line describes how it seems in the morning stillness, a freedom borrowed from the poet's mood.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983)

  10. the very houses seem asleep... that mighty heart ll. 13-14

    'Dear God!' is an exclamation of wonder, almost a prayer. The city becomes a single sleeping body whose heart is the centre of London and of the nation's life. 'Lying still' can mean resting or lifeless, and critics have discussed whether the final line carries a shadow of mortality or simply describes deep calm before the day's activity. The poem's peace depends on the absence of people; its stillness is a moment out of time.

    Stephen Gill, ed., William Wordsworth: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics), notes to the poem; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983); Geoffrey Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (Yale, 1964)
