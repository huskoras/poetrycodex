Nature

Surprised by Joy--Impatient as the Wind

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poetical Works, ed. Knight (1896), vol. VI

Nature

Surprised by joy--impatient as the Wind
I turned to share the transport--Oh! with whom
But Thee, deep buried in the silent tomb,
That spot which no vicissitude can find?
Love, faithful love, recalled thee to my mind--
But how could I forget thee? Through what power,
Even for the least division of an hour,
Have I been so beguiled as to be blind
To my most grievous loss?--That thought's return
Was the worst pang that sorrow ever bore,
Save one, one only, when I stood forlorn,
Knowing my heart's best treasure was no more;
That neither present time, nor years unborn
Could to my sight that heavenly face restore.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Catherine Wordsworth (1808-1812) l. 1

    Wordsworth told Isabella Fenwick that the sonnet 'was in fact suggested by my daughter Catherine long after her death'. Catherine was born on 6 September 1808 and died on 4 June 1812; her brother Thomas died later the same year. Wordsworth placed the poem among his 'Miscellaneous Sonnets' in 1815. The sonnet records a moment, months or years later, when the poet felt a sudden happiness and turned to share it with her before remembering she was dead. C. S. Lewis took the opening phrase as the title of his autobiography Surprised by Joy (1955).

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; Juliet Barker, Wordsworth: A Life (London: Viking, 2000)

  2. impatient as the Wind l. 1

    The simile captures the instinctive speed of the turn: before thought intervenes, he has already turned. The dash after 'Wind' and the run-on into line 2 enact the movement. Wind in Wordsworth is often a figure for spontaneous feeling (compare the 'correspondent breeze' of The Prelude, Book I). Here the impulse that feels like freedom becomes the occasion of fresh grief.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; M. H. Abrams, 'The Correspondent Breeze: A Romantic Metaphor' (1957), repr. in English Romantic Poets: Modern Essays in Criticism, ed. Abrams (1960)

  3. I turned (1815: 'I wished') l. 2

    Knight records that 1815 read 'I wished' and that 'I turned' appears from 1820. The revision makes the moment bodily and instinctive: he actually turns, as if she were in the room, which makes the recognition more painful. The change is a good example of Wordsworth's lifelong revision of his poems. 'Turned' also anticipates 'That thought's return' in line 9: the poem is built on turnings of body and mind, from the impulse to share joy to the return of grief.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants

  4. transport l. 2

    'Transport' meant a state of being 'carried away' by strong emotion, especially delight (OED, from Latin transportare, to carry across). It echoes 'joy' in line 1 and sets up the shock of 'Oh! with whom / But Thee': there is no one else he would share it with. The word belongs to the elevated eighteenth-century vocabulary for strong feeling, and its conventionality makes the collapse into 'Oh! with whom' all the more abrupt.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'transport'

  5. deep buried in the silent tomb l. 3

    Knight records the 1815 reading 'long buried', changed to 'deep buried' in 1820. 'Long' fitted the Fenwick note's 'long after her death'; 'deep' stresses how far beyond reach she is. Catherine was buried in Grasmere churchyard. Note the double space in this text after 'buried', a typesetting accident. The silent tomb contrasts with the impulse to speak and share: the one person he turns to is the one who can no longer hear.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; Juliet Barker, Wordsworth: A Life (London: Viking, 2000)

  6. vicissitude l. 4

    From Latin vicissitudo, change or alternation (OED). The line is grimly double: the grave is beyond the changes of fortune that bring joy and sorrow to the living, but it is also beyond the reach of the very change of feeling that the poem has just experienced. The question mark makes the whole of lines 1-4 a cry: with whom else would I share joy?

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'vicissitude'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants

  7. Love, faithful love l. 5

    The repetition 'Love, faithful love' asserts constancy at the very moment the speaker accuses himself of forgetting. The paradox drives the octave: love recalled her to mind, so how could he have forgotten her, even for an instant? The self-reproach is the subject of the poem as much as the grief. 'Recalled thee to my mind' is literal, since love's impulse brought her to mind, and ironic, since that same impulse shows she had for a moment been absent from it.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; Juliet Barker, Wordsworth: A Life (London: Viking, 2000)

  8. the least division of an hour l. 7

    'Division' is the term for a fraction or subdivision of time (OED). The careful, almost legalistic phrase measures how short the lapse was and how seriously he takes it. Elegies traditionally lament that the dead are forgotten by the world; here the elegist convicts himself. The phrase suggests the hour divided into minutes, making the lapse measurable and so, to the speaker's conscience, inexcusable.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'division'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants

  9. beguiled l. 8

    'Beguile' means to deceive or charm away (OED); it can also mean to while away time pleasantly, a sense that adds bitterness here. Being 'blind / To my most grievous loss' completes the image: joy made him blind, and the return of sight brings the 'worst pang'. The line break after 'blind' throws weight onto 'To my most grievous loss', and the question mark closes the long sentence that began at 'Through what power'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'beguile'

  10. Petrarchan sonnet and mid-line turn l. 9

    The octave rhymes Wind/find/mind/blind and whom/tomb/power/hour; the sestet alternates return/forlorn/unborn with bore/more/restore. Instead of a break between octave and sestet, the question runs on into line 9, and the turn ('That thought's return') comes mid-line, enacting the jolt of remembering. Wordsworth had revived the Miltonic sonnet in 1802, and Milton's practice of carrying sense across the octave is visible here.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; Wordsworth, 'Nuns fret not at their convent's narrow room' and other Miscellaneous Sonnets, in Knight, ed., vol. 4

  11. the worst pang, save one ll. 10-12

    The phrase 'Save one, one only' insists on exactness, as if grading pains. The original grief was learning that his 'heart's best treasure was no more'; Catherine died suddenly, aged three. The present pang, renewing that knowledge, is next worst. 'Forlorn' means abandoned, desolate (OED). The grammar insists on ranking griefs, a precision whose restraint many readers find more moving than open lament.

    Juliet Barker, Wordsworth: A Life (London: Viking, 2000); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants; Oxford English Dictionary, 'forlorn'

  12. my heart's best treasure l. 12

    The sermon contrasts treasure on earth, which moth and rust corrupt, with treasure in heaven. Wordsworth's phrase fits both: his treasure was on earth and is now lost, but the last line calls her face 'heavenly'. The quiet biblical echo hints at Christian consolation without stating it; the sonnet ends on the fact of loss. 'Best' also implies other treasures, his wife and surviving children, without lessening the singularity of this loss.

    Bible, King James Version, Matthew 6:19-21; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants

  13. that heavenly face ll. 13-14

    'Neither present time, nor years unborn' covers all time: the face is gone for ever from sight, if not from memory. 'Heavenly' means beautiful and also now in heaven. The final line's inverted syntax (object before verb) places 'restore' last, the word for what cannot happen. Readers compare the earlier poem on Catherine, 'Characteristics of a Child three Years Old' (1811), which Knight cites.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 6 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and variants
