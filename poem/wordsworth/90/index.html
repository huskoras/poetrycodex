The Solitary Reaper

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

NatureArts & Sciences

Behold her, single in the field,
Yon solitary Highland Lass!
Reaping and singing by herself;
Stop here, or gently pass!
Alone she cuts, and binds the grain,
And sings a melancholy strain;
O listen! for the Vale profound
Is overflowing with the sound.

No Nightingale did ever chaunt
So sweetly to reposing bands
Of Travellers in some shady haunt,
Among Arabian Sands:
No sweeter voice was ever heard
In spring-time from the Cuckoo-bird,
Breaking the silence of the seas
Among the farthest Hebrides.

Will no one tell me what she sings?
Perhaps the plaintive numbers flow
For old, unhappy, far-off things,
And battles long ago:
Or is it some more humble lay,
Familiar matter of today?
Some natural sorrow, loss, or pain,
That has been, and may be again!

Whate'er the theme, the Maiden sung
As if her song could have no ending;
I saw her singing at her work,
And o'er the sickle bending;
I listen'd till I had my fill;
And, as I mounted up the hill,
The music in my heart I bore,
Long after it was heard no more.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Solitary Reaper: occasion and source st. 1

    William and Dorothy Wordsworth toured the Highlands in August and September 1803; Dorothy's Recollections, under 13 September, note harvest reapers and say it is not uncommon in the lonelier parts of the Highlands to see a single person so employed. Wordsworth's note says the poem was suggested by a sentence in a manuscript tour of Scotland by a friend (Thomas Wilkinson), and that the last line was taken from it verbatim. The poem was thus written after the event, probably in 1805, from memory and a borrowed sentence. It was published in 1807 and later classed among the 'Poems of the Imagination'. The text here is a revised one; the 1807 version read 'More welcome notes to weary bands' and 'A voice so thrilling ne'er was heard'.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Thomas Wilkinson, Tours to the British Mountains (1824), as quoted by Knight; Dorothy Wordsworth, Recollections of a Tour Made in Scotland, 13 Sept. 1803 (as quoted by Knight)

  2. single l. 1

    The opening stanza piles up near-synonyms: 'single', 'solitary', 'by herself', 'Alone'. The repetition is not padding but emphasis, making solitude the condition of the song's power. Wilkinson's sentence had 'a female who was reaping alone'. 'Behold' and 'Yon' are imperative and demonstrative, pointing as if the scene were present before the reader; the poem begins in the present tense and only in the last stanza reveals that it is a memory.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'single, adj.'

  3. Highland Lass l. 2

    The Highlands in 1803 were still largely Gaelic-speaking, and Wilkinson specifies that the reaper 'sung in Erse', the older English name for Scottish Gaelic. The speaker cannot understand the words, which is why he must ask 'Will no one tell me what she sings?' Wordsworth wrote several poems on Highland women on this tour, including 'To a Highland Girl'. The reaper is seen and heard but never spoken to: an encounter in which language barriers make the song pure sound and feeling.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lass'; Thomas Wilkinson, Tours to the British Mountains (1824), as quoted by Knight

  4. stanza and metre st. 1, l. 4

    Each stanza opens with a quatrain (rhyming abab, though in the first and last stanza only the even lines rhyme) followed by two couplets. The fourth line, here 'Stop here, or gently pass!', is a trimeter in every stanza ('Among Arabian Sands', 'And battles long ago', 'And o'er the sickle bending'), creating a slight pause at the stanza's midpoint before the couplets flow on. The imperative to stop or 'gently pass' asks the reader not to break the solitude; it echoes the conventions of inscription poems, which address the passer-by.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; E. de Selincourt and Helen Darbishire, eds., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth (Oxford, 1940-49), notes to the poem

  5. strain l. 6

    'Strain' in this sense means a tune or a piece of music, often with a suggestion of feeling (OED). The adjective comes from Wilkinson's prose. The following lines extend the sound into space: the 'Vale profound', a deep valley (Latinate inversion of noun and adjective, from Latin profundus), 'Is overflowing with the sound', as if the song were water filling a vessel. The image anticipates the ending, where the music is carried in the heart after it ceases.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strain, n.2'; Thomas Wilkinson, Tours to the British Mountains (1824), as quoted by Knight

  6. the Nightingale among Arabian Sands ll. 9-12

    'Chaunt' is an older spelling of 'chant', already archaic and poetic in 1807. The nightingale is the conventional bird of poetry and of night; Wordsworth sets it in an exotic, hot, Oriental setting, travellers in caravans resting in 'some shady haunt'. In 1807 the line read 'More welcome notes to weary bands'; the reading here is a later revision. The two comparisons of this stanza place the Highland song between extremes, south and north, desert and sea, heat and cold, as if it summed up the whole world.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chant / chaunt'

  7. the Cuckoo-bird ll. 13-16

    The cuckoo is the harbinger of spring in English poetry, and Wordsworth had written 'To the Cuckoo' (1802), where the bird is a voice rather than a visible creature, 'a wandering Voice'. Here too the bird is pure voice. Its call is welcome because it ends the winter silence of the remote isles. In 1807 the line read 'A voice so thrilling ne'er was heard'; the reading here is a later revision.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; Wordsworth, 'To the Cuckoo', Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

  8. 'Breaking the silence of the seas' l. 15

    In 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' (Lyrical Ballads, 1798), Part II, the ship is becalmed and the crew speak merely to break the dreadful stillness. Wordsworth's line reverses the mood: in his poem the silence is broken by a welcome natural voice. Whether or not the echo is deliberate, it shows the two poets' shared vocabulary. The line also makes sound the subject of a visual landscape, a typical Wordsworthian fusion of the senses.

    William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem; S. T. Coleridge, 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' (1798), Part II

  9. the farthest Hebrides l. 16

    The Hebrides were the heartland of Gaelic speech, so the comparison links the reaper's song with its linguistic home. They had literary associations too: Milton's 'Lycidas' imagines the drowned King's body hurled 'beyond the stormy Hebrides' (l. 156), and Johnson and Boswell had published accounts of their Hebridean tour (1775, 1785). Wordsworth himself never visited the outer isles; the place is imagined as the edge of the known world, balancing the 'Arabian Sands'.

    Milton, 'Lycidas' l. 156 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  10. 'Will no one tell me what she sings?' l. 17

    The question opens the third stanza and changes the poem from description to speculation. Critics have taken the speaker's ignorance in different ways: as a sign that music communicates feeling beyond words; as an act of imaginative sympathy in which the poet supplies what he cannot understand; or as an appropriation of a Gaelic woman's voice by an English observer who never speaks to her. The speaker's two guesses (heroic past or ordinary present grief) map the two kinds of poetry, epic and lyrical ballad.

    Geoffrey H. Hartman, Wordsworth's Poetry 1787-1814 (New Haven, 1964); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  11. plaintive numbers ll. 18-20

    'Numbers' for verse, metrical lines counted out, is a neoclassical poetic idiom (OED). The line 'For old, unhappy, far-off things, / And battles long ago' is one of Wordsworth's most quoted: it suggests the tradition of Gaelic heroic song, which the controversy over Macpherson's Ossian (1760s) had made famous throughout Europe. Some readers also hear the recent Highland past, the Jacobite defeat at Culloden (1746) and the Clearances; Wordsworth does not specify, and the vagueness is the point.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'number, n. (numbers = verses)'; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  12. 'some more humble lay' ll. 21-24

    'Lay' is an old word for a short song or narrative poem. The choice between heroic past and 'Familiar matter of today' echoes the programme of Lyrical Ballads, whose Preface prefers incidents from common life; Wordsworth's sympathy plainly inclines toward the 'natural sorrow' that recurs in every life. The final line of the stanza, 'That has been, and may be again', gives the private grief a cyclical permanence like the seasons of harvest.

    Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800; revised 1802); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lay, n.4'

  13. the shift to the past tense ll. 27-30

    The poem began 'Behold her' and 'O listen!', as if happening now; it ends 'I saw her singing' and 'I listen'd till I had my fill'. The shift reveals the poem's real subject, the persistence of an experience in memory, in keeping with Wordsworth's claim in the 1800 Preface that poetry takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity. 'As if her song could have no ending' contrasts with the fact that the hearer must move on ('as I mounted up the hill'), and the poem resolves the contrast by transferring the endlessness from the song to the listener's heart.

    Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800; revised 1802); William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem

  14. the last line, from Wilkinson ll. 31-32

    Wordsworth acknowledged the debt in his note of 1807. Wilkinson, a Quaker friend who lived near Yanwath on the Eamont, had toured Scotland in 1787 and lent his manuscript to the Wordsworths; it was published only in 1824. The borrowing shows how Wordsworth turned another traveller's sentence into his own act of memory: the music 'in my heart I bore' becomes the poem itself, which carries the song to readers who never heard it.

    Thomas Wilkinson, Tours to the British Mountains (1824), as quoted by Knight; William Knight, ed., The Poetical Works of William Wordsworth, vol. 2 (1896), headnote, Fenwick note and notes to the poem
