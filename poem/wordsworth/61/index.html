Nature

It is a beauteous Evening, calm and free

by William Wordsworth 1770–1850

From Poems, in Two Volumes (1807)

NatureReligion

It is a beauteous Evening, calm and free;
The holy time is quiet as a Nun
Breathless with adoration; the broad sun
Is sinking down in its tranquillity;
The gentleness of heaven is on the Sea:
Listen! the mighty Being is awake
And doth with his eternal motion make
A sound like thunder--everlastingly.
Dear Child! dear Girl! that walkest with me here,
If thou appear'st untouch'd by solemn thought,
Thy nature is not therefore less divine:
Thou liest in Abraham's bosom all the year;
And worshipp'st at the Temple's inner shrine,
God being with thee when we know it not.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Calais, August 1802 l. 1

    During the brief Peace of Amiens (1802) William and Dorothy Wordsworth crossed to Calais to meet Annette Vallon, whom Wordsworth had loved in France in 1791-92, and their daughter Caroline, born in December 1792, whom he had never seen. The aim was partly to settle matters before his marriage to Mary Hutchinson in October 1802. Dorothy's journal records evening walks by the sea during the month they spent there. The sonnet was published in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807). The Victorian editors who printed it did not know the child's identity; the Vallon affair became public only through G. M. Harper (1916) and Émile Legouis (1922).

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes; Dorothy Wordsworth, Journals, entries for August 1802 (Calais), ed. William Knight (1897); Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford, 1989), on the Calais visit of 1802; 'It is a beauteous evening, calm and free', Wikipedia (dating and context summary)

  2. Beauteous l. 1

    'Beauteous' was by Wordsworth's day a literary word, used in hymns and older poetry; together with 'calm and free' it makes the opening line a slow, stately pentameter of evening peace. 'Free' suggests both openness of space and freedom from care or constraint, and perhaps the freedom of peacetime travel. The comma-separated adjectives create an unhurried rhythm, and the line contains no strong verb, so that the poem opens in pure stillness before the motion of the sea is heard in line 6.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'beauteous'; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes

  3. Evening as a nun at prayer ll. 2-3

    The simile casts the whole evening as a religious act. 'The holy time' recalls the canonical hours of prayer, particularly vespers at sunset, and the nun, silent and 'breathless' in adoration, embodies a stillness so intense that even breath is suspended. In a Protestant poet the Catholic figure is notable, and fitting to French Catholic surroundings. The enjambment of 'Nun / Breathless' briefly holds the breath across the line ending.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'adoration'

  4. 'the mighty Being' ll. 6-8

    The 'mighty Being' whose 'eternal motion' makes 'a sound like thunder' can be read as the sea, the natural power before them, or as God, whose presence is heard through it. The capital B and the solemn 'doth' push toward the divine; the immediate context ('the Sea') suggests the ocean. Most commentators take the ambiguity as deliberate: the sea is the visible sign of the eternal. 'Thunder' in the Bible is often the voice of God (Psalm 29:3, 'the God of glory thundereth: the LORD is upon many waters'), which supports the reading that the sea's sound is a divine voice.

    The Bible (Authorized King James Version), Psalm 29:3; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes; Representative Poetry Online, 'It is a Beauteous Evening, Calm and Free', notes

  5. 'everlastingly' and the volta l. 8

    The sonnet is Petrarchan in shape (octave abbaacca, sestet defdfe in its rhyme pattern), and the octave ends with the dash and the single drawn-out word 'everlastingly', whose length imitates the endless sound of the sea. At line 9 the voice turns from the scene to the companion: 'Dear Child! dear Girl!' The movement from cosmic to intimate is the sonnet's central structure.

    Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes; Representative Poetry Online, 'It is a Beauteous Evening, Calm and Free', notes

  6. 'Dear Child! dear Girl!': Caroline l. 9

    The child is Caroline Wordsworth (Anne-Caroline), daughter of Wordsworth and Annette Vallon. The doubled address, both tender and slightly formal, has been read as the speech of a father meeting a daughter he hardly knows. The poem neither names her nor explains the relation, and for a long time readers could not identify her. Some biographers find a note of self-justification in the sonnet's assurance that the girl's nature is divine even if she seems unmoved, a way of blessing a child he could not raise.

    Stephen Gill, William Wordsworth: A Life (Oxford, 1989), on the Calais visit of 1802; 'It is a beauteous evening, calm and free', Wikipedia (dating and context summary); Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes

  7. The unthinking child ll. 10-11

    This is a compact statement of a belief Wordsworth develops in the 'Intimations' Ode (begun 1802, the same year): the young child lives closer to God than the reflective adult, even without knowing it. The adult must consciously feel 'solemn thought'; the child simply is in communion. 'Therefore' answers an implied objection that piety requires awareness. The sonnet thus anticipates the Ode's 'best Philosopher', the child who reads the eternal deep without knowing it (stanza 8).

    William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality' (1807), st. 8; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes

  8. Abraham's bosom l. 12

    In Jesus' parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31), the poor man dies and is carried by angels into Abraham's bosom, the place of comfort for the righteous. The phrase came to mean heaven or the bliss of the faithful. Wordsworth's twist is that the child lies there 'all the year', in this life and continuously, not after death: her ordinary existence is already blessed. RPO's annotation cross-refers the line to Luke 16:22.

    The Bible (Authorized King James Version), Luke 16:22; Representative Poetry Online, 'It is a Beauteous Evening, Calm and Free', notes; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes

  9. The Temple's inner shrine l. 13

    The 'inner shrine' alludes to the Holy of Holies, the innermost room of Solomon's Temple where the Ark of the Covenant stood (1 Kings 6:16-19; Hebrews 9:3-7). Only the high priest entered it, once a year. The child, by contrast, worships there constantly and without effort, an image of the most intimate access to God. The line parallels the previous one: Abraham's bosom (rest) and the inner shrine (worship).

    The Bible (Authorized King James Version), 1 Kings 6:16-19; Hebrews 9:3-7; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes

  10. 'God being with thee when we know it not' l. 14

    The closing line, in an absolute construction, may echo Genesis 28:16, where Jacob wakes from his dream of the ladder and realises God was present unnoticed. Here the unknowing is the adults' ('we know it not'): God is with the child even when the observers cannot perceive it, and perhaps when the child herself is unaware. The quiet monosyllables end the sonnet with the stillness of the opening.

    The Bible (Authorized King James Version), Genesis 28:16; Jared Curtis, ed., Poems, in Two Volumes, and Other Poems, 1800-1807 (Cornell Wordsworth, 1983), text and notes
