Vatan Şarkısı

by Namık Kemal 1840–1888

History & Politics

Âmâlimiz efkârımız ikbâl-i vatandır
Serhadimize kal’a bizim hâk-i bedendir
Osmanlılarız ziynetimiz kanlı kefendir
Gavgâda şehâdetle bütün kâm alırız biz
Osmanlılarız, cân veririz, nâm alırız biz.

Kan ile kılıçdır görünen bayrağımızda
Cân korkusu gezmez ovamızda, dağımızda
Her gûşede bir şîr yatar toprağımızda
Gavgâda şehâdetle bütün kâm alırız biz
Osmanlılarız, cân veririz, nâm alırız biz.

Osmanlı adı her duyana lerze-resândır;
Ecdâdımızın heybeti ma'rûf-ı cihândır
Fıtrat değişir sanma! Bu kan yine o kandır
Gavgâda şehâdetle bütün kâm alırız biz
Osmanlılarız, cân veririz, nâm alırız biz.

Top patlasın, âteşleri etrâfa saçılsın
Cennet kapısı cân veren ihvâna açılsın
Dünyâda ne bulduk ki ölümden de kaçılsın
Gavgâda şehâdetle bütün kâm alırız biz
Osmanlılarız, cân veririz, nâm alırız biz.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Vatan Şarkısı: the song and its occasion st. 1, l. 1

    Akün's encyclopedia article mentions the well-known 'Vatan' song of the play Vatan yahut Silistre, first staged at the Gedikpaşa theatre on 1 April 1873. The performance set off demonstrations ('Yaşasın vatan! Yaşasın Kemal!'), and within days İbret was closed and Kemal was exiled to Famagusta, though Akün argues the exile was not directly caused by the play. Gibb printed a verse translation of this song in 1882 (refrain: 'We're Ottomans! our lives we give, our gain is glory bright'), saying it was 'composed on the occasion of the last Russian attack on Turkey', that is, the war of 1877–78, and attributing it to a 'Ref'et Beg'. The sources thus disagree on author and occasion; the attribution to Kemal is the accepted one in Turkish scholarship.

    Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  2. Form: a five-line song stanza with a two-line refrain each stanza, ll. 4–5

    The four stanzas rhyme aaaBB: three lines on a new rhyme (vatandır / bedendir / kefendir; bayrağımızda / dağımızda / toprağımızda; and so on) and then the same couplet, the nakarat or refrain, ending 'alırız biz'. The şarkı was the Ottoman form written to be set to music, typically in four-line stanzas with a repeated line; here the stanza is enlarged with a two-line chorus, as in a European march. The plural 'biz' (we) throughout makes it a song for a group of volunteers or soldiers, not a lover's private complaint, which is the usual voice of the şarkı.

    Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Şarkı' (literature), TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 38 (2010), pp. 357–358; Tevfik Rüştü Topuzoğlu, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 17 (1998), pp. 302–304

  3. Metre: hezec-i ahreb st. 1, l. 1

    Scanned in aruz, the first line runs Â-mâ-li / mi-zef-kâ-rı / mı-zik-bâ-li / ve-tan-dır, with the final consonants of 'âmâlimiz' and 'efkârımız' carried over (vasl) onto the next vowel. The pattern is mef'ûlü mefâîlü mefâîlü feûlün, the hezec-i ahreb. Topuzoğlu's article on hezec notes that this variety was the metre most often used for the şarkı and for the müstezat. Its galloping opening of two long syllables gives the song its march-like energy; compare the same metre in the rain of snow in Cenab's 'Elhân-ı Şitâ' (ll. 39–50).

    Tevfik Rüştü Topuzoğlu, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 17 (1998), pp. 302–304; Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  4. âmâl, efkâr, ikbâl st. 1, l. 1

    'Âmâl' is the Arabic broken plural of emel, hope or ambition; 'efkâr' is the plural of fikr, thought, a word Kemal's generation used for public opinion (as in the newspaper Tasvîr-i Efkâr); 'ikbâl' is prosperity or rising fortune. The line declares that all private hopes and thoughts are absorbed into the good of the vatan. Gibb remarked that Kemal raised 'watan' to the sense of French patrie; the song shows that sense in its most popular form.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'emel'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'ikbal'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907)

  5. Bodies as the frontier fortress st. 1, l. 2

    'Serhad' (Persian sar-ḥadd) is the frontier, the march where Ottoman ghazi warfare had always been fought; 'kal'a' (Arabic qalʿa) is a fortress; 'hâk-i beden' is the earth or dust of the body. The line fuses two ideas Kemal used repeatedly: that men's bodies are made of the country's soil and that their death on the frontier becomes the frontier's wall. The play Vatan yahut Silistre is set at the defence of the Danube fortress of Silistre in the Crimean War, so the image would have had a concrete referent for its first audience.

    Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  6. 'Our ornament is a bloody shroud' st. 1, l. 3

    'Ziynet' (Arabic zīna) is adornment, finery; 'kefen' (Arabic kafan) is the white cloth in which the dead are wrapped. A martyr (şehîd) in Islamic law is buried in the clothes he died in, unwashed, so the 'bloody shroud' is the martyr's mark of honour. Gibb's translation renders it 'a gory shroud our robe of honour grand', bringing out the idea of the robe of honour (hil'at) given by a sultan. The same kanlı kefen appears in Kemal's elegy 'Vâveylâ', where the fatherland itself wears it.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'kefen'; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  7. Refrain: 'we give our lives, we win a name' st. 1, ll. 4–5 (refrain)

    'In battle, through martyrdom (şehâdet), we obtain all our desire (kâm); we are Ottomans: we give our life, we take a name.' Two value systems meet: the Islamic reward of martyrdom, which opens paradise (st. 4), and the heroic, almost Homeric exchange of life for lasting fame (nâm). The self-name 'Osmanlılarız' is equally telling: it names a political community, not Turks or Muslims as such, in keeping with the Young Ottoman ideal of Ottomanism. Gibb's refrain, 'We're Ottomans! our lives we give, our gain is glory bright', keeps this exchange.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'şehadet'

  8. The banner: blood and sword st. 2, l. 1

    The line describes the red Ottoman flag, read as blood, with a sword on it. Gibb's translation reads 'A sabre on a blood-red field', and his note to it explains that the banner of the Janissaries displayed the Zû-'l-Fiqâr, the double-pointed sword of the caliph Ali. Whether Kemal meant a historical banner or the colour of the national flag, the line turns heraldry into a promise of sacrifice. It also shows the song's theatrical character, a tableau the audience can see.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes

  9. A lion in every corner st. 2, l. 3

    'Gûşe' (Persian gōsha) is a corner or nook; 'şîr' (Persian shīr) is the lion. The lion is Kemal's favourite emblem of the people: in the Hürriyet Kasidesi it is chained and then wounded and asleep. Here the verb 'yatar' (lies) is ambiguous: the lion lies in wait, but perhaps lies buried, since 'toprağımızda' (in our soil) suggests the graves of the fallen. Either way the 'fear for life' of the previous line has no place in such a land.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141

  10. fıtrat: inborn nature st. 3, l. 3

    'Fıtrat' (Arabic fiṭra) is the innate nature with which God creates a being. The line answers a fear felt across the late Ottoman world, that the empire's defeats showed a decline of the race. Kemal insists on continuity with the 'heybet' (awe-inspiring majesty) of the ancestors (ecdâd) known to the world. 'Lerze-resân' in the stanza's first line (Persian larza-rasān, 'bringing trembling') makes the Ottoman name itself a cause of fear. Gibb translates the line 'Think not that nature changeth — nay, this blood is yon blood still.'

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'lerze'

  11. The gate of paradise for martyrs st. 4, ll. 1–3

    The last stanza joins modern artillery (top, cannon) to the Qur'anic promise that those killed in God's way are not dead but living with their Lord (Qur'an 3:169). 'İhvân' (Arabic ikhwān, plural of akh) means brothers, here comrades in arms. The third line is a classical contempt of the world (dünya), a commonplace of Ottoman moralising verse, turned into a reason for courage. The movement from banner to blood to cannon to paradise gives the song a dramatic climax suitable for the stage.

    Qur'an 3:169; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'şehadet'

  12. Variant text: Gibb's 1882 version refrain; stanza order

    Gibb's translation (Ottoman Poems, 1882) has the stanzas in the order 1, 3, 2, 4 of the archive text, and its fourth line in every stanza is a battle cry: '"God is Most Great!" we shout in rush and charge on field of fight', where the archive has 'Gavgâda şehâdetle bütün kâm alırız biz'. Gibb also gives the poet as 'Ref'et Beg' and says the song was composed on the occasion of the last Russian attack on Turkey (the war of 1877–78), on information from H. A. Homes of New York. A song sung in theatres, barracks and schools naturally changes as it travels; the archive text is the one printed by Turkish Wikisource. Students should cite the version they quote.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text and notes; Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378
