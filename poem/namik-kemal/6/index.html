Vaveyla

by Namık Kemal 1840–1888

History & Politics

I

Feminin rengi aksedip tenine
Yeni açmış güle misal olmuş
İn'itafiyle bak! ne al olmuş!
Serv-i simin safalı gerdenine
Bu letafetle ol nihal-i revan
Giriyor göz yumunca rü'yama
Benziyor aynı, kendi hülyama
Bu tasavvur dokundu sevdama
Ah böyle gezer mi hiç canan?
Gül değil arkasında kanlı kefen
Sen misin sen misin ey garib vatan!

II

Bu güzellikte hiç bu çağında
Yakışır mıydı boynuna o kefen?
Cisminin her mesamı yare iken
Tuttun evladını kucağında
Sen gider isen bizi kalır sanma
Şühedan oldu mevt ile handan
Sağ kalanlar durur mu hiç giryan?
Tende yaştan ziyadedir al kan
Söyleyen söylesin sen aldanma!
Sen gidersen bütün helak oluruz
Koynuna can atar da hak oluruz

III

Git vatan! Kabe'de siyaha bürün
Bir kolun Ravza-i Nebi'ye uzat
Birini Kerbela'da Meşhed'e at
Kainatta o hey'etinle görün!
Bu temaşaya Hak da aşık olur
Göze bir alem eyliyor izhar
Ki cihanda büyük letafeti var
O letafet olunsa ger inkar
Mezhebimce demek muvafık olur
Aç vatan göğsünü İlah'ına aç!
Şühedanı çıkar da ortaya saç!

IV

De ki Yâ Râb bu Hüseyn'indir
Şu mubârek Habîb-i zî-şânın
Şu kefensiz yatan şehîdânın
Kimi Bedr-in kimi Hüneyn'indir
Tazelensin mi kanlı yâreleri?
Mey dökülsün mü kabr-i eshâba?
Yakışır mı sanem bu mihrâba?
Haç mı konsun bedel şu mîzâba?
Dininin kalmasın mı bir eseri?
Adem evlâdı bir takım cânî
Senden alsın mı sâr-ı şeytânî?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Vâveylâ: title, date and occasion title

    'Vâveylâ' (Arabic wā-waylā, 'O woe!') is the wailing cry raised over a death; in modern Turkish it simply means a scream. Akün's article ties the poem to the disasters of the 1877–78 war (Doksanüç Harbi, named from the Rumi year 1293), which also produced other famous patriotic poems; Nazlı states that Kemal wrote it on Midilli, where he was sent under Abdülhamid II and stayed some five years, together with 'Murabba' and 'Vatan Mersiyesi'. His son Ali Ekrem Bolayır called it a patriotic poem no other Turkish poet, Abdülhak Hâmid included, had equalled. The title tells the reader at once that this is an elegy (mersiye) for a living, wounded country.

    Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'vaveyla'

  2. Form: four eleven-line stanzas; metre hafîf st. I, l. 1

    Each of the four sections has eleven lines with the same rhyme scheme: an enclosed quatrain (tenine / olmuş / olmuş / gerdenine), a five-line unit (revan / rü'yama / hülyama / sevdama / canan) and a closing couplet that carries the climax ('kefen / vatan'). This is not a classical fixed form; it is the kind of free stanza Tanzimat poets built from old elements under French influence. The metre is the hafîf: Fe-mi-nin ren / gi ak-se-dip / te-ni-ne scans feilâtün mefâilün feilün (. . − − / . − . − / . . −), the first foot sometimes fâilâtün. The scheme and scansion given here are from the text itself.

    Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  3. fem, in'itâf: the opening portrait st. I, ll. 1–3

    'Fem' (Arabic fam) is the mouth, in divan poetry always tiny and red. The opening lines draw a classical portrait: the red of the mouth reflected on the skin makes it like a rose newly opened (yeni açmış gül); with her bending (in'itâf, Arabic inʿiṭāf, 'inclining') her silver-white cypress neck has turned red. Nazlı notes that Ertaylan judged such images wholly 'Eastern'. The reader is meant to take the figure for the poet's beloved until the stanza's last line reveals the 'rose' as a bloody shroud and the beloved as the fatherland.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  4. serv-i sîmîn: the silver cypress st. I, l. 4

    In Ottoman lyric the beloved's stature (kad, kâmet) is a cypress (serv) and her gait a walking sapling (nihâl-i revân, l. 5); 'sîmîn' (Persian sīmīn, 'of silver') gives her whiteness. Andrews explains how these fixed images form a shared code of the beloved's body that poets recombine rather than invent. Kemal deploys the code so that its first readers would recognise a love poem, and then breaks it. The neck (gerden) prepares the shroud hung round it (boynuna o kefen) in stanza II.

    Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  5. The turn: 'Is it you, is it you, O poor fatherland?' st. I, ll. 10–11

    'That is no rose behind you but a blood-stained shroud: is it you, is it you, O forlorn fatherland?' The couplet is a sudden recognition, a dramatic device Kemal knew from the stage. 'Garîb' (Arabic gharīb) means a stranger, someone far from home, and so wretched and pitiable: the country itself is an exile. Nazlı reads the section as a portrait of vatan as a woman, and stanza II as moving her to the higher image of a mother. Compare the 'faithless beloved' vatan of the Hürriyet Kasidesi.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141

  6. The shroud round her neck st. II, ll. 1–2

    The question turns the bridal imagery of the first stanza into funeral imagery: a shroud (kefen, Arabic kafan) worn where a necklace should be. In Kemal's 'Vatan Şarkısı' the bloody shroud is the soldier's 'ornament'; here it is the country's. The pathos depends on youth (bu çağında), since the fatherland is imagined as young and beautiful, not as an old declining empire.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'kefen'

  7. Vatan as a mother st. II, ll. 3–4

    'Mesâm' (Arabic masāmm, pores of the skin) are each a wound (yâre), yet she holds her children (evlâd, Arabic awlād) in her lap. Nazlı identifies the shift here from beloved to mother, a move that lifts the image to something sacred and self-sacrificing. The image of the motherland (anavatan) became central in later Turkish patriotic writing; Kemal's poem is an early, vivid instance.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mesame'

  8. Martyrs laughing in death st. II, l. 6

    'Şühedâ' (Arabic shuhadāʾ) is the plural of şehîd, martyr; the Persian plural -ân is added again (şühedân) for the rhyme. 'Handân' (Persian khandān) is laughing, 'giryân' (Persian giryān) weeping, a classical antithesis. The martyrs are joyful because death has brought them to paradise; the living, by contrast, are tempted to stand and weep. 'There is more red blood in the body than tears' (l. 8): the survivors still have blood to give.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'şüheda'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'handan'; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  9. 'We throw our lives into your bosom and become earth' st. II, ll. 10–11

    'Hâk' (Persian khāk) is earth or dust (the archive spells it 'hak'). To become dust is to die, but also, as Nazlı reads it, a union (vuslat) rather than a separation: the dead mingle with the soil of the vatan and lie in her bosom. The thought matches the Hürriyet Kasidesi, where the body is kneaded from the soil of the fatherland. The language of love, 'koynuna can atmak', to throw oneself into a beloved's arms, keeps the erotic register of stanza I.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141

  10. 'Wrap yourself in black at the Kaaba' st. III, l. 1

    The Kaaba in Mecca, the House of God towards which Muslims pray, is draped in a black covering (kisve). Ünal's encyclopedia article explains that the white silk cover introduced under the caliph al-Maʾmūn was later turned black, in the time of the Abbasid caliph al-Nāṣir li-Dīn Allāh, and that the black has been kept since. Kemal turns this ritual blackness into mourning dress: the fatherland is to stand at the centre of the Islamic world wearing black. Nazlı argues that the holy places named in this stanza mark the bounds of a spiritual as well as a geographical homeland.

    Sadettin Ünal, 'Kâbe' (part 1), TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 24 (2001), pp. 14–21; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  11. Ravza-i Nebî and Kerbelâ st. III, ll. 2–3

    'Ravza-i Nebî' (the Prophet's Garden) is the area round the tomb of the Prophet Muhammad in the mosque at Medina. The 'Meşhed' at Kerbelâ is the shrine of Hüseyin, the Prophet's grandson, killed there with his family by Umayyad forces on 10 Muharram 61 (10 October 680), the archetypal martyrdom of Islamic history. The personified fatherland stretches out her arms between the grave of the Prophet and the grave of the martyr, a posture of supplication. Both places lay within the Ottoman Empire in 1878.

    Mustafa Öz, 'Kerbelâ' (part 1), TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 25 (2022), pp. 271–272; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  12. 'Say: O Lord, this is your Hüseyin's' st. IV, ll. 1–4

    Kemal asks the vatan to present the unshrouded martyrs (kefensiz yatan şehîdân) to God as kin of the holiest dead. 'Habîb-i zî-şân' (the glorious Beloved) is the Prophet, called 'Habîbullah', God's beloved. Badr (624) was the Muslims' first victory over the Meccans, and Hunayn (630) a battle won after near-defeat; both are named in the Qur'an. Nazlı, following İsmet Özel, reads Badr as the struggle to win existence and Hunayn as the struggle to keep it. By equating the soldiers of 1877–78 with these martyrs Kemal gives the war a sacred dimension.

    Qur'an 3:123; Qur'an 9:25; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Mustafa Öz, 'Kerbelâ' (part 1), TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 25 (2022), pp. 271–272

  13. The cross instead of the golden spout st. IV, ll. 5–9

    The mîzâb (Arabic mīzāb, 'spout') is the golden rain-spout on the Kaaba's roof, the 'Mîzâb-ı Rahmet' (spout of mercy); Ünal records that in Muharram 1021 (March 1612), during repairs under Ahmed I, the worn spout was replaced by a new one decorated with gold over a silver plating. 'Eshâb' are the Companions of the Prophet; 'sanem' (Arabic ṣanam) an idol; 'mihrâb' the prayer niche. Each question imagines conquest as desecration: wine on graves, idols in the mosque, the cross over Islam's holiest place. Nazlı counts seven such questions in the stanza. The rhetoric reflects the fear of 1878, when Russian armies reached the outskirts of Istanbul.

    Sadettin Ünal, 'Kâbe' (part 1), TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 24 (2001), pp. 14–21; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'sanem'

  14. sâr: blood-revenge st. IV, l. 11

    'Sâr' (Arabic thaʾr) is blood-vengeance. Nazlı, following İsmet Özel, stresses that Kemal chose it over 'intikam', the revenge exacted by right (God is al-Muntaqim, the Avenger), because the enemy's attack is unjust: a satanic vengeance, Satan's old grudge against Adam's line. 'Âdem evlâdı bir takım cânî' (a pack of criminals among Adam's children) refuses the enemy the dignity of a nation. The poem thus ends without resolution, as a cry (vâveylâ) addressed to God.

    Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73
