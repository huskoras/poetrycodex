Hürriyet Kasidesi

by Namık Kemal 1840–1888

History & Politics

Görüp ahkâm-ı asrı münharif sıdk u selametten
Çekildik izzet ü ikbal ile bab-ı hükûmetten

Usanmaz kendini insan bilenler halka hizmetten
Mürüvvet-mend olan mazluma el çekmez ianetten

Hakir olduysa millet şanına noksan gelir sanma
Yere düşmekle cevher sakıt olmaz kadr ü kıymetten

Vücudun kim hamir-i mâyesi hâk-i vatandandır
Ne gam rah-ı vatanda hak olursa cevr ü mihnetten

Muini zalimin dünyada erbab-ı denaettir
Köpektir zevk alan sayyad-ı bi-insafa hizmetten

Hemen bir feyz-i baki terk eder bir zevk-i faniye
Hayatın kadrini âli bilenler hüsn-i şöhretten

Nedendir halkta tul-i hayata bunca rağbetler
Nedir insana bilmem menfaat hıfz-ı emanetten

Cihanda kendini her ferdden alçak görür ol kim
Utanmaz kendi nefsinden de ar eyler melametten

Felekten intikam almak demektir ehl-i idrake
Edip tezyid-i gayret müstefid olmak nedametten

Durup ahkam-ı nusret ittihad-ı kalb-i millette
Çıkar asar-ı rahmet ihtilaf-ı rey-i ümmetten

Eder tedvir-i alem bir mekînin kuvve-i azmi
Cihan titrer sebat-ı pay-ı erbab-ı metanetten

Kaza her feyzini her lutfunu bir vakt için saklar
Fütur etme sakın milletteki za'f u betaetten

Değildir şîr-i der-zencire töhmet acz-i akdamı
Felekte baht utansın bi-nasib- erbab-ı himmetten

Ziya dûr ise evc-i rif'atinden iztırâridir
hicâb etsin tabiat yerde kalmış kabiliyetten

Biz ol nesl-i kerîm-i dûde-i Osmaniyânız kim
Muhammerdir serâpâ mâyemiz hûn-ı hamiyetten

Biz ol âlî-himem-i erbâb-ı cidd ü ictihâdız kim
Cihangirâne bir devlet çıkardık bir aşiretten

Biz ol ulvi-nihâdânız ki meydân-ı hamiyette
Bize hâk-i mezar ehven gelir hâk-i mezelletten

Ne gam pür âteş-i hevl olsa da gavgâ-yı hürriyet
Kaçar mı merd olan bir can için meydân-ı gayretten

Kemend-i can-güdâz-ı ejder-i kahr olsa cellâdın
Müreccahtır yine bin kerre zencîr-i esâretten

Felek her türlü esbâb-ı cefasın toplasın gelsin
Dönersem kahbeyim millet yolunda bir azîmetten

Anılsın mesleğimde çektiğim cevr ü meşakkatler
Ki ednâ zevki aladır vezâretten sadâretten

Vatan bir bî-vefâ nâzende-i tannâza dönmüş kim
Ayırmaz sâdıkân-ı aşkını âlâm-ı gurbetten

Müberrâyım recâ vü havfden indimde âlidir
Vazifem menfaatten hakkım agrâz-ı hükümetten

Civânmerdân-ı milletle hazer gavgâdan ye bidâd
Erir şemşîr-i zulmün âteş-i hûn-i hamiyetten

Ne mümkün zulm ile bidâd ile imhâ-yı hürriyet
Çalış idrâki kaldır muktedirsen âdemiyetten

Gönülde cevher-i elmâsa benzer cevher-i gayret
Ezilmez şiddet-i tazyikten te'sir-i sıkletten

Ne efsunkâr imişsin ah ey didâr-ı hürriyet
Esîr-i aşkın olduk gerçi kurtulduk esâretten

Senindir şimdi cezb-i kalbe kudret setr-i hüsn etme
Cemâlin ta ebed dûr olmasın enzâr-ı ümmetten

Ne yâr-ı cân imişsin ah ey ümmid-i istikbâl
Cihanı sensin azad eyleyen bin ye's ü mihnetten

Senindir devr-i devlet hükmünü dünyaya infâz et
Hüdâ ikbâlini hıfzeylesin hür türlü âfetten

Kilâb-ı zulme kaldı gezdiğin nâzende sahrâlar
Uyan ey yâreli şîr-i jeyân bu hâb-ı gafletten

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hürriyet Kasidesi: title, date and publication beyit 1 (ll. 1–2)

    The popular title 'Hürriyet Kasidesi' (Ode to Liberty) is not Kemal's own. Akün's encyclopedia article records that the poem took its final form during the Serbian and Montenegrin uprisings and was published in Vakit (no. 236, 2 June 1876) under the heading 'Besâlet-i Osmâniyye ve Hamiyyet-i İnsâniyye' (Ottoman Valour and Human Zeal). Gariper and Bayraktar add that its first couplets had appeared earlier in Hürriyet, the Young Ottoman paper Kemal and Ziya ran in London from 1868. It went quickly into school books and anthologies and drew many imitations (nazire). Tanpınar called it a kaside of heroism (hamaset kasidesi): it keeps the classical kaside's monorhyme and its long run of couplets, but it praises an idea, liberty, where the old kaside praised a sultan or a vizier.

    Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Ahmet Hamdi Tanpınar, 19'uncu Asır Türk Edebiyatı Tarihi (Istanbul: Çağlayan, 1988), p. 374, as cited by Gariper and Bayraktar (2017)

  2. Kaside form, rhyme and redif beyit 1, and every second line

    A kaside is a long monorhymed poem in couplets (beyit) whose first couplet (matla) rhymes in both lines. Here the matla pairs 'selâmetten' and 'hükûmetten', and every later couplet's second line ends the same way: 'iânetten', 'kıymetten', 'mihnetten', down to 'gafletten'. The fixed final syllable -ten (the ablative case, 'from') is the redif; the varying -et before it (selâm-et, hükûm-et) is the true rhyme (kafiye). The ablative fits the poem's argument, which is built on contrasts of turning away from one thing towards another: from office, from servility, from captivity. Kemal kept the old form deliberately; the novelty, as Gariper and Bayraktar stress, is in the subject, not the mould.

    Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  3. Metre: hezec (mefâîlün × 4) beyit 1 (ll. 1–2)

    Read in aruz, the line falls into four identical feet: Gö-rüp ah-kâ / mı as-rı mün / ha-rif sıd-ku / se-lâ-met-ten, each short-long-long-long (. − − −), the pattern called mefâîlün. Four such feet to the line, eight to the couplet, make the hezec-i müsemmen-i sâlim, the 'complete eight-footed hezec'. As often in Ottoman verse, some short open Turkish syllables have to be held long to fit the pattern (imâle), and izafet vowels are lengthened or shortened as the metre needs. The insistent, almost marching beat of the unbroken mefâîlün suits public declamation, and the poem was in fact recited and memorised by generations of students. The scansion here is offered from the text; check it against a prosody handbook.

    Tevfik Rüştü Topuzoğlu, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 17 (1998), pp. 302–304; Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  4. bâb-ı hükûmet: 'the gate of government' l. 2

    'Seeing the rulings of the age had swerved from truth and soundness, we withdrew, with honour and good fortune, from the gate of government.' The first person plural is the Young Ottoman 'we'. The couplet recalls the moment in 1867 when Kemal and Ziya left their posts and fled to Europe, but Gariper and Bayraktar read it more broadly: the protest is aimed not at the state as such but at the corrupt officials who run it. 'Münharif' (Arabic munḥarif, 'deviated, turned aside') is a key word: the age itself has gone off course. 'Bâb' (Arabic bāb, 'gate') recalls the Bâb-ı Âlî, the Sublime Porte, seat of the grand vizier.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'münharif'; Abdullah Uçman, 'Ziyâ Paşa', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 44 (2013), pp. 475–479

  5. halk, hizmet: serving the people beyit 2 (ll. 3–4)

    'Halk' had long meant simply 'people, creatures'; here it is the public whom the state should serve. Gariper and Bayraktar argue that the poem brings the modern idea of 'the people' as a value into Turkish literature, setting halk against the officials who neglect it. 'Mürüvvet-mend' is a Persian-suffixed adjective from Arabic muruwwa, 'manly virtue, generosity': the generous man does not withdraw his help (iâne, Arabic iʿāna) from the oppressed (mazlûm). The couplet works as a definition of humanity by duty, the first of the 'principles' that Kaplan saw in couplets 1–14.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mürüvvet'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'iane'

  6. cevher: the jewel that falls beyit 3 (ll. 5–6)

    'If the nation has been held in contempt, do not think its glory diminished; a jewel does not lose its value and worth by falling to the earth.' The couplet is a classical proof-by-image (the second line illustrates the first, a device called irsâl-i mesel, using a proverb-like example). 'Millet' is one of Kemal's charged words: Gibb noted that it had meant a religious community and was now made to stand for 'the nation' or 'the people'. 'Cevher' (Arabic jawhar, from Persian gawhar) means both jewel and essence, so the line also claims that the nation's essence is untouched by its present humiliation. The jewel returns in beyit 26.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'cevher'; Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141

  7. hâk-i vatan: 'the soil of the fatherland' beyit 4 (ll. 7–8)

    'Since the leaven of the body's dough is the soil of the fatherland, what grief if one becomes dust on the fatherland's road through torment and pain?' (The archive text spells the second 'hâk' without the circumflex, 'hak olursa'.) The image adapts the Qur'anic creation of man from clay to the new idea of vatan, so that dying for the country becomes a return to one's own matter. Gibb (whose History of Ottoman Poetry was published posthumously in 1907, edited by E. G. Browne) credited Kemal with raising 'watan' from a vague word between 'home' and 'birthplace' to the equivalent of French patrie. Gariper and Bayraktar describe the speaker here as a 'mystic of the fatherland'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907); Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'hamiyet'

  8. The dog and the merciless hunter beyit 5 (ll. 9–10)

    'The helpers of the tyrant in this world are the base; it is a dog that takes pleasure in serving the merciless hunter.' 'Denâet' (Arabic danāʾa) is baseness; 'sayyâd' (Arabic ṣayyād) is the hunter. In divan poetry the sayyâd is usually the beloved, hunting hearts with the snare of her curls; Kemal turns the stock figure political, so that the hunter is the oppressive ruler and his willing servants are hounds. Gariper and Bayraktar read the couplet as another reason for the speaker's resignation: to serve such a government would be a dog's work. The dog comes back as 'kilâb-ı zulm', the dogs of tyranny, in the last couplet.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'denaet'; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  9. ittihâd and ihtilâf: unity and disagreement beyit 10 (ll. 19–20)

    The couplet balances two Arabic verbal nouns: 'ittihâd' (union) of hearts gives victory, while 'ihtilâf' (difference of opinion) within the community (ümmet) brings blessing. The second line echoes a saying widely circulated as a hadith (its authenticity is disputed), that the disagreement of the community is a mercy, and turns it into an argument for free debate, a theme of Kemal's journalism. Uniting the hearts but freeing the opinions is close to the Young Ottoman programme of constitutional consultation (meşveret). Note the movement from 'millet' to 'ümmet' within the couplet: the national and the religious community are still held together.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378

  10. şîr-i der-zencîr: the lion in chains beyit 13 (ll. 25–26)

    'It is no reproach to the lion in chains that it cannot step forward; let fortune in the heavens be ashamed before men of high resolve who are left without a share.' The Persian construction şîr-i der-zencîr (lion in chains) gives the first appearance of the poem's emblem of the nation: powerful but bound. Gariper and Bayraktar, quoting Göçgün's prose version, read the couplet as placing the blame for the nation's weakness on its rulers, not on the people. The lion is unchained in the final couplet, where he is called to wake.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  11. ziyâ: the light kept from its height beyit 14 (ll. 27–28)

    'Ziyâ' (Arabic ḍiyāʾ, light) is far from the 'evc', the apogee or highest point of a heavenly body's course, a term from Ptolemaic astronomy; and 'rif'at' is height, exaltation. Its distance is 'ıztırârî', forced, not natural. Gariper and Bayraktar take the light as a metaphor for the nation's gifted individuals, who should shine on high but have been kept down. The astronomical image continues the attack on 'felek' (the sky, fate) in the previous couplet: the cosmos is shamed by the waste of human capacity (kabiliyet), a word that points to the Enlightenment vocabulary of talent and merit.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'felek'

  12. 'We are that noble race of the house of Osman' beyits 15–17 (ll. 29–34)

    Kaplan's division of the poem, as summarised by Gariper and Bayraktar, gives couplets 15–17 to admiration for Ottoman history. The anaphora 'Biz ol...' ('We are those...') is a fakhr, the self-praise of a tribe or hero inherited from Arabic poetry. 'Dûde' (Persian dūda, 'smoke, hearth, family') means a dynasty; 'muhammer' (Arabic muḥammar) is 'reddened'; 'hûn-ı hamiyet' is 'the blood of zeal'. The second couplet's famous line recalls the founding legend in which Osman's small tribe (aşiret) grew into a world-conquering state (cihangirâne bir devlet). The third prefers the earth of the grave to the earth of humiliation (hâk-i mezar / hâk-i mezellet), a pun on the two senses of hâk.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Hürriyet Kasidesi / Namık Kemal' (from p. 39 in the current Dergâh printing); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'hamiyet'

  13. The poem's design and its critics beyit 18 (l. 35)

    Kaplan's analysis, as given by Gariper and Bayraktar, divides the 31 couplets into four groups: principles of high value (1–14); admiration for Ottoman history (15–17); fearlessness and the poet's own conduct (18–24); and liberty (25–31). It is worth noticing that the word 'hürriyet' first appears in beyit 18, 'the battle of freedom', so that the poem's title names its goal, not its starting point. Critics differ on its merit. It is often praised as the first important poem of the new content, but its long chain of maxims also invites the charge of rhetoric over poetry, and Tanpınar's label, a kaside of heroism, places it in a tradition of declamation rather than lyric.

    Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Hürriyet Kasidesi / Namık Kemal' (from p. 39 in the current Dergâh printing); Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Ahmet Hamdi Tanpınar, 19'uncu Asır Türk Edebiyatı Tarihi (Istanbul: Çağlayan, 1988), p. 374, as cited by Gariper and Bayraktar (2017)

  14. The executioner's lasso and the chain of captivity beyit 19 (ll. 37–38)

    'Kemend' is the lasso or noose (in divan poetry, the beloved's curling lock); 'can-güdâz' (Persian jān-gudāz) is 'soul-melting'; 'ejder' (Persian aždar) is a dragon. A long Persian chain of genitives builds the most frightening possible image of death, only to dismiss it in the second line: the noose is preferable (müreccah) to the chain (zencîr) of captivity (esâret). Gariper and Bayraktar note that this is the first couplet of the liberty theme proper. The noose against the chain is also a contrast between a quick death and a long bondage.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'ejder'; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'esaret'

  15. vezâret, sadâret: the vizierate and the grand vizierate beyit 21 (ll. 41–42)

    'Let the torments and hardships I have borne on my path be remembered, for the least pleasure in them is higher than the vizierate, than the grand vizierate.' 'Sadâret' is the office of the sadrazam, the grand vizier, the highest post open to a subject. The couplet answers the opening one: the man who walked out of the 'gate of government' now says the highest offices are worth less than the pains of exile. Kemal had in fact been exiled to Famagusta in 1873, where he spent some thirty-eight months; he was later allowed to live on Midilli (1877) and was appointed its mutasarrıf in December 1879, so the poet who scorned office also served the state as a provincial governor.

    Ömer Faruk Akün, 'Nâmık Kemal', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 32 (2006), pp. 361–378; Kadri Nazlı, 'Namık Kemal ve "Vâveylâ" Şiiri Üzerine Notlar', Uluslararası Sosyal Bilimler Dergisi (Çanakkale) 1/1 (2016), pp. 61–73

  16. Vatan as a faithless, coquettish beloved beyit 22 (ll. 43–44)

    'The fatherland has turned into a faithless, coquettish darling who does not spare her sincere lovers the sorrows of exile.' Every term is borrowed from the ghazal: 'bî-vefâ' (faithless), 'nâzende' (coquettish, Persian nāzanda), 'tannâz' (teasing, flirtatious), the sincere lovers (sâdıkân-ı aşk), the pains of separation (gurbet). In divan love poetry the cruel beloved who makes her lovers suffer is a convention; Kemal makes the country itself that beloved, with a bitter irony, since the patriots' 'reward' is banishment. Gariper and Bayraktar link it to Kemal's own exiles. It is one of the clearest examples of old imagery serving new ideas.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'nazende'

  17. The diamond of zeal beyit 26 (ll. 51–52)

    'Gayret' (Arabic ghayra) means zeal, effort, protective pride. The couplet compares it to the diamond (elmâs, from Greek adamas through Arabic almās), the hardest substance known, and plays on 'cevher' in both senses, jewel and essence ('cevher-i elmâs ... cevher-i gayret'). 'Şiddet-i tazyîk' (force of pressure) and 'te'sîr-i sıklet' (effect of weight) have a faint scientific colour, the vocabulary of physics newly entering Ottoman prose. Compare the fallen jewel of beyit 3: the nation's value survives a fall, its zeal survives crushing.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'cevher'

  18. Liberty as the beloved's face beyits 27–30 (ll. 53–60)

    'What a sorceress you are, ah, O face of liberty! We became the captives of your love, though we were freed from captivity.' The paradox of the lover's willing slavery, a commonplace of the ghazal, now celebrates freedom. 'Dîdâr' (Persian dīdār) is the face, the sight of the beloved; 'efsunkâr' (Persian afsūnkār) is enchanting. The following couplets beg her not to veil her beauty from the community's eyes and call her 'yâr-ı cân', beloved of the soul. Gariper and Bayraktar note that liberty here takes a woman's image and becomes the highest value of the poem. Read with beyit 22, the poem divides the old figure of the beloved in two: the faithless fatherland and the faithful liberty.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'didar'

  19. kilâb-ı zulm: the dogs of tyranny beyit 31 (l. 61)

    'Kilâb' is the Arabic broken plural of kalb, dog. 'The graceful deserts where you roamed have been left to the dogs of oppression.' 'Nâzende sahrâlar' (graceful wastes) are the lion's old hunting ground, that is, the lands of the empire. The line returns to the dog of beyit 5, now in the plural: the officials who served the hunter have taken over the country. Gariper and Bayraktar read it as the ruling class whose tyranny the awakened people will end.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'zulüm'

  20. 'Wake, O wounded raging lion' beyit 31 (l. 62)

    'Wake, O wounded, roaring lion, from this sleep of heedlessness!' 'Jeyân' (Persian žayān) means raging, fierce; 'hâb-ı gaflet' is the sleep of negligence, a phrase from Sufi preaching, where 'gaflet' is forgetfulness of God, here forgetfulness of one's rights. The chained lion of beyit 13 is now wounded and asleep, and the poem ends not with a prayer for the sultan, as the old kaside did, but with a call to the people. The poem entered school books and anthologies soon after its publication, which helps explain why this last line is so widely remembered.

    Cafer Gariper and Yasemin Bayraktar, 'Hürriyet Kasidesi'nin Değerler Dizgesi Üzerinde Kimi Belirlemeler', Yeni Türk Edebiyatı Araştırmaları 18 (2017), pp. 127–141
