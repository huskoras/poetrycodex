Death & Dying

Rose Aylmer

by Walter Savage Landor 1775–1864

From Imaginary Conversations and Poems: A Selection

Death & DyingLove

Ah what avails the sceptred race,
Ah what the form divine!
What every virtue, every grace!
Rose Aylmer, all were thine.
Rose Aylmer, whom these wakeful eyes
May weep, but never see,
A night of memories and of sighs
I consecrate to thee.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Written for Rose Aylmer, a friend of Landor's youth who died young in Wales; Charles Lamb called its two quatrains “all that is worth reading” in a much longer memory.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening, form and publication l. 1

    'What avails' means 'what is the use of' (OED, 'avail'). The rhetorical question is the classical consolation formula turned upside down: rank, beauty, virtue and grace are of no avail against death. The poem is in common (hymnal) metre, alternating eight- and six-syllable lines rhyming abab cdcd. Simonidea, the 1806 collection, was named after the Greek poet Simonides, famed for epitaphs, which suggests how Landor wanted his elegies read: in the compressed, lapidary manner of Greek inscriptions. The text here follows Landor's later collected Works (1846).

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes; Oxford English Dictionary, 'avail'

  2. the sceptred race l. 1

    'Sceptred' (bearing a sceptre, royal) is used loosely for aristocratic descent. Compare John of Gaunt's 'This royal throne of kings, this sceptred isle' (Richard II 2.1.40). The phrase means 'what is the use of noble birth', and it is the first of four gifts (birth, beauty, virtue, grace) that the poem lists only to cancel. The Aylmer barony belonged to the peerage of Ireland. The opening thus begins with the most worldly of the gifts and moves towards the spiritual.

    Shakespeare, Richard II, 2.1.40 (MIT Complete Works); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes

  3. the form divine l. 2

    Milton, lamenting his blindness, lists what he can no longer see: 'Or flocks, or herds, or human face divine' (Paradise Lost III.44). Landor's inverted 'form divine' borrows the cadence and the sense of loss. The adjective after the noun gives the line a Latin or Greek formality appropriate to an epitaph. Milton's line, from the invocation to light, is itself a lament for what cannot be seen again, which suits Landor's later 'May weep, but never see'.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost, Book 3, l. 44 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes

  4. every virtue, every grace l. 3

    The paired 'every' generalizes praise in the manner of eighteenth-century epitaphs. 'Grace' covers elegance of manner and movement and, by association, the three Graces of classical myth; it can also suggest the Christian sense of divine favour, so that the line ends the catalogue on spiritual as well as social gifts. Line 4 then gathers all four into the name: 'Rose Aylmer, all were thine.'

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'grace'; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes

  5. Rose Aylmer (1779-1800) l. 4

    Landor met her around 1796-97 in South Wales (he was living at Swansea). She lent him Clara Reeve's The Progress of Romance (1785), in which he found the tale that became his poem Gebir (1798). After her mother's remarriage she went to India in 1798 with her aunt, Lady Russell, wife of Sir Henry Russell, and died there of cholera. She was buried in the South Park Street Cemetery, Calcutta, where her tomb still stands. The archive's headnote says she died in Wales; the records place her death in Calcutta.

    Families in India Society (FIBIS) database, burial record of Rose Whitworth Aylmer, South Park Street Cemetery, Calcutta; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes; Wikipedia, 'Walter Savage Landor'

  6. the repeated name ll. 4-5

    Repeating the final words of one unit at the start of the next is the figure called anadiplosis. Here it bridges the two stanzas and turns the close of the eulogy into the start of the lament. The name, with its soft liquids and long vowels, is the music of the poem; in October 1831 Henry Crabb Robinson told Landor that Lamb, drunk or sober, was ever muttering 'Rose Aylmer'.

    E. V. Lucas, The Life of Charles Lamb, vol. 2 (1905); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes

  7. these wakeful eyes / May weep, but never see ll. 5-6

    'Wakeful' prepares the night of vigil in line 7. The contrast of weeping and seeing makes the eyes the instrument of both loss and mourning. Landor had not seen her since she sailed for India, so the line holds two separations, of distance and of death. In November 1831 he told Crabb Robinson, half joking, that he had written the poem while cleaning his teeth before bed, but added that the night of sorrow had really been devoted to her.

    E. V. Lucas, The Life of Charles Lamb, vol. 2 (1905); Families in India Society (FIBIS) database, burial record of Rose Whitworth Aylmer, South Park Street Cemetery, Calcutta

  8. A night of memories and of sighs / I consecrate ll. 7-8

    To 'consecrate' (Latin consecrare, to make sacred) is to set apart for a religious purpose (OED). The night of memories and sighs is an offering like those made at a tomb in classical epitaph. Charles Lamb, writing to Landor in 1832, said the poem had 'a charm I cannot explain' and that he 'lived upon it for weeks'. Its fame rests on this restraint: eight short lines with no image of her, only the name and the vow.

    E. V. Lucas, The Life of Charles Lamb, vol. 2 (1905); Oxford English Dictionary, 'consecrate'; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Rose Aylmer', ed. H. Kerpneck, notes
