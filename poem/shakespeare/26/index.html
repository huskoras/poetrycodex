The Mind

Sonnet 27

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

The Mind

Weary with toil, I haste me to my bed,
The dear respose for limbs with travel tir'd;
But then begins a journey in my head
To work my mind, when body's work's expired:
For then my thoughts, from far where I abide,
Intend a zealous pilgrimage to thee,
And keep my drooping eyelids open wide,
Looking on darkness which the blind do see:
Save that my soul's imaginary sight
Presents thy shadow to my sightless view,
Which, like a jewel hung in ghastly night,
Makes black night beauteous, and her old face new.
Lo! thus, by day my limbs, by night my mind,
For thee, and for myself, no quiet find.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Weary with toil: the sleepless night l. 1

    Sonnets 27 and 28 form a pair on sleeplessness during absence, followed by the famous 29. They imply that the poet is travelling away from the friend (see Sonnets 48-51). The insomniac lover was a convention of Petrarchan verse: Petrarch's Rime and Sidney's Astrophil and Stella (e.g. sonnets 38-39, the address to sleep) supply models. Shakespeare's version is notably physical, opening with tired limbs before moving to the mind's 'journey'.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 1; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 27; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 27 (text, notes and synopsis)

  2. The dear respose [repose] l. 2

    The line stands in apposition to 'my bed': the bed is the 'dear repose', i.e. the welcome rest, for tired limbs. The archive text reads 'respose', an error for the standard reading 'repose' (Quarto 'repose'). 'Dear' means precious and longed-for; it may also suggest costly, since the rest is never in fact granted. The line's slow, heavy rhythm enacts the tired body sinking into bed, before the mind's restlessness takes over in line 3. Readers using the archive should keep the correct spelling in mind when quoting.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 27 (text, notes and synopsis); Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 2

  3. travel / travail l. 2

    In 1609 'travel' and 'travail' were spelling variants of one word, and the Quarto prints 'trauaill'. The sense here is both: limbs tired by a journey and by labour (the 'toil' of line 1). The doubleness matters because the sonnet sets the body's journey against the mind's 'journey in my head' and the body's work against the mind's work. Modern editions must choose a spelling, which loses one sense; Booth discusses the pun at length.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, l. 2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'travail'

  4. a journey in my head ll. 3-4

    Line 4 plays on 'work': the mind is set to work just as the body's work has 'expired' (ended, with a hint of dying, as in the expiry of a lease). The neat symmetry of body and mind is the structure of the whole poem and is summed up in the couplet ('by day my limbs, by night my mind'). Vendler notes how the 'journey' becomes a 'pilgrimage' in line 6, raising the friend to a sacred object.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 27; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, ll. 3-4; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 27, l. 4

  5. Intend l. 6

    From Latin intendere, to stretch towards or direct one's course. The thoughts 'intend' a pilgrimage both in the sense that they plan it and that they actually set off towards the friend 'from far where I abide' (from the distant place where I am staying). The older sense of directed motion is recorded in the OED. The physical motion of the thoughts across distance is the point: the body lies still while the mind travels, the paradox on which the whole sonnet turns.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'intend'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, l. 6

  6. zealous pilgrimage l. 6

    'Zealous' means ardent, fervent, with religious overtones (zeal for God). Representing love as pilgrimage and the beloved as a saint is a Petrarchan commonplace; Shakespeare stages it in the 'palmers' sonnet' shared by Romeo and Juliet (1.5). After the Reformation, pilgrimage carried some Catholic colouring, which lends the image a hint of forbidden or excessive devotion. The pilgrimage keeps the eyes 'open wide' (l. 7), so devotion is also insomnia.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'zealous'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 6; William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet 1.5 (the 'pilgrim' sonnet), Folger Shakespeare

  7. Looking on darkness which the blind do see l. 8

    The paradox of seeing darkness prepares for the 'imaginary sight' of the next line, which sees what the eyes cannot. The blind are said to 'see' darkness, so the lover is for the moment as good as blind. The line's heavy monosyllables and the long 'o' sounds of 'Looking on darkness' slow the verse to the pace of a sleepless night. The paradox of sight in darkness recurs in Sonnet 43 ('When most I wink, then do mine eyes best see').

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 27; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 8; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 27 (text, notes and synopsis)

  8. imaginary sight l. 9

    'Imaginary' here means belonging to the imagination (the faculty that forms images), not simply false. In Renaissance faculty psychology the imagination or 'fancy' presented images to the soul independently of the senses, particularly in sleep and dreams. Shakespeare uses the same idea in Henry V's Prologue, asking the audience to work their 'imaginary forces'. The soul's sight replaces the 'sightless view' (l. 10) of the eyes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'imaginary'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, l. 9; William Shakespeare, Henry V, Prologue ('imaginary forces'), Folger Shakespeare

  9. thy shadow l. 10

    'Shadow' in Elizabethan English meant an image, reflection, portrait or phantom, as opposed to the 'substance' (compare Sonnet 53, 'What is your substance, whereof are you made, / That millions of strange shadows on you tend?'). The 1609 Quarto prints 'their shadow', one of several places where 'their' appears where the sense requires 'thy' (also Sonnet 35); editors usually assume compositor's misreading of a manuscript abbreviation. A few defend 'their' as referring to the thoughts.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, l. 10; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 10

  10. like a jewel hung in ghastly night l. 11

    Renaissance lapidaries held that certain stones, especially the carbuncle, emitted their own light and could shine in darkness, a belief that may lie behind the image. The image of a gem against night recurs in Romeo's 'like a rich jewel in an Ethiop's ear' (Romeo and Juliet 1.5). 'Ghastly' means terrifying, ghost-like; the night is a frightening old woman whose 'old face' (l. 12) becomes 'new' and beautiful in the jewel's light.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 27, l. 11; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, ll. 11-12; William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet 1.5 ('a rich jewel in an Ethiop's ear'), Folger Shakespeare

  11. by day my limbs, by night my mind ll. 13-14

    The couplet's chiastic balance (day/limbs, night/mind; for thee/for myself) neatly summarises the octave. 'For thee' can mean on your account (because of you) and 'for myself' because of my own longing; Booth notes that it also suggests the lover finds no quiet for the friend's benefit, a submissive service. The theme of unrest continues directly in Sonnet 28 ('How can I then return in happy plight / That am debarr'd the benefit of rest?').

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 27, ll. 13-14; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 27; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 28 (text, notes and synopsis)
