Nature

Sonnet 73

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

That time of year thou mayst in me behold
When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang
Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,
Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.
In me thou see'st the twilight of such day
As after sunset fadeth in the west;
Which by and by black night doth take away,
Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.
In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire,
That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,
As the death-bed, whereon it must expire,
Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by.
This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong,
To love that well, which thou must leave ere long.

Study notes by the editors

  1. That time of year: the three images ll. 1-12

    Each quatrain offers a new metaphor for the speaker's age, introduced by a variation on 'thou mayst in me behold' / 'In me thou see'st'. The images run from a season (late autumn) to a day (twilight) to a single fire burning down, so that the time left grows shorter in each, and the images become more intimate. Vendler and others note the increasing sense of enclosure: open woods, then the closing night, then the fire smothered in its own ashes. The addressee is made a spectator ('behold', 'see'st', 'perceiv'st'), which prepares the couplet's turn to the beloved's response.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 73; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73

  2. yellow leaves, or none, or few l. 2

    Yellow leaves are a conventional sign of old age; compare Macbeth 5.3, 'my way of life / Is fall'n into the sere, the yellow leaf'. The odd order of the series, 'yellow leaves, or none, or few', is expressive: the speaker first says some leaves remain, then that none do, then corrects himself to 'few', as though unwilling to admit or to exaggerate his decline. Booth discusses the line as an example of the sonnet's hesitant, self-qualifying movement.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Shakespeare, Macbeth 5.3, Folger edition

  3. Bare ruin'd choirs l. 4

    The 'choir' (spelled quire in the period) is the part of a church where the singers sat. The branches, bare of leaves, are like the roofless choirs of ruined churches, and the birds that sang there are like choristers. Many commentators connect the image with the abbeys and monastic churches left to decay after Henry VIII's dissolution of the monasteries (1536-41), a familiar sight in Shakespeare's England; others think this is reading in too much. William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) made the line a famous example of meaning that works through many associations at once (carved wood, singing, the shelter of the church) without one being primary. The 1609 Quarto reads 'Bare rn'wd quiers', which is universally emended to 'ruin'd choirs'.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; William Empson, Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930), ch. 1, on Sonnet 73; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73

  4. where late the sweet birds sang l. 4

    'Late' means lately, recently. The birds who sang in summer have gone, as choirboys have gone from ruined churches. Many readers see a further application to the poet: his own song (his poetry) belongs to a past season. The line's slow, heavy monosyllables, with stresses crowded together in 'Bare ruin'd choirs', contrast with the lighter close 'where late the sweet birds sang', so that the sound itself moves from desolation to memory.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 73

  5. twilight ll. 5-6

    The second quatrain moves from the year to the day. Twilight is the faint light lingering in the west after sunset, the last remainder of a day that is already over. The archaic verb 'fadeth' slows the line. As with the autumn tree, the speaker presents himself not as dead but as near the end, a remnant of something already past its prime.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73

  6. by and by l. 7

    In Elizabethan English 'by and by' usually meant 'immediately' or 'very soon' (OED 'by and by' adv., sense 2), a stronger sense than its later meaning 'eventually'. The phrase stresses how little time remains between twilight and full dark. Shakespeare often uses it in this urgent sense in the plays, where a character promises to come 'by and by', meaning at once. Read with the modern sense, the line would lose its pressure: the quatrain moves from fading light to its sudden removal, and the black night that 'doth take away' the twilight arrives almost as soon as it is mentioned, which is the point of the speaker's warning about his own short time.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'by and by'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73

  7. Death's second self l. 8

    A 'second self' is a double or alter ego. Night is Death's second self because it brings darkness and rest that resemble death, and because it brings sleep, traditionally Death's twin. In Hesiod's Theogony (211-12, 756-66) Night gives birth to Sleep (Hypnos) and Death (Thanatos), and in the Iliad (16.672, 682) Sleep and Death are twin brothers who carry away the body of Sarpedon. The idea was a Renaissance commonplace (Samuel Daniel calls sleep 'brother to Death' in Delia). The phrase prepares the darker reference to 'death-bed' in line 11.

    Hesiod, Theogony 211-12, 756-66 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, 1914, Theoi Project); Homer, Iliad 16.666-683 (trans. A. T. Murray, Perseus); Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73

  8. seals up l. 8

    To 'seal up' is to close firmly, as a letter or a tomb is sealed, and also to close the eyes in sleep or death. 'All' extends the darkness to the whole world. The language of sealing appears elsewhere in Shakespeare for night's closing of the eyes, and the same word could be spelled like 'seel', the falconer's term for sewing up a hawk's eyelids (compare Macbeth 3.2, 'Come, seeling night'), a resonance some readers hear here.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seal'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Shakespeare, Macbeth 3.2, Folger edition

  9. the glowing of such fire l. 9

    The third quatrain is the most intimate and the most complex. The speaker is not a blazing fire but the glow of embers lying on 'the ashes of his youth', that is, the fire's (his = its) own earlier fuel. Youth has become the ash on which age now lies. The image of life as a flame was traditional, and Shakespeare's Sonnet 1 already speaks of the youth feeding 'thy light's flame with self-substantial fuel'.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 73

  10. his youth l. 10

    'Its' was only just entering English in Shakespeare's time, and 'his' was the normal neuter possessive (OED 'his' pron.). The 'ashes of his youth' are the fire's own burnt fuel; the glow lies on them as on a bed. Since the fire represents the speaker, the line also says that his youth has been consumed and now lies under him. The bed image leads directly into 'death-bed' in the next line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'its'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73

  11. death-bed l. 11

    The ashes become 'the death-bed, whereon it must expire'. 'Expire' means both to die and, in its Latin root (exspirare), to breathe out, so the fire's last breath is also a dying man's. The image of a deathbed brings the poem closest to literal death; the first two quatrains spoke of seasons and of night, but this one names the bed on which a person dies.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'expire'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73

  12. Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by l. 12

    The line's paradox is that what once nourished the fire (its fuel) now, as ash, smothers it. 'With' can mean 'by' or 'along with', so the fire is consumed by its fuel and together with it. Renaissance emblem culture expressed the idea in the Latin motto 'Quod me nutrit me destruit' ('what nourishes me destroys me'), which appears on the portrait at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, often thought to be of Christopher Marlowe (1585). Applied to the speaker, the line suggests that the passions or energies that sustained his life are what wear it away. Compare Sonnet 1, 'Feed'st thy light's flame with self-substantial fuel'.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73; Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, portrait of an unknown man (1585) with motto 'Quod me nutrit me destruit'

  13. the couplet: love that well, which thou must leave ll. 13-14

    The couplet turns from the speaker to the addressee. On the common reading, perceiving the speaker's age makes the friend love him more strongly, because he must soon lose him: 'leave' means 'lose' or 'part from'. But 'that which thou must leave ere long' could instead be the friend's own youth, which he too must give up, so the poem would warn him to value his own life. Some critics (Booth among them) find the couplet less confident than it sounds: the speaker may be hoping, even pleading, rather than describing the friend's feelings. 'This' gathers up all three images.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 73; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 73; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 73
