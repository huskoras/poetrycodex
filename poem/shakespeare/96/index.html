Nature

Sonnet 97

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

How like a winter hath my absence been
From thee, the pleasure of the fleeting year!
What freezings have I felt, what dark days seen!
What old December's bareness everywhere!
And yet this time removed was summer's time;
The teeming autumn, big with rich increase,
Bearing the wanton burden of the prime,
Like widow'd wombs after their lords' decease:
Yet this abundant issue seem'd to me
But hope of orphans, and unfather'd fruit;
For summer and his pleasures wait on thee,
And, thou away, the very birds are mute:
Or, if they sing, 'tis with so dull a cheer,
That leaves look pale, dreading the winter's near.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnet 97 and the absence sonnets l. 1

    Sonnets 97-99 share the theme of separation and the language of seasons and flowers; Sonnet 98 begins 'From you have I been absent in the spring'. The argument of 97 is a paradox. The absence actually fell in summer and autumn, yet it felt like winter. 'How like' opens an exclamation, not a question. The sonnet's three quatrains move from winter (ll. 1-4) to the actual summer and autumn (ll. 5-10) and to the silence of the birds (ll. 11-12), so the year is gone through twice, once as feeling and once as fact.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97

  2. absence l. 1

    Editors point out that the absence is his own, though 'thou away' (l. 12) later reverses the point of view. Running the sentence on from 'been' to 'From thee' stresses the separation. Absence as winter is an old figure, but Shakespeare's version makes the beloved the source of the year's 'pleasure'. The paradox that the traveller, not the stay-at-home, feels the winter is part of the poem's wit.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97

  3. fleeting year l. 2

    The phrase makes the beloved the joy that gives the year its meaning; without him the year is only time going by. The quickness of 'fleeting' is set against the speaker's slow 'freezings' and 'dark days'. The year passes quickly because time is short and beauty fades, a theme of the earlier sonnets. Here, though, the speed is set against the speaker's experience, for whom the time of absence dragged like a long winter. The paradox of a year that is both fleeting and endless prepares for the clash between the actual season and the felt one in ll. 5-10.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  4. Old December l. 4

    The year was often pictured as a human life, with December as old age (compare The Winter's Tale 4.4). 'Bareness' suggests both the leafless season and poverty. The three exclamations of ll. 3-4 build up the picture of winter before the turn at 'And yet' (l. 5). The word 'everywhere' extends the private winter to the whole world. The bare month is the opposite of the 'teeming autumn' that follows.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  5. time removed l. 5

    Booth notes both senses: the time when I was away, and a time at a distance from now. 'Was summer's time' corrects the opening simile, because it really was summer, though it felt like winter. As editors observe, the season meant runs from late summer into autumn, the time of harvest. So the line marks the turn from the felt season to the real one.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97

  6. Teeming autumn l. 6

    'Teem' means to bear offspring in abundance, and 'big' means pregnant. 'Increase' is the Bible's word for the crops of the land (Leviticus 26:4, 'the land shall yield her increase'). The early procreation sonnets use the same vocabulary ('From fairest creatures we desire increase', Sonnet 1). The image of the fruitful season, normally a sign of joy, is preparing to turn into mourning in l. 8.

    Bible (KJV), Leviticus 26:4; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'teem, v.1'; William Shakespeare, Sonnet 1, l. 1

  7. The wanton burden of the prime l. 7

    'Wanton' means playful, luxuriant and sexually free. A 'burden' is what is carried, here a child in the womb; the word could also mean a song's refrain, a possible second sense. 'Prime' is spring. The image tells the seasons as a story: conception in spring, birth in autumn. The joining of fertility and wantonness also recalls the sexual energy of the procreation sonnets.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wanton, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'burden, n.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  8. Widowed wombs l. 8

    The simile makes the harvest sad. The crops are children born after their father has died. 'Lords' means husbands, with a hint of aristocratic widowhood. The father is summer, who 'waits on' the absent beloved (l. 11), so the fruit is fatherless. Posthumous children were a familiar fact of early modern life. The image brings together the poem's two worlds, nature's harvest and human grief, and makes autumn's very abundance a sign of loss.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97

  9. Hope of orphans l. 10

    'Hope of orphans' can mean orphans still to come, or the only hope that orphans have, which is a bleak inheritance. A child who had lost one parent could be called an orphan. 'Unfather'd fruit' joins the plant and human senses of 'fruit', as in 'the fruit of the womb' (Psalm 127:3). 'Abundant issue' in l. 9 means a plentiful crop or progeny, with 'issue' a legal word for heirs.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 127:3; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97

  10. Summer waits on thee l. 11

    'Wait on' means serve and attend, and also wait for. The idea that the beloved makes the season reverses nature, since the year's warmth follows him rather than the calendar. The beloved has become the season's lord, almost a god of plenty like Bacchus, whom summer serves. Since summer and its pleasures have followed him, the land the speaker sees has its fruit but none of its delight, which explains why the harvest seemed to him only orphaned. The line gives the reason ('For') for the whole paradox of ll. 5-10.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  11. Mute birds l. 12

    'Thou away' is an absolute construction meaning 'you being away'. The birds' silence attributes human grief to nature, but it is also a fact of late summer (compare Sonnet 102: 'As Philomel in summer's front doth sing ... But that wild music burthens every bough'). The couplet then allows that the birds may sing, though sadly. The silence of the birds was a convention for grief.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97; William Shakespeare, Sonnet 102

  12. cheer l. 13

    The word comes from Old French chiere ('face'), and in Shakespeare's time it could still mean a person's look as well as their mood. In the last line the leaves 'look pale', as if from fear, at the approach of winter, so their autumn yellowing becomes dread. The ending returns to the winter of l. 1, now no longer a metaphor but a season on its way.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cheer, n.1'; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 97; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary
