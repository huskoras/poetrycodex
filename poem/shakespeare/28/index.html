Sonnet 29

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Religion

When in disgrace with fortune and men's eyes
I all alone beweep my outcast state,
And trouble deaf heaven with my bootless cries,
And look upon myself, and curse my fate,
Wishing me like to one more rich in hope,
Featur'd like him, like him with friends possess'd,
Desiring this man's art, and that man's scope,
With what I most enjoy contented least;
Yet in these thoughts my self almost despising,
Haply I think on thee, and then my state,
Like to the lark at break of day arising
From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven's gate;
For thy sweet love remember'd such wealth brings
That then I scorn to change my state with kings.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When in disgrace: structure and situation ll. 1-9

    The sonnet is a single sentence. Lines 1-8 form an extended subordinate clause beginning 'When', listing the speaker's misery, envy and self-contempt; the main clause arrives only after the turn in line 9 ('Yet'), and its relief is delayed to line 10 ('Haply I think on thee'). The structure enacts the experience: a long, grinding period of depression followed by a sudden lift. 'Disgrace' means being out of favour, not public scandal; one commentary stresses that it describes a failure to gain standing rather than a fall from it. Biographical readings link the poem to Shakespeare's position as an actor or to the closing of the theatres in the plague years of 1592-94, but there is no evidence for a specific occasion.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 29

  2. fortune and men's eyes l. 1

    Fortune is the classical and medieval goddess Fortuna, who raises and lowers people on her wheel without regard to merit. Shakespeare elsewhere in the Sonnets blames 'the guilty goddess of my harmful deeds' (Sonnet 111) for his lot as a public entertainer. 'Men's eyes' adds the social dimension: to be in disgrace is to be looked down on. The two together set up the poem's opposition between external valuation (fortune, rank, wealth, others' esteem) and the inward wealth of being loved, which the couplet will set above kings.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 111

  3. beweep ... outcast state l. 2

    'Beweep' (Old English bewepan) is a transitive form of 'weep', meaning to lament or weep over something; its be- prefix gives it the solemn, slightly archaic tone of biblical language (compare 'bewail', 'bemoan'). 'Outcast' means rejected or exiled; commentators suspect deliberate exaggeration, since the speaker goes on to envy others' talents and friends rather than describing real banishment. 'State' is the first of three appearances of the word (ll. 2, 10, 14), each with a different weight: here it is his miserable condition.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'beweep'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  4. trouble deaf heaven with my bootless cries l. 3

    Heaven is 'deaf' because it does not answer, and the cries are 'bootless' (useless). The speaker's cursing of his fate (l. 4) echoes Job 3:1-3, where Job 'cursed his day' and the night of his conception. The image is answered later in the poem: the 'deaf heaven' of line 3 becomes the 'heaven's gate' at which the lark sings in line 12, so that the same heaven which seemed closed is now reached by song. The verb 'trouble' is pointedly domestic and petty, as if the speaker recognises his complaints as an annoyance.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 3:1-3; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 29

  5. bootless l. 3

    'Bootless' derives from Old English bot, 'help, remedy, compensation', which survives in the phrase 'to boot' (in addition, as a bonus). A bootless cry achieves nothing. The word is common in Shakespeare (e.g. 'bootless prayers', 'bootless errand') and belongs to the same vocabulary of profit and loss as 'rich', 'possess'd', 'wealth' and 'kings' later in the poem: the speaker's spiritual state is consistently described in terms of economic and social fortune.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bootless'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  6. one more rich in hope l. 5

    The speaker wishes himself like someone 'more rich in hope', with better prospects of advancement. Lines 5-7 then pile up the advantages of other men in a list (prospects, looks, friends, skill, range), using the repeated pronouns 'him ... him ... this man's ... that man's' to suggest that he envies a whole series of people, each for a different gift. The catalogue is an instance of the rhetorical figure of accumulation, and its broken rhythm ('Featur'd like him, like him with friends possess'd') mimics restless comparison.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29

  7. Featur'd like him l. 6

    'Featured' means formed or shaped in face and body, here 'good-looking' (OED 'featured' adj., sense 1, with a qualifying word). The repetition 'like him, like him' turns a simple wish into a compulsive one, and different men seem to be meant: one has the looks, another the friends. Readers have connected the envy with the speaker's ageing self-image elsewhere in the Sonnets (e.g. Sonnet 62, 'Beated and chopp'd with tann'd antiquity'), though this poem does not specify what he lacks.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'featured'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  8. art ... scope l. 7

    'Art' means skill or learning; 'scope' means range, whether of mind, opportunity or freedom of action (OED 'scope' n.2). Because the speaker is a poet, some critics take 'art' as literary skill and connect the line to the rival-poet sonnets (78-86), where Shakespeare depicts himself as outclassed; others warn against narrowly biographical reading, since the sentence presents a general mood of envy. The balanced phrasing ('this man's ... that man's') continues the catalogue begun in line 5.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scope'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29

  9. With what I most enjoy contented least l. 8

    The line is a chiasmus of opposites (most/least, enjoy/contented): in depression, even his chief pleasures fail. Editors disagree on what these pleasures are. Some take them as his own art, which he despises in comparison with others'; others, noting that 'enjoy' could mean 'possess', take the line to refer to his possessions or advantages in general; a few hear an anticipation of the beloved, whom he 'enjoys' but has forgotten in his gloom until line 10. The ambiguity prepares the turn, in which what restores him is precisely something he already has.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 29; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  10. Yet: the volta l. 9

    The sonnet keeps the traditional turn after the octave, but makes it gradual. 'Yet' introduces line 9, which still continues the misery ('my self almost despising'), and only with 'Haply' in line 10 does the change arrive. Quatrain and sentence structure therefore pull against each other: the third quatrain is split between despair and joy. Vendler and others note how the rhythm lifts at line 11 as the lark rises, so that the poem's sound mirrors its emotional reversal.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 29; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29

  11. Haply l. 10

    'Haply' means 'by hap', by chance or perhaps (OED 'haply' adv.). The deliverance is unplanned: the speaker happens to think of the beloved, as if by fortune, so that the 'fortune' that disgraced him in line 1 is answered by a lucky chance. Elizabethan spelling and sound make 'haply' close to 'happily', and many readers hear both senses at once. Note too that he thinks of 'thee', the first appearance of the addressee, delayed until the tenth line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'haply'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  12. the lark at break of day arising ll. 11-12

    The skylark sings as it climbs high into the sky at dawn, a traditional emblem of joy and of the soul's ascent. The syntax is ambiguous: what is like the lark? Either 'my state' (l. 10), which rises from sullen earth and sings, or 'I' who think of the beloved. In the 1609 Quarto line 11 is printed within parentheses and there is no comma after 'earth', so 'From sullen earth' can attach either to the lark's rising or to the singing. Shakespeare uses the same image in Cymbeline 2.3, in the song 'Hark, hark, the lark at heaven's gate sings'. The enjambment across lines 11-12 makes the verse itself climb.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; Shakespeare, Cymbeline 2.3 (song 'Hark, hark, the lark'), Folger edition

  13. sullen l. 12

    'Sullen' derives from a word meaning solitary (Old French solain, from Latin solus) and came to mean gloomy, morose, sluggish or dark-coloured (OED 'sullen' adj.). The earth is 'sullen' because it is still dark at daybreak and because it is heavy and unmoving compared with the soaring lark. The word transfers the speaker's mood to the landscape, recalling his earlier solitude ('I all alone', l. 2).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sullen'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  14. hymns at heaven's gate l. 12

    The 'deaf heaven' troubled by 'bootless cries' in line 3 is now approached by 'hymns', songs of praise. The poem thus moves from complaint to worship, a religious vocabulary that some critics find daring when applied to human love. 'Heaven's gate' was a conventional phrase for the entrance to paradise and also for the dawn sky, the 'gate' through which the sun rises; both apply to the lark's song at break of day.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 29

  15. such wealth l. 13

    The couplet translates love into the economic terms the poem has used throughout: the man who wanted to be 'more rich' (l. 5) now possesses 'such wealth'. Note that it is love 'remember'd', not present: the beloved is absent, and the remedy is an act of thought. 'Sweet love' may mean the beloved's love for the speaker, the speaker's love for him, or the beloved himself; the phrase leaves this open.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 29

  16. state ... kings l. 14

    'State' appears three times (ll. 2, 10, 14), at or near the end of its line. It means first a wretched condition, then a mood that rises like the lark, and finally a status he would not trade for a king's. Since 'state' also meant high rank, pomp, and a kingdom (as in 'affairs of state'), the last use plays on royal estate. The final reversal of the opening is complete: the man in disgrace with fortune now scorns the highest fortune of all. Booth and Vendler discuss the repetition as a structural key to the poem.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 29; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 29; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'state'
