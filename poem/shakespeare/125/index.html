Love

Sonnet 126

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

O thou, my lovely boy, who in thy power
Dost hold Time's fickle glass, his fickle hour;
Who hast by waning grown, and therein show'st
Thy lovers withering, as thy sweet self grow'st.
If Nature, sovereign mistress over wrack,
As thou goest onwards, still will pluck thee back,
She keeps thee to this purpose, that her skill
May time disgrace and wretched minutes kill.
Yet fear her, O thou minion of her pleasure!
She may detain, but not still keep, her treasure:
Her audit (though delayed) answered must be,
And her quietus is to render thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. O thou, my lovely boy: the envoi l. 1

    Sonnet 126 is not a sonnet in form: it has six pentameter couplets (aabbccddeeff) and no final quatrain-plus-couplet. It closes the Fair Youth sequence; Sonnet 127 begins the poems on the 'dark lady'. 'Envoi' (from French, a 'sending-off') is the closing stanza or poem that sends a work on its way. 'Lovely boy' is the most direct description of the youth's age in the sequence. The address sums up its themes: Time, Nature, beauty, and the final reckoning.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, headnote; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 126; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'envoy'

  2. Time's fickle glass, his fickle hour l. 2

    The 1609 Quarto prints 'times fickle glasse, his sickle, hower': 'fickle' for the glass but 'sickle' for the hour. The archive text reads 'fickle' twice, an error of the kind produced by the long s, which is easily mistaken for f. Editors print 'sickle', Time's reaping hook (compare 'his bending sickle's compass', Sonnet 116), and debate the Quarto comma: 'his sickle, hour' (two separate attributes of Time) or 'his sickle hour' (the hour of death, the reaping hour). The 'glass' is the hourglass and perhaps also a mirror, 'fickle' because it never stays still. The power the youth holds is paradoxical: he seems to have the instruments of Time himself in his hand.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 126, l. 2; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, l. 2

  3. by waning grown l. 3

    'Waning' is the language of the moon. The youth has grown more beautiful by growing older (waning towards death), and thereby 'show'st / Thy lovers withering' (shows by contrast how his admirers, including the poet, are ageing). Booth and Vendler note that the paradox is both a compliment and a warning: what wanes must finally be gone. The rhyme 'show'st / grow'st' underlines the contrast between the youth's growth and others' withering.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 126, ll. 3-4; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 126; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, ll. 3-4

  4. Nature, sovereign mistress over wrack l. 5

    'Wrack' means ruin or destruction (a variant of 'wreck'). Nature is a 'sovereign mistress' who governs decay and can 'pluck thee back' (l. 6) as he 'goest onwards' in time, keeping him young. Her purpose (ll. 7-8) is to show her skill by disgracing Time and killing 'wretched minutes'. The Quarto spells 'minutes' oddly ('mynuit'), which editors normalise. The quatrain stages a contest between Nature and Time in which the youth is merely the instrument of Nature's display.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wrack'; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, ll. 5-8

  5. minion of her pleasure l. 9

    From French mignon, darling. 'Minion' could mean a beloved favourite (often of a monarch) and, pejoratively, a servile dependant; both senses appear in Shakespeare. 'Of her pleasure' means kept for her enjoyment and subject to her will. The warning begins: 'Yet fear her'. The double sense matters: the youth is cherished, but cherished as property, and Nature's favour will last only as long as it serves her. Shakespeare uses the word both affectionately and contemptuously elsewhere, as in Macbeth ('valour's minion', 1.2) and 1 Henry IV ('minions of the moon', 1.2).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'minion'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 126, l. 9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, l. 9

  6. detain, but not still keep l. 10

    The verbs distinguish temporary holding ('detain') from permanent possession ('keep'). 'Still' means 'for ever, continually', its usual Shakespearean sense. The youth is Nature's 'treasure', a word that leads into the financial language of the couplet. Nature is imagined as a temporary custodian who must give up what she holds when the debt falls due. The line marks the shift from Nature's apparent power over Time to her ultimate subjection to it, a reversal that the financial metaphor of the final couplet makes explicit and inescapable.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 126, l. 10

  7. audit l. 11

    An 'audit' is the formal examination and settlement of accounts. Nature has borrowed time; her account, though postponed, 'answered must be' (must be paid). The bookkeeping metaphor recalls the early sonnets ('what acceptable audit canst thou leave?', Sonnet 4). A pun on 'audit' as hearing (Latin audire) is possible, which the empty brackets that follow would make poignant. The audit is the last of the sequence's many reckonings.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'audit'; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 4 (text, notes and synopsis); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 126, l. 11

  8. quietus l. 12

    From the Latin formula 'quietus est' ('he is quit', i.e. discharged), written in accounts when a debt was settled. 'Render' means hand over, pay. The word also meant death or release from life, as in Hamlet's 'he himself might his quietus make / With a bare bodkin' (Hamlet 3.1). The last word of the Fair Youth poems is therefore a grim settlement: the youth himself is the payment.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quietus'; William Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1 ('his quietus make'), Folger Shakespeare; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, l. 12

  9. The missing couplet: the Quarto's empty brackets after l. 12

    The archive prints twelve lines only, but the Quarto places two pairs of empty parentheses beneath them, where lines 13 and 14 would be. Whether they were added by the printer (who noticed the poem was short) or reflect the author's design is unknown. Many modern critics read the blank as meaningful: an image of the empty hourglass, the open grave, or a silence after the final reckoning (Duncan-Jones, Burrow and others discuss it). Some editions, following the Quarto, reproduce the brackets.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 126, ll. 13-14; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (2002), note on Sonnet 126, headnote
