Death & Dying

Sonnet 66

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Death & Dying

Tired with all these, for restful death I cry:
As to behold desert a beggar born,
And needy nothing trimm'd in jollity,
And purest faith unhappily forsworn,
And gilded honour shamefully misplac'd,
And maiden virtue rudely strumpeted,
And right perfection wrongfully disgrac'd,
And strength by limping sway disabled
And art made tongue-tied by authority,
And folly, doctor-like, controlling skill,
And simple truth miscall'd simplicity,
And captive good attending captain ill:
Tir'd with all these, from these would I be gone,
Save that, to die, I leave my love alone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Tired with all these: list and anaphora ll. 1-14

    Line 1 announces exhaustion and the wish for death; lines 2-12 list the abuses that cause it, ten of them beginning with 'And' (anaphora). The list breaks the usual quatrain structure: the sonnet is nearly one continuous sentence, and the couplet repeats the opening ('Tir'd with all these') before turning in its last line to the beloved. Hamlet's 'To be, or not to be' (3.1) offers a closely comparable catalogue of 'the spurns / That patient merit of th' unworthy takes'. The Oxquarry commentary, citing Duncan-Jones's headnote, notes that multiples of six carry adverse biblical associations through the beast of Revelation 13. The poem has had a long political afterlife: Shostakovich set Pasternak's Russian version in his Six Romances, Op. 62 (1942).

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 66, headnote; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1, Folger Shakespeare; 'Sonnet 66', Wikipedia (on Shostakovich's setting, Op. 62)

  2. restful death l. 1

    The epithet 'restful' (giving rest) makes death a sleep after labour, a commonplace both classical and biblical (Job 3.17: 'There the wicked cease from troubling; and there the weary be at rest'). The cry for death, a convention of the complaint, is the same that Hamlet considers, though as the Oxquarry commentary notes his weariness has different causes. The sonnet's satire on the age belongs to a tradition of complaint against the world's injustice.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Job 3.17, King James Version; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 66, l. 1

  3. desert a beggar born l. 2

    'Desert' (stressed on the second syllable) is merit, deserving, here personified as a deserving person condemned by birth to beggary. Each line that follows similarly pairs a personified virtue or vice with an unjust fate. The complaint that worth goes unrewarded while rank and wealth are inherited was especially sharp in a patronage society in which writers and soldiers depended on court favour.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'desert'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 2

  4. needy nothing trimm'd in jollity l. 3

    'Needy nothing' is a person who is nothing in himself and lacks every good quality; 'trimm'd' means decked, adorned; 'jollity' is gaiety and also festive, showy clothing. The figure is the overdressed courtier or upstart who wins favour by appearance, perhaps, as the Oxquarry commentary suggests, at desert's expense. The line pairs with line 2: the deserving go in rags, the worthless in silks.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'jollity'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 3

  5. gilded honour shamefully misplac'd l. 5

    'Gilded' suggests the gold chains and regalia of office and also mere surface: gilding covers base metal. 'Misplac'd' means wrongly conferred. The line can be read against the late Elizabethan court and its scramble for honours, though, as the Oxquarry commentary argues of the poem's topical readings, a general sense is more likely. The pairing of an attractive adjective with a damning adverb ('gilded', 'shamefully') is typical of the list, which keeps setting appearance against desert.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 66, l. 5; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gild'

  6. strumpeted l. 6

    'Strumpet' as a verb, meaning to make a strumpet of or to brand as one, is rare; Shakespeare also uses it in The Comedy of Errors ('Being strumpeted by thy contagion', 2.2). 'Rudely' means roughly, violently. The Oxquarry commentary hears a play on 'trumpeted', suggesting public shaming. The line is one of several in which a virtue is degraded by an active verb.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'strumpet'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 6

  7. limping sway disabled l. 8

    'Sway' is rule or authority; 'limping' makes it lame, ineffective, perhaps aged. The paradox is that the strong are disabled by the weak. Metrically the line needs 'dis-a-bel-ed' (four syllables) to fill out the pentameter, an Elizabethan pronunciation. Duncan-Jones sees a possible topical reference to the ageing Queen's restraint of the Earl of Essex around 1600-01; the Oxquarry commentary, reporting this, prefers the broader sense of youth held back by age and custom.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 66, l. 8; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 8

  8. art made tongue-tied by authority l. 9

    'Art' means skill, learning, and the arts, including the poet's own. 'Tongue-tied' recurs in the Sonnets for the poet's silenced Muse (Sonnet 80, 85). Elizabethan printing and playing were controlled by licensing, and censorship was real though erratic, as Booth notes. The line has been read by later artists as speaking to their own situation: the critic Ian MacDonald argued that Shostakovich's setting of the sonnet commented obliquely on Soviet repression, a reading others have qualified.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 9; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); 'Sonnet 66', Wikipedia (Shostakovich and Pasternak)

  9. folly, doctor-like l. 10

    The 'doctor' may be the academic doctor in gown and hood (the pompous fool of Erasmus's Praise of Folly), or a physician, since 'skill' is often medical knowledge in Shakespeare; Booth discusses both. 'Controlling' means overruling, checking, censuring. The line continues the theme of line 9: pretended learning in authority silences true ability. Erasmus's Folly herself mocks the learned professions, grammarians, lawyers, physicians and theologians, which makes the costume apt.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 10; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'control'

  10. simple truth miscall'd simplicity l. 11

    'Simple' here means plain, unadorned, sincere; 'simplicity' in the period frequently meant silliness or stupidity (OED). The pun turns the virtue's own name against it, so that the line enacts the 'miscalling' it describes. The same corruption of language, in which virtues are given the names of vices, is a commonplace of satire going back to Thucydides' account of civil strife.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'simplicity'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 66, l. 11

  11. captive good attending captain ill l. 12

    The alliterating pair 'captive' and 'captain' ('captive' from Latin capere, to take; 'captain' from caput, head) makes the last item of the list a military image: the good are prisoners serving the bad as their commander. 'Attending' means waiting on, serving. The word 'captain' could mean any chief or ruler as well as a military rank, as the Oxquarry commentary notes. The line closes the catalogue with an image of total inversion: the natural order, in which the good should command, is turned upside down.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'captain'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'captive'

  12. the couplet: to die, I leave my love alone ll. 13-14

    Line 13 returns to line 1 with 'Tir'd with all these', making the poem circular, but line 14 introduces the beloved for the first time, the only personal turn in the poem. 'Alone' may mean solitary, unprotected among the evils listed, and also, with the beloved's own vulnerability to such a world, it hints at the theme of the following sonnets (67-68), which lament that the youth lives 'with infection'. The single private line weighs against eleven lines of public complaint.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 66; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 66 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 66, ll. 13-14
