Time & Brevity

Sonnet 30

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

When to the sessions of sweet silent thought
I summon up remembrance of things past,
I sigh the lack of many a thing I sought,
And with old woes new wail my dear time's waste:
Then can I drown an eye, unused to flow,
For precious friends hid in death's dateless night,
And weep afresh love's long since cancell'd woe,
And moan the expense of many a vanish'd sight:
Then can I grieve at grievances foregone,
And heavily from woe to woe tell o'er
The sad account of fore-bemoaned moan,
Which I new pay as if not paid before.
But if the while I think on thee, dear friend,
All losses are restor'd and sorrows end.

Study notes by the editors

  1. sessions l. 1

    The opening sets up a legal conceit. 'Sessions' were sittings of a court (as in quarter sessions), and 'summon' (l. 2) is the word for calling a defendant or witness before it. The court here is the speaker's own 'sweet silent thought', a private tribunal. Booth and other editors show how the legal vocabulary soon slides into the financial (waste, cancell'd, expense, account, pay), so that the poem is built on two overlapping systems of reckoning. The sibilance of line 1 (sessions, sweet, silent) sounds like a whisper or a sigh.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'session, n.'

  2. remembrance of things past l. 2

    Commentators note that the phrase occurs in the apocryphal Wisdom of Solomon 11:12, where the Egyptians suffer a double grief and 'a groaning for the remembrance of things past'; whether Shakespeare consciously echoes it is uncertain, but the joining of grief and memory is close. In 1922 C. K. Scott Moncrieff used 'Remembrance of Things Past' as the English title of Proust's A la recherche du temps perdu, a title often criticised for losing the sense of searching in the French. 'Summon up' continues the court metaphor: memories are called up like witnesses (some readers add, like spirits raised by a conjuror).

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30; The Wisdom of Solomon 11:12, in the Apocrypha of the Geneva (1560) and Authorized (1611) Bibles; Wikipedia, 'In Search of Lost Time'

  3. new wail my dear time's waste l. 4

    'New wail' means 'freshly lament'; 'wail' is a verb. 'Dear' has its double Elizabethan sense: beloved, and costly. 'Time's waste' can mean the time I have wasted, or the destruction worked by Time (waste as devastation). Booth's commentary shows how the line holds several readings at once, a characteristic of the Sonnets. It also begins the play on 'old' and 'new' that runs through the poem: old woes, new wail; weep afresh; new pay. The run of w-sounds (with, woes, wail, waste) prolongs the lament.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 30; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'waste, n.'

  4. drown an eye, unused to flow l. 5

    'Drown an eye' is a hyperbole for weeping copiously; 'unused to flow' insists on his usual self-command, which makes the present grief more striking. 'Then can I' (repeated at l. 9) has the sense 'then I am able to', even 'then I am apt to', as if remembering gives him leave to mourn. The second quatrain turns from general regret to grief for dead friends, and the water image of flooding tears contrasts with the dry ledger language that follows.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 30

  5. death's dateless night l. 6

    'Dateless' means without limit or end: a 'date' was a fixed term, especially the end of a lease or bond (compare Sonnet 18, 'summer's lease hath all too short a date'). Death is thus a contract with no expiry. The word also suggests a night with no calendar and no dawn. 'Hid' presents the dead friends as concealed rather than destroyed, which perhaps prepares for the couplet's restoration. The vocabulary of terms and dates keeps the poem's legal-financial conceit going.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dateless, adj.'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30

  6. cancell'd l. 7

    To cancel was to cross out a written debt or bond as discharged (Latin cancelli, the lattice of crossing lines drawn through an entry). The speaker had settled these old love-sorrows, but memory reopens the account and he weeps 'afresh', another turn of the old/new paradox. Some editors hear a faint pun in 'woe' and 'owe', which sharpens the sense that sorrow is a debt.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cancel, v.'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30

  7. expense l. 8

    'Expense' means expenditure, loss, the drain on one's resources (compare Sonnet 129, 'The expense of spirit in a waste of shame'). The 'vanish'd sight' may be people seen no more, or past experiences. 'Moan' is a transitive verb: he bewails the expense. The metre requires 'the expense' to be elided, roughly 'th'expense', as often in Shakespeare. The line continues the financial ledger of the poem: grief is recorded as an outlay.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'expense, n.'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30

  8. grievances foregone l. 9

    'Foregone' means past, previously experienced, not 'given up'. Repeating a word in different forms (grieve / grievances, woe to woe, moan / fore-bemoaned) is the figure called polyptoton, and its accumulation in the third quatrain makes the grieving sound mechanical and self-perpetuating, like a sum done over and over. Vendler draws attention to this verbal repetition as the poem's enactment of obsessive memory.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 30; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'foregone, adj.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30

  9. tell o'er / The sad account ll. 10-11

    'Tell' means to count (as in a bank teller, or 'telling' beads), so 'tell o'er' is to count again, adding up an account of griefs already mourned ('fore-bemoaned moan'). Line 12 completes the commercial conceit: he pays the debt again as though it had never been paid. The joke, gentle but real, is that grief is an account that can be settled in full and still be demanded again. Booth and Kerrigan both point out how thoroughly the quatrain is built from the language of money and accounts.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tell, v.'; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 30; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 30

  10. the couplet turn ll. 13-14

    After twelve lines of mourning, the couplet turns with 'But', the volta delayed to the final two lines as is usual in Shakespeare's form (three quatrains abab cdcd efef and a couplet gg). The legal and financial images are resolved: losses are 'restor'd' as if a debt were repaid in full. 'Dear friend' takes up 'dear time' (l. 4) and 'precious friends' (l. 6): one living friend outweighs all the dead. Readers divide over whether the couplet's swiftness is moving or too easy against the weight of the preceding lines; Vendler's commentary is a good starting point for that debate.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 30; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 30; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 30 (text and synopsis)
