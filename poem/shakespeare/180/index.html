Nature

Winter

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Nature

When icicles hang by the wall
And Dick the shepherd blows his nail,
And Tom bears logs into the hall,
And milk comes frozen home in pail;
When blood is nipt, and ways be foul,
Then nightly sings the staring owl
Tuwhoo!
Tuwhit! Tuwhoo! A merry note!
While greasy Joan doth keel the pot.

When all around the wind doth blow,
And coughing drowns the parson's saw,
And birds sit brooding in the snow,
And Marian's nose looks red and raw:
When roasted crabs hiss in the bowl--
Then nightly sings the staring owl
Tuwhoo!
Tuwhit! Tuwhoo! A merry note!
While greasy Joan doth keel the pot.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song of Winter l. 1

    Love's Labour's Lost (c. 1595) ends not with the expected weddings but with a year's postponement, and Armado introduces a debate in song between Ver, the spring, 'maintained by the Cuckoo', and Hiems, the winter, 'maintained by the Owl'. This is the winter song. Armado closes the play: 'The words of Mercury are harsh after the songs of Apollo.' The homely English winter scene contrasts with the play's courtly wit, and many critics see the songs as returning the play to the real seasons and duties the lords must now face. Palgrave printed it as 'Winter' in The Golden Treasury.

    Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes); H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), Book I (Project Gutenberg)

  2. blows his nail l. 2

    Literally, the shepherd blows on his fingernails to warm his hands. 'To blow one's nails' was also an idiom for waiting patiently with nothing to do; compare 3 Henry VI 2.5.3, where the shepherd 'blowing of his nails' cannot tell whether it is day or night. Dick, Tom, Joan and Marian are typical English rustic names, giving the song its village cast.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes)

  3. milk comes frozen home in pail l. 4

    The detail is realistic rather than conventional and contributes to the song's admired particularity. Unlike the pastoral idealisation common in Elizabethan verse, the scene is cold, domestic and working. Critics often cite this song as an example of Shakespeare's plain English realism. The plain monosyllables and run of 'and' clauses give the song the feel of a ballad or folk-song. The milkmaid's work outdoors is set against the kitchen work within, both part of the shared household economy.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes)

  4. When blood is nipt, and ways be foul l. 5

    'Nipt' (nipped) means pinched with cold, frostbitten; the Elizabethans thought cold literally congealed the blood. 'Ways be foul' means the roads are deep in mud, a real hardship of winter travel before paved roads. The subjunctive 'be' is a normal Elizabethan form after 'when'. The idea of cold blood connects with Galenic medicine, in which winter was the cold and moist season, ruled by phlegm. The 'nipping' cold recurs in Shakespeare (Hamlet 1.4, 'a nipping and an eager air').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2

  5. the staring owl l. 6

    'Staring' describes the owl's large fixed eyes. The owl was normally a bird of ill omen (compare Macbeth 2.2), and in the companion spring song the cuckoo's call is 'unpleasing to a married ear' because it mocks cuckolds. The inversion is witty: the spring bird threatens, while the winter owl sings 'a merry note', because the winter scene is one of shared domestic comfort.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes)

  6. Tu-whit, tu-whoo l. 7

    The refrain imitates the owl's call; 'tu-whit, tu-whoo' combines the female tawny owl's 'kewick' and the male's hoot. The short lines break the four-stress pattern of the stanza and imitate the bird's rhythm. The refrain's final line returns each time to 'greasy Joan' at the pot, so that the cold outdoors is always answered by the warm kitchen. The final line of the stanza returns to the hearth, linking the owl's cry with the comfort of shared food.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes)

  7. greasy Joan l. 9

    'Joan' was the stock name of a rustic woman or servant (compare the proverb 'Joan is as good as my lady in the dark', and Berowne's 'some men must love my lady, and some Joan' in this play, 3.1). 'Greasy' is affectionately literal: she is sweaty and spattered from cooking. Her presence at the fire anchors the song in domestic labour.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  8. keel the pot l. 9

    'Keel' derives from Old English celan, to cool. To 'keel the pot' was to prevent it from boiling over, by stirring, skimming or pouring in a little cold water. The verb survived in northern dialect. The detail completes the realistic kitchen scene that answers the frozen world outside. Some modern writers have taken it as a brewer's term for a cooling vat, but the OED sense of cooling is standard.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; The Phrase Finder, discussion of 'keel the pot'

  9. the parson's saw l. 11

    A 'saw' is a saying or moral maxim (Old English sagu), here the parson's sententious sermon. Winter coughing in the cold church drowns it out, a gently comic touch at the expense of the clergy. Compare Jaques's justice 'full of wise saws and modern instances' (As You Like It 2.7). The comedy is gentle but social: the sermon is lost in the noise of a cold congregation, much as the play's own wit is lost in the final reckoning of winter.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2

  10. Dick ... Tom ... Marian l. 13

    Dick, Tom, Joan and Marian are generic English rustic names; 'Maid Marian' was also a figure of the May games, which may give the winter scene an ironic echo of spring festivity. The comic close-up of 'Marian's nose' looking 'red and raw' with cold is typical of the song's affectionate realism. The names were common enough among English villagers to need no further identification, and the song invites the audience to recognise neighbours.

    H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, Love's Labor's Lost 5.2 (text and notes)

  11. roasted crabs l. 14

    'Crabs' are crab-apples, roasted and then put into hot ale, often spiced and sugared (a drink later called 'lambswool'). They 'hiss' as they meet the liquid. Puck in A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1 lurks 'in a gossip's bowl / In very likeness of a roasted crab'. The image of festive warmth contrasts with the 'birds brooding in the snow'. The roasted apples were also used in wassail.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; H. R. Woudhuysen, ed., Love's Labour's Lost (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1998), note to 5.2; Folger Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1
