Love

Sonnet 138

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutor'd youth,
Unlearned in the world's false subtleties.
Thus vainly thinking that she thinks me young,
Although she knows my days are past the best,
Simply I credit her false-speaking tongue:
On both sides thus is simple truth suppressed:
But wherefore says she not she is unjust?
And wherefore say not I that I am old?
O! love's best habit is in seeming trust,
And age in love, loves not to have years told:
Therefore I lie with her, and she with me,
And in our faults by lies we flatter'd be.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication: The Passionate Pilgrim (1599) l. 1

    William Jaggard's anthology The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), sold under Shakespeare's name, opened with a version of this poem, alongside Sonnet 144. The 1599 text differs in several lines ('Unskilful' for 'Unlearned', 'I smiling credit' for 'Simply I credit', 'a soothing tongue' for 'in seeming trust', a different couplet). Scholars disagree whether it is an early draft by Shakespeare or a corrupt text. The archive also holds the 1599 version as 'The Passionate Pilgrim: 1'. 'Made of truth' means entirely truthful, but also faithful in love.

    The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary); Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 138, headnote; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, headnote

  2. swears l. 1

    'Swears' implies oath-taking, which in the context of the sequence (cf. Sonnet 152, 'I am perjur'd most') carries ironic weight: oaths in these sonnets are routinely broken. Booth argues that the very fact of her swearing signals that she is not to be believed. 'Truth' here means fidelity as much as honesty, so the line is about sexual faithfulness. The present tense ('swears') suggests a habitual exchange, repeated as part of the couple's routine.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, l. 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 1; The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary)

  3. untutor'd youth l. 3

    'Untutored' means untaught, unschooled. The speaker pretends to believe her so that she will think him young and innocent. 'Unlearned in the world's false subtleties' (l. 4) completes the idea: ignorant of the clever tricks and deceits of the world. The 1599 version reads 'Unskilful in the world's false forgeries'. The irony is that he is, in fact, very well schooled in deceit.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'untutored'; The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary); Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 138, ll. 3-4

  4. vainly l. 5

    'Vainly' means in vain, uselessly, and foolishly (OED); the sense of personal vanity is also available. The speaker knows his thought is empty, since she 'knows my days are past the best'. Booth notes the circle of thinking and knowing in ll. 5-6: he thinks she thinks him young, though she knows he is not. The doubled verbs of thinking make the self-deception look elaborate and slightly comic.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vainly'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, ll. 5-6; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 138

  5. my days are past the best l. 6

    The self-portrait of an ageing lover recurs in the Sonnets (cf. Sonnets 62 and 73). Shakespeare was in his early to mid thirties when the 1599 version was printed, which seems young now but was well into middle age by Elizabethan reckoning. Biographical critics have tried to date and identify the relationship (A. L. Rowse's claim for Emilia Lanier is widely rejected); most editors treat the persona as a literary construction.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 6; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 138, l. 6; The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary)

  6. Simply ... simple truth ll. 7-8

    'Simply I credit her false-speaking tongue' means I believe her as a fool would, pretending to be simple. 'Credit' means believe, trust, with a pun on financial credit. In l. 8 'simple truth' is plain truth (cf. Sonnet 66, 'simple truth miscall'd simplicity'), and it is 'suppressed' on both sides: neither admits what both know. Booth notes the play between the two 'simple' words.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, ll. 7-8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'simply'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 7

  7. unjust l. 9

    'Unjust' in Shakespeare often means dishonest or faithless, especially in sexual relations (OED). The two rhetorical questions of ll. 9-10 ('wherefore', = why) ask why neither partner admits the truth: her infidelity, his age. The 1599 text reads 'says my love that she is young', a different and weaker question. Burrow and Duncan-Jones both gloss 'unjust' as unfaithful. The balanced pair of questions gives the two lovers equal guilt: her infidelity matches his concealment of his age.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unjust'; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 138, l. 9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 9

  8. love's best habit l. 11

    'Habit' means both a customary practice and a costume or dress (OED); love dresses itself best in a show of trust. 'Seeming trust' is ambiguous: trust that is only apparent, or trust that seems (appears) to be given. The 'O!' marks the move into general maxim; Vendler reads it as the beginning of proverbial wisdom. The 1599 version has the simpler 'a soothing tongue'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'habit'; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 138; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, l. 11

  9. years told l. 12

    'Told' means counted (as in 'telling' money or beads) and also spoken. 'Age in love' is an older person in love, who does not want the count of his years mentioned. The line is proverbial in tone, balancing l. 11. Booth notes the double sense of 'told', linking counting to the speech and lies of the poem. The maxim matches l. 11, and together the two lines generalize the couple's private bargain into a rule about love.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tell'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, l. 12; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 12

  10. I lie with her, and she with me l. 13

    'Lie with' means both to lie to and to have sexual intercourse with. The couplet makes the mutual lying the basis of the sexual relationship, the most obvious and best-known pun in the poem. The 1599 text reads 'Therefore I'll lie with love, and love with me', more general and less pointed. Critics disagree on tone: some find the couplet wry and comic, others bitter and self-lacerating (Booth stresses its cynicism; Vendler sees accommodation).

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 138, ll. 13-14; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 138; The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary)

  11. in our faults by lies we flatter'd be l. 14

    'Flatter'd' means soothed, gratified, deceived with pleasing falsehoods (OED). 'Faults' are their failings, but also, sexually, lapses or falls. The 1599 couplet ends 'Since that our faults in love thus smother'd be', so the 1609 text makes flattery, not concealment, the result. Duncan-Jones notes the pun on 'lies' and sexual lying continued from l. 13. The final word 'be' leaves the state open-ended: the lovers remain in mutual flattery rather than reaching any resolution.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flatter'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 138, l. 14; The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), poem 1; variants summarised in 'Sonnet 138', Wikipedia (secondary)

  12. Structure: questions, maxims, couplet ll. 9-14

    The argument is closely structured: ll. 1-8 set out the deception (she lies about fidelity, he pretends to believe so as to seem young); ll. 9-10 ask two parallel questions beginning 'wherefore'; ll. 11-12 answer with two proverbial maxims; the couplet ('Therefore') concludes logically, as if from a syllogism. Vendler stresses this progression from complaint to acceptance. The pattern of question and answer is clearer in the 1609 version than in 1599.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 138; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 138, headnote; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 138 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine
