Sonnet 146

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Religion

Poor soul, the centre of my sinful earth,
My sinful earth these rebel powers array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Painting thy outward walls so costly gay?
Why so large cost, having so short a lease,
Dost thou upon thy fading mansion spend?
Shall worms, inheritors of this excess,
Eat up thy charge? Is this thy body's end?
Then soul, live thou upon thy servant's loss,
And let that pine to aggravate thy store;
Buy terms divine in selling hours of dross;
Within be fed, without be rich no more:
So shall thou feed on Death, that feeds on men,
And Death once dead, there's no more dying then.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Poor soul: a dialogue of soul and body l. 1

    Sonnet 146 is unique in the sequence as a direct address to the soul, in the medieval tradition of debates between body and soul and of the meditations on death (memento mori) common in Elizabethan devotional writing. 'Sinful earth' is the body, made of earth or dust (Genesis 2:7; 3:19); the soul is at its 'centre', as the earth was at the centre of the Ptolemaic universe. It stands among the Dark Lady sonnets yet does not mention love, and critics differ on whether it is a sincere religious poem or another witty exercise; Vendler reads it as argumentative and self-reproachful, while others stress its traditional piety.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 146; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 146 (text and synopsis)

  2. the line 2 crux l. 2

    The Quarto line, 'My sinfull earth these rebbell powres that thee array', repeats the end of line 1 and has twelve syllables; it is generally agreed to be a compositor's slip. The archive's text keeps the repeated words but drops 'that thee', restoring the syllable count. Editors have proposed many substitutes for the first two syllables, among them 'Fool'd by', 'Thrall to', 'Lord of', 'Pressed by' and 'Feeding'; 'feeding' is the one most often favoured, and Duncan-Jones discusses it in her note. No reading is certain. 'Rebel powers' are the body's unruly passions in revolt against the soul, their rightful ruler.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), the first Quarto; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 146; Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 146'; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 146

  3. array l. 2

    'Array' can mean to dress (the body as the soul's clothing) or to marshal troops ('rebel powers' as an army). Booth and others note that both senses are active: the body is at once a costly garment and a rebel force. The double sense prepares the next lines, where the soul 'paints' its outward walls while starving within. The word was common in both senses in the period, so the ambiguity would have been immediately available to Shakespeare's readers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'array, v.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 146

  4. pine within and suffer dearth l. 3

    'Pine' means to waste away, especially through hunger; 'dearth' is scarcity, famine. The imagery of feeding and starving runs through the poem and returns in reversed form in line 10 ('let that pine'), where the body is to starve instead. The paradox that outward richness means inward poverty is a commonplace of Christian moral writing, as in Christ's warning against laying up treasures on earth (Matthew 6:19-20).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pine, v.'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 6:19-20; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146

  5. Painting thy outward walls l. 4

    The body is a house or fortress whose outer walls the soul is painting. 'Painting' recalls cosmetics, which Elizabethan moralists denounced and which the Dark Lady sonnets associate with false beauty (Sonnet 127). 'Costly gay' means expensively showy. The body as a castle with rebel powers inside is an old allegory, found for instance in Spenser's House of Alma (The Faerie Queene II.ix).

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146; Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene, Book II, canto ix (the House of Alma); John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 146

  6. so short a lease l. 5

    The soul is a tenant, not an owner: it holds the body on a lease that will soon run out at death. The poem's legal and financial vocabulary (cost, lease, spend, inheritors, charge, terms, buy, selling, store) asks the soul to be a wiser economist. Compare Sonnet 18's 'summer's lease' and the lease of beauty in Sonnet 13. A tenant who spends lavishly on a house he must soon leave is a fool, and that is precisely the soul's folly here.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lease, n.3'; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 146

  7. fading mansion l. 6

    'Mansion' meant a dwelling or abode in general. The 'fading mansion' of the body recalls 2 Corinthians 5:1, where Paul calls the body 'our earthly house of this tabernacle' that will be dissolved, and contrasts with Christ's promise, 'In my Father's house are many mansions' (John 14:2). The soul is spending on a house that is crumbling while neglecting the eternal one.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Corinthians 5:1; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 14:2; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146

  8. worms, inheritors l. 7

    A traditional memento mori image: the worms are the heirs who will consume the body's estate in the grave (compare Job 19:26, 'though after my skin worms destroy this body', and Hamlet's 'Your worm is your only emperor for diet', 4.3). 'Charge' (l. 8) means the expense, or the thing in one's keeping, so the worms eat up the investment. 'Is this thy body's end?' puns on end as death and as purpose: is this what the body is for?

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 19:26; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 146

  9. thy servant's loss l. 9

    The third quatrain gives the remedy, reversing the soul's earlier conduct. The body is the soul's 'servant', a hierarchy affirmed by Platonic and Christian moralists alike. Let the body 'pine' (starve) so as to 'aggravate thy store', where 'aggravate' means increase, add weight to (from Latin aggravare, to make heavy), a sense now obsolete. The ascetic logic is that what is withheld from the flesh enriches the spirit.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aggravate, v.'; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 146; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146

  10. Buy terms divine in selling hours of dross l. 11

    'Terms' are periods of tenure, as in a lease, so the soul exchanges its short lease (l. 5) for divine, eternal terms. 'Dross' is the scum thrown off in smelting metal, hence worthless refuse. The commercial bargain recalls Gospel sayings about gaining or losing one's soul (Mark 8:36, 'what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?'). Line 12 sums up the reversal in balanced, chiastic form: within be fed, without be rich no more.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dross, n.'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Mark 8:36; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 146

  11. feed on Death ... Death once dead ll. 13-14

    The feeding imagery culminates: Death, who devours human beings, will itself be devoured by the soul. The final line echoes Paul, 'The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death' (1 Corinthians 15:26) and 'Death is swallowed up in victory' (15:54), words familiar from the burial service. Donne's Holy Sonnet 'Death be not proud' ends with a similar paradox ('Death, thou shalt die'). Critics debate the tone: Vendler finds the soul's victory partly harsh and punitive, while others read the couplet as plain Christian triumph. The archive prints 'shall thou' where most editions have 'shalt thou'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:54; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 146; John Donne, Holy Sonnets, 'Death be not proud' (printed 1633)
