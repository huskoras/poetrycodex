Sonnet 130

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Religion

My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;
Coral is far more red, than her lips red:
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.
I have seen roses damask'd, red and white,
But no such roses see I in her cheeks;
And in some perfumes is there more delight
Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.
I love to hear her speak, yet well I know
That music hath a far more pleasing sound:
I grant I never saw a goddess go;
My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:
And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare,
As any she belied with false compare.

Study notes by the editors

  1. My mistress' eyes: anti-Petrarchan praise l. 1

    Sonnet 130 belongs to the group addressed to or about a woman (Sonnets 127-152), often called the 'dark lady'. It takes up the blazon, the itemised praise of a mistress's body inherited from Petrarch and multiplied in English sonnet sequences of the 1590s, and denies each conventional comparison in turn: eyes, lips, breasts, hair, cheeks, breath, voice, gait. Booth describes the poem as gently mocking the mechanical use of Petrarchan metaphors; Patrick Cruttwell (1960) suggested a poem of Thomas Watson's Hekatompathia (1582) as a target, and E. G. Rogers argued for a chain from Watson through Richard Linche's Diella (1596). The poem is not an insult to the woman but to bad poets: its couplet makes the realism a form of praise.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 130; Patrick Cruttwell, The Shakespearean Moment (Random House, 1960); E. G. Rogers, Shakespeare Quarterly 11.2 (1960), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 130'; E. G. Rogers, 'Sonnet CXXX: Watson to Linche to Shakespeare', Shakespeare Quarterly 11.2 (1960); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 130

  2. eyes ... nothing like the sun l. 1

    Comparing the mistress's eyes to the sun (or her eyes' brightness to stars) was a stock figure: the eyes shine, warm, and dazzle the lover. Shakespeare himself uses the conceit seriously elsewhere (Romeo and Juliet 2.2, 'It is the east, and Juliet is the sun'). Here 'nothing like' is blunt and colloquial, and the plain monosyllables deliberately deflate the convention. In Sonnet 127 the mistress's eyes are said to be 'raven black', which may explain why they are nothing like the sun.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 130; Patrick Cruttwell, The Shakespearean Moment (Random House, 1960); E. G. Rogers, Shakespeare Quarterly 11.2 (1960), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 130'

  3. Coral ... lips l. 2

    Lips 'red as coral' was a commonplace of Elizabethan love poetry. The line's word order is awkward on purpose ('Coral is far more red, than her lips red'), as if the speaker were measuring rather than praising. The inverted, repetitive syntax mocks the comparison-making habit itself, turning the beloved into an object of measurement against a standard she fails to meet.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 130

  4. dun l. 3

    'Dun' is a dull brownish-grey colour, the colour of a mouse or a horse's coat (OED 'dun' adj.). Poets routinely made a lady's breasts or neck whiter than snow or ivory. The conditional 'If snow be white' treats the convention as a logical premise and draws an unflattering conclusion. The plain word brings the poem 'down to earth', one commentary observes, after the lofty claims of other poets. The darkness of skin also links to the dark colouring of the mistress elsewhere in this group.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dun'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130

  5. black wires l. 4

    Fine gold thread or wire was a common simile for fair hair; Richard Linche's Diella (1596) praises hair that 'exceeds gold forced in the smallest wire'. The comic shock is that the wires on this mistress's head are black. Commentators note that the conventional comparison already made hair sound metallic and stiff; Shakespeare's literal-minded use exposes how odd it is. Rogers argued that the line responds directly to Linche (and through him to Watson).

    Patrick Cruttwell, The Shakespearean Moment (Random House, 1960); E. G. Rogers, Shakespeare Quarterly 11.2 (1960), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 130'; E. G. Rogers, 'Sonnet CXXX: Watson to Linche to Shakespeare', Shakespeare Quarterly 11.2 (1960); Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130

  6. damask'd l. 5

    The damask rose (named after Damascus) was a fragrant pink rose; 'damask'd' means marked or mingled with red and white, like the rose or like damask fabric with its woven patterns (OED 'damasked'). Gerard's Herbal (1597) distinguishes white, red and damask roses. Petrarchan poets regularly found roses (and lilies) in a lady's cheeks; the speaker reports, with mock-scientific candour, that he sees no such roses there.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'damasked'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130

  7. reeks l. 8

    'Reek' originally meant to emit smoke or vapour (OED 'reek' v.1), and in Shakespeare's time it could simply mean 'to be exhaled'. Its modern sense 'to stink' was not yet dominant, though the word already suggested steamy, sweaty or smoky emanations. Editors therefore differ over how harsh the line is: The line is usually read as comic rather than disgusting. The line still undercuts the convention that a lady's breath is sweeter than perfume.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reek'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 130

  8. music ll. 9-10

    Poets compared the mistress's voice to music or to the harmony of the spheres. Lines 9-10 are the first in which the speaker states a positive feeling ('I love to hear her speak'), even as he concedes the comparison fails. The shift prepares the couplet: liking does not depend on comparison. The phrase 'yet well I know' gives the confession a tone of tender realism rather than mockery.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 130

  9. I never saw a goddess go l. 11

    'Go' means 'walk', as the next line makes clear (OED 'go' v., sense 1). Love poets claimed their ladies moved like goddesses; the classical source is Aeneid 1.405, where Venus, disguised as a huntress, is revealed to Aeneas by her walk ('vera incessu patuit dea', the true goddess was revealed by her gait). The speaker says he has never seen a goddess walk, so he cannot compare; but his mistress, he knows, 'treads on the ground', a down-to-earth (literally) contrast with goddesses who glide above it.

    Virgil, Aeneid 1.405 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'go'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130

  10. treads on the ground l. 12

    The line completes the contrast with the goddess of line 11. 'Treads' is a heavy, physical verb, emphasising weight and contact with the ground. The line is the last of the denials, and it describes not a defect but a fact: she is mortal and real. That factual plainness becomes the basis of the couplet's praise. Editors also observe that the poem's denials are spread evenly through the three quatrains, two or three per quatrain, so that the catalogue feels like a systematic inventory of the blazon; the twelfth line closes the inventory with the most basic fact of all, that she is a woman who walks on earth, before the couplet turns the whole list into praise.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 130

  11. the couplet: as rare l. 13

    The couplet reverses the poem's apparent mockery. 'By heaven' is an oath, and 'rare' means precious, exceptional. 'My love' may mean both his beloved and his love for her. The claim is that she is as remarkable as any woman whose praise was falsified by exaggerated similes, so true praise must be plain. Vendler and Booth both read the couplet as the poem's key: the target throughout has been false poetry, not the mistress.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 130; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 130; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130

  12. any she belied with false compare l. 14

    'Any she' uses the pronoun as a noun, 'any woman' (OED 'she' n.). 'Belied' means falsely represented, told lies about (OED 'belie' v.). 'Compare' is a noun, 'comparison', common in Elizabethan usage. Women described with conventional similes are 'belied': the false comparisons tell lies about them, even when meant as praise. One commentary suggests a glance at 'compeer' (an equal), though most editors take 'compare' in its simple sense.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'belie'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'compare'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 130
