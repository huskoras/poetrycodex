Religion

Sonnet 35

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Religion

No more be griev'd at that which thou hast done:
Roses have thorns, and silver fountains mud:
Clouds and eclipses stain both moon and sun,
And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud.
All men make faults, and even I in this,
Authorizing thy trespass with compare,
Myself corrupting, salving thy amiss,
Excusing thy sins more than thy sins are;
For to thy sensual fault I bring in sense;
Thy adverse party is thy advocate,
And 'gainst myself a lawful plea commence:
Such civil war is in my love and hate,
That I an accessary needs must be,
To that sweet thief which sourly robs from me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. No more be griev'd: the friend's offence l. 1

    Sonnets 33-35 deal with an offence by the friend against the poet; 33 and 34 use the image of the sun hidden by clouds, and 34 ends with the friend's tears as 'ransom'. Here the poet tells the friend to stop grieving. The offence is never specified, but many critics connect it with Sonnets 40-42, in which the friend takes the poet's mistress, because of the 'sensual fault' (l. 9) and the 'thief' of the couplet. The opening line's simple generosity is undercut as the sonnet goes on.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 1; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 35; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 35 (text, notes and synopsis)

  2. Roses have thorns ... fountains mud ll. 2-4

    The catalogue (thorny roses, muddy springs, clouds and eclipses on sun and moon, the canker in the bud) uses proverbial commonplaces: 'No rose without a thorn' is listed in Tilley's dictionary of Elizabethan proverbs. Each pair puts something lovely beside a defect. The examples also flatter the friend by comparing him to roses, fountains, sun and moon. Vendler and Booth point out that the poet later accuses himself for making such excusing comparisons ('Authorizing thy trespass with compare').

    M. P. Tilley, A Dictionary of the Proverbs in England in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries (Ann Arbor, 1950), s.v. rose; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 35; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, ll. 2-4

  3. Clouds and eclipses stain l. 3

    'Stain' means darken or dim, but also disgrace or sully; the moral sense prepares for 'sins'. Sonnet 33 ends 'Suns of the world may stain when heaven's sun staineth', and this line echoes and generalises it. Eclipses were also traditionally seen as ominous (compare 'The mortal moon hath her eclipse endur'd', Sonnet 107). The movement from earthly to celestial examples widens the excuse to the whole cosmos: if even sun and moon have blemishes, the friend's fault is part of nature's order rather than a personal failing.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, l. 3; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 3; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 33 (text, notes and synopsis)

  4. canker l. 4

    In Shakespeare 'canker' usually means the cankerworm, a caterpillar destroying buds and leaves (it can also mean the wild dog-rose, as in Sonnet 54, or an ulcer). Unlike the thorn or the mud, the canker 'lives' inside the sweetest bud, so the series ends with corruption at the very heart of beauty. Sonnets 70 and 95 return to the image ('canker vice', 'Like a canker in the fragrant rose').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'canker'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 4; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 35, l. 4 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online)

  5. All men make faults, and even I in this ll. 5-6

    The line begins with a proverbial excuse ('Every man has his fault') and then turns against the speaker: 'even I in this' means 'even I am at fault in this', i.e. in writing these consoling lines. This is the sonnet's turn, arriving early in line 5 rather than at line 9; Vendler reads the poem as a self-accusation in which forgiveness becomes complicity.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 35; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, ll. 5-6; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 5

  6. Authorizing thy trespass with compare l. 6

    'Authorizing' means sanctioning or giving warrant (stressed on the second syllable, au-THOR-iz-ing). 'Compare' is a noun, comparison or simile, as also in Sonnet 21 ('a couplement of proud compare') and 130 ('belied with false compare'). 'Trespass' is a sin or offence, with legal and biblical force ('forgive us our trespasses'). The poet recognises that poetic comparison can become moral excuse, a self-conscious comment on the methods of the sonnet itself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'compare'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, l. 6; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 6

  7. salving thy amiss l. 7

    To 'salve' is to heal with an ointment, and figuratively to smooth over or explain away. 'Amiss' is used as a noun, 'misdeed' (OED records this use). The poet is 'corrupting' himself by excusing another's corruption. The medical image recalls Sonnet 34, in which the friend's apology is a 'salve' that heals the wound but cannot cure the disgrace. The paradox is sharp: by healing the friend's wound, the poet infects himself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'amiss'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'salve'; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 34 (text, notes and synopsis)

  8. Excusing thy sins more than thy sins are l. 8

    The line has been read in two ways: 'excusing your sins more than they deserve to be excused' or 'making excuses that are larger than your sins'. Either way, the excess of excuse becomes the poet's own sin. The 1609 Quarto reads 'Excusing their sins more then their sins are', and most editors emend to 'thy', as in Sonnet 27; some retain 'their' as meaning 'the sins of roses, fountains, moon' or men generally (Booth discusses both).

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, l. 8; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 35, l. 8

  9. to thy sensual fault I bring in sense l. 9

    'Sensual fault' is a sin of the senses or of lust, the strongest hint at the nature of the offence. 'Sense' can mean reason or reasoned argument, and the poet brings it 'in' as an advocate brings in a plea. The legal language now becomes explicit: the poet is counsel for the defence in a case against himself. The chiming of 'sensual' and 'sense' turns the antithesis between body and mind into wordplay, as if reason were simply recruited to serve appetite.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, l. 9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 9; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sensual'

  10. adverse party ... advocate ... lawful plea ll. 10-11

    'Adverse party' is the opposing side in a lawsuit; 'advocate' is a pleader in court; 'commence ... a lawful plea' is to begin legal proceedings. The poet, who should be the friend's adversary, becomes his advocate and prosecutes himself instead. Shakespeare draws on the law-court vocabulary common in the Sonnets (compare 46 and 87). The paradox culminates in 'civil war' within the poet's feelings.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, ll. 10-11; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, ll. 10-11; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 35

  11. civil war ... love and hate l. 12

    Civil war, the war of a state against itself, is a strong image for internal conflict; in the 1590s the memory of the Wars of the Roses, dramatised in Shakespeare's history plays, gave the figure weight. 'Love and hate' is a classic Catullan antithesis (Catullus 85, 'Odi et amo'), here applied to the friend. The admission of 'hate' is unusual in the Fair Youth sonnets and reveals the cost of the generosity of line 1.

    Catullus, Carmina 85 ('Odi et amo'), Latin text, The Latin Library; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 35; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 12

  12. accessary l. 13

    An 'accessary' (spelling of 'accessory') in law is a person who aids or abets a crime without being the principal. The poet's excuses make him an accomplice to the 'thief' who robs him. 'Needs must' means necessarily. Booth notes the paradox that the victim is also complicit in the crime against himself. The legal sense would have been familiar from the courts: in felony an accessory could be punished alongside the principal, so the poet half condemns himself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'accessory'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 35, l. 13; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 35 (text, notes and synopsis)

  13. that sweet thief which sourly robs from me l. 14

    The oxymoron reproduces the 'love and hate' of line 12. 'Sourly' describes the bitter effect of the theft on the poet. What the friend has stolen is unstated; many readers connect it to the mistress in Sonnets 40-42 ('I do forgive thy robbery, gentle thief', 40.9). The link is plausible but not proved. Editors note how the sweet/sour pairing reproduces in miniature the poem's mixed feelings.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 40 (text, notes and synopsis); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 35, l. 14; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (2002), note on Sonnet 35, l. 14
