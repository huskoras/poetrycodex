Nature

Sonnet 94

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

They that have power to hurt, and will do none,
That do not do the thing they most do show,
Who, moving others, are themselves as stone,
Unmoved, cold, and to temptation slow;
They rightly do inherit heaven's graces,
And husband nature's riches from expense;
They are the lords and owners of their faces,
Others, but stewards of their excellence.
The summer's flower is to the summer sweet,
Though to itself, it only live and die,
But if that flower with base infection meet,
The basest weed outbraves his dignity:
For sweetest things turn sourest by their deeds;
Lilies that fester, smell far worse than weeds.

Study notes by the editors

  1. They that have power to hurt l. 1

    The octave seems to praise people of self-possession who do not use their power to wound. But 'power to hurt' may include sexual power, the power to break hearts, and the cold, unmoved figure of ll. 3-4 sounds less admirable than calculating. Empson's famous chapter reads the sonnet as an ironic network of praise and blame directed at someone like the young man of the surrounding sonnets (Sonnet 93 speaks of a beautiful but possibly false face). Booth argues the syntax never lets the reader settle. Older critics such as Tyler took the praise more simply, while noting Angelo in Measure for Measure as the corrupt version of such a character.

    William Empson, 'They That Have Power', in Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 3; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, l. 1; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 94, l. 1 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 94

  2. do not do the thing they most do show l. 2

    The line hangs on the repeated verb 'do' (do not do / most do show). It may mean they do not do the hurt they show they could do, or they do not act on the passion their beauty seems to promise. Either way the line separates outward show from deed, a theme of Sonnet 93 too. Booth notes the deliberately slippery syntax; Duncan-Jones relates it to the young man's beauty, which invites a love he does not return.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, l. 2; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 2; William Empson, 'They That Have Power', in Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 3

  3. moving others, are themselves as stone ll. 3-4

    The paradox of the 'unmoved mover' sits in 'moving others ... Unmoved'. Stone is an image of hardness and coldness (a Petrarchan commonplace for the cruel beloved), and Shakespeare stresses the coldness with 'Unmoved, cold'. 'To temptation slow' could be virtue (chastity, Stoic constancy) or mere lack of feeling. Tyler compares Hamlet's praise of Horatio, who is 'not a pipe for Fortune's finger'. Empson and later readers hear coldness, not virtue, in the accumulated adjectives.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), notes on Sonnet 94, ll. 2-8 (public domain; Internet Archive); William Empson, 'They That Have Power', in Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 3; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, ll. 3-4

  4. inherit heaven's graces l. 5

    'Inherit' recalls the Beatitudes ('the meek ... shall inherit the earth', Matthew 5:5) and Matthew 25:34 ('inherit the kingdom prepared for you'). 'Heaven's graces' are both spiritual grace and the physical graces (beauty) given by heaven. 'Rightly' means justly, deservedly, but also by right of inheritance. Booth notes that the economic and religious senses mix here, leading to 'husband' and 'stewards'.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:34; Bible (KJV), Matthew 5:5; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, l. 5

  5. husband ... from expense l. 6

    'Husband' as a verb means to manage thriftily, to save (OED), from the sense of a householder who manages an estate. 'Expense' means spending, waste. They do not squander their beauty or energy, which Tyler glosses as not spending themselves in passionate outbursts. Readers of the procreation sonnets (1-17) will recall the opposite charge, that the young man wastes himself by not having children; Booth and Duncan-Jones note the tension. 'Expense' returns, in a sexual sense, in Sonnet 129.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'husband'; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 94, l. 6 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 6

  6. lords and owners of their faces l. 7

    To be 'lord and owner' is to have full legal possession. They govern their own faces, showing no involuntary emotion (Tyler: not giving ready expression to feeling), and they possess their beauty absolutely rather than holding it in trust. The phrase looks back to Sonnet 93, where the friend's face may hide falseness. Vendler and Booth note the property language that runs from 'inherit' through 'husband' to 'owners' and 'stewards'.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 94, l. 7 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 94; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, l. 7

  7. stewards of their excellence l. 8

    A steward manages property for an owner and must account for it. The line is ambiguous: 'their excellence' may be the excellence of these self-possessed people, served by others; or other people's own excellence, which they merely hold on loan. The parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) and the parable of the unjust steward (Luke 16:1-8) lie behind the language; 1 Peter 4:10 calls Christians 'good stewards of the manifold grace of God'. Empson notes the irony that the parable of the talents condemns the servant who hoards rather than uses his gift, which turns the octave's praise of 'husbanding' against itself.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:14-30; Bible (KJV), Luke 16:1-8; William Empson, 'They That Have Power', in Some Versions of Pastoral (London: Chatto & Windus, 1935), ch. 3; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 94, l. 8 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online)

  8. The summer's flower ll. 9-10

    The turn at l. 9 abandons the economic language for an image from nature. The flower is sweet to the summer even if it 'only' lives and dies for itself, in solitude. The lines may praise self-contained beauty or hint at its sterility, recalling the self-loving youth of the procreation sonnets (cf. Sonnet 54, also on flowers and scent). Tyler's headnote says the flower is still sweet though its sweetness is wasted on the desert air (the phrase is Gray's, from his Elegy). Vendler treats the sestet as a revision of the octave's ideal.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), headnote to Sonnet 94 (public domain; Internet Archive); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 94; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, ll. 9-10

  9. base infection l. 11

    'Base' means low, ignoble, and its repetition in 'basest weed' (l. 12) ties the corruption to the lowest of plants. 'Infection' suggests a canker or blight in the flower, and morally, contamination by vice. Many readers connect the line with the canker-worm imagery elsewhere in the sequence (Sonnets 35, 70, 95) and with venereal infection, though that reading is not certain. Booth discusses the range of sense.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, l. 11; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 11; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'infection'

  10. outbraves his dignity l. 12

    'Brave' in Elizabethan English often means splendid, finely dressed; to 'outbrave' is to outdo in splendour or to defy (OED). The weed, despised but uncorrupted, now looks finer than the infected flower. 'His dignity' means its rank or worth ('his' as the old neuter possessive). The line sets up the proverbial couplet. The reversal of high and low (flower beneath weed) is the sestet's key move, since rank and beauty count for nothing once corruption sets in.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'outbrave'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 12; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 94, l. 12

  11. sweetest things turn sourest l. 13

    The Latin proverb 'the corruption of the best is the worst' was widely known in the period, and editors gloss the line as proverbial. 'By their deeds' brings back the distinction between showing and doing in l. 2: what people do, not what they show, determines their worth. The alliteration and antithesis (sweetest / sourest) give the couplet the force of a maxim.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 13; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 94, ll. 13-14; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 94 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine

  12. Lilies that fester (Edward III) l. 14

    The line appears in Edward III, Act 2 (scene numbering varies), in the Earl of Warwick's speech on the corruption of great persons, in a scene many scholars assign to Shakespeare. Which came first is disputed: the play was printed in 1596, but the sonnet may have been written earlier or later. Lilies are emblems of purity, so their festering (rotting, putrefying) makes the stench of corruption worse by contrast. Tyler takes the lilies as colourless, passionless natures, as against the blushing rose. The poem ends on a moral smell, answering the 'sweet' flower of l. 9.

    The Reign of King Edward III (Q1 1596), 2.1 (line numbering varies by edition); Internet Shakespeare Editions transcription of Q1; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 94, l. 14 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 94, l. 14; Michael Delahoyde, annotated notes to Edward III, Act 2, Washington State University
