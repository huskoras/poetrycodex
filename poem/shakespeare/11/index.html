Nature

Sonnet 12

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

When I do count the clock that tells the time,
And see the brave day sunk in hideous night;
When I behold the violet past prime,
And sable curls, all silvered o'er with white;
When lofty trees I see barren of leaves,
Which erst from heat did canopy the herd,
And summer's green all girded up in sheaves,
Borne on the bier with white and bristly beard,
Then of thy beauty do I question make,
That thou among the wastes of time must go,
Since sweets and beauties do themselves forsake
And die as fast as they see others grow;
And nothing 'gainst Time's scythe can make defence
Save breed, to brave him when he takes thee hence.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When I do count the clock: the sonnet of the hours l. 1

    One of the seventeen 'procreation' or 'marriage' sonnets (1-17) urging the young man to have a child. 'Count the clock' means to count the strokes as the clock strikes the hour, so the opening line itself ticks: its monosyllables fall like strokes. Critics have long observed that the sonnet about the clock stands twelfth in the 1609 sequence, just as Sonnet 60, on minutes, stands sixtieth; whether the numbering is Shakespeare's design or a happy accident is debated. The sonnet is built on a sequence of 'When ... When ... Then' clauses: eight lines of observed decay before the turn to the young man at line 9.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, l. 1; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 12; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 12 (text, notes and synopsis)

  2. brave l. 2

    In Elizabethan English 'brave' commonly meant handsome, splendid, showily dressed (OED, sense 'finely dressed, splendid'). The 'brave day' is the bright day in its glory, which sinks into 'hideous night', a phrase that makes nightfall a small death. Shakespeare repeats the word in the couplet ('to brave him'), where it is a verb meaning to challenge or defy. The echo binds the poem's first image of loss to its final answer: the splendour that time takes can be set against time only by producing new splendour in a child. Booth notes how such repeated words create a pattern beneath the argument.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brave'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 12, ll. 2, 14; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, l. 14 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online)

  3. the violet past prime l. 3

    'Prime' is the first or best season (spring, and the springtime of life; Latin primus, first). The violet blooms early and wilts quickly, which made it a standard emblem of brief beauty; Laertes calls Hamlet's love 'A violet in the youth of primy nature, / Forward, not permanent' (Hamlet 1.3). Shakespeare's line therefore shows beauty failing even at the season of its peak. The colours of the octave are carefully ranged: violet, sable, silver, white, green, and white again, a palette that runs from youth to age; the note reproduced on Shakespeare Online lists this colour imagery.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, ll. 3-4 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'prime'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, l. 3

  4. sable curls, all silvered o'er l. 4

    'Sable' is black (also the heraldic name for black). The 1609 Quarto reads 'or siluer'd ore with white'. Edmond Malone emended 'or' to 'all', treating it as a misprint, and most editors follow him; the archive text prints this emended reading. A minority (e.g. T. G. Tucker, 1924) keep 'or' and read it as the heraldic colour gold, though the syntax is awkward. The image of a dark head grizzled with age is close to Horatio's description of the dead King's beard, 'A sable silver'd' (Hamlet 1.2). Either way the line moves from colour to whiteness, the colour of age that returns in the 'white and bristly beard'.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, l. 4 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 12, l. 4

  5. erst ... canopy the herd ll. 5-6

    'Erst' means formerly, a word already slightly archaic in 1609. The trees, now 'barren of leaves', once spread a 'canopy' over the herd; the verb makes them a cloth of state held over a throne or a bed, so that the bare boughs are a dismantled grandeur. 'Barren' quietly carries the poem's argument, since the procreation sonnets press the young man not to be barren himself. Compare the 'bare ruined choirs' of Sonnet 73, where leafless boughs are again the image of age.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'erst'; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, l. 6 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, ll. 5-6

  6. summer's green ... girded up in sheaves ll. 7-8

    The green crop of summer is cut and 'girded up' (bound tightly) in sheaves, then loaded on a cart. Shakespeare turns the harvest wagon into a funeral 'bier' (the frame on which a corpse is carried) and the bearded ears of barley or wheat into the 'white and bristly beard' of a dead old man. The conceit makes a scene of plenty into a scene of mourning. Harvest was more usually a figure of fulfilment; here the poet stresses only the ending. There may also be a memory of the biblical image of man as grass that is cut down (Isaiah 40:6-7).

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, ll. 7-8 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 12, ll. 7-8; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 40:6-7

  7. white and bristly beard l. 8

    'Bristly' describes both the stiff whiskers on barley and wheat ears and the stubble of an old man's chin. The colour 'white' recalls the 'silvered' curls of line 4, so the two images of human ageing frame the two images from nature (violet, trees). The effect is that all the octave's examples, natural and human, end in the same pale, bearded figure of Time himself, who appears with his scythe in line 13. Vendler notes how the octave's details become steadily more personified.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 12; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, l. 8

  8. When ... Then: the turn l. 9

    The sonnet is a single sentence of the form 'When I see X ... Then I ask about you', an argumentative pattern Shakespeare uses also in Sonnets 15 and 64. The first eight lines gather evidence; line 9 draws the inference. 'Of thy beauty do I question make' means 'I begin to doubt about your beauty' or 'I put your beauty in question', with a faint legal colouring of examination. The third quatrain generalises (all sweets die), and the couplet offers the only remedy. The structure is close to the logical sonnet of Petrarchan tradition, with the turn after the octave.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 12; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 12, l. 9; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 12 (text, notes and synopsis)

  9. the wastes of time l. 10

    'Wastes' can be read in more than one sense, as Booth stresses: the ravages or devastations that time causes; the desolate tracts ('wastes', wildernesses) of passing time; and the waste products or discards of time. The youth 'must go' among them, joining the violets, trees and harvest already described. The phrase anticipates the 'wasteful Time' of Sonnet 15 and the 'waste of shame' of Sonnet 129.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 12, l. 10; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'waste'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, l. 10

  10. sweets and beauties do themselves forsake ll. 11-12

    'Sweets and beauties' are sweet and beautiful things. They 'forsake' themselves, that is, they cease to be what they were, as the violet ceases to be fresh. Line 12 states the law of the whole procreation group: things die at the same rate as others grow, so beauty survives only as a succession. The Ovidian sense of ceaseless change (Metamorphoses 15) lies behind this, but Shakespeare uses it for a practical argument: since replacement is the rule of nature, the youth should replace himself.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, ll. 11-12

  11. Time's scythe l. 13

    The image of Time with a scythe (and often an hourglass) came into Renaissance emblem books through the conflation of Saturn or Kronos, the harvest god with a sickle, with Chronos, Time (Greek chronos). The scythe completes the harvest imagery of lines 7-8: the youth will be cut down like the grain. Shakespeare uses the figure repeatedly in the Sonnets ('his scythe' in 60, 'his bending sickle' in 116, 'his sickle, hour' in 126). Erwin Panofsky's essay 'Father Time' in Studies in Iconology (1939) is the standard account of the iconography.

    Erwin Panofsky, 'Father Time', in Studies in Iconology (New York: Oxford University Press, 1939); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 12, l. 13; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scythe'

  12. Save breed, to brave him l. 14

    'Breed' here means offspring, children; 'brave' means defy or challenge (picking up 'brave day' in line 2). 'Hence' is 'from here', that is, from life. The couplet condenses the argument of Sonnets 1-17, which derives partly from Erasmus's epistle urging a young man to marry (translated in Thomas Wilson's Arte of Rhetorique, 1553). Note that the poet does not yet offer his own verse as a remedy, as he will in Sonnets 15-19; here biology is the only defence.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 12, l. 14 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique (1553), including Erasmus's 'Epistle to persuade a young gentleman to marriage'; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (2002), note on Sonnet 12, l. 14
