Love

Sonnet 20

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

A woman's face with nature's own hand painted,
Hast thou, the master mistress of my passion;
A woman's gentle heart, but not acquainted
With shifting change, as is false women's fashion:
An eye more bright than theirs, less false in rolling,
Gilding the object whereupon it gazeth;
A man in hue all 'hues' in his controlling,
Which steals men's eyes and women's souls amazeth.
And for a woman wert thou first created;
Till Nature, as she wrought thee, fell a-doting,
And by addition me of thee defeated,
By adding one thing to my purpose nothing.
But since she prick'd thee out for women's pleasure,
Mine be thy love and thy love's use their treasure.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Feminine rhymes throughout l. 1

    All fourteen lines end in feminine (two-syllable, falling) rhymes: painted/acquainted, passion/fashion, rolling/controlling, and so on. Critics commonly observe that it is the only sonnet in the sequence with feminine rhymes throughout (Sonnet 87 comes close); the formal choice enacts the poem's subject, a man with a woman's beauty. Some critics further connect the extra syllable at line-end with the 'one thing' added by Nature in line 12.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 20', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet

  2. with nature's own hand painted l. 1

    'Painted' plays on the cosmetic painting of women's faces, a frequent target of satire. The youth's beauty is natural, made by Nature as artist. The motif recurs in the sonnets' attack on cosmetics and false hair (Sonnets 67-8, 127). Nature is personified throughout the poem as a craftswoman who first intends to make a woman and then changes her design.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  3. master mistress of my passion l. 2

    The Quarto prints 'master Mistris'. Readings vary: the youth is a male version of the sonneteer's mistress; he is the dominant 'mistress' who rules the poet's passion; or 'passion' means a love poem, as in Thomas Watson's 'passions' (1582), so that the youth is the subject of this very poem. Critics have argued the phrase keeps the addressee's gender uncertain. Whether the sonnet expresses homoerotic desire, as Joseph Pequigney argued, or chaste friendship is a central debate in Sonnets criticism.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line; 'Sonnet 20', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  4. shifting change, as is false women's fashion l. 4

    The idea that women are changeable ('la donna è mobile') is a stock topos of Renaissance literature. The sonnet praises the youth by contrast with women, and critics have discussed how its compliments depend on disparaging women; some read the misogyny as conventional, others as integral to the sequence's later treatment of the 'dark lady'. 'Fashion' means habit, custom, with the suggestion of passing mode.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  5. less false in rolling l. 5

    A 'rolling' eye was a wandering, flirtatious one. The youth's eye is brighter than women's but 'less false', and it 'gilds' whatever it looks at, as the sun gilds what it shines on. The image reflects the old theory that the eye emits rays, and it ennobles the beloved's gaze. Compare Sonnet 33, 'Gilding pale streams with heavenly alchemy', of the sun.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  6. A man in hue, all 'hues' in his controlling l. 7

    The line means that the youth is a man in appearance ('hue', form or complexion) who holds all forms or colours in his power. In the 1609 Quarto the second word is printed 'Hews', italicised and capitalised, which led eighteenth-century commentators, and then Oscar Wilde's story 'The Portrait of Mr. W. H.' (1889), to imagine a boy actor named Willie Hughes. No such person is documented. Editors also note that the line's syntax ('steals men's eyes and women's souls amazeth') is ambiguous. Modern texts vary in whether they mark 'hues' typographically.

    Oscar Wilde, 'The Portrait of Mr. W. H.' (Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine, July 1889), in Lord Arthur Savile's Crime; The Portrait of Mr. W. H., and Other Stories (Project Gutenberg); 'Sonnet 20', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line

  7. Nature ... fell a-doting l. 10

    Nature is pictured as a craftswoman who began to fashion a woman and, while working, became infatuated with what she was making. Critics compare the story of Pygmalion (Ovid, Metamorphoses 10), the sculptor who loved his statue, and the Platonic myth of the androgyne. The fable explains the youth's feminine beauty with an invented myth of creation. 'Wrought' means fashioned.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet

  8. adding one thing ... nothing l. 12

    The bawdy is clear: the 'one thing' added is the male member, which 'defeats' (cheats, deprives) the poet of the youth. 'To my purpose nothing' means of no use to my desire, but critics note that 'nothing' was also slang for the female genitals (the 'O' or zero), as Gordon Williams's glossary documents, which complicates the line's sexual logic. Whether the poem thereby disavows physical desire for the youth, or playfully acknowledges it, is debated.

    Gordon Williams, A Glossary of Shakespeare's Sexual Language (London: Athlone, 1997), s.v. 'nothing', 'prick', 'use'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 20', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  9. prick'd thee out l. 13

    To 'prick out' was to select by marking with a pin-prick or a mark on a list, as sheriffs were chosen. The sexual pun on 'prick' is unmistakable and links back to the 'one thing' of line 12. Nature chose the youth for women's pleasure, so the poet claims only his 'love', leaving women the 'use' of it. Editors cite the sense of choosing names from a list for office.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Gordon Williams, A Glossary of Shakespeare's Sexual Language (London: Athlone, 1997), s.v. 'nothing', 'prick', 'use'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  10. Mine be thy love and thy love's use their treasure l. 14

    'Use' means both sexual employment and interest on money lent (usury), a sense developed in the procreation sonnets (Sonnets 4, 6). The couplet separates love from its 'use', and many readers take it as the poet renouncing sexual relations with the youth. Others, notably Joseph Pequigney, argue that the distinction is less stable than it seems and that later sonnets suggest a sexual relationship. The financial 'treasure' suggests that women will receive the profit, perhaps children, of the youth's love.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Gordon Williams, A Glossary of Shakespeare's Sexual Language (London: Athlone, 1997), s.v. 'nothing', 'prick', 'use'; 'Sonnet 20', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)
