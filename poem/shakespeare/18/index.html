Time & Brevity

Sonnet 19

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

Devouring Time, blunt thou the lion's paws,
And make the earth devour her own sweet brood;
Pluck the keen teeth from the fierce tiger's jaws,
And burn the long-liv'd phoenix, in her blood;
Make glad and sorry seasons as thou fleets,
And do whate'er thou wilt, swift-footed Time,
To the wide world and all her fading sweets;
But I forbid thee one most heinous crime:
O! carve not with thy hours my love's fair brow,
Nor draw no lines there with thine antique pen;
Him in thy course untainted do allow
For beauty's pattern to succeeding men.
Yet do thy worst, old Time; despite thy wrong,
My love shall in my verse ever live young.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Devouring Time l. 1

    Ovid's Pythagoras calls Time 'tempus edax rerum' in Metamorphoses 15.234, a phrase that became a Renaissance commonplace; the Oxquarry commentary treats 'Time devours all things' as a recorded proverb of the age. Shakespeare had used the idea in Love's Labour's Lost ('cormorant devouring Time', 1.1). The sonnet begins the poet's direct address to Time, here a giant, Saturn-like figure who eats his own creatures. The first quatrain grants Time its ordinary powers in a series of imperatives, a rhetorical concession before the forbidding that follows. The Folger synopsis describes the poem as continuing the 'war with Time' begun in Sonnet 15.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 19 (text and synopsis), ed. Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 19, l. 1

  2. the lion's paws ... the fierce tiger's jaws ll. 1-3

    The examples are emblems of strength and ferocity. A lion with blunted claws and a tiger with pulled teeth are old animals; the commentary notes that loss of teeth was the sign of age, and that the tiger was for Shakespeare's audience an almost fabulous beast. The violent verbs ('blunt', 'pluck', 'burn') make Time a torturer, yet the speaker apparently licenses them, an odd tone that reads as bravado before the real request.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 19; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 19, ll. 1-3

  3. the earth devour her own sweet brood l. 2

    Earth is both womb and tomb: all things born of her return to dust (Genesis 3.19). The image of a parent devouring offspring recalls the myth of Kronos or Saturn, the god identified with Time, who swallowed his children, a connection the Oxquarry commentary draws. Time here forces the earth to repeat its own crime. The 'sweet brood' prepares for the 'fading sweets' of line 7.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Genesis 3.19, King James Version; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 19, l. 2

  4. burn the long-liv'd phoenix, in her blood l. 4

    Classical writers (Herodotus, Ovid in Metamorphoses 15.391-407, Pliny) describe the phoenix, the unique bird that lived about five hundred years before dying and being reborn. It was an emblem of immortality and of resurrection. 'In her blood' is ambiguous: 'alive, in full vigour' (as in the hunting phrase 'in blood', i.e. in good condition), or 'in her own body and lineage'. Burning her alive would be the ultimate cruelty, yet even the phoenix renews herself, a hint of the renewal the couplet will claim for verse. Shakespeare returns to the bird in The Phoenix and the Turtle.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 19, l. 4; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blood'

  5. fleets l. 5

    To 'fleet' is to glide or pass quickly away (OED); the Oxquarry commentary cites the sense 'pass swiftly and imperceptibly'. The 1609 Quarto reads 'fleet'st'; the archive's text, with many editors, prints 'fleets', an older second-person form, while the Quarto and the Oxquarry text keep 'fleet'st'; either way the word rhymes with 'sweets' in line 7. 'Glad and sorry seasons' are spring and summer, autumn and winter, but also happy and sad times in human life; 'sorry' means sorrowful, gloomy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fleet'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 19 (text and synopsis), ed. Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 19, l. 5

  6. swift-footed Time l. 6

    The epithet is a Homeric-style compound (Homer's 'swift-footed Akhilleus' comes to mind, though there is no clear allusion). Time in Renaissance emblems is often winged and running, with forelock and scythe. The concession 'do whate'er thou wilt' completes the octave's apparent surrender, which the sestet will qualify. The world 'and all her fading sweets' (beautiful, perishable things) is handed over to him.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 19, l. 6

  7. But I forbid thee one most heinous crime l. 8

    The 'But' of line 8 comes a line before the usual volta at line 9, so the prohibition straddles octave and sestet. Having authorized the destruction of lions, tigers and the phoenix, the speaker treats the ageing of the young man as the one unpardonable outrage, a hyperbole that measures his love. 'Heinous' (from Old French haine, hatred) means atrocious, odious; it was a legal term for the gravest offences.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'heinous'; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 19; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  8. carve not with thy hours my love's fair brow l. 9

    Time is now a sculptor or engraver, and the hours his tools; carving turns into drawing in the next line. Wrinkles as lines cut or drawn on the brow are a common Sonnets image (compare Sonnet 2's 'deep trenches' and Sonnet 60's 'delves the parallels'). The phrase 'my love' openly names the youth as the poet's love, as the Oxquarry commentary notes.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 19, l. 9

  9. antique pen l. 10

    Elizabethan 'antique' covered both 'old, ancient' and 'antic', meaning grotesque, fantastic, clownish. Time's pen is very old, and it draws absurd, disfiguring lines. The pen also anticipates the couplet: Time writes lines on the face, the poet writes lines of verse, and the contest between the two writers is the subject of the poem. 'Nor draw no lines' is an emphatic double negative, normal in Elizabethan English.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'antic'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 19, l. 10; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 19, l. 10

  10. pattern l. 12

    A 'pattern' is a model to be copied, an exemplar; the idea that the beloved is the original from which all beauty is drawn is Neo-Platonic and recurs in Sonnets 53 and 98 ('you pattern of all those'). 'Untainted' means unhurt, unblemished, and was used of something not touched by infection or a blow. 'Succeeding men' are people of later times. The line ends the procreation logic of Sonnets 1-17: the youth is now to be preserved as he is, not reproduced.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pattern'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  11. the couplet: verse against Time ll. 13-14

    The couplet concedes that the prohibition is useless ('Yet do thy worst') and replaces it with a boast in the tradition of Horace's 'exegi monumentum' (Odes 3.30) and Ovid's epilogue to the Metamorphoses: poetry outlasts physical decay. 'My love' can mean the beloved or the poet's love for him. The stresses crowd at the end on 'ever live young', so the claim closes with a burst of energy, and the speaker shifts from powerless forbidder to maker.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 19; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 19 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Horace, Odes 3.30, Latin text, The Latin Library; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 19, ll. 13-14
