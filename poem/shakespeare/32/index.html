The Mind

Sonnet 33

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

The Mind

Full many a glorious morning have I seen
Flatter the mountain tops with sovereign eye,
Kissing with golden face the meadows green,
Gilding pale streams with heavenly alchemy;
Anon permit the basest clouds to ride
With ugly rack on his celestial face,
And from the forlorn world his visage hide,
Stealing unseen to west with this disgrace:
Even so my sun one early morn did shine,
With all triumphant splendour on my brow;
But out! alack! he was but one hour mine,
The region cloud hath mask'd him from me now.
Yet him for this my love no whit disdaineth;
Suns of the world may stain when heaven's sun staineth.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnet 33 in the sequence l. 1

    Sonnets 33-35 deal with an offence the young man has committed against the speaker, which is never named. Sonnet 34 continues the weather image ('Why didst thou promise such a beauteous day'), and 35 begins 'No more be grieved at that which thou hast done'. Sonnet 33 is one extended simile: an octave about the sun, a quatrain applying it to 'my sun', and a forgiving couplet. 'Full many' is an intensive idiom meaning 'very many'. Some, including Michael Wood, have linked the poem to the death of Shakespeare's son Hamnet (1596) through a sun/son pun, but that reading is speculative.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 33; Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 33'

  2. Flatter l. 2

    'Flatter' sets the courtly tone of the octave, in which the sun is a monarch handing out favour with its 'sovereign eye'. Commentators note that flattery implies something false or short-lived in that favour, which prepares for its withdrawal. Flattery was a constant worry of Tudor court writing, and the word warns from the start that the sun's kindness may not be what it seems. The verbs of the first quatrain, 'Flatter', 'Kissing', 'Gilding', all describe tender or glamorous actions, and the octave's list of graces makes their loss harder to bear.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flatter, v.1'

  3. The sun as monarch l. 2

    Comparing the sun to a king, and a king to the sun, was a commonplace of Tudor political imagery, and Shakespeare's history plays use it (compare Richard II 3.2). The sun's 'eye' is its face or disc. The image lets the young man's later neglect be read as the withdrawal of a patron's or a king's favour. 'Sovereign' also hints at healing.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sovereign, adj.'

  4. Heavenly alchemy l. 4

    Alchemy tried to turn base metals into gold, and the alchemists' sign for gold was the sun (Sol). The sun's 'golden face' (l. 3) 'gilds' the streams. But the change is only on the surface, a gilding, which hints that the beloved's favour may also be a show. Compare Sonnet 114, where the speaker's eye practises a flattering 'alchemy'. The word thus carries both the sun's power and a doubt about it.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary; William Shakespeare, Sonnet 114, l. 4

  5. The basest clouds l. 5

    'Base' means low in position and also low in rank. In 1 Henry IV 1.2 Hal compares himself to the sun 'Who doth permit the base contagious clouds / To smother up his beauty from the world'. The parallel suggests that the sun allows itself to be covered, so the young man is answerable for the company he keeps. 'Anon' means soon after.

    William Shakespeare, 1 Henry IV 1.2 (Hal's soliloquy); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary

  6. rack l. 6

    Malone glossed 'rack' as the quick motion of the clouds, and the OED records the sense of drifting cloud. Compare Hamlet 2.2 ('the rack stand still') and The Tempest 4.1 ('Leave not a rack behind'). The sun's 'celestial face' is spoiled by an 'ugly' covering, which may suggest disgrace, scandal, or a blemish on the face. 'Ride' (l. 5) adds a picture of a mounted troop.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rack, n.1'; William Shakespeare, Hamlet 2.2 ('the rack stand still'); Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 33

  7. forlorn l. 7

    The word comes from Old English forloren, 'utterly lost'. The world is 'forlorn' because the sun has hidden his face, and the line looks ahead to the abandoned speaker. The sun 'Stealing unseen to west' (l. 8) sets in secret, slipping away like a guilty person. 'Disgrace' means both disfigurement and dishonour. The sun 'hides' its face, as God hides his face from the sinner in the Psalms.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'forlorn, adj.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary

  8. My sun l. 9

    The third quatrain applies the octave to the speaker's own experience. 'One early morn' suggests a short, early spell of favour, and 'triumphant splendour on my brow' pictures the beloved's glory crowning him. The sun/son pun is hidden here and comes into the open in the couplet. The movement from 'Full many' mornings in the octave to 'one early morn' narrows the scale from the whole world to the speaker's own life. The change of tense to the past also suggests that this brief morning is already over.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 33

  9. out! alack! l. 11

    As an exclamation 'out' expresses dismay or anger (compare Lady Macbeth's 'Out, damned spot'). 'Alack' expresses regret. In 'he was but one hour mine', the favour is very brief; 'one hour' may simply be an exaggeration. The two exclamations break the smooth descriptive syntax of the octave: this is the only point where the speaker cries out instead of reasoning, and the outburst prepares for the strained forgiveness of the couplet. The irregular stresses of 'But out! alack!' also break the iambic rhythm.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'out, int.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'alack, int.'; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  10. region cloud l. 12

    Older cosmology divided the air into lower, middle and upper 'regions', so a 'region cloud' is a cloud high in the sky. Compare Hamlet 2.2: 'the region kites' and 'the dreadful thunder / Doth rend the region'. The cloud 'hath mask'd him': 'mask' means to conceal and also to disguise, as in a court masque. The cloud is high and distant, beyond the speaker's reach.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'region, n.'; William Shakespeare, Hamlet 2.2; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 33

  11. no whit l. 13

    'Whit' comes from Old English wiht, 'thing, creature', and survives in 'not a whit'. The couplet turns to forgiveness, but the logic is strained: the comparison that excuses the beloved also admits that he is stained. The verb 'disdaineth' and its rhyme word 'staineth' are feminine endings (unstressed final syllables), which soften the close. The archaic -eth forms give the couplet the tone of a formal maxim or proverb.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'whit, n.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  12. Suns ... stain l. 14

    'Stain' is used without an object, both of the sun being darkened and of a moral blot. Most editors notice the pun on 'suns' and 'sons', the men of the world. Critics disagree about the couplet's tone. Some hear generous forgiveness. Booth and others stress that the excuse is also an accusation, since 'stain' admits a moral fault and the forgiveness leans on a cosmic precedent. Sonnet 34 shows that the hurt is not over.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford, 2002), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stain, v.'
