Sonnet 55

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

History & Politics

Not marble, nor the gilded monuments
Of princes, shall outlive this powerful rhyme;
But you shall shine more bright in these contents
Than unswept stone, besmear'd with sluttish time.
When wasteful war shall statues overturn,
And broils root out the work of masonry,
Nor Mars his sword, nor war's quick fire shall burn
The living record of your memory.
'Gainst death, and all-oblivious enmity
Shall you pace forth; your praise shall still find room
Even in the eyes of all posterity
That wear this world out to the ending doom.
So, till the judgement that yourself arise,
You live in this, and dwell in lovers' eyes.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Not marble ... monuments: the immortality topos ll. 1-2

    Horace ends his third book of Odes claiming to have built a monument 'more lasting than bronze' (aere perennius) and higher than the pyramids, which neither rain nor wind nor the flight of years can destroy. Ovid ends the Metamorphoses (15.871 ff.) with a similar claim: his work will survive Jove's anger, fire, the sword and devouring time. Shakespeare had these lines in Latin from school and in Golding's 1567 translation. Editors (Booth, Duncan-Jones, Burrow) all note the debt. Elizabethan sonneteers used the topos often (cf. Spenser, Amoretti 75), so the reader would have heard the opening as a confident, even conventional, boast. The twist, which critics stress, is that the boast is made for the beloved ('you shall shine') rather than for the poet's own fame, though the 'powerful rhyme' remains the real survivor.

    Horace, Odes 3.30 ('Exegi monumentum'), Latin text, The Latin Library; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, ll. 1-2

  2. gilded monuments l. 1

    'Monuments' here are chiefly funeral monuments: the tomb effigies of princes and nobles, often painted and gilded, that filled English churches in the late sixteenth century. Westminster Abbey's royal tombs, with their gilt bronze effigies, are the grandest instance. The gilding is a sign of wealth and vanity, and the next lines say it will not last: the stone will be left 'unswept' and smeared. Duncan-Jones and Burrow gloss the word in this sense of tomb and memorial; the OED records 'monument' as a sepulchral structure as well as a record or written document, and the second sense is quietly at work too, since the poem itself is offered as the true 'monument'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'monument'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 1; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 1

  3. this powerful rhyme l. 2

    'Rhyme' in Elizabethan usage often means verse or poetry in general, not only matched sounds (OED). The deictic 'this' points at the very poem the reader holds, a move repeated in 'these contents' (l. 3) and 'You live in this' (l. 14). Vendler observes that the sonnet tells us almost nothing about the young man's looks or character; what is praised is his survival in the poem. Critics differ on how to read the boast: some take it as sincere celebration of the friend, others (following Booth and Kerrigan) hear the poet's pride in his own art crowding out its subject.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rhyme'; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 55; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 2

  4. these contents l. 3

    'Contents' means the matter contained in the written poem, as in the contents of a letter or book. Stress falls on the second syllable to keep the iambic line regular. The phrase sets the written page against the stone of l. 4: the youth will 'shine' brighter in ink than in a gilded effigy. Booth notes the paradox that black ink is said to make him shine, a paradox Shakespeare repeats in Sonnet 65 ('in black ink my love may still shine bright').

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 3; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 3; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 55 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine

  5. unswept stone l. 4

    The picture is of a memorial stone or brass in a church floor or tomb that no one cleans any more, once the family of the dead has gone. Duncan-Jones and Burrow both read it as a church monument. Grammatically the comparison is compressed: 'you shall shine more bright in these contents / Than [you would shine in] unswept stone'. The image prepares for the verdict on time in the second half of the line.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 4; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 4; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 4

  6. besmear'd with sluttish time l. 4

    In Shakespeare's English a 'slut' was first a dirty, slovenly person, especially a careless housekeeper, and 'sluttish' meant unclean or untidy (OED); the sense of sexual looseness existed too. Time is personified as a slatternly servant who lets the stone get filthy instead of sweeping it, which ties back to 'unswept'. 'Besmear'd' adds grime or soot. Editors note the contrast between the bright, shining youth and the smeared stone. The word is a sharp, homely touch in a poem otherwise grand in tone.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sluttish'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 4; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 4

  7. broils l. 6

    A 'broil' is a tumult, riot or quarrel (OED), often of civil war, the sense in Shakespeare's history plays. 'Root out' extends the metaphor: masonry is treated like a plant torn up by the roots. The quatrain moves from war's general destruction of statues (l. 5) to civil commotion and then to fire. Readers in the 1590s had seen real iconoclasm: monuments and church images destroyed in the English Reformation, which gives the lines a concrete edge, as several editors note.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'broil'; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 6; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, ll. 5-6

  8. Mars his sword ... quick fire ll. 7-8

    'Mars his sword' is the old 'his-genitive', meaning 'Mars's sword'. 'Quick' means swift-moving and also living, so fire is a living force set against 'the living record'. The pairing of sword and fire echoes the end of Ovid's Metamorphoses (15.871-72), where the poet claims his work cannot be destroyed by Jove's anger, fire, iron (ferrum, the sword) or devouring age. Bate and Burrow treat this as one of Shakespeare's most direct borrowings from Ovid in the Sonnets. The grammar is loose: 'shall burn' governs both subjects with 'nor ... nor'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, ll. 7-8

  9. The living record of your memory l. 8

    'Record' can mean a written account, a document, and (in Shakespeare's pronunciation) a re-cord, i.e. something kept in mind (Latin cor, heart). 'Your memory' means the memory of you held by others. The poem is 'living' because each reading revives it; stone is dead matter. Booth notes the double sense of 'memory' as both remembrance and the record itself. The line is the climax of the octave's contrast between material and verbal monuments.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'record'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 8; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 8

  10. all-oblivious enmity l. 9

    'Oblivious' here is active: causing oblivion, making all things forgotten, rather than merely forgetful (OED records this older sense). 'Enmity' personifies forgetting as an enemy, joined with death. The sestet begins with this volta-like turn from the destroyers of stone to the deeper foes, death and oblivion, which the poem claims to defeat. Shakespeare likes such 'all-' compounds (cf. 'all-eating shame', Sonnet 2).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'oblivious'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 9; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 9

  11. Shall you pace forth l. 10

    The beloved is pictured walking forward against death and oblivion, the opposite of a fixed stone effigy lying on a tomb. Some editors (Kerrigan, Duncan-Jones) hear the stately pace of a triumphal or funeral procession, so that the youth walks on while his marble image lies still. 'Your praise shall still find room' means praise of you shall always find a place; 'still' = always, continually.

    John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 55, l. 10; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 10; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 55

  12. posterity ... ending doom ll. 11-12

    'Posterity' is plural in sense: 'That wear' agrees with the generations it implies. 'Wear this world out' means live through it until it is exhausted, like a worn garment. 'The ending doom' is the Last Judgement, the end of time in Christian belief ('doom' = judgement, OED). The poem's claim is therefore limited, and editors point out the limit: the verse lasts until the end of the world, not for ever. The Christian frame then takes over in the couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'doom'; Bible (KJV), Revelation 20:12; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 12

  13. till the judgement that yourself arise l. 13

    'That' means 'when': until the Judgement at which you yourself shall arise. The line alludes to the general resurrection of the body, as in 1 Thessalonians 4:16 ('the dead in Christ shall rise first') and Revelation 20:12-13, where the dead stand before God. Until then the beloved lives in the poem; afterwards he will live in his own body. Critics note the tactful theology: poetic immortality is presented as a stand-in until real resurrection, not as a rival to it.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Bible (KJV), Revelation 20:13; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 55, l. 13

  14. You live in this, and dwell in lovers' eyes l. 14

    'This' is the poem; 'lovers' are those who love you or love the verse, that is, future readers. Each reading puts the youth back 'in' a lover's eyes. The couplet repeats the claim of Sonnet 18 ('So long lives this, and this gives life to thee'). Vendler stresses the shift in the couplet from public monuments to private, loving reading. The two verbs, 'live' and 'dwell', give a home to the beloved that the 'unswept stone' cannot.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 55; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 55, l. 14; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 55 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine
