Nature

Sonnet 135

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

Whoever hath her wish, thou hast thy 'Will,'
And 'Will' to boot, and 'Will' in over-plus;
More than enough am I that vex'd thee still,
To thy sweet will making addition thus.
Wilt thou, whose will is large and spacious,
Not once vouchsafe to hide my will in thine?
Shall will in others seem right gracious,
And in my will no fair acceptance shine?
The sea, all water, yet receives rain still,
And in abundance addeth to his store;
So thou, being rich in 'Will,' add to thy 'Will'
One will of mine, to make thy large will more.
Let no unkind 'No' fair beseechers kill;
Think all but one, and me in that one 'Will.'

Study notes by the editors

  1. the 'Will' sonnet l. 1

    Sonnets 135 and 136 are the 'Will' sonnets, in which the word occurs some twenty times across the pair. The 1609 Quarto capitalizes and italicizes several instances, as it often does with proper names, which suggests the name William; but the Quarto's italics are inconsistent, and editors disagree about which uses are names. The opening varies a proverbial phrase ('Women will have their wills'): other women may have their wish, but you have your Will, both your desire and a man (or men) called Will. 'Hath' may mean 'may have'.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: G. Eld for T. T., 1609), Quarto text as given in Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, l. 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 135, headnote

  2. the senses of 'will' l. 2

    The commentaries lay out overlapping senses: wish or volition; carnal desire; stubborn wilfulness; slang for the penis and the vagina; the auxiliary of the future tense; and the name Will. 'To boot' means in addition and 'over-plus' means surplus, so line 2 implies that the lady has more than one Will: perhaps the poet himself, perhaps the friend (if he too was called William, as some argue for William Herbert), perhaps her husband. The RPO notes gloss 'Will to boot' as William the poet and 'Will in over-plus' as possibly the friend. The ambiguity is deliberate and cannot be fully resolved.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, ll. 1-2; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shake-speares Sonnets (London: G. Eld for T. T., 1609), Quarto text as given in Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'will'

  3. vex'd thee still l. 3

    'Still' has its regular Elizabethan sense of always, continually, as the RPO note glosses it. 'Vex'd' means troubled or annoyed. The speaker presents himself, with self-deprecation, as more than enough, an excess added to her 'sweet will' 'thus', by his very insistence. The mercantile 'addition' continues the language of surplus begun in line 2. 'Addition' was also the word for a title or mark of honour added to a name, so the poet adds his Will to hers as a supplement to her name.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: G. Eld for T. T., 1609), Quarto text as given in Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still'

  4. whose will is large and spacious l. 5

    The phrase plays on 'will' as both appetite and the female sexual organ: the lady's will is wide enough to take in more. The RPO note glosses line 5's 'will' as the vagina and line 6's 'my will' as the penis. The sexual joke is coarse, but the tone is also one of abject pleading: he asks her to 'vouchsafe' (grant, condescend) to admit him once among the rest. 'Gracious' in line 7 means pleasing, favoured, and has a theological ring (in a state of grace).

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: G. Eld for T. T., 1609), Quarto text as given in Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, ll. 5-6

  5. The sea, all water, yet receives rain ll. 9-10

    The comparison echoes Ecclesiastes 1.7: 'All the rivers run into the sea; yet the sea is not full.' Shakespeare uses a similar image of love's capacity in Twelfth Night 1.1, where Orsino's love 'Receiveth as the sea' yet everything that enters it falls into abatement, a parallel the Oxquarry commentary notes. Here the image is turned to bawdy argument: she is like the sea, and one more contribution will not overfill her. 'Still' again means always.

    Ecclesiastes 1.7, King James Version; Shakespeare, Twelfth Night 1.1, Folger Shakespeare; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, ll. 9-10

  6. rich in 'Will,' add to thy 'Will' l. 11

    The language of wealth and addition (store, abundance, rich, add, more) makes desire a matter of accumulation, a cynical parody of the financial imagery of the procreation sonnets. Line 12, 'to make thy large will more', joins sexual and verbal excess: the sonnet's own piling-up of 'will' imitates the abundance it describes. The insistent repetition of a single word in shifting senses is a rhetorical figure (antanaclasis) that Elizabethan handbooks taught, here pushed to comic excess.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, ll. 11-12

  7. Let no unkind 'No' fair beseechers kill l. 13

    The Quarto reads 'Let no vnkinde, no faire beseechers kill', which editors have punctuated in different ways. The archive's text makes 'No' a noun, an unkind refusal. Other editors print 'Let no unkind, no fair beseechers kill', taking 'unkind' as a noun ('unkind one, kill no fair suitors') or as describing the suitors. The RPO edition glosses 'unkind' as describing the beseechers. 'Kill' plays on the Petrarchan convention that the lady's refusal slays her lover.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: G. Eld for T. T., 1609), Quarto text as given in Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, l. 13; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 135, l. 13

  8. Think all but one, and me in that one 'Will' l. 14

    The last line asks her to regard all her suitors as one (they are all 'Will', desire) and to include the poet in that single will. It can also be heard against the proverb 'one is no number', used in the next sonnet (136): one more makes no difference. The Oxquarry commentary also hears the idiom 'all's one' (it does not matter). The closing pun on the poet's own name makes the sonnet a signature, since the speaker is identified as Will.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 135 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 135, l. 14; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 135
