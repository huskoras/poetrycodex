Sonnet 87

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

History & Politics

Farewell! thou art too dear for my possessing,
And like enough thou know'st thy estimate,
The charter of thy worth gives thee releasing;
My bonds in thee are all determinate.
For how do I hold thee but by thy granting?
And for that riches where is my deserving?
The cause of this fair gift in me is wanting,
And so my patent back again is swerving.
Thyself thou gav'st, thy own worth then not knowing,
Or me to whom thou gav'st it, else mistaking;
So thy great gift, upon misprision growing,
Comes home again, on better judgement making.
Thus have I had thee, as a dream doth flatter,
In sleep a king, but waking no such matter.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Farewell! l. 1

    The sonnet begins abruptly with farewell and argues, in the language of contracts, that the poet never truly deserved the friend. Twelve lines end with feminine (unstressed) syllables, all except lines 2 and 4. Helen Vendler hears the alternation as enacting the speaker's longing; Carl Atkins hears a sombre monotony of loss. Michael Andrews and Murray Krieger read the coolness of the legal vocabulary as a self-protective irony that the couplet's dream finally breaks open. It opens a run of sonnets concerned with the friend's estrangement and the poet's sense of unworthiness.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 87', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  2. too dear l. 1

    'Dear' means both precious and expensive. 'Possessing' introduces the vocabulary of property: the poet speaks as if he had held the friend on terms he can no longer afford. The pun sets the tone of the sonnet, in which emotional facts are expressed as transactions. Booth observes how every key word in the quatrain doubles as a legal or financial term.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; 'Sonnet 87', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  3. estimate l. 2

    'Estimate' here is a noun meaning valuation or worth. The friend, the poet suspects, now knows his market value. Murray Krieger saw the contrast between affectionate 'dear' and calculating 'estimate' as the source of the poem's bitterness. The line is one of only two in the poem without a feminine ending. The financial idiom continues the pun on 'dear' and anticipates the bonds, charter and patent of the following lines. The friend's knowing his worth is the cause of the poet's loss.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 87', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  4. charter ... releasing l. 3

    A charter was a legal document granting rights or privileges. The friend's worth is itself a charter giving him 'releasing', freedom from obligation. Booth and Vendler read the quatrain as treating the relationship as a contract that becomes void once the friend recognises his superior worth. The legal fiction allows the poet to present desertion as the friend's lawful right rather than as betrayal, a courtesy that is also a reproach. The word 'releasing' anticipates the legal 'determinate' of line 4.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  5. My bonds in thee are all determinate l. 4

    'Bonds' are binding agreements, deeds of obligation, with the further sense of emotional ties. 'Determinate' is a legal term meaning terminated or expired, as of a lease that has reached its term. The poet's rights in the friend have lapsed. The legal vocabulary is consistent with Shakespeare's frequent use of law terms, though it is not evidence of formal legal training.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line

  6. patent ... swerving l. 8

    Letters patent were open documents by which the Crown granted privileges, including the monopolies much resented in the 1590s. The poet's 'patent' to the friend is returning to its source because there is no 'cause' (legal consideration, merit) in him to justify the grant. 'Swerving' means returning, turning aside. Vendler notes that the poem's key word, 'gift', is entirely absent from the couplet, enacting the friend's withdrawal.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  7. misprision l. 11

    'Misprision' means mistake or misapprehension (from Old French mesprendre); in law it also denoted a wrongful act or concealment, as in 'misprision of treason'. The friend gave himself either undervaluing his own worth or overvaluing the poet's. Because the gift 'grew' upon an error, it 'comes home again' when the friend makes a better judgment. The economic pun on 'growing' (as interest grows) continues the poem's financial idiom.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  8. on better judgement making l. 12

    'Making better judgement' means forming a more accurate estimate, but the legal vocabulary suggests also a court's judgment revising an earlier decision. 'Comes home' is the phrase for property reverting to its owner. The quatrain concludes the poem's legal argument before the couplet changes register entirely. The reversal is complete: what the friend gave in error he now takes back in wisdom, and the poet's possession is revealed as never truly his. The calm tone makes the loss the more poignant.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  9. as a dream doth flatter, / In sleep a king l. 13

    The couplet abandons legal language for the image of a dream in which the sleeper is a king, a commonplace (compare Romeo's dream in Romeo and Juliet 5.1). 'Flatter' means deceive pleasingly; Richard Strier stresses the brief, potent joy the word allows. Booth notes possible sexual senses in 'had' and 'matter'. Many readers find the plainness of 'no such matter' the most moving moment of the poem, the point where the ironic self-protection of the quatrains gives way.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 87', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet
