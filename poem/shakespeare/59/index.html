Sonnet 60

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

History & Politics

Like as the waves make towards the pebbled shore,
So do our minutes hasten to their end;
Each changing place with that which goes before,
In sequent toil all forwards do contend.
Nativity, once in the main of light,
Crawls to maturity, wherewith being crown'd,
Crooked eclipses 'gainst his glory fight,
And Time that gave doth now his gift confound.
Time doth transfix the flourish set on youth
And delves the parallels in beauty's brow,
Feeds on the rarities of nature's truth,
And nothing stands but for his scythe to mow:
And yet to times in hope, my verse shall stand.
Praising thy worth, despite his cruel hand.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Like as the waves ... (Ovid, Met. 15) ll. 1-4

    In Metamorphoses 15.179-85 Ovid's Pythagoras says that time moves like a river: as wave is driven by wave, pressed by the one behind and pressing the one ahead, so the moments fly and follow at once and are always new. Golding's 1567 translation renders the passage in fourteeners Shakespeare knew. Bate argues the whole sonnet draws on Book 15, the three quatrains corresponding roughly to Ovid's passages on flux, the ages of man, and devouring time. 'Like as' is an old form of 'just as'. 'Make towards' means move towards. 'Pebbled' shore adds the sound of waves on shingle.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, ll. 1-4

  2. our minutes ... sonnet number 60 l. 2

    The surface sense is simple: the small units of our lives rush to their close, like waves to the shore. Critics have noted that this is Sonnet 60 and that it speaks of 'minutes', just as Sonnet 12 counts the clock's hours; Kerrigan makes the point in his Penguin introduction. Whether the 1609 ordering is numerically designed is debated, and the pairing may be coincidence. Dympna Callaghan links Sonnets 12 and 60 to the new mechanical clock-time, in her essay 'Confounded by Winter: Speeding Time in Shakespeare's Sonnets'.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 2; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), introduction; Dympna Callaghan, 'Confounded by Winter: Speeding Time in Shakespeare's Sonnets', in James Schiffer, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets: Critical Essays (New York: Garland, 1999)

  3. sequent toil ... contend ll. 3-4

    'Sequent' means following in order, in succession (Latin sequi, to follow). 'Toil' and 'contend' make the movement effortful and combative, as if each moment strained against the others. Booth notes that words of struggle (toil, contend, fight, transfix, scythe) run through the poem and dramatize the war with Time. The line closely renders Ovid's image of each wave pressing and being pressed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sequent'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, ll. 3-4; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library

  4. Nativity, once in the main of light l. 5

    'Nativity' (birth) stands for the newborn person. 'The main of light' is hard: 'main' means a broad expanse, especially the open sea (OED), so the phrase carries the waves of quatrain 1 into a 'sea of light', the world into which the baby is born. Tyler (1890) glosses it as the world imagined as a wide ocean lit by the sun. Editors also connect Ovid's 'editus in lucem' (born into the light, Met. 15.221), where the newborn lies helpless. Some hear a hint of the sun's course as well, preparing for 'eclipses'.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 5 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'main'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, l. 5

  5. Crawls to maturity l. 6

    The literal crawling of the infant becomes the slow, laborious ascent of a life. Tyler notes a sense of human abjection too, comparing Hamlet's 'crawling between earth and heaven'. The movement also suggests the sun climbing to its height at noon, in keeping with 'crown'd' and 'glory'. Ovid's account of the ages of man (Met. 15.221 ff.) moves similarly from helpless infancy through creeping childhood to strength and decline.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 6 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 6

  6. wherewith being crown'd l. 6

    'Wherewith' means 'with which (maturity)'. Maturity is a crown, the height of life, like the sun at noon or a king at his coronation. The image of royal glory makes the next line's 'eclipses' and 'fight' read as a usurpation. Booth notes the merging of solar, regal and military images here. The grammar also allows a reading in which the crown is the very moment that triggers decline.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, ll. 6-7; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 6; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 60, l. 6

  7. Crooked eclipses l. 7

    In Elizabethan astrology eclipses were malign portents, bringing misfortune to rulers. 'Crooked' means perverse, malignant, twisted (OED), and may also hint at the bent shape of old age. Tyler glosses the phrase as adverse circumstances that turn a life from its course. 'His glory' refers to Nativity, i.e. the person at the height of life, now pictured as a sun or king obscured. Compare Sonnet 35 ('Clouds and eclipses stain both moon and sun').

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 7 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'crooked'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 7

  8. confound l. 8

    'Confound' in Shakespeare often means destroy, ruin, bring to nothing (OED), stronger than the modern 'confuse'. Time is both giver and destroyer, a classical paradox Ovid states in the same book (15.234: 'tempus edax rerum', time the devourer of things). The line ends the second quatrain's arc from birth to ruin. Tyler glosses it as 'spoil and render worthless his gift'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'confound'; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 8 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library

  9. transfix the flourish l. 9

    'Flourish' means a blossoming, bloom, and also a decorative ornament or embellishment (OED), so youth's beauty is both flower and added decoration. 'Transfix' usually means pierce through, as with a spear; editors debate whether Shakespeare also intends the Latin sense 'fix across', or a sense close to 'remove' or 'destroy'. Booth sets out the ambiguity and counts 'transfix' among the poem's violent words. Tyler glosses the line as 'doth kill and destroy youthful beauty'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, l. 9; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 9 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flourish'

  10. delves the parallels l. 10

    'Delves' means digs. The 'parallels' are the lines of wrinkles across the brow, imagined as trenches dug in a field or in siegeworks (in military usage 'parallels' were later the trenches dug in front of a besieged town). The image recalls Sonnet 2, 'And dig deep trenches in thy beauty's field'. Duncan-Jones and Burrow note the agricultural and military senses together. The action prepares for the scythe of l. 12.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 10; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 60, l. 10; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'parallel'

  11. Feeds on the rarities l. 11

    Ovid's Pythagoras calls time 'edax rerum', devourer of things (Met. 15.234), the source of Shakespeare's 'devouring Time' in Sonnet 19. 'Rarities of nature's truth' are the rare, true perfections of nature, genuine beauty as opposed to cosmetic art, as Tyler explains. 'Truth' may also mean the fixed pattern or faithfulness of nature. Bate sets this line within the third Ovidian movement of the sonnet.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 60, l. 11 (public domain; online at Shakespeare Online)

  12. his scythe to mow l. 12

    Renaissance iconography gave Father Time a scythe, an attribute merged from the Greek Kronos (Saturn's sickle) and the harvest reaper; the figure is common in emblem books. 'Nothing stands but for' means nothing stands up except in order to be mown down. The image completes the farming sequence (delves, feeds, mow) of quatrain 3. Compare Sonnet 12 ('nothing 'gainst Time's scythe can make defence') and Sonnet 100, where the same instrument appears.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 12; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, l. 12; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 60 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine

  13. to times in hope ... my verse shall stand ll. 13-14

    'Times in hope' means future times, times that exist only in hope or expectation. Some editors hear 'in hope' as attaching to the verse too: the poet only hopes his verse will stand. 'Stand' answers 'nothing stands' in l. 12 directly. Vendler calls the couplet a reversing couplet that pushes back against the whole body of the poem; others read it as weaker than the twelve lines of destruction.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 60; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 60, l. 13; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 60, l. 13
