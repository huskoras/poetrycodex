The Mind

Sonnet 71

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

The Mind

No longer mourn for me when I am dead
Than you shall hear the surly sullen bell
Give warning to the world that I am fled
From this vile world with vilest worms to dwell:
Nay, if you read this line, remember not
The hand that writ it, for I love you so,
That I in your sweet thoughts would be forgot,
If thinking on me then should make you woe.
O if, I say, you look upon this verse,
When I perhaps compounded am with clay,
Do not so much as my poor name rehearse;
But let your love even with my life decay;
Lest the wise world should look into your moan,
And mock you with me after I am gone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. No longer mourn for me: the mortality group l. 1

    Sonnets 71-74 imagine the poet's death and burial and set his mortal body against his surviving verse. Sonnet 71 is paradoxical: it asks the friend to mourn only briefly, and even to forget, out of love. Critics read the self-effacement differently: as tender selflessness, as a manipulative plea that guarantees remembrance (Vendler), or as anxiety about the friend's social shame. Christina Rossetti's sonnet 'Remember' (written 1849, published 1862) is a later poem on the same theme: it asks to be remembered, then releases the beloved to forget.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 71; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 71, l. 1; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 71 (text, notes and synopsis)

  2. the surly sullen bell ll. 2-3

    In Elizabethan parish practice a 'passing bell' was rung when someone was dying, and a knell after death, to call for prayers; the number of strokes could indicate the sex and age of the dead person. The bell 'gives warning' (announces) to the world. 'Surly' and 'sullen' (gloomy, heavy) personify it. Compare 2 Henry IV 1.1: news of death 'Sounds ever after as a sullen bell, / Remember'd tolling a departing friend'. Donne's Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624), Meditation 17, is the most famous later meditation on the custom.

    William Shakespeare, Henry IV, Part 2, 1.1 ('a sullen bell'), Folger Shakespeare; John Donne, Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624), Meditation 17, Project Gutenberg; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 71, ll. 2-3

  3. vile world with vilest worms to dwell l. 4

    The repetition 'vile ... vilest' (Quarto 'vilde ... vildest', a common Elizabethan form of the word) makes the grave a comic-grim downgrade from a world already despised. Worms feeding on the buried body are a biblical and medieval commonplace ('though after my skin worms destroy this body', Job 19:26). The word 'fled' suggests a flight from bad company, an escape that turns out to be no better.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Bible (KJV), Job 19:26; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 71, l. 4

  4. if you read this line, remember not ll. 5-6

    The paradox is that the request for forgetting is made in writing that will survive, and by being read it cannot help prompting memory. 'The hand that writ it' presents the manuscript as a physical relic of the dead writer. Many critics see the sonnet's self-erasure as rhetorically double: the more the poet asks to be forgotten, the more he is remembered. The reference to the reader is common in the mortality sonnets (compare 74, 'When thou reviewest this').

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 71; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 71, ll. 5-6; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 74 (text, notes and synopsis)

  5. make you woe l. 8

    Shakespeare and his contemporaries could use 'woe' predicatively, as in 'make you woe' (make you sad), a use the OED records. The rhyme 'so / woe' closes the second quatrain with a plain, almost conversational phrase that contrasts with the formality of the opening. Readers should not take it as the noun: the sense is simply 'that you should be sad'. The same adjectival use appears elsewhere in Shakespeare, for example in The Tempest ('I am woe for't', 5.1), and it gives the end of the octave an informal, spoken quality.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'woe'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 71, l. 8

  6. compounded am with clay l. 10

    'Compounded' means mixed into one substance. The image draws on Genesis 3:19, 'for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return', and on the Burial Service in the Book of Common Prayer ('earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust'). 'Perhaps' (l. 10) is quietly significant: the poet does not know when he will die, and the friend's reading may come soon or late.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; The Book of Common Prayer (1559), The Order for the Burial of the Dead; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 71, l. 10

  7. rehearse l. 11

    'Rehearse' in Shakespeare usually means to repeat, recount or say over (from Old French rehercier, to harrow again). To 'rehearse' a name might also suggest naming in prayers for the dead or in an epitaph. The poet calls his name 'poor', in keeping with the self-depreciation of the Rival Poet sonnets that follow (78-86). The request not to name the poet is the more striking because the sonnet itself is the place where his memory would survive; Sonnet 72 develops it into the wish that his name be buried with his body.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rehearse'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 71, l. 11

  8. let your love even with my life decay l. 12

    'Even with' means at the same moment as, or equally with. The line reverses the sequence's usual hope that love outlasts life (compare Sonnet 116). Booth and Vendler note the emotional pressure of such a request, since the friend who loves cannot obey it, so the line both releases and binds him. The phrasing turns the language of mortality ('decay') onto love itself, so that the friend's affection is to be buried with the poet.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 71, l. 12; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 71

  9. the wise world ... mock you ll. 13-14

    'Wise' is ironic: worldly, knowing, cynical. 'Look into your moan' means pry into, examine the cause of, your lament. The couplet suggests a social gap between the poet and the noble friend, so that grief for the poet would expose the friend to ridicule. Critics divide on whether the tone is bitter (the world's scorn) or loving (protection of the friend); Sonnet 72 continues the thought ('My name be buried where my body is').

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 71; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 71, ll. 13-14; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 72 (text, notes and synopsis)
