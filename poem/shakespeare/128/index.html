The Mind

Sonnet 129

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

The Mind

The expense of spirit in a waste of shame
Is lust in action: and till action, lust
Is perjur'd, murderous, bloody, full of blame,
Savage, extreme, rude, cruel, not to trust;
Enjoy'd no sooner but despised straight;
Past reason hunted; and no sooner had,
Past reason hated, as a swallow'd bait,
On purpose laid to make the taker mad:
Mad in pursuit and in possession so;
Had, having, and in quest, to have extreme;
A bliss in proof, and proved, a very woe;
Before, a joy propos'd; behind a dream.
All this the world well knows; yet none knows well
To shun the heaven that leads men to this hell.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The expense of spirit l. 1

    'Expense' means expenditure, outlay (OED); 'spirit' means the vital spirits, the vital force of body and mind, and also semen, a sense editors (Booth, Kerrigan, Duncan-Jones) all note. The common belief that each sexual act cost a man some of his life underlies the phrase. The Quarto prints 'Th' expence', making the line regular; the archive text spells out 'The expense', so the first foot must be read with elision. The line is impersonal: unlike most sonnets in the sequence, 129 has no 'I' and no 'you', as Vendler notes, and speaks as general moral analysis.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 1; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 1; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'expense'

  2. a waste of shame l. 1

    The phrase works several ways at once: the spending happens in shameful waste; it takes place in a desert ('waste') of shame; and, with Elizabethan spelling and pronunciation, 'waste' could pun on 'waist', the body (Booth, Duncan-Jones). 'Shame' may also refer to the genitals, as in the Latin pudenda. Booth gives the fullest account of the layered meanings and argues that the line's density is the point.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 1; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 1; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'waste'

  3. Is lust in action: and till action, lust ll. 1-2

    The sentence first defines lust as consummated act, then turns ('and till action') to lust before consummation. The chiasmus (lust in action / till action, lust) traps the reader between the two states. The sonnet's structure follows the stages of desire: before, during and after. 'In action' may also carry a theatrical or legal sense (an action at law), which some readers connect with 'perjur'd'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 1-2; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 1-2

  4. perjur'd, murderous, bloody ... (asyndeton) ll. 3-4

    The list runs over two lines and nine descriptive terms with no 'and', the rhetorical figure asyndeton, which conveys compulsive, uncontrolled energy. 'Perjur'd' because lust breaks vows and swears false oaths; 'bloody' and 'murderous' because it can lead to violence, as in Lucrece; 'rude' = brutal, violent; 'not to trust' = untrustworthy. Vendler and Booth both treat the cramming of the quatrain as an enactment of frenzy. Riding and Graves print the Quarto's spellings ('periurd', 'murdrous', 'blouddy') and discuss their pronunciation.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 3-4; Laura Riding and Robert Graves, A Survey of Modernist Poetry (1927), ch. 3, 'William Shakespeare and E. E. Cummings: A Study in Original Punctuation and Spelling'

  5. Enjoy'd no sooner but despised straight l. 5

    'Straight' means immediately, straightaway. The line states the classic post-coital disgust found in moral and medical writing since antiquity; editors compare Lucrece (from l. 690), where Tarquin's lust turns to loathing after the rape. The rapid 'no sooner ... but' construction is repeated in l. 6, setting up the parallel lines of hunting and hating. The speed of the verse mimics the speed of the change from appetite to disgust.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 5; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 5; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 129 (text and synopsis), ed. Mowat and Werstine

  6. Past reason hunted ... Past reason hated ll. 6-7

    'Past reason' means beyond the control of reason, the faculty that should govern appetite in Renaissance moral psychology. The repetition at the start of l. 6 and l. 7 (anaphora) shows that desire and loathing are equally irrational. 'Hunted' leads into the bait and trap imagery that follows. Booth notes the way the verbs slide from the lustful person to lust itself as their subject.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 6-7; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 6-7

  7. a swallow'd bait ... to make the taker mad ll. 7-8

    The image is of a hunting or fishing bait laced with poison, laid 'on purpose' to madden the creature that takes it. 'Taker' is the one who takes the bait (and, sexually, who takes the partner). The idea of a trap laid on purpose suggests a deliberate snare, perhaps by the woman, or by nature, or by the devil; critics differ on who lays it. The word 'mad' is then picked up at once in l. 9, linking quatrains.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, ll. 7-8; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 7; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems, Oxford Shakespeare (Oxford UP, 2002), note on Sonnet 129, ll. 7-8

  8. Mad in pursuit (Quarto 'Made In pursut') l. 9

    The 1609 Quarto reads 'Made In pursut', which most editors since the eighteenth century print as 'Mad in pursuit', taking 'Made' as a spelling or compositor's error. Riding and Graves (1927) argued that the original spelling and punctuation keep a richer sense, and that modern editing flattens the poem. Booth discusses the case and keeps 'Mad' while acknowledging the interplay. The line says lust is mad both while pursuing and while possessing ('in possession so').

    Laura Riding and Robert Graves, A Survey of Modernist Poetry (1927), ch. 3, 'William Shakespeare and E. E. Cummings: A Study in Original Punctuation and Spelling'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 9

  9. Had, having, and in quest, to have l. 10

    The line runs through the verb 'have' in three forms (polyptoton): past ('Had'), present ('having') and future or desired ('in quest, to have'). It summarizes the whole sonnet's analysis of before, during and after. 'Extreme' applies to every stage. Riding and Graves argue that the Quarto's lighter punctuation lets the line connect with the previous and next lines rather than standing alone. The verb 'have' also had sexual force.

    Laura Riding and Robert Graves, A Survey of Modernist Poetry (1927), ch. 3, 'William Shakespeare and E. E. Cummings: A Study in Original Punctuation and Spelling'; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 10

  10. A bliss in proof, and proved, a very woe l. 11

    'In proof' means in the experience, while being tried; 'proved' means once tried, afterwards; 'very' means true, real. The Quarto prints 'A blisse in proofe and proud and very wo'. Most editors emend 'proud' to 'prov'd' and add commas, so that bliss comes during and woe after. Riding and Graves argued that the Quarto's version makes lust a bliss and a woe at every stage, and that modern punctuation reduces it to a simple sequence. Booth weighs both and notes that 'proud' could also suggest sexual swelling. The disagreement remains a classic crux.

    Laura Riding and Robert Graves, A Survey of Modernist Poetry (1927), ch. 3, 'William Shakespeare and E. E. Cummings: A Study in Original Punctuation and Spelling'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 11; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 11; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'proof'

  11. a joy propos'd; behind a dream l. 12

    'Propos'd' means set before one as an aim, anticipated. 'Behind' means afterwards, looking back. The 'dream' is the empty memory of a pleasure that seems unreal once over. The line completes the time-sequence of the sonnet in compact antithesis. Editors compare the dream language of Lucrece, ll. 211-12, where lustful pleasure is called 'a dream, a breath, a froth of fleeting joy'.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 12; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 12; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 12

  12. the world well knows; yet none knows well l. 13

    The reversal of 'well knows' and 'knows well' (chiasmus, or antimetabole) moves from mere knowledge to practical wisdom: knowing a fact versus knowing how to live by it. The couplet acknowledges the Ovidian and Pauline truth that knowledge of the good does not stop us doing evil (compare Romans 7:19: 'the good that I would I do not'). Vendler highlights the couplet's turn from analysis to the admission of helplessness.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on Sonnet 129; Bible (KJV), Romans 7:19; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 13

  13. the heaven ... this hell l. 14

    The religious antithesis of heaven and hell is literal (the soul's damnation for sin) and psychological (bliss turning to torment). Booth and Duncan-Jones note that 'hell' was Elizabethan slang for the vagina, a sense used in Sonnet 144 ('I guess one angel in another's hell'). The line therefore ends in a pun that is both disgusted and theological. Critics disagree on how misogynistic the poem is: some read the trap as laid by the woman, others by human nature itself.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 129, l. 14; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 129, l. 14; Shakespeare, Sonnet 144, l. 12 (Folger text)
