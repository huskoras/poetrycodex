Death & Dying

Fidele

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Death & Dying

Fear no more the heat o' the sun,
Nor the furious winter's rages:
Thou thy worldly task hast done,
Home art gone, and ta'en thy wages:
Golden lads and girls all must,
As chimney-sweepers, come to dust.

Fear no more the frown o' the great,
Thou art past the tyrant's stroke;
Care no more to clothe and eat;
To thee the reed is as the oak:
The sceptre, learning, physic, must
All follow this, and come to dust.

Fear no more the lightning flash
Nor the all-dreaded thunder-stone;
Fear not slander, censure rash;
Thou hast finish'd joy and moan:
All lovers young, all lovers must
Consign to thee, and come to dust.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Fear no more: the dirge for Fidele l. 1

    Imogen, disguised as the boy Fidele, has taken a drug she believed to be medicine and lies apparently dead. Her unknown brothers, raised in the Welsh mountains as 'Polydore' and 'Cadwal', mourn her. Since their voices 'have got the mannish crack', they decide to speak the song rather than sing it ('We'll speak it then'). The audience knows Imogen is alive, so the dirge's consolations are ironic as well as moving. Palgrave titled it 'Fidele' in The Golden Treasury; the play (c. 1610) gives it a fourth stanza, a charm against exorcisers and ghosts, not printed in this text.

    Folger Shakespeare, Cymbeline 4.2 (text and notes); J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), Book I (Project Gutenberg)

  2. Metre and structure l. 2

    Each six-line stanza is in trochaic tetrameter (often seven syllables, stressed first), rhyming in three couplets (aabbcc), the couplet always ending in 'dust'. The first stanza dismisses the fears of nature (sun, winter), the second those of society (the great, the tyrant), the third those of the heavens and of other men's tongues (lightning, slander). The pattern of release is cumulative and incantatory. Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925) repeatedly quotes the first line as a refrain of consolation.

    J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2; Folger Shakespeare, Cymbeline 4.2 (text and notes)

  3. Home art gone, and ta'en thy wages l. 4

    Life is pictured as a day's work ('worldly task') and death as going home paid. The image is biblical: Job 7:1-2 compares man's days to those of 'an hireling' who 'looketh for the reward of his work', and the parable of the labourers in the vineyard (Matthew 20) pays the workers at evening. 'Home' also suggests heaven or the grave, the 'long home' of Ecclesiastes 12:5.

    The Bible (King James Version), Job 7:1-2; The Bible (King James Version), Ecclesiastes 12:5; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  4. Golden lads and girls ... chimney-sweepers l. 5

    The main sense contrasts golden (fair, privileged, precious) young people with chimney-sweepers, the lowest labourers, both reduced to dust. A widely repeated claim holds that in Warwickshire a flowering dandelion was a 'golden lad' and a seeding one a 'chimney-sweeper', which would turn the couplet into a nature fable. The anecdote appears to derive from a mid-twentieth-century report (credited to W. Arrowsmith, via Guy Davenport) printed by Hugh Kenner in The Pound Era (1971). Britten and Holland's Dictionary of English Plant-Names (1886) records 'chimney-sweeper' in Warwickshire and Northamptonshire, but for the heads of ribwort plantain, and other plants bear the name elsewhere. The reading is attractive but unproven.

    Hugh Kenner, The Pound Era (Berkeley: U of California P, 1971), with endnote crediting W. Arrowsmith via Guy Davenport; discussed at Reconstructionary Tales; James Britten and Robert Holland, A Dictionary of English Plant-Names (1886), s.v. chimney-sweeper; discussed at Plant-Lore; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  5. chimney-sweepers l. 6

    Chimney-sweeping was a lowly trade, already associated with soot and dust. 'As chimney-sweepers' means 'just as chimney-sweepers do', setting the highest and lowest side by side in death, a commonplace of the danse macabre. The line's democratic levelling continues in the second stanza's sceptre and learning. Some critics have argued that the comparison has a social edge, since sweeps were among the poorest workers, and others note its rhyming function with 'must'. The image is in any case the central crux of the couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  6. the frown o' the great ... the tyrant's stroke l. 7

    The second stanza moves from nature to society. The singers are princes raised in exile because their father, King Cymbeline, was misled into banishing Belarius, who stole them in revenge; the court's 'frown' is the play's root of suffering. 'Care no more to clothe and eat' releases the dead from basic labour and want. The princes, raised in a cave and ignorant of court life, here voice the play's pastoral critique of the court, a contrast developed by Belarius earlier in act 3, scene 3.

    Folger Shakespeare, Cymbeline 4.2 (text and notes); J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  7. the reed is as the oak l. 10

    In Aesop's fable the proud oak is uprooted by the storm while the pliant reed bends and survives. Here the distinction is cancelled: to the dead, strength and weakness are alike. The proverbial pair also suggests the humble and the great, continuing the stanza's social levelling. The fable was a familiar schoolroom text in the period. Its use here suits the brothers' rustic upbringing, since they speak in images drawn from nature.

    Aesop, 'The Oak and the Reeds', in Three Hundred Aesop's Fables, trans. G. F. Townsend (Project Gutenberg); J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  8. The sceptre, learning, physic l. 11

    'Physic' is medicine, the physician's art. The list of powers that cannot escape death belongs to the tradition of the dance of death and the memento mori. A close parallel is Thomas Nashe's song 'Adieu, farewell, earth's bliss' in Summer's Last Will and Testament (written 1592, printed 1600), which also lists beauty, strength, wit and 'Physic himself' as subject to death.

    Thomas Nashe, Summer's Last Will and Testament (1600), song 'Adieu, farewell, earth's bliss' ; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  9. the all-dreaded thunder-stone l. 14

    The 'thunder-stone' was believed to be a stone or bolt falling with lightning; fossils and prehistoric stone axes were often identified as such. Shakespeare uses the word also in Julius Caesar 1.3 ('bared my bosom to the thunder-stone'). Jupiter's thunderbolt is a recurrent image in Cymbeline, culminating in the god's descent in act 5. The 'lightning flash' and thunder of this stanza thus anticipate the play's theophany, and some critics read the dirge as setting up Jupiter's eventual intervention.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2; Folger Shakespeare, Cymbeline 4.2 (text and notes)

  10. slander, censure rash l. 15

    'Censure' means judgment, opinion, often unfavourable. Imogen's suffering in the play stems from Iachimo's slander and Posthumus's 'rash' belief in it, so the line is pointedly apt. 'Thou hast finish'd joy and moan' means you have done with both pleasure and grief. The sequence of fears, nature, the great, the heavens and finally human tongues, ends with the evil that the play has shown to be most destructive. The phrase 'censure rash' inverts the normal adjective-noun order for the rhyme.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  11. Consign to thee l. 18

    'Consign' here means to set one's seal or signature to the same agreement, to subscribe to the same terms (Latin consignare, to seal). All lovers must ratify the contract of mortality the dead youth has signed. The legal metaphor is characteristic of Shakespeare's late style. The OED records this sense of 'consign', to sign or seal, especially in agreement. Editors note that the word carries the sense that lovers must 'deliver themselves' to the dead, joining them in the grave.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2

  12. come to dust l. 6

    God's sentence on Adam, 'for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19), was echoed in the Book of Common Prayer's burial service ('earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust'). Each stanza closes on this phrase, so the varied catalogues of life reduce to the same end. The pun on 'dust' and 'must' in the couplets gives the refrain its sense of inevitability.

    The Bible (King James Version), Genesis 3:19; J. M. Nosworthy, ed., Cymbeline (Arden Shakespeare, 2nd series, 1955), note to 4.2
