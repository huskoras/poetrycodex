Time & Brevity

Sonnet 3

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

Look in thy glass and tell the face thou viewest
Now is the time that face should form another;
Whose fresh repair if now thou not renewest,
Thou dost beguile the world, unbless some mother.
For where is she so fair whose unear'd womb
Disdains the tillage of thy husbandry?
Or who is he so fond will be the tomb,
Of his self-love to stop posterity?
Thou art thy mother's glass and she in thee
Calls back the lovely April of her prime;
So thou through windows of thine age shalt see,
Despite of wrinkles this thy golden time.
But if thou live, remember'd not to be,
Die single and thine image dies with thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Look in thy glass: the mirror sonnet l. 1

    A 'glass' is a mirror, and mirrors frame the whole sonnet: the youth's own looking-glass in line 1, the youth as his mother's 'glass' in line 9, and the 'windows' through which he will see his child in old age. Looking in a mirror recalls Narcissus, who loved his own reflection and died of it, the fate the poet fears for a youth absorbed in 'self-love' (l. 8). The Folger edition prints passages from Erasmus's praise of marriage, translated in Wilson's Arte of Rhetorique (1553), beside lines 5-8 of this sonnet, whose farming metaphor is closely paralleled there.

    Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 3, l. 1; Ovid, Metamorphoses 3 (Narcissus), Latin text, The Latin Library

  2. fresh repair l. 3

    'Repair' here means renovation, restoration of something worn, the sense used of buildings; Sonnet 10 urges the youth to 'repair' his 'beauteous roof'. The face is pictured as a house that must be kept up, and a child is the way to do it. 'Fresh' insists that the repair is new life rather than patching. Shakespeare Online glosses the phrase as 'healthy state'; the Oxquarry commentary reads it as the renewal of a fast-decaying face.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'repair'

  3. beguile; unbless l. 4

    'Beguile' means to cheat or defraud; the world is owed the copy of his beauty, an idea already present in Sonnet 1's 'the world's due'. 'Unbless' is a rare word meaning to deprive of blessing, here the blessing of children, which Scripture and the marriage service treated as God's gift ('Lo, children are an heritage of the Lord', Psalm 127.3). The phrase 'some mother' imagines the unknown woman who would be made a mother by him.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unbless'; Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Psalm 127.3, King James Version

  4. unear'd womb ... tillage of thy husbandry ll. 5-6

    'Ear' is an old verb meaning to plough (Old English erian, cognate with Latin arare). The question is rhetorical: what woman is so beautiful that her unploughed womb would refuse his cultivation? The metaphor of the woman as field and the man as sower was ancient and reflected the period's belief that the male seed alone formed the child. 'Husbandry' (farming, thrifty management) puns on 'husband'. The Folger edition sets Erasmus's similar argument, from Wilson's translation, beside these lines; modern readers often note how the metaphor reduces the woman to soil.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ear'; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes

  5. fond ... the tomb / Of his self-love ll. 7-8

    'Fond' means foolish. The syntax can be taken two ways: the man becomes a tomb in which his self-love is buried, or, through self-love, he becomes the tomb that 'stops' (blocks, ends) his posterity. Either way the self-regarding youth turns his body into a grave rather than a womb's partner, the opposite of the fertile field of lines 5-6. The Oxquarry commentary hears in lines 7-8 an additional sexual meaning, a glance at masturbation (compare the biblical Onan, who spilled his seed); others keep the sense moral rather than sexual. Compare Sonnet 1's 'Thy self thy foe' and its 'tender churl'.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 3, ll. 7-8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fond'

  6. thy mother's glass l. 9

    The mirror image returns, now turned outward: the youth's face reflects his mother's, so that in him she 'calls back' her own springtime. The line implies that beauty is passed down the female as well as the male line, and makes the young man a living proof of the procreation argument he resists. Critics have read the sudden mention of the mother biographically (Southampton's mother, Mary Browne, Countess of Southampton, has been suggested), but the Sonnets give no names, and most editors treat the identification as speculative.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 3, l. 9

  7. April of her prime l. 10

    'Prime' is the first and best season of life (Latin primus), often synonymous with spring. April, the month of blossom, figures youth throughout the Sonnets ('proud-pied April' in Sonnet 98). 'Calls back' is the language of memory and also of recall from exile: the past beauty is summoned back into the present. Shakespeare Online reads the line as showing how much the son's youth means to his mother.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'prime'; Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  8. windows of thine age l. 11

    The 'windows' may be the old man's eyes (eyes as windows of the soul, a commonplace), perhaps also spectacles or the wrinkled, lattice-like face; or they may be the child, through whom the father looks back on his past. Editors differ. The Oxquarry commentary reads the image as both a barrier and a point of contact with the past. 'Despite of wrinkles' concedes that age will come; the child does not prevent ageing but lets the father see beyond it.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 3, l. 11; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 3, l. 11

  9. golden time l. 12

    'Golden' evokes both value (continuing the language of treasure in the procreation group) and the classical Golden Age, the first and happiest of the ages of the world in Hesiod and Ovid, after which all declined into silver, bronze and iron. The youth's present is thus his own golden age, and old age his age of iron; a child would let him look back on that golden time as on a lost world restored. 'Golden' may also suggest the youth's fair hair and complexion, gold being the conventional colour of beauty in Elizabethan verse.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.89-112 (the Golden Age), Latin text, The Latin Library; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 3, l. 12

  10. couplet: remember'd not to be ... thine image dies ll. 13-14

    'Remember'd not to be' can mean 'intending not to be remembered' or simply 'so as not to be remembered'. The couplet's last word picks up the mirror imagery: 'image' is the reflection in the glass and the likeness passed to a child; to die single is to break the last mirror. The sardonic imperative, 'Die single', is typical of the procreation sonnets' rhetorical pressure. 'Single' also means unmarried and alone, as in Sonnet 8's 'Thou single wilt prove none'.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 3, notes; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 3 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 3
