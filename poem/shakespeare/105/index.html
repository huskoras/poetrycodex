Love

Sonnet 106

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

When in the chronicle of wasted time
I see descriptions of the fairest wights,
And beauty making beautiful old rime,
In praise of ladies dead and lovely knights,
Then, in the blazon of sweet beauty's best,
Of hand, of foot, of lip, of eye, of brow,
I see their antique pen would have express'd
Even such a beauty as you master now.
So all their praises are but prophecies
Of this our time, all you prefiguring;
And for they looked but with divining eyes,
They had not skill enough your worth to sing:
For we, which now behold these present days,
Have eyes to wonder, but lack tongues to praise.

Study notes by the editors

  1. chronicle of wasted time l. 1

    A chronicle was a historical record in order of time (Holinshed's Chronicles, 1577 and 1587, were a major source for Shakespeare's history plays). 'Wasted time' means time used up and gone, the past, with a hint that it was squandered. The sonnet goes on to describe chivalric romance rather than history proper, so 'chronicle' here covers the whole range of old writing about 'ladies dead and lovely knights'. Some commentators think of Spenser's Faerie Queene, but the sonnet names no source.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chronicle, n.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 106'

  2. wights l. 2

    'Wight' (Old English wiht, a creature or being) was archaic by 1600 and associated with older poetry, especially Chaucer and Spenser's imitation of him. Shakespeare uses it for antique or mock-solemn effect elsewhere, as when Iago says 'She was a wight, if ever such wight were' (Othello 2.1). Here the archaism gives period colour, like 'old rime' in the next line: the poem imitates the very style it describes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wight, n.'; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 106; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106

  3. beauty making beautiful old rime l. 3

    The beautiful subjects lend beauty to the poems ('rime', rhyme, verse), reversing the usual idea that poets confer beauty. 'Rime' is an older spelling, kept from the 1609 Quarto. Line 4's 'lovely knights' uses 'lovely' in its older sense of worthy of love, applied without strangeness to men. The pairing 'ladies dead and lovely knights' places both sexes in the old poems, which suits a sonnet addressed to a young man whose beauty is praised in terms used for both.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rhyme, n.'; Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), the first Quarto

  4. ladies dead and lovely knights l. 4

    The phrase sums up the matter of medieval and Elizabethan romance: the fair ladies and knights of Arthurian story, Chaucer's tales and Spenser's Faerie Queene. The line pairs 'ladies dead' with 'lovely knights', so that 'dead' and 'lovely' sit side by side at its centre; the ladies are long dead, and the knights' loveliness survives only on the page. For Shakespeare's readers 'lovely' did not suggest effeminacy, but the sonnet's later comparison of these beauties with the young man lets him stand for both ladies and knights at once, in keeping with the androgynous praise of Sonnet 20.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lovely, adj.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 106

  5. blazon l. 5

    To 'blazon' was to describe a coat of arms in heraldic terms; in poetry the blazon (French blason) is a list praising the beloved's body part by part, derived from Petrarch and very common in Elizabethan sonnets. Line 6 performs a miniature blazon: hand, foot, lip, eye, brow. Shakespeare parodies the convention in Sonnet 130 ('My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun'), and Nancy Vickers argued influentially that the Petrarchan blazon fragments and controls the body it praises, a reading that can be extended to this sonnet's male beloved.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blazon, n.'; Nancy J. Vickers, 'Diana Described: Scattered Woman and Scattered Rhyme', Critical Inquiry 8 (1981); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106

  6. as you master now l. 8

    'Master' means own, possess, have at command; it may also suggest 'surpass'. The old poets, says the speaker, were trying to express exactly the kind of beauty the young man has now. Readers have connected the word with 'master-mistress of my passion' in Sonnet 20, another poem where the young man's beauty crosses gender lines. 'Antique pen' (l. 7) means the pens of ancient writers, though 'antique' and 'antic' (grotesque) were not distinguished in spelling, so a faint second sense is possible.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 106; Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 106'

  7. praises as prophecies l. 9

    The third quatrain introduces a quasi-religious idea: the earlier poets were unknowingly prophesying the beloved, as Christian typology read Old Testament figures as foreshadowing, 'prefiguring', Christ. 'Prefiguring' (l. 10) is a technical word of typological reading (on which see Auerbach's essay 'Figura'). The idea is extravagant, and some critics find it close to the idolatry that Sonnet 105 ('Let not my love be called idolatry') has just disowned. The Quarto spells 'prophesies'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'prefigure, v.'; Erich Auerbach, 'Figura' (1938; trans. in Scenes from the Drama of European Literature, 1959)

  8. divining eyes l. 11

    'For' means 'because'. The old writers looked 'but with divining eyes', seeing by conjecture or prophecy rather than direct sight, so they lacked the skill to praise his true worth. In line 12 the 1609 Quarto reads 'still' rather than 'skill' ('They had not still enough your worth to sing'); most editors emend to 'skill', as the archive does, though a few defend 'still' in the sense of 'yet' or 'always'.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), the first Quarto; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 106

  9. eyes to wonder, but lack tongues to praise l. 14

    The couplet turns the logic round: the ancients had the words but not the sight; we have the sight but not the words. This is the modesty topos, the poet's claim that his subject exceeds his powers, and its effect is to praise the beloved beyond any praise. The speaker includes himself ('we') among those who lack tongues, a graceful paradox in a poem that is itself praise. The balanced antithesis (eyes / tongues, wonder / praise) gives the couplet the shape of an epigram.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 106; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 106; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 106
