Death & Dying

A Sea Dirge

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Death & Dying

Full fathom five thy father lies:
Of his bones are coral made;
Those are pearls that were his eyes:
Nothing of him that doth fade,
But doth suffer a sea-change
Into something rich and strange;
Sea-nymphs hourly ring his knell:
Hark! now I hear them,--
Ding, dong, Bell.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Full fathom five: Ariel's song l. 1

    In The Tempest (c. 1611), act 1 scene 2, the invisible spirit Ariel, serving Prospero, leads the shipwrecked prince Ferdinand across the island with songs. This one tells him that his father, King Alonso of Naples, lies drowned; Ferdinand responds that 'the ditty does remember my drowned father'. In fact Alonso is alive, so the song is a fiction serving Prospero's design. The title 'A Sea Dirge' was given by F. T. Palgrave in The Golden Treasury (1861), the source of this text. A fathom is six feet, so the body lies thirty feet down.

    Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes); Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), Book I (Project Gutenberg)

  2. thy father lies l. 1

    The audience will discover that Alonso survived the shipwreck, so Ariel's dirge is consolingly false. The song's comfort, that the dead are transformed rather than destroyed, prepares for the play's pattern of apparent loss and recovery, in which the 'sea-change' happens to the living (Alonso's repentance, Ferdinand's love for Miranda). Editors have stressed how the song's beauty and its untruth work together.

    Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2; Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes)

  3. Of his bones are coral made l. 2

    The image transforms the horror of decomposition into a jewel-like metamorphosis. Coral and pearl are both sea-born and precious, and both were thought to form by hardening. The verse is trochaic (stressed syllable first) and largely seven-syllable, as in many of Shakespeare's songs for spirits and fairies. The inversion 'Of his bones are coral made' stresses the bones and the change.

    Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2; Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes)

  4. Those are pearls that were his eyes l. 3

    Eliot quotes this line in 'The Burial of the Dead' (l. 48), in Madame Sosostris's tarot reading of 'the drowned Phoenician Sailor', and again in 'A Game of Chess' (l. 125). In Eliot's poem the Tempest's promise of transformation through death is set against modern sterility. The line has thus become a modernist touchstone as well as one of the best known in Shakespeare's songs.

    T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land (1922), ll. 48 and 125 (Project Gutenberg); Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2

  5. suffer a sea-change l. 5

    'Suffer' means undergo, not necessarily with pain. A 'sea-change' is literally a change caused by the sea; this passage is the OED's earliest example of the compound, and the idiom of a profound transformation derives from it. 'Nothing of him that doth fade' means 'no part of him that is perishable but undergoes'. The phrase captures the play's concern with metamorphosis and redemption.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2; Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes)

  6. Into something rich and strange l. 6

    'Rich' means precious, and 'strange' means wonderful and foreign. Percy Bysshe Shelley drowned in 1822 in the Gulf of La Spezia; his ashes were buried by Edward Trelawny in the Protestant Cemetery in Rome, and his gravestone bears the lines 'Nothing of him that doth fade, / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange'. The quotation, chosen for a drowned poet, shows how the song came to stand for the transforming power of art over death.

    'Protestant Cemetery, Rome', Wikipedia; Poets' Graves, 'Percy Bysshe Shelley'; Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2

  7. Sea-nymphs hourly ring his knell l. 7

    A 'knell' is the bell tolled at a death or funeral. Classical sea-nymphs (Nereids) are given an English parish custom, the passing bell. 'Hourly' suggests unending mourning. The juxtaposition of pagan nymphs and Christian bell typifies the play's mixed world. The bell motif is completed by the 'ding, dong' of the burden. Editors also note that 'hourly' may suggest the regular ringing of a church clock as well as perpetual mourning, and that the passing bell was rung as a person died, asking prayers for the soul.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes)

  8. Ding, dong, Bell l. 9

    In the First Folio the song has a 'Burthen: ding dong', a refrain sung by invisible spirits, after which Ariel sings 'Hark, now I hear them, ding dong bell'. A setting by Robert Johnson, lutenist to the King's Men, was printed in John Wilson's Cheerfull Ayres (Oxford, 1659), and is possibly the music used in early performances. The onomatopoeia imitates the knell of the previous line.

    Choral Public Domain Library, 'Full fathom five (Robert Johnson II)'; Virginia Mason Vaughan and Alden T. Vaughan, eds., The Tempest (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1999), note to 1.2; Folger Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2 (text and notes)
