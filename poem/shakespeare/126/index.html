Sonnet 127

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

In the old age black was not counted fair,
Or if it were, it bore not beauty's name;
But now is black beauty's successive heir,
And beauty slander'd with a bastard shame:
For since each hand hath put on Nature's power,
Fairing the foul with Art's false borrowed face,
Sweet beauty hath no name, no holy bower,
But is profan'd, if not lives in disgrace.
Therefore my mistress' eyes are raven black,
Her eyes so suited, and they mourners seem
At such who, not born fair, no beauty lack,
Sland'ring creation with a false esteem:
Yet so they mourn becoming of their woe,
That every tongue says beauty should look so.

Study notes by the editors

  1. In the old age: opening of the dark lady sonnets l. 1

    'In the old age' means in former times. 'Fair' meant both beautiful and light-complexioned, so in conventional terms 'black' could not be 'fair'. The sonnet argues that the fashion has changed: because cosmetics have made true fair beauty indistinguishable from false, blackness now inherits beauty's name. The identity of the dark lady (if any real woman lies behind her) is unknown; candidates such as Emilia Lanier or Mary Fitton are speculative. The paradox of a black beauty also appears in Love's Labour's Lost and in Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 7.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 127, headnote; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 127; William Shakespeare, Love's Labour's Lost 4.3 (Berowne on his black-browed lady), Folger Shakespeare

  2. beauty's successive heir l. 3

    'Successive' means coming by right of succession, so that, as Tyler's note puts it, beauty's esteem passes to black. The image makes beauty a dynasty in which black has succeeded to the title. Its opposite is the 'bastard shame' of line 4: true beauty is now slandered as illegitimate because counterfeit beauty has usurped its place. The language of succession would have had topical force in the years of Elizabeth's childless reign, though the sonnet's date is uncertain.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 127, l. 3 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'successive'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 127, l. 3

  3. bastard shame l. 4

    A 'bastard' is an illegitimate child, and figuratively anything spurious or counterfeit. Because cosmetics have made fair complexions suspect, natural fairness is slandered with the shame of being a bastard, i.e. a fake. Tyler's note sees the shame as the product of art, which falsely claims to rival Nature. The language of legitimacy continues the dynastic image of line 3, and the rhyme 'name / shame' puts beauty's title and its disgrace side by side.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 127, l. 4 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bastard'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 127, l. 4

  4. each hand hath put on Nature's power l. 5

    'Each hand' is every hand that applies cosmetics; to 'put on' Nature's power is to usurp it, as one puts on a garment or a role. Giving beauty was Nature's prerogative. The attack on face-painting is a frequent topic in the period's sermons and satires, and in Shakespeare (Hamlet to Ophelia: 'God hath given you one face, and you make yourselves another', 3.1).

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 127, l. 5 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); William Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1 ('God hath given you one face'), Folger Shakespeare; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 127, l. 5

  5. Fairing the foul l. 6

    'Fairing' is a verb formed from 'fair': making fair, both in the sense of beautifying and of whitening the skin with ceruse (white lead) and other cosmetics. 'Foul' is the antonym of 'fair' in both senses: ugly and dark. 'Art's false borrowed face' is the cosmetic mask, and 'borrowed' may also suggest wigs made of other people's hair, attacked in Sonnet 68.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fair'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 127, l. 6; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 68 (text, notes and synopsis)

  6. Sweet beauty hath no name, no holy bower l. 7

    A 'bower' is a dwelling or inner chamber, here a shrine where beauty might be honoured as a deity. Beauty is 'profan'd' (desecrated, from Latin profanus, outside the temple) by counterfeits. The Quarto spells 'boure'. 'If not lives in disgrace' means 'if it does not (merely) live in disgrace'. The religious language anticipates the mourning of the third quatrain. The line's two negatives ('no name, no holy bower') stress beauty's dispossession.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 127, l. 7 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bower'; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations)

  7. my mistress' eyes are raven black l. 9

    The 'Therefore' at line 9 marks the turn from general argument to the particular mistress. Raven black is the deepest gloss-black. Petrarchan convention required golden hair and bright eyes, so the dark mistress is an anti-Petrarchan figure (compare Sonnet 130, 'My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun'). Sidney had praised Stella's black eyes, explaining that Nature dressed them in mourning (Astrophil and Stella 7), the conceit Shakespeare reworks here.

    Sir Philip Sidney, Astrophil and Stella, sonnet 7 ('When Nature made her chief work, Stella's eyes'), Representative Poetry Online; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 130 (text, notes and synopsis); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 127, l. 9

  8. Her eyes so suited l. 10

    The Quarto reads 'Her eyes so suted', and the archive keeps it. The repetition of 'eyes' from the line before has struck many editors as a compositor's error, and nineteenth-century and later editors proposed 'brows' or 'hairs'. A strong support for 'brows' is Berowne's speech in Love's Labour's Lost 4.3: 'O, if in black my lady's brows be deck'd, / It mourns that painting and usurping hair / Should ravish doters with a false aspect'. Others, including the Folger text, keep 'eyes', reading the repetition as emphatic. The note reproduced on Shakespeare Online calls the sense 'clothed' for 'suited' questionable.

    Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions; William Shakespeare, Love's Labour's Lost 4.3 (Berowne on his black-browed lady), Folger Shakespeare; Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 127, l. 10 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations)

  9. they mourners seem ll. 10-12

    Black was the colour of mourning dress. The eyes seem to mourn 'At such who, not born fair, no beauty lack' (people who, though not naturally beautiful, are not without beauty, because they paint), 'Sland'ring creation with a false esteem' (defaming God's or Nature's work by a counterfeit reputation for beauty). The conceit goes back to Sidney's Astrophil and Stella 7, where Stella's black eyes are said to wear mourning.

    Sir Philip Sidney, Astrophil and Stella, sonnet 7 ('When Nature made her chief work, Stella's eyes'), Representative Poetry Online; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 127, ll. 10-12; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 127, ll. 10-12

  10. so they mourn becoming of their woe l. 13

    'Becoming of' means gracing or suiting: the mourning becomes them, they look beautiful in it. The couplet completes the paradox of line 1: black, which once could not be called fair, is now the very standard of beauty ('beauty should look so'). Vendler notes the poem's wit in turning fashion into argument. The paradox will be darkened in later sonnets (e.g. 131, 'In nothing art thou black save in thy deeds').

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 127; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 127, ll. 13-14; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 131 (text, notes and synopsis)
