Time & Brevity

Sonnet 15

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

When I consider everything that grows
Holds in perfection but a little moment,
That this huge stage presenteth nought but shows
Whereon the stars in secret influence comment;
When I perceive that men as plants increase,
Cheered and checked even by the self-same sky,
Vaunt in their youthful sap, at height decrease,
And wear their brave state out of memory;
Then the conceit of this inconstant stay
Sets you most rich in youth before my sight,
Where wasteful Time debateth with Decay
To change your day of youth to sullied night,
And all in war with Time for love of you,
As he takes from you, I engraft you new.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When I consider: the turn to verse ll. 1-2

    Sonnet 15 opens in the same 'When ... Then' pattern as Sonnet 12, but its couplet ends with the poet's own act ('I engraft you new') rather than with 'breed'. Most commentators mark it as the turning point where the theme of immortality through poetry enters the sequence; 16 and 17 then weigh poetry against procreation. Some note that 'consider' may carry its supposed Latin root in sidus, 'star' (considerare, to observe the stars), which suits the stars of line 4, though the etymology is uncertain.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 15; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, ll. 1, 14; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'consider'

  2. Holds in perfection but a little moment l. 2

    'Holds in perfection' means remains in its finished, full-grown state. The feminine ending of 'moment' (an extra unstressed syllable) lets the line itself linger and fall away. 'Moment' could mean both a brief time and weight or consequence (as in 'of great moment'), and the second sense may lurk: perfection has little lasting significance. The line states the Ovidian truth of perpetual flux that the whole sonnet develops.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'moment'; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library

  3. this huge stage l. 3

    The idea that the world is a stage (Latin theatrum mundi) goes back to antiquity and was a commonplace in Shakespeare's time; Jaques's 'All the world's a stage' (As You Like It 2.7) is the famous instance, and the Globe's motto was reputedly 'Totus mundus agit histrionem'. 'Presenteth' is a theatrical verb (to stage or perform) and 'shows' are spectacles or pageants, with the suggestion of mere appearances. A playwright's metaphor, it places human life before an audience of stars who 'comment' on it.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 3; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 15 (text, notes and synopsis); William Shakespeare, As You Like It 2.7 ('All the world's a stage'), Folger Shakespeare

  4. the stars in secret influence comment l. 4

    'Influence' was an astrological term: the ethereal fluid believed to flow ('in-flow', Latin influere) from the stars and shape earthly lives and events. It is 'secret' because invisible and unknowable. The stars also 'comment', like an audience whispering remarks on a play, or like commentators on a text. Booth notes the double role: the stars both watch the show and secretly control it. The line joins the theatre image to cosmology and prepares for the plant image of the next quatrain, in which the 'self-same sky' both cheers and checks.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'influence'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 4; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 4

  5. men as plants increase l. 5

    The comparison of human life with plant life is biblical ('As for man, his days are as grass: as a flower of the field, so he flourisheth', Psalm 103:15) as well as classical. It also links to the poem's final horticultural word, 'engraft'. 'Increase' is the key word of the procreation sonnets ('From fairest creatures we desire increase', Sonnet 1), so even the decline described here is set in the language of breeding.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 103:15; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 5; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 15

  6. Cheered and checked l. 6

    The alliterating verbs describe both the weather's effect on plants (warm sunshine cheers growth, cold checks it) and an audience's response to performers, so the theatre metaphor of the first quatrain continues. 'Checked' also meant rebuked. That both effects come from 'the self-same sky' emphasises that the same power that makes things flourish also ends them; the stars of line 4 are again in view.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 6; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'check'; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 15, l. 6

  7. Vaunt in their youthful sap l. 7

    'Vaunt' means boast or display proudly. 'Sap' is the plant's vital fluid and figuratively youthful vigour, including sexual energy. The line traces a whole life in a few words: boasting growth, peak ('at height'), then decrease. The rhythm itself turns at the comma after 'sap'. The imagery of rising and falling sap keeps the human and vegetable worlds fused, so that young men are literally treated as plants, which prepares for the gardener's grafting in the couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vaunt'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sap'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 7

  8. wear their brave state out of memory l. 8

    'Brave state' is splendid condition or rich dress (on 'brave', compare Sonnet 12), extending the theatrical image of costumes. To 'wear ... out of memory' is both to wear clothes until they are threadbare and to persist until no one remembers them. The 1609 Quarto prints 'were', and editors normally modernise to 'wear'; the old spelling lets a reader also hear 'were', i.e. they existed until beyond memory, a faint second sense.

    Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, textual notes (Quarto readings and emendations); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 8; Shake-speares Sonnets (London: Thomas Thorpe, 1609), facsimile, Internet Shakespeare Editions

  9. the conceit of this inconstant stay l. 9

    'Conceit' means idea or conception (not yet mainly 'witty figure'), from Latin conceptum. 'Inconstant stay' is an oxymoron: a 'stay' is a remaining, a period of residence, but this one is 'inconstant', that is, impermanent. The paradox concentrates the octave: everything only stays a moment. In line 10 the knowledge of transience 'sets' the youth before the poet as a jewel is set, making his present youth precious.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'conceit'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, ll. 9-10

  10. wasteful Time debateth with Decay l. 11

    'Debateth' can mean discuss or deliberate (Time and Decay confer as co-conspirators) or, from older French debatre, fight (they contend with each other over the spoils). Editors differ on which sense dominates, and both can be kept. 'Wasteful' means destructive, laying waste, as in the 'wastes of time' of Sonnet 12. 'Sullied night' (l. 12) is darkness that soils, contrasting with the 'day of youth'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 11; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'debate'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 11

  11. all in war with Time l. 13

    'All in war' means wholly at war. The couplet turns the poet into a champion who fights Time, the destroyer personified in line 11. Such defiant addresses to Time recur in Sonnets 19 ('Devouring Time'), 60, 63-65 and 123 ('No, Time, thou shalt not boast'). The heroic posture derives ultimately from Ovid's claim that his verse will escape devouring time (Metamorphoses 15.871-79), the source also of Sonnets 55 and 60.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Jonathan Bate, Shakespeare and Ovid (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), on the Sonnets and Metamorphoses 15; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 15

  12. I engraft you new l. 14

    To 'engraft' (or 'ingraft') is to insert a scion from one plant into another so it lives on there. As Time takes life away from the youth, the poet adds new life by setting him into poetry. The metaphor completes the plant imagery of the second quatrain and, as some readers suggest, there may be a pun on Greek graphein, 'to write', the supposed root of 'graft' (stylus-shaped scion), so that grafting is also writing. This is the first time the Sonnets claim that verse can renew the youth.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'engraft'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 15, l. 14; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 15, l. 14
