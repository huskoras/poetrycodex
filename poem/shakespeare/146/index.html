The Mind

Sonnet 147

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

The Mind

My love is as a fever longing still,
For that which longer nurseth the disease;
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
The uncertain sickly appetite to please.
My reason, the physician to my love,
Angry that his prescriptions are not kept,
Hath left me, and I desperate now approve
Desire is death, which physic did except.
Past cure I am, now Reason is past care,
And frantic-mad with evermore unrest;
My thoughts and my discourse as madmen's are,
At random from the truth vainly express'd;
For I have sworn thee fair, and thought thee bright,
Who art as black as hell, as dark as night.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnet 147 and the 'dark lady' l. 1

    The poem develops a single medical conceit: love is a disease, and reason is the doctor who gives up the case. It follows Sonnet 146, the soul's address to the body, and some readers connect the two through the idea of feeding (146 ends 'And death once dead, there's no more dying then'). 'Still' means always: the fever keeps longing for what feeds it. These sonnets are often called the 'dark lady' sonnets, but the label is the critics', not Shakespeare's.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  2. nurseth l. 2

    Galenic medicine taught that a sick appetite craves what harms it, and that giving in to it 'feeds' the illness. 'Nurse' can mean either to heal or to keep alive, and Shakespeare uses both: what nurses this fever is no cure. Line 3 repeats the idea with 'preserve the ill'. The paradox of a sick man who wants to stay sick is the core of the poem, and the first quatrain states it three times over.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nurse, v.'; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  3. appetite l. 4

    'Uncertain' means fickle and changeable, as a fever's cravings are. The inverted word order ('the ... appetite to please') holds back the verb. Renaissance writers on melancholy and love-sickness treat erotic desire as a disorder of the appetite; Robert Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) later gathered that tradition together. The quatrain is one long sentence. Its subject is the fever of l. 1, which feeds on the very thing that keeps the illness going.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'appetite, n.'; Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621), Part 3, 'Love-Melancholy'; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  4. Reason the physician l. 5

    In Renaissance psychology reason was meant to rule the appetites, and moralists often called it the soul's physician. Here the doctor is 'Angry' because his 'prescriptions' (orders, regimen) are not 'kept' (obeyed). Like a real doctor dropping a hopeless case, he walks out and leaves the patient to his delirium. The physician's anger is a comic touch in a grim poem.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  5. approve l. 7

    The older sense of 'approve' (from Latin approbare, to prove) is to show or learn by trial, not to give consent. 'Desperate' means without hope, and it also carries the theological sense of despair, the sin of refusing to trust in God's mercy. So the line describes a man who learns the truth only after it is too late to act on it. The theological echo matters, because the sonnet that precedes it is about the soul's salvation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'approve, v.1'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  6. Desire is death ... except l. 8

    'Physic' is medicine, and 'except' most likely means object to or forbid. So medicine forbade the desire that he now finds to be death. Some editors take 'which' to refer to death, which physic kept away as long as he obeyed it. 'Death' may also carry the period's sexual sense of orgasm, and many readers think the 'disease' is venereal. Compare Romans 6:23 ('the wages of sin is death') and James 1:15, where lust gives birth to sin and sin to death.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147; Bible (KJV), James 1:15; Romans 6:23

  7. Past cure, past care l. 9

    Shakespeare uses the proverb elsewhere: 'Things past redress are now with me past care' (Richard II 2.3) and 'past cure is still past care' (Love's Labour's Lost 5.2). Here he reverses its order: because Reason is past caring, the patient is past cure. The chiasmus of 'cure ... care' makes the sound of the proverb part of the argument. 'Reason' is now capitalized in the archive, marking the faculty as a character in the little drama.

    M. P. Tilley, A Dictionary of the Proverbs in England in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries (1950), s.v. 'past cure, past care'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  8. frantic-mad l. 10

    In ancient and Renaissance medicine phrenitis was a fever with delirium, so 'frantic-mad' completes the medical picture. 'Evermore unrest' means endless restlessness. Lines 11-12 list the symptoms: both thought and speech run 'At random from the truth', 'vainly' (uselessly, falsely) expressed. The words 'unrest' and 'random' push the poem from medical description into the language of madness. The patient has lost both health and sense.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'frantic, adj.'; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary

  9. discourse l. 11

    Compare Hamlet's 'a beast that wants discourse of reason' (1.2). 'Random' in the next line (Quarto 'randon') meant headlong or impetuous, from Old French randon, the rush of a gallop. So his very reasoning has become a symptom. The quatrain prepares for the couplet, which gives the clearest example of his false speech: his praise of the lady. The confession that his words have been 'vainly express'd' also casts doubt on the earlier sonnets in her praise.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'discourse, n.'; William Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.2; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147

  10. The couplet ll. 13-14

    'Fair' and 'bright' mean light-skinned and also beautiful or good. 'Black' and 'dark' likewise have both a physical and a moral sense. Sonnet 127 had praised blackness as the new beauty; here it becomes damnation. Critics have noted how violent the couplet is. Some read it as the height of the speaker's disgust with himself; others point out that the excuse of delirium does not soften the insult. 'Black as hell' and 'dark as night' are proverbial similes.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 1997; rev. 2010), commentary; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard, 1997), on Sonnet 147; Shakespeare's Sonnets (shakespeares-sonnets.com), commentary on Sonnet 147; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale, 1977), commentary
