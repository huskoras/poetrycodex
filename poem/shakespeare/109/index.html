Religion

Sonnet 110

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Religion

Alas! 'tis true, I have gone here and there,
And made my self a motley to the view,
Gor'd mine own thoughts, sold cheap what is most dear,
Made old offences of affections new;
Most true it is, that I have look'd on truth
Askance and strangely; but, by all above,
These blenches gave my heart another youth,
And worse essays prov'd thee my best of love.
Now all is done, save what shall have no end:
Mine appetite I never more will grind
On newer proof, to try an older friend,
A god in love, to whom I am confin'd.
Then give me welcome, next my heaven the best,
Even to thy pure and most most loving breast.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Alas! 'tis true: the confession sonnet l. 1

    Sonnets 109-112 are a group in which the poet admits absence and infidelity and seeks forgiveness. 'Gone here and there' means roamed, both literally (travelled) and figuratively (strayed in affection). Sonnet 110 has long been read biographically, together with Sonnet 111's complaint of 'public means which public manners breeds', as Shakespeare's regret at his life as a travelling player and playwright; most editors treat such readings cautiously, since the language may be figurative.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 110, l. 1; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 110

  2. a motley l. 2

    'Motley' is cloth of mixed colours, the dress of the household or stage fool, and hence the fool himself (Jaques in As You Like It: 'Motley's the only wear'). 'To the view' means in public. The phrase invites a reading in terms of Shakespeare's acting career, but the Oxquarry commentary observes that it is unknown whether he ever played the fool, and that the line is more likely a self-mocking image of conduct than a reference to roles. Either way, it admits public self-exposure.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'motley'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 110, l. 2

  3. Gor'd l. 3

    'Gore' means to pierce, wound, as with a horn; it may also mean to stain or defile (gore as filth or clotted blood). A heraldic sense has been proposed: a 'gore' was a triangular figure on a shield, which some heralds treated as a mark of dishonour, and Shakespeare's father had been granted a coat of arms in 1596. Commentators disagree on how far the heraldic sense is present. 'Sold cheap what is most dear' continues the self-accusation, with 'dear' meaning both beloved and costly, and 'sold' hinting at prostitution of his gifts.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gore'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 110, l. 3

  4. Made old offences of affections new l. 4

    The line is compressed and has been read in several ways: new affections (love affairs) have been turned into the old offences of infidelity; or by taking up new affections he has offended against an old love. 'Old' can also mean habitual, customary. The Oxquarry commentary notes that subject and object seem reversed. The reversibility of the phrase fits a sonnet whose language of new and old (youth, newer proof, older friend) runs throughout.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 110, l. 4; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 110, l. 4

  5. look'd on truth / Askance and strangely ll. 5-6

    'Askance' means sideways, obliquely, with suspicion or disdain; 'strangely' means as a stranger would, distantly. 'Truth' here is constancy in love as much as veracity. 'By all above' is a mild oath, 'by heaven', which anticipates the religious language of the sestet. The admission 'Most true it is' echoes the opening 'Alas! 'tis true', so the octave is framed as a double confession before any excuse is offered.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'askance'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  6. blenches l. 7

    To 'blench' is to flinch, swerve, or turn aside; the noun 'blench', a sideways movement or deviation, is unique in Shakespeare, though the verb appears several times (Hamlet: 'If he but blench'). Other suggested senses include sidelong glances and blemishes. Paradoxically, these lapses 'gave my heart another youth': they renewed his love, or (a darker reading the Oxquarry commentary also allows) gave him a new, younger lover.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blench'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 110, l. 7

  7. worse essays l. 8

    'Essay' (from French essai) means a trial or attempt; 'worse essays' are experiments with inferior loves, which demonstrated by contrast that the beloved is 'my best of love'. 'Prov'd' means both tested and showed to be. The argument that infidelity proved and renewed love is the rationalization of the octave; the sestet abandons it for a vow. Bacon's Essays (1597) and Florio's Montaigne (1603) were bringing in the literary sense, but here the older meaning of a trial or test governs.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'essay'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 110, l. 8

  8. Mine appetite I never more will grind / On newer proof ll. 10-11

    The image is of a blade sharpened on a whetstone: appetite is whetted by new trials ('proof', experiment). Sonnet 118 develops the same idea, of sharpening the appetite with 'eager compounds'. Booth comments on 'grind', and the Oxquarry commentary hears in it a suggestion of loveless sexual toil. 'Try' means test, and perhaps torment. Lines 9-12 are a vow of future fidelity: 'Now all is done, save what shall have no end' means that apart from his everlasting love, the wandering is over; the Quarto reads 'haue what shall haue no end', and 'save' is an editorial emendation that most editions, and the archive, adopt.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 110, ll. 10-11; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grind'

  9. A god in love, to whom I am confin'd l. 12

    The phrase raises the beloved to divinity, the language of devotion that Sonnet 105 ('Let not my love be call'd idolatry') half-defends. 'Confin'd' means bound, restricted to one, as a worshipper to one god or a prisoner to his cell. The religious register continues into the couplet, where the beloved is 'next my heaven the best'. The Oxquarry commentary compares Donne's Holy Sonnet 'Batter my heart', where confinement is likewise sought.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 110; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 110, l. 12

  10. couplet: next my heaven the best ll. 13-14

    'Next my heaven the best' is a vocative: you who are the best thing after heaven. The returning wanderer welcomed to a loving breast evokes the parable of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15.20), as the Oxquarry commentary suggests. The doubled superlative 'most most loving' is emphatic and unusual, crowning the earlier 'best' and 'Most true'. It can also be read ironically, as a glance at the beloved's own many loves.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 110 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Luke 15.11-32 (the Prodigal Son), King James Version; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 110, l. 14
