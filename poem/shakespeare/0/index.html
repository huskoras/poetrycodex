Death & Dying

Sonnet 1

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Death & Dying

From fairest creatures we desire increase,
That thereby beauty's rose might never die,
But as the riper should by time decease,
His tender heir might bear his memory:
But thou, contracted to thine own bright eyes,
Feed'st thy light's flame with self-substantial fuel,
Making a famine where abundance lies,
Thyself thy foe, to thy sweet self too cruel:
Thou that art now the world's fresh ornament,
And only herald to the gaudy spring,
Within thine own bud buriest thy content,
And tender churl mak'st waste in niggarding:
Pity the world, or else this glutton be,
To eat the world's due, by the grave and thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The procreation sonnets l. 1

    Sonnets 1-17 form a group in which the poet urges the youth to beget an heir. The argument draws on a familiar humanist exercise: Erasmus's epistle persuading a young man to marry, which Thomas Wilson translated in The Arte of Rhetorique (1553). Whether the sequence is autobiographical, and who the youth was (Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton, and William Herbert, Earl of Pembroke, are the usual candidates), remains unresolved. Some critics, Helen Vendler among them, treat this sonnet as a kind of preface that announces themes the group develops. The general 'we' of the opening line widens the appeal from a private request to a law of nature.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  2. beauty's rose l. 2

    In the 1609 Quarto the word is printed 'Rose', with capital and italic, a typographical emphasis that has prompted speculation. Some biographical critics have linked it to the name Wriothesley (sometimes pronounced 'Rosely'), but this is unproven, and many editors treat the italics as a compositor's habit with emblematic nouns. The rose was a conventional figure for transient beauty and for the beloved (usually a woman), so applying it to a young man is itself notable. The line establishes the sequence's central concern with the survival of beauty against time.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  3. tender heir l. 4

    The heir is 'tender' because young and delicate, and because cherished. 'Heir' was pronounced much like 'air', and 'tender' (young, soft, but also something offered) carries a note of delicate dependence. The heir bearing the parent's 'memory' sets up the sonnet's paradox: the youth is the world's most beautiful creature, but his beauty will be lost unless he reproduces. 'Riper' means mature, older.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  4. contracted l. 5

    'Contracted' combines two senses: engaged by a marriage contract and drawn together, narrowed. The youth is betrothed to his own eyes (and to the beauty they see in the mirror) and so reduced to them. Critics have connected this self-absorption with the myth of Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection; the next lines develop the image of a self-consuming flame. The legal and marital idea of a contract runs through the procreation sonnets, which repeatedly treat beauty as something held on trust and owed to the world.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  5. Feed'st thy light's flame with self-substantial fuel l. 6

    The image is of a lamp or candle that burns its own substance: the youth's 'light' (his beauty and the light of his eyes) is fed by fuel drawn from himself. 'Self-substantial' (made of one's own substance) is a rare compound, apparently first recorded in this line (see OED). The paradox is economic and physical at once: what should be spent outward in begetting children is being consumed within. Booth observes how the lines pile up contradictory images of feeding, burning and starving, a density characteristic of the sequence's opening.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  6. Making a famine where abundance lies l. 7

    In Ovid's Metamorphoses (3.466) Narcissus, recognising that he loves his own reflection, cries 'inopem me copia fecit', 'my abundance has made me poor'. Shakespeare's line turns the paradox outward: by keeping his beauty to himself the youth makes a famine where there should be plenty. Arthur Golding's 1567 translation, which Shakespeare knew, rendered the Narcissus episode at length. 'Thyself thy foe' completes the thought: self-love is self-destruction.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.466, trans. Brookes More (Perseus Digital Library); John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  7. only herald to the gaudy spring l. 10

    'Gaudy' derives from 'gaud', an ornament or a joy (Latin gaudium), and in Shakespeare's time could mean simply brilliant or festive. 'Only' may mean sole or pre-eminent. As herald of spring the youth is youthful promise itself; but a herald announces something to come, and the line implies that the promise must be fulfilled by offspring. 'Fresh ornament' in the preceding line similarly makes the youth an adornment of the whole world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line

  8. Within thine own bud buriest thy content l. 11

    The bud returns to the rose of line 2: a flower that never opens is a beauty that never sets seed. 'Content' carries several senses: contents (what the bud contains, potential offspring), contentment, and perhaps the essential matter or 'substance' of the self. 'Buriest' prepares the grave of the couplet. The alliteration of b-sounds ('bud buriest') has been noted as enacting the closing-in of the image.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  9. tender churl ... niggarding l. 12

    A 'churl' was originally a peasant and then a rude, ungenerous person, a miser. The youth is 'tender' (young, delicate, dear) and yet churlish, and he 'mak'st waste in niggarding', a paradox in which hoarding is itself wasteful. The verb 'niggard', to be stingy, comes from Middle English and is etymologically unrelated to the later racial slur. Critics have treated this line as the turning point of the poem's accusations, gathering its economic vocabulary of abundance, famine, waste and hoarding.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  10. Pity the world, or else this glutton be l. 13

    The couplet offers a choice. The world is owed the youth's beauty; if he does not pass it on, he and the grave between them will devour it. 'By the grave and thee' is compressed: the world's due is eaten 'by means of the grave and you', the grave consuming his body at death and he having already consumed his beauty by self-love. Editors differ over the syntax, some reading 'to eat the world's due, [that is, to be eaten] by the grave and thee'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line; 'Sonnet 1', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)
