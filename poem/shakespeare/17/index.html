Nature

Sonnet 18

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer's lease hath all too short a date:
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
And often is his gold complexion dimm'd,
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
By chance, or nature's changing course untrimm'd:
But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st,
Nor shall death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st,
So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,
So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Shall I compare thee: the opening and its place in the sequence l. 1

    Sonnet 18 follows the seventeen 'procreation' sonnets in the 1609 Quarto, which urge the young man to marry and reproduce his beauty in a child. Here, for the first time, the poet claims that his own lines will do that work. The opening is a rhetorical question that the poem at once answers by refusing the comparison: the summer's day proves an inadequate standard because it is changeable and brief. The poem is a Shakespearean sonnet (three quatrains rhyming abab cdcd efef and a couplet gg) in regular iambic pentameter. Editors note that the poem never says what the beloved actually looks like; its praise is built almost entirely out of what summer lacks, so the subject becomes the power of comparison and of poetry itself.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford World's Classics, 2002), note on Sonnet 18; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18

  2. temperate l. 2

    'Temperate' means moderate and equable, without excess. Its Latin root, temperare, means to blend in the right proportion, and in Renaissance physiology a 'temperate' person was one whose four humours were well balanced. The word therefore applies both to weather (a temperate climate) and to character (a temperate disposition), and the poem exploits both senses: the following lines catalogue the intemperance of summer (rough winds, scorching sun, clouds), against which the beloved's composure is measured. Compare 'complexion' in line 6, which likewise joins weather to the humours.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'temperate'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18

  3. darling buds of May l. 3

    'Darling' means dearly loved or favourite (from Old English deorling, 'little dear'). The buds are the vulnerable new growth of the year, shaken by rough winds. Commentators generally take it that May belonged to summer in Elizabethan reckoning: May was the month of May Day festivities and was reckoned the start of summer, as the sonnet's own pairing of 'May' with 'summer' suggests. The line thus shows summer damaging its own best things almost as soon as it begins, which prepares the reflection on decline in lines 7-8.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'darling'; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford World's Classics, 2002), note on Sonnet 18

  4. lease ... date l. 4

    A 'lease' is a contract letting property for a fixed term; 'date' here means the term or limit of that contract (OED 'date' n.2, a period or term of duration). Summer is imagined as a tenant who must give up possession when the lease runs out. The legal language recurs throughout the Sonnets (see Sonnet 13, 'that beauty which you hold in lease') and returns in this poem with 'possession' and 'ow'st' in line 10, where the beloved, unlike summer, is said to keep what he owns. The contrast is between beauty held on a temporary tenancy and beauty held outright through the poem.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lease'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18

  5. the eye of heaven l. 5

    The 'eye of heaven' is the sun, imagined as the sky's eye. The periphrasis was conventional (it goes back to classical poetry, where the sun is the eye that sees all), and Shakespeare also uses it in Richard II 1.3, where Gaunt says 'All places that the eye of heaven visits' are ports and happy havens for a wise man. In the sonnet the sun becomes a person with a face ('his gold complexion'), so that summer is personified as a rival beauty, and one that fails: sometimes it burns, often it is clouded.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Shakespeare, Richard II 1.3 (Gaunt's consolation of Bolingbroke), Folger edition

  6. gold complexion l. 6

    In Shakespeare's English 'complexion' meant both the colour of the face and, more technically, a person's constitution or temperament as determined by the mixture of the four humours (OED senses 1 and 4). The sun's 'gold complexion' is its shining face, which clouds 'dim' (darken). The double sense ties the line to 'temperate' in line 2: the sun has an unstable complexion, whereas the beloved's temper does not vary. Gold is also the conventional colour of a fair lover's hair and of value, so the sun is made a beauty whose brilliance is unreliable.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'complexion'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18

  7. every fair from fair l. 7

    The line plays on two uses of 'fair'. The first 'fair' is a noun meaning a beautiful person or thing; the second means beauty itself, as in Shakespeare's frequent use of 'fair' for 'beauty' (compare line 10, 'that fair thou ow'st'). The figure, repeating a word in different grammatical roles, is the rhetorical device called polyptoton or antanaclasis. 'Declines' suggests both a falling-off and the sun's descent in the sky, so the general law of decay is drawn from the same celestial imagery as lines 5-6.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fair'

  8. untrimm'd l. 8

    Beauty declines 'by chance' (accident) or by 'nature's changing course' (the ordinary process of ageing and the seasons). 'Untrimm'd' can mean deprived of its trimmings or adornments, as a dress is stripped of decoration; or it can carry a sailing sense, a ship whose sails and ballast are out of 'trim', so that it is carried by the course of nature. The grammar is uncertain: 'untrimm'd' may refer to 'every fair', to 'nature's changing course', or to both. Some editors keep all these antecedents open. Most editors take the line simply: beauty is stripped of its adornment by accident or by time.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'untrimmed'

  9. But thy eternal summer: the turn l. 9

    The adversative 'But' marks the turn (volta) at line 9, the traditional point of change in a sonnet, inherited from the Italian form's octave and sestet even when the Shakespearean rhyme scheme divides the poem into quatrains. The octave has proved summer an unworthy comparison; the third quatrain now claims a paradox, a summer that is 'eternal'. 'Eternal' appears twice in this quatrain (ll. 9 and 12), and the second occurrence explains the first: the summer will be eternal because it is set in 'eternal lines'. 'Shall not fade' is followed by two 'Nor' clauses, which extend the negation to loss and to death.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 18

  10. ow'st l. 10

    In Elizabethan English 'owe' commonly meant 'own' or 'possess' (OED 'owe' v., sense 1, now obsolete); 'that fair thou ow'st' is 'the beauty you possess'. The modern sense, 'to be in debt', was also current, and some readers hear it, especially after the legal 'lease' of line 4: beauty may be a debt owed to nature (compare Sonnet 4, where Nature's bequest 'gives nothing but doth lend'). 'Possession' in the same line keeps up the property language. The beloved, unlike summer the tenant, is presented as an owner, but only because the poem secures his title.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'owe'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18

  11. death ... his shade l. 11

    Death is personified as a braggart who boasts of the souls he has captured. 'Shade' combines two traditions. The biblical one is Psalm 23:4, 'though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death'. The classical one is the underworld of Homer and Virgil, where the dead are 'shades' (umbrae) wandering without substance, as in Aeneid VI. The beloved will not 'wander' among these shadows because he will be fixed in the poem. The defiance of death may also recall 1 Corinthians 15:55, 'O death, where is thy sting?', though this is an association rather than a demonstrated source.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 23:4; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:55; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18

  12. eternal lines ... thou grow'st l. 12

    The 'eternal lines' are the lines of the poem. The beloved 'grows' to time in them, an image of grafting: he is joined to time as a scion is joined to a stock, and so grows with it. Compare the close of Sonnet 15, 'As he takes from you, I engraft you new'. The phrase may also glance at the 'lines' of descent urged in the procreation sonnets, now replaced by lines of verse. The boast belongs to the classical topos of poetry as a monument outlasting time, famous from Horace, Odes 3.30 ('Exegi monumentum aere perennius') and the close of Ovid's Metamorphoses (15.871-79), which Shakespeare echoes more directly in Sonnet 55.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18; Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford World's Classics, 2002), note on Sonnet 18; Horace, Odes 3.30 (Latin text, Perseus Digital Library)

  13. the couplet ll. 13-14

    The final couplet, set off and indented in this edition, sums up the claim. Its two lines are built on parallel 'So long' clauses, and 'this' (the sonnet) is both the thing that lives and the thing that confers life. The immortality is carefully limited: it lasts only 'so long as men can breathe, or eyes can see', that is, as long as there are people to read the poem aloud or silently. Critics such as Vendler and Booth note how the beloved disappears into the poem: 'this' is the subject of both clauses, and 'thee' is the last word but the grammatical object. Some readers find a quiet doubt in the conditional.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 18; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 18; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 18
