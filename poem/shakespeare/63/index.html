Time & Brevity

Sonnet 64

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

When I have seen by Time's fell hand defac'd
The rich-proud cost of outworn buried age;
When sometime lofty towers I see down-raz'd,
And brass eternal slave to mortal rage;
When I have seen the hungry ocean gain
Advantage on the kingdom of the shore,
And the firm soil win of the watery main,
Increasing store with loss, and loss with store;
When I have seen such interchange of state,
Or state itself confounded, to decay;
Ruin hath taught me thus to ruminate:
That Time will come and take my love away.
This thought is as a death which cannot choose
But weep to have, that which it fears to lose.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When I have seen ... When ... When ll. 1-9

    The sonnet is built as one long period: 'When I have seen' (l. 1), 'When ... I see' (l. 3), 'When I have seen' (l. 5) and 'When I have seen' (l. 9), the last opening the third quatrain. The repeated anaphora gives the effect of accumulated witness, as if the speaker's whole experience testified to Time's power. The main clause comes only at line 11, 'Ruin hath taught me'. The instances move from human works to nature to abstract 'state'. The poem belongs with Sonnets 55, 60 and 65 among the great meditations on mutability.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 64; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes

  2. fell; defac'd l. 1

    'Fell' means fierce, ruthless (Old French fel, cruel). 'Deface' is to mar the face or surface of something, and was the word used for the iconoclasts' destruction of church images and tombs; the Oxquarry commentary hears an allusion to Reformation vandalism. Time is personified as a destroyer whose hand is like that of a human wrecker. The past participle 'defac'd' is held back to the end of the line, so the sentence's first image is of the destroying hand itself; what it has defaced is revealed only in line 2. The same hand appears in Sonnet 63 ('Time's injurious hand') and Sonnet 65 ('his swift foot').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fell'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'deface'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  3. rich-proud cost of outworn buried age l. 2

    'Cost' means costly things, expensive works, especially tombs and monuments raised at great expense; 'rich-proud' fuses wealth with arrogance. 'Outworn buried age' is the past era, now worn out and buried, and those buried in it. Tyler's note (on Shakespeare Online) reads it as the sumptuous buildings and possessions of a vanished people; the Oxquarry commentary suggests church and graveyard monuments.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cost'

  4. sometime lofty towers ... down-raz'd l. 3

    'Sometime' means formerly, though 'sometimes' is also possible. 'Down-razed' means razed, levelled to the ground (Latin radere, to scrape). English readers of the 1590s had before them the ruined abbeys left by the dissolution of the monasteries in the 1530s, and the Oxquarry commentary suggests the image draws on that memory; compare Sonnet 73's 'Bare ruin'd choirs'. The general image of fallen towers is also classical, from the ruins of Troy and Rome.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'raze'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 64, l. 3

  5. brass eternal slave to mortal rage l. 4

    The syntax allows two readings: 'brass [once thought] eternal [is] slave to mortal rage', or 'brass [is the] eternal slave to mortal rage'. Brass was the proverbial everlasting material (Horace's monument 'aere perennius', more lasting than bronze). 'Mortal rage' is deadly, destructive force, perhaps the rage of mortals (human destroyers), perhaps death's. Tyler identifies it with Mutability and Decay; editors also connect brass with church memorial brasses, worn by feet or stripped and sold after the Reformation, as the Oxquarry commentary notes (citing Blakemore Evans and Eamon Duffy).

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Horace, Odes 3.30, Latin text, The Latin Library; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 64, l. 4

  6. the hungry ocean ... the kingdom of the shore ll. 5-8

    The second quatrain draws on the speech of Pythagoras in Ovid's Metamorphoses 15, which Shakespeare knew in Arthur Golding's translation (1567): Pythagoras has seen firm land turned to sea and sea to dry land. The Folger edition prints Golding's lines 287-95 beside this sonnet. Shakespeare adds military and political language: the ocean is 'hungry', it gains 'advantage on the kingdom of the shore', the soil 'wins' from the 'watery main' (the open sea). 2 Henry IV 3.1 contains a similar image of the sea overrunning the continent, noted by Tyler.

    Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553); Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  7. Increasing store with loss, and loss with store l. 8

    The line is an exact chiasmus (store-loss / loss-store), a figure that enacts the reversal it describes. 'Store' means abundance or accumulated wealth. Tyler explains that the sea gains by the land's loss and loses by its gain; the balance is perpetual but nothing is stable. The mercantile word 'store' echoes the language of hoarding and spending in the procreation sonnets.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 64; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'store'

  8. interchange of state; state itself confounded ll. 9-10

    'Interchange of state' is alternation of condition, the swapping of land and sea; the Arden edition, as the Oxquarry commentary reports, takes this as the primary sense, with a political sense secondary. In line 10 'state itself' suggests magnificence or the body politic, kingdoms themselves, 'confounded' (ruined, destroyed) 'to decay'. Tyler noted the shift from 'condition' to 'magnificence'. Golding's Ovid speaks of places that have 'exchanged their estate', which may lie behind the phrase.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 64, ll. 9-10; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553)

  9. Ruin ... ruminate l. 11

    'Ruminate' (Latin ruminare, to chew the cud) means to ponder at length, turning a thought over as a cow chews; it continues the motif of devouring. Its first syllables echo 'Ruin', so that the very word for thinking contains the decay it thinks about. Ruin is personified as a teacher. The lesson, given in line 12, is the poem's personal turn: Time will take away 'my love', which can mean the beloved or the speaker's own love.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ruminate'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 64

  10. the couplet: weeping to have ll. 13-14

    The couplet does not offer the consolation of verse (as Sonnets 55, 60 and 65 do) but stays with grief. The thought 'cannot choose / But weep' (cannot help weeping); possession itself becomes painful because loss is foreseen. Tyler calls this an anticipatory pang of separation. It is one of the bleakest endings in the sequence: knowledge of mutability poisons present happiness.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 64, notes (Thomas Tyler, ed., 1890); Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 64; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 64 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)
