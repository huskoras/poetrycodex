Death & Dying

Sonnet 107

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Death & Dying

Not mine own fears, nor the prophetic soul
Of the wide world dreaming on things to come,
Can yet the lease of my true love control,
Supposed as forfeit to a confin'd doom.
The mortal moon hath her eclipse endur'd,
And the sad augurs mock their own presage;
Incertainties now crown themselves assur'd,
And peace proclaims olives of endless age.
Now with the drops of this most balmy time,
My love looks fresh, and Death to me subscribes,
Since, spite of him, I'll live in this poor rime,
While he insults o'er dull and speechless tribes:
And thou in this shalt find thy monument,
When tyrants' crests and tombs of brass are spent.

Study notes by the editors

  1. the prophetic soul / Of the wide world ll. 1-2

    The 'prophetic soul / Of the wide world dreaming on things to come' is the anticipation shared by humanity at large, a kind of anima mundi (world soul) that foresees events. Hamlet uses the same phrase when the Ghost confirms his suspicion: 'O my prophetic soul!' (Hamlet 1.5). In 1603 the death of a childless queen had been the object of decades of anxious speculation and prophecy, which is one reason many critics date the sonnet then.

    William Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.5 ('O my prophetic soul'), Folger Shakespeare; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, ll. 1-2; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 107

  2. the lease of my true love ll. 3-4

    A lease grants possession for a limited term. To 'control' it is to restrict or overrule it. 'Supposed as forfeit to a confin'd doom' means 'which was thought to be forfeited, subject to a limited term (or a sentence of confinement)'. 'Doom' means judgement or sentence (Old English dom). Legal metaphors of leases and forfeits recur in the Sonnets (e.g. 13, 'beauty which you hold in lease'; 18, 'summer's lease'). Some read 'confin'd doom' as a reference to the imprisonment of the Earl of Southampton, released by James I in April 1603.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 107, ll. 3-4; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, ll. 3-4; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'doom'

  3. The mortal moon hath her eclipse endur'd l. 5

    Elizabeth was celebrated as Cynthia, Diana or Belphoebe, the virgin moon goddess (as in Ralegh's 'The Ocean to Cynthia'), so the 'mortal moon' is widely taken as the queen. 'Hath her eclipse endur'd' can mean 'has suffered her eclipse', i.e. has died (1603), or 'has come through her eclipse', i.e. survived a danger. The note reproduced on Shakespeare Online (from Tyler's 1890 edition) takes the moon as Elizabeth, as Massey and Minto did, and, following Dowden, stresses 'endur'd': she has come through her eclipse, which it connects with the Essex rising of 1601; others think of her illness in 1599-1600 or the dangerous 'grand climacteric' of her sixty-third year (1595-6). Leslie Hotson (1949) read the crescent-shaped Spanish Armada of 1588. Some read a purely apocalyptic sign.

    Thomas Tyler, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (London: D. Nutt, 1890), note on Sonnet 107, l. 5 (as reproduced on Shakespeare Online); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, l. 5; Leslie Hotson, Shakespeare's Sonnets Dated, and Other Essays (London: Hart-Davis, 1949)

  4. the sad augurs mock their own presage l. 6

    An augur in Rome was a priest who foretold the future from birds and omens (Latin augur). 'Sad' means grave, solemn, as well as sorrowful. 'Presage' is prediction. The prophets of disaster either laugh at their own predictions, now disproved, or their predictions mock them. If the sonnet celebrates 1603, the expected chaos of the succession did not come: James VI of Scotland succeeded peacefully.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'augur'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'presage'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 107, l. 6

  5. Incertainties now crown themselves assur'd l. 7

    'Incertainties' (uncertainties) personify the anxious doubts about the future. They 'crown themselves assur'd', i.e. proclaim themselves certain, the verb suggesting the crowning of a new monarch. Readers who date the poem to 1603 see a reference to the secure accession of James I, whose coronation took place on 25 July 1603. Whatever the date, the line expresses the relief of a crisis that has passed: uncertainty, once feared, has crowned itself with assurance.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, l. 7; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 107; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (shakespeares-sonnets.com), note on the sonnet

  6. peace proclaims olives of endless age l. 8

    The olive is the classical and biblical emblem of peace (Noah's dove returns with an olive leaf, Genesis 8:11). James I, who ended the long war with Spain in 1604, took 'Beati pacifici' (Blessed are the peacemakers, Matthew 5:9) as a motto, and contemporary accession verse hailed his reign as an age of peace. 'Proclaims' suggests a royal proclamation. Those who date the poem earlier point instead to peace negotiations of the late 1590s.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 8:11; Bible (KJV), Matthew 5:9; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, l. 8

  7. the drops of this most balmy time l. 9

    'Balmy' means soothing, fragrant, like balm (aromatic resin used as healing ointment). The 'drops' may be gentle spring rain or dew, which makes the poet's love 'look fresh' like a plant. Balm was also the oil of royal anointing at a coronation (compare 'Not all the water in the rough rude sea / Can wash the balm off from an anointed king', Richard II 3.2). The combination fits the 1603 reading, though it does not require it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'balmy'; William Shakespeare, Richard II 3.2 ('the balm off from an anointed king'), Folger Shakespeare; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, l. 9

  8. Death to me subscribes l. 10

    To 'subscribe' is literally to sign one's name beneath (Latin subscribere), hence to assent or submit to. Death acknowledges the poet's victory because the poet will survive in verse. The reversal is bold: the poet, mortal in Sonnets 71-74, now outlives Death through his 'poor rime'. Compare the more anxious mortality of Sonnets 71-74, where it is the poet who must yield; here, buoyed by the 'balmy time', he can claim mastery. The legal and documentary flavour of 'subscribe' also suits a sonnet full of leases and forfeits.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'subscribe'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 107, l. 10

  9. this poor rime l. 11

    'Rime' and 'rhyme' were variant spellings; the Quarto has 'rime'. The modesty of 'poor' is conventional, but it sharpens the paradox: this humble verse will survive tyrants and brass. The phrase recalls the boast of Sonnet 55 ('this powerful rhyme'). The humility topos (the poet's verse as 'poor', 'barren', 'pupil pen') is frequent in the Sonnets, especially in the Rival Poet group, and here it heightens the contrast with Death's defeat.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rhyme'; Folger Shakespeare, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 55 (text, notes and synopsis)

  10. insults o'er dull and speechless tribes l. 12

    'Insults' means exults or triumphs arrogantly (Latin insultare, to leap upon); 'o'er' places Death standing over the defeated. The 'dull and speechless tribes' are the multitudes who are inarticulate and so leave no memorial; there may be a glance at 'barbarous' peoples without letters. The contrast is between the anonymous dead and those preserved by poetry. Some editors hear a contemptuous note in 'tribes', a word often used of savage or heathen peoples.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'insult'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 107, l. 12; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, l. 12

  11. thy monument ... tombs of brass ll. 13-14

    The couplet restates the classical claim that poetry outlasts bronze (Horace, Odes 3.30, 'monumentum aere perennius') and Ovid's close of the Metamorphoses (15.871-79), as in Sonnets 55 and 65. 'Crests' are heraldic devices on helmets and tombs; 'tombs of brass' are monumental brasses or bronze effigies. 'Tyrants' may be a general word for rulers, but readers attending to the political context note the irony after a reign has just ended. 'Spent' means used up, worn away.

    Horace, Odes 3.30 ('Exegi monumentum'), Latin text, The Latin Library; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15 (Latin text), The Latin Library; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, ll. 13-14

  12. Dating Sonnet 107 whole poem

    Because of its apparent topical references (the mortal moon, failed prophecies, peace, a balmy time, release from a 'confin'd doom'), Sonnet 107 has been used to date the whole sequence. The main proposals are: 1588 (the Armada; Hotson); 1595-6 (the queen's climacteric); 1599-1601 (illness, the Essex rising); and 1603 (Elizabeth's death on 24 March, the peaceful accession and coronation of James I, and the release of Southampton from the Tower). Many modern editors, including Duncan-Jones, favour 1603; others warn that the allusions are too vague to be decisive, and Booth stresses the poem's general images of crisis and renewal.

    Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 107, headnote; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 107, headnote; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 107
