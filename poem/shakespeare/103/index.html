Nature

Sonnet 104

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

To me, fair friend, you never can be old,
For as you were when first your eye I ey'd,
Such seems your beauty still. Three winters cold,
Have from the forests shook three summers' pride,
Three beauteous springs to yellow autumn turn'd,
In process of the seasons have I seen,
Three April perfumes in three hot Junes burn'd,
Since first I saw you fresh, which yet are green.
Ah! yet doth beauty like a dial-hand,
Steal from his figure, and no pace perceiv'd;
So your sweet hue, which methinks still doth stand,
Hath motion, and mine eye may be deceiv'd:
For fear of which, hear this thou age unbred:
Ere you were born was beauty's summer dead.

Study notes by the editors

  1. To me, fair friend l. 1

    The poem marks three years since the first meeting and asserts that, to the poet's eye, the friend has not aged. 'To me' immediately qualifies the claim: it is the lover's perception. Biographical critics have used the 'three years' to try to date the sequence, but many editors treat the figure as a poetic convention rather than a record, and the poem itself casts doubt on the reliability of the lover's eye.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary; 'Sonnet 104', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings)

  2. your eye I ey'd l. 2

    The repetition of 'eye' as noun and verb (polyptoton) makes the first meeting an exchange of looks, the traditional origin of love in Petrarchan poetry, where love enters through the eyes. The play also anticipates 'mine eye may be deceiv'd' in line 12: the organ that began the love is the one whose judgment is questioned. Elizabethan readers would know the doctrine that love is born in the eyes, as in Shakespeare's song 'Tell me where is fancy bred' (The Merchant of Venice 3.2).

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet

  3. Three winters ... three summers ... three springs l. 3

    The second quatrain counts the years through the seasons: three winters have shaken off three summers' leaves, three springs turned to autumn, three Aprils burnt into Junes. The syntax runs on from line 3 ('Three winters cold, / Have from the forests shook') across the quatrain break. 'Pride' means splendour, the summer foliage. The order is irregular (winter, summer, spring, autumn, April, June), perhaps to suggest the blur of passing time. The rhetorical figure of repeated 'three' is anaphora.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  4. In process of the seasons l. 6

    'Process' here means the onward course of time. The phrase sounds legal as well as natural, as in the 'process' of law, but its main sense is the steady progression that the poem fears. 'Which yet are green' (line 8) means the friend is still youthful, with a contrast between green youth and the burnt Junes and yellow autumns. The word thus quietly joins the friend's unchanging face to the very movement of time that will alter it, a tension the third quatrain makes explicit.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line

  5. like a dial-hand l. 9

    A 'dial-hand' is the pointer on a clock or the shadow on a sundial, whose movement cannot be seen. Beauty 'steals' from the figure in the same way. Booth notes that the metre requires 'dial' to be two syllables. The dial is a recurrent emblem of time in the sonnets (Sonnets 77, 'Thy dial how thy precious minutes waste'). The image overturns the opening confidence: the friend may be changing without the poet perceiving it.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 104', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings); Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  6. Steal from his figure l. 10

    'His' means 'its' (the usual Elizabethan neuter possessive). The dial-hand 'steals' (moves stealthily) from its 'figure', the number on the dial; at the same time beauty steals away from the friend's figure, or shape. 'No pace perceiv'd' means no step being seen. The double sense makes clock and face the same object. Booth's commentary shows how the sonnets often make one image serve two objects in this way. The thief-like motion recalls 'Time's thievish progress' in Sonnet 77.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note to the line

  7. mine eye may be deceiv'd l. 12

    The admission undermines the opening line. The friend's 'hue' (appearance, complexion), which 'methinks' still stands, has motion. The sonnets repeatedly question the lover's eye (compare Sonnets 113, 137, 148), and this poem dramatises the gap between perception and reality. It also prepares the couplet's paradoxical consolation. The sonnet's turn at line 9 ('Ah! yet') marks the point where certainty yields to doubt; the poem's claim of permanence is undercut by its own evidence. The admission is followed by a defensive address to posterity.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary

  8. thou age unbred l. 13

    'Age unbred' is the age not yet begotten, posterity. The address 'hear this' (with a mid-line reversal of stress) turns the poem from the friend to future readers, as the sequence elsewhere turns to posterity to guarantee the friend's fame (Sonnets 18, 55). Here the message is that the perfect beauty those future ages might hope for has already passed.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; 'Sonnet 104', Wikipedia (summary of critical readings); Colin Burrow, ed., The Complete Sonnets and Poems (Oxford UP, 2002), note to the line

  9. Ere you were born was beauty's summer dead l. 14

    The couplet's hyperbole makes the friend the absolute standard of beauty: whatever the future sees, its 'summer' died before it was born. The line recalls the seasonal imagery of the quatrains and the famous comparison to 'a summer's day' (Sonnet 18). Some readers find the compliment consoling, others hear in it a concession that the friend too will age and die.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), commentary on the sonnet; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (New Haven: Yale UP, 1977; rev. 2000), commentary on the line; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary
