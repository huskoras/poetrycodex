Time & Brevity

Sonnet 65

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Time & Brevity

Since brass, nor stone, nor earth, nor boundless sea,
But sad mortality o'ersways their power,
How with this rage shall beauty hold a plea,
Whose action is no stronger than a flower?
O! how shall summer's honey breath hold out,
Against the wrackful siege of battering days,
When rocks impregnable are not so stout,
Nor gates of steel so strong but Time decays?
O fearful meditation! where, alack,
Shall Time's best jewel from Time's chest lie hid?
Or what strong hand can hold his swift foot back?
Or who his spoil of beauty can forbid?
O! none, unless this miracle have might,
That in black ink my love may still shine bright.

Study notes by the editors

  1. the opening list and its link to Sonnet 64 l. 1

    The syntax is compressed: 'since there is no brass, stone, earth or sea but that sad mortality overrules its power'. The four items move from human monuments (brass, stone) to the elements (earth, sea), taking up the 'brass eternal' and the encroaching ocean of Sonnet 64, which this poem follows directly. The list of supposedly durable things recalls the classical claim that poetry outlasts bronze (Horace's 'aere perennius', Odes 3.30), here turned to show that even bronze perishes. The sonnet belongs to the young-man group that meditates on Time's destruction.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; Horace, Odes 3.30 ('Exegi monumentum aere perennius'); Wikipedia, 'Sonnet 64'

  2. o'ersways l. 2

    'O'ersway' means to rule over, overpower. 'Sad' carries its older senses of grave, solemn and heavy as well as sorrowful. Mortality is personified as a ruler whose sway extends over brass and sea. The poem quickly moves from this political metaphor to legal and military ones (plea, action, siege), so that Time is in turn monarch, litigant and besieging army.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'oversway, v.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65

  3. hold a plea l. 3

    'Hold a plea' is a legal phrase meaning to maintain or try a suit. 'This rage' is the destructive fury of mortality, picking up 'mortal rage' in Sonnet 64. The legal sense continues in 'action' (l. 4), which means a lawsuit as well as a deed or force: beauty's case is no stronger than a flower. The setting of courtroom against garden is typical of the Sonnets' mixing of vocabularies.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 65; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 65

  4. no stronger than a flower l. 4

    The flower as a figure of brief life is biblical: 'All flesh is grass, and all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field' (Isaiah 40:6), echoed in 1 Peter 1:24 and in the Prayer Book burial service ('He cometh up, and is cut down, like a flower'). The irony is that the most fragile thing is set against brass and stone. Some editors also hear in 'action' the sense of a plant's force or operation, a botanical as well as a legal pun.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 40:6; The Book of Common Prayer (1559), Order for the Burial of the Dead; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65

  5. summer's honey breath l. 5

    Summer's 'honey breath' is its perfumed air, like the scented roses of Sonnet 54. The image is deliberately soft, and it is set against a hard military figure in the next line: can such breath 'hold out' (endure a siege) against the battering of days? The figure of a besieged city or castle continues through 'rocks impregnable' and 'gates of steel', and the contrast between soft breath and hard stone sharpens the poem's despair.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 65

  6. wrackful siege of battering days l. 6

    'Wrackful' means destructive, causing wreck or ruin ('wrack' is a variant of 'wreck'). 'Battering' calls up the battering ram used against city walls and gates. Time does not destroy in one stroke but by a slow siege of days, each one a blow. The next two lines extend the figure: impregnable rocks (natural fortresses) and steel gates also fall, 'but Time decays' them, where 'decays' is transitive, meaning 'causes to decay'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wrackful, adj.'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; Shakespeare Navigators (Eastern Washington University), notes on Sonnet 65

  7. O fearful meditation! l. 9

    The exclamation marks the turn in the poem's argument. 'Fearful' means both frightening and full of fear. The quatrain shifts from statement to urgent rhetorical questions (where? what? who?), which all expect the answer 'none'. The repeated apostrophes ('O!' in ll. 5, 9 and 13) give the poem a heightened, lamenting tone quite different from the confident boast of Sonnet 55.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 65; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65

  8. Time's best jewel from Time's chest l. 10

    A much-discussed line. 'Time's best jewel' is the young man, the finest thing Time has produced. 'Time's chest' may be Time's treasure-box, into which he puts back his jewels (he reclaims what he has given), or a coffin; 'chest' could mean both. So the jewel must be hidden from its own owner. Booth discusses how the line makes Time both giver and thief; other editors also stress the coffin sense. The question is answered only in the couplet: the hiding place is the ink of the poem.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 65; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Arden Shakespeare, 3rd series, 1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 65

  9. hold his swift foot back l. 11

    Time's swiftness was proverbial (tempus fugit), and Renaissance images gave him wings; here he is a runner. 'Spoil' (l. 12) means plunder: Time despoils beauty as a conqueror loots a city, completing the military figure of the second quatrain. 'Forbid' implies legal or royal prohibition, returning to the language of power from line 2. For the iconography of Father Time see Panofsky's classic essay.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spoil, n.1'; Erwin Panofsky, 'Father Time', in Studies in Iconology (1939)

  10. in black ink my love may still shine bright l. 14

    The couplet answers all the questions with 'none, unless...'. The miracle is the poem itself: black ink, the opposite of brightness, will make the beloved shine. The paradox of light out of darkness is deliberate and witty. 'Still' means always, for ever, as well as 'nevertheless'. Compared with Sonnet 55, the claim is tentative ('unless this miracle have might'), and many readers, Vendler among them, hear doubt in the conditional. 'My love' means both the beloved and the poet's love for him.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard University Press, 1997), on Sonnet 65; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets (Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 65; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets commentary, Sonnet 65
