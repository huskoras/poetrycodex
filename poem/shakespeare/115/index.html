Love

Sonnet 116

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

Let me not to the marriage of true minds
Admit impediments. Love is not love
Which alters when it alteration finds,
Or bends with the remover to remove:
O, no! it is an ever-fixed mark,
That looks on tempests and is never shaken;
It is the star to every wandering bark,
Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken.
Love's not Time's fool, though rosy lips and cheeks
Within his bending sickle's compass come;
Love alters not with his brief hours and weeks,
But bears it out even to the edge of doom.
If this be error and upon me prov'd,
I never writ, nor no man ever lov'd.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Let me not to the marriage of true minds ll. 1-2

    The sonnet is one of the most famous definitions of love in English and a favourite wedding reading, yet it belongs to the sequence addressed to a young man, and its 'marriage' is of minds, not a church wedding. Its opening echoes the Book of Common Prayer's marriage service, where the priest asks whether anyone knows of an 'impediment' to the union. Helen Vendler reads the poem as a reply in an argument, refuting an unspoken claim that love changes with circumstance (hence the emphatic 'O, no!'). The poem proceeds largely by negatives: it defines love by what it is not.

    The Book of Common Prayer (1559), 'The Form of Solemnization of Matrimony', Internet Shakespeare Editions transcription; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 116; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116

  2. Admit impediments l. 2

    In the 1559 Book of Common Prayer the priest charges the couple that 'if either of you do know any impediment' why they may not lawfully marry, they should confess it, and the rubric provides for anyone who will 'allege and declare any impediment'. Shakespeare's speaker refuses to admit (acknowledge, allow) any such obstacle to the union of true minds. The enjambment ('true minds / Admit impediments') sets the key word at the start of a line, and the full stop in mid-line 2 gives the sentence a judicial firmness. Compare the attempts to stop the wedding in Much Ado About Nothing.

    The Book of Common Prayer (1559), 'The Form of Solemnization of Matrimony', Internet Shakespeare Editions transcription; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'impediment'

  3. alters ... alteration; remover to remove ll. 3-4

    Lines 3-4 use polyptoton, repeating a word in different forms: 'alters ... alteration', 'remover ... remove'. The figure enacts the false love it describes, which simply mirrors the other person's movements. 'Alteration' may mean a change in the beloved's affection, looks or fortune. 'Remover' is one who moves away, perhaps one who is unfaithful; 'bends ... to remove' means inclines to withdraw in turn. The word 'alters' returns in line 11 ('Love alters not'), so the poem answers its own definition.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116

  4. O, no! l. 5

    The exclamation breaks the poem's measured rhetoric with a spoken outburst. Vendler takes it as evidence that the poem is a rebuttal of another speaker's view; Booth notes its forceful, almost over-insistent tone. Some critics (following this line of argument) find the poem's very emphasis a sign of anxiety: the speaker protests that love cannot change precisely because he fears that it does.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116

  5. an ever-fixed mark l. 5

    A 'mark' (or sea-mark) was a conspicuous fixed object, such as a beacon, tower or lighthouse, used by sailors to steer (OED 'mark' n.1; 'sea-mark'). Shakespeare uses 'sea-mark' in Coriolanus 5.3 and Othello 5.2. Love 'looks on tempests and is never shaken': it observes storms without being moved, unlike the 'bark' of line 7. The image begins the nautical sequence of the second quatrain.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mark'; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116

  6. the star to every wandering bark l. 7

    The 'star' is usually taken to be the Pole Star (Polaris), which stays almost fixed above the north pole and was the navigator's chief guide. A 'bark' is a small sailing ship. Love is the fixed point by which errant lovers ('wandering' ships) find their way. 'Wandering' anticipates the 'error' of line 13 (Latin errare, to wander), so that the couplet's word for a mistake recalls the lost ship. The star was a familiar Petrarchan image for the beloved as guide.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bark'

  7. Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken l. 8

    Sailors 'took the height' (measured the altitude) of the Pole Star with a quadrant or astrolabe to find their latitude. Its 'worth', however, cannot be known: the star's nature and its influence (in astrological thinking, its power over human lives) remain beyond calculation. The line says that love can be used and measured without being understood or fully valued. One commentary notes that 'taking the height' of something could also mean estimating its worth, which sharpens the contrast. 'His' here means 'its'.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint (Penguin, 1986), commentary on Sonnet 116

  8. Time's fool l. 9

    A 'fool' was a court jester kept for a master's amusement, or more generally a dupe, someone made sport of. Love is not Time's fool: Time cannot mock it or make it ridiculous by altering the lovers' looks. Shakespeare uses the phrase elsewhere, notably Hotspur's dying 'But thought's the slave of life, and life time's fool' (1 Henry IV 5.4). Personified Time carries his traditional sickle (l. 10).

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Shakespeare, 1 Henry IV 5.4, Folger edition

  9. his bending sickle's compass l. 10

    Time, like Death, was depicted as a reaper with a curved sickle or scythe. 'Compass' means range or reach, and also the arc swept by a curved blade (and the instrument that draws circles). Rosy lips and cheeks (youthful beauty) come within its reach and are cut down. The 'bending' sickle is answered by love, which does not bend (compare 'bends with the remover', l. 4). The quatrain concedes that beauty dies while insisting that love does not.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'compass'

  10. the edge of doom l. 12

    'Doom' means judgement, and 'the edge of doom' is the brink of Doomsday, the Last Judgement at the end of the world (compare Macbeth 4.1, 'the crack of doom'). 'Bears it out' means endures, holds out (like a ship riding out a storm, picking up the nautical images). Some read 'doom' more narrowly as death, so that love lasts until the lovers die; most editors prefer the apocalyptic sense, which matches the poem's absolute claims.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'doom'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 20:11-12

  11. If this be error and upon me prov'd l. 13

    'Error' means a mistake, but it also meant heresy (a wandering from true doctrine), and the Latin root errare recalls the 'wandering bark' of line 7. 'Upon me prov'd' is courtroom language: proved against me by evidence. Editors also note the legal term 'writ of error'. The couplet stakes everything on the definition, but its logic is circular: the existence of the poem itself is offered as proof that the speaker has 'writ'.

    Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'error'

  12. I never writ, nor no man ever lov'd l. 14

    'Writ' was a normal past tense of 'write'. 'Nor no man' is an emphatic double negative, common in Shakespeare. The claim is a deliberate impossibility: the poet obviously has written, and men have loved, so the definition must stand. Critics differ on the tone. Some read a triumphant seal on the argument; others, noting that the couplet relies on a sophistical proof, sense defensiveness. The legal pun on 'writ' (a written legal document) continues the language of 'prov'd'.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (Yale UP, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 116; Shakespeare's Sonnets, ed. with commentary (Oxquarry Books), note on Sonnet 116; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Harvard UP, 1997), on Sonnet 116
