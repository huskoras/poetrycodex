Nature

Sonnet 98

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Nature

From you have I been absent in the spring,
When proud-pied April, dress'd in all his trim,
Hath put a spirit of youth in every thing,
That heavy Saturn laugh'd and leap'd with him.
Yet nor the lays of birds, nor the sweet smell
Of different flowers in odour and in hue,
Could make me any summer's story tell,
Or from their proud lap pluck them where they grew:
Nor did I wonder at the lily's white,
Nor praise the deep vermilion in the rose;
They were but sweet, but figures of delight,
Drawn after you, you pattern of all those.
Yet seem'd it winter still, and you away,
As with your shadow I with these did play.

Study notes by the editors

  1. absent in the spring l. 1

    Sonnets 97-99 form a group on absence: 97 ('How like a winter hath my absence been') is set in summer and autumn, 98 in spring, and 99 continues 98's flower catalogue. The order of the seasons has puzzled editors, and some think the absence lasted most of a year. The idea that the lover's absence makes summer seem winter is a Petrarchan commonplace, which Shakespeare develops through a densely pictorial spring scene.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 98, l. 1; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, Sonnet 98 (text and synopsis), ed. Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine

  2. proud-pied April, dress'd in all his trim l. 2

    'Pied' means dappled, of mixed colours (as in Love's Labour's Lost's 'When daisies pied'); 'proud' is regularly used of rich clothing, so the compound suggests splendidly varicoloured. 'Trim' is ornamental dress, finery. April is masculine ('his trim'), a young gallant in his best clothes. The Oxquarry commentary notes that the Quarto has no hyphen, and that some link 'pied' to the motley of a fool, though this is uncertain.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pied'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trim'

  3. a spirit of youth in every thing l. 3

    The line recalls the opening of Chaucer's General Prologue, where April showers and Zephyrus inspire new growth and Nature stirs living things; Booth notes the resemblance to the Prologue in lines 1-5. 'Spirit' carries its root sense of breath (Latin spiritus) as well as liveliness. The animation of the world prepares for the comic image of the next line. 'Every thing' (two words in the Quarto and the archive) stresses that nothing is left untouched by spring except the absent lover.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 98, ll. 1-5; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue, ll. 1-11, Harvard Chaucer Page

  4. heavy Saturn laugh'd and leap'd with him l. 4

    In astrology Saturn, the slowest and most distant of the known planets, was cold, dry and 'heavy' (associated with lead), governing melancholy, old age and winter; a 'saturnine' temperament is gloomy. In myth Saturn (Kronos) was the old god, father of Jupiter, and often confused with Time. For him to laugh and leap is the strongest possible measure of spring's power, and an unexpectedly comic image. It sets up the contrast with the speaker, whom spring could not cheer.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'saturnine'; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 98, l. 4

  5. lays l. 5

    A 'lay' is a short song or lyric poem; the OED records its use for the songs of birds from the fourteenth century, as the Oxquarry commentary notes. With 'the sweet smell / Of different flowers' the quatrain moves through the senses, hearing and smell, before turning to sight in the third quatrain. 'Different ... in odour and in hue' means differing in scent and colour.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lay'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)

  6. summer's story l. 7

    The phrase appears to invert the proverbial 'winter's tale', a sad or idle story told by the fire (Shakespeare later used it as a play title: 'A sad tale's best for winter', The Winter's Tale 2.1). The Oxquarry commentary notes that 'summer's story' seems to be Shakespeare's own coinage. The speaker cannot be moved even to tell a happy tale or to pick the flowers from the 'proud lap' of the earth, the ground from which they grow.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale 2.1, Folger Shakespeare; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 98, l. 7

  7. their proud lap l. 8

    The 'lap' is the bosom of the earth or of the flowerbed in which flowers grow; 'proud' repeats the word from line 2 and may mean splendid, or swollen and luxuriant (as land is 'proud' when lush). Grammatically 'their' refers to the flowers, so the lap is theirs, the ground they spring from. Not plucking them shows the speaker's indifference.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'proud'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 98, l. 8

  8. the lily's white ... the deep vermilion in the rose ll. 9-10

    The white lily and the red rose were the conventional emblems of the beautiful complexion in Petrarchan poetry (white skin, red cheeks and lips). 'Vermilion' is a brilliant red pigment, made from cinnabar, so the rose is described as if painted. 'White' may be a noun (the lily's whiteness) or the end of a phrase, 'the lily's white [colour]'. Sonnet 99 continues the thought, accusing the flowers of stealing their colours from the beloved; the Folger edition pairs that sonnet with a poem in Henry Constable's Diana.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vermilion'; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553)

  9. figures of delight l. 11

    'Figures' are representations, images, shapes; the flowers are merely sweet, merely images of what truly delights. The thought is Neo-Platonic: the beloved is the ideal form (the 'pattern') and the flowers are imperfect copies 'drawn after' him, as a painter draws after a model. The Oxquarry commentary compares Sonnets 53 and 106, where the beloved's beauty is the original of all earlier beauty.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'figure'; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 98

  10. Drawn after you, you pattern of all those l. 12

    'Drawn after' means copied from, as an artist draws after a model; 'pattern' is the exemplar or original (compare Sonnet 19's 'beauty's pattern'). The repeated 'you' gives the line the force of an address in the midst of description. In Sonnet 53 the corresponding terms are 'substance' and 'shadows'. The line thus turns the description of spring into a compliment: the season's beauty was not ignored because it was poor, but because its original was missing.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pattern'; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 98, l. 12

  11. couplet: winter still ... your shadow ll. 13-14

    'You away' is an absolute construction: 'you being away'. The flowers are now the beloved's 'shadow', an image or reflection (the opposite of 'substance'), echoing Sonnet 53's question about the 'millions of strange shadows' that attend the beloved. 'Play' may mean amuse oneself or toy, and the Oxquarry commentary hears an erotic overtone. The return of winter at the end links the sonnet back to Sonnet 97.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 98 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 98, ll. 13-14; Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 98
