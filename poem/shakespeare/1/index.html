Love

Sonnet 2

by William Shakespeare 1564-1616

Love

When forty winters shall besiege thy brow,
And dig deep trenches in thy beauty's field,
Thy youth's proud livery so gazed on now,
Will be a tatter'd weed of small worth held:
Then being asked, where all thy beauty lies,
Where all the treasure of thy lusty days;
To say, within thine own deep sunken eyes,
Were an all-eating shame, and thriftless praise.
How much more praise deserv'd thy beauty's use,
If thou couldst answer 'This fair child of mine
Shall sum my count, and make my old excuse,'
Proving his beauty by succession thine!
This were to be new made when thou art old,
And see thy blood warm when thou feel'st it cold.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When forty winters: the procreation argument l. 1

    Sonnets 1-17 urge a beautiful young man to marry and father a child. Their arguments draw on a commonplace Renaissance oration in praise of marriage, Erasmus's Encomium Matrimonii, which reached English readers in Thomas Wilson's Arte of Rhetorique (1553); the Folger edition prints excerpts beside Sonnets 3, 4, 6 and 13. 'Forty winters' adds forty years to the youth's present age; since Elizabethans counted forty as the threshold of old age, the line pictures a man well past his prime. Measuring years by winters already shows the age as cold and barren. The sonnet is one of the few that circulated in manuscript before 1609: copies in seventeenth-century miscellanies (one headed 'Spes Altera', 'another hope') differ markedly from the Quarto, and scholars dispute whether they preserve an early draft or a later rewriting.

    Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553); Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Purificación Ribes Traver, 'New ways of looking into handwritten miscellanies of the seventeenth century: the case of "Spes Altera"', Journal of English Studies 18 (2020); Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 2, l. 1

  2. besiege ... trenches ... field ll. 1-2

    'Besiege' sets up an extended siege metaphor. Besieging armies dug trenches before a town's walls; so the forty winters dig lines into the 'field' of the young man's face. 'Field' works doubly: a field of battle (and the 'field' of a heraldic shield), and a farmed field whose furrows the wrinkles resemble, an agricultural reading that anticipates the 'tillage' and 'husbandry' of Sonnet 3. The lines hover between the two senses without settling on either. The image recurs in Sonnet 60, where Time 'delves the parallels in beauty's brow'. Compare also the 'livery' of the next line, which continues the language of the noble household and its retainers.

    Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 2, ll. 1-2; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes

  3. livery; tatter'd weed ll. 3-4

    'Livery' was the distinctive dress given to the servants and retainers of a great household, often lavish because it displayed the lord's wealth. The youth wears his beauty proudly, as such a uniform, and is 'gazed on' as it is worn. A 'weed' is a garment (as in 'widow's weeds'), not a plant, though the plant sense of something worthless is surely heard. The 1609 Quarto spells the adjective 'totter'd', an older form of 'tattered' which may also suggest tottering, unsteady age; the archive's text modernizes it to 'tatter'd'. 'Of small worth held' introduces the language of value and account that the rest of the sonnet develops.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'livery'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weed'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes

  4. lusty l. 6

    In Elizabethan English 'lusty' chiefly meant healthy, vigorous, lively (OED), and the sense of sexual desire was available beside it. The question of where 'all the treasure' of these days lies implies that beauty is a hoard that ought to be invested, a mercantile metaphor that runs throughout the procreation group (Sonnet 1's 'niggarding', Sonnet 4's 'Unthrifty loveliness'). The answer the young man would give, that his beauty lies in his 'deep sunken eyes', is cruelly ironic: the only place the treasure would remain is the ruined face that has lost it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lusty'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes

  5. all-eating shame; thriftless praise l. 8

    'All-eating' (all-consuming) gives shame the appetite usually given to Time ('Devouring Time' in Sonnet 19) or to gluttony, as in Sonnet 1's glutton who eats 'the world's due'. 'Thriftless' means unprofitable, yielding no thrift or gain: praise of a beauty that no longer exists brings no return. The paired abstractions also continue the economic vocabulary: the youth's treasure has been squandered rather than put out to 'use'. Shakespeare Online glosses 'thriftless' as unprofitable; the line's words mix moral and financial judgement.

    Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 2, l. 8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'thriftless'

  6. use l. 9

    'Use' was the common Elizabethan word for interest on money lent (usury), as well as for employment or service, and it carried a sexual sense. Sonnet 4 makes the financial pun explicit ('Profitless usurer', 'thy unus'd beauty'), and Sonnet 6 argues that such 'use is not forbidden usury' when it produces a child. Here the poet claims that beauty put to use would deserve more praise than beauty hoarded. The Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25.14-30), in which the servant who buried his money is condemned, lies behind the whole argument; the Folger edition pairs it with Sonnet 4.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'use'; Folger Shakespeare Library, Shakespeare's Sonnets, 'Appendix of Intertextual Material' (Golding's Ovid, Metamorphoses 15; Erasmus, Encomium Matrimonii, trans. Thomas Wilson, The Arte of Rhetorique, 1553); Matthew 25.14-30 (Parable of the Talents), King James Version; John Kerrigan, ed., The Sonnets and A Lover's Complaint, New Penguin Shakespeare (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1986), note on Sonnet 2, l. 9

  7. sum my count, and make my old excuse l. 11

    The language is that of the accountant's reckoning: to 'sum' a 'count' is to total and close an account, so the child balances the books of the father's life, a secular version of the final reckoning before God. 'Make my old excuse' is ambiguous: it may mean 'justify me when I am old', 'serve as the excuse for my having grown old', or (less likely) 'repeat the familiar old excuse'. The Oxquarry commentary lists all three possibilities and hears a bawdy pun in 'count'. The grammar is quoted speech: the poet imagines the young man able to point to 'This fair child of mine' as living proof.

    Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes; Stephen Booth, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Edited with Analytic Commentary (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977), commentary on Sonnet 2, l. 11

  8. succession l. 12

    'Succession' is the legal term for inheritance and the passing of title from one generation to the next, so the beauty becomes an estate handed down like land or a name. 'Proving' means demonstrating, but also testing, as metal is proved; the child is the evidence that confirms the claim. The procreation sonnets often read like legal and dynastic argument addressed to an aristocrat whose line must be continued, one reason critics have connected them with real patrons such as the Earl of Southampton or William Herbert, though the identification remains unproved.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'succession'; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com); Shakespeare Online, Sonnet 2, notes

  9. the couplet: new made when thou art old ll. 13-14

    Like most of Shakespeare's sonnets, this one ends with a rhyming couplet that resolves the argument of the three quatrains. Here the antitheses are tightly paired, new/old and warm/cold, and the second line turns on 'see' and 'feel'. The idea that blood grows cold in age comes from the physiology of the humours, in which youth was hot and moist and age cold and dry. The procreation sonnets repeatedly end on such neat reversals, which give the conclusion the air of a proverb; Vendler's essays on the group are a good guide to these closing strategies.

    Helen Vendler, The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 1997), essay on Sonnet 2; Katherine Duncan-Jones, ed., Shakespeare's Sonnets, Arden Shakespeare Third Series (1997; rev. 2010), note on Sonnet 2, ll. 13-14; Oxquarry Books, Shakespeare's Sonnets: text and line-by-line commentary, Sonnet 2 (shakespeares-sonnets.com)
