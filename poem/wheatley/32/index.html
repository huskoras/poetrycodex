To S. M. a Young African Painter, on Seeing His Works

by Phillis Wheatley c. 1753–1784

From Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773)

Arts & SciencesSocial Commentaries

TO show the lab'ring bosom's deep intent,
And thought in living characters to paint,
When first thy pencil did those beauties give,
And breathing figures learnt from thee to live,
How did those prospects give my soul delight,
A new creation rushing on my sight?
Still, wond'rous youth! each noble path pursue,
On deathless glories fix thine ardent view:
Still may the painter's and the poet's fire
To aid thy pencil, and thy verse conspire!
And may the charms of each seraphic theme
Conduct thy footsteps to immortal fame!
High to the blissful wonders of the skies
Elate thy soul, and raise thy wishful eyes.
Thrice happy, when exalted to survey
That splendid city, crown'd with endless day,
Whose twice six gates on radiant hinges ring:
Celestial Salem blooms in endless spring.

Calm and serene thy moments glide along,
And may the muse inspire each future song!
Still, with the sweets of contemplation bless'd,
May peace with balmy wings your soul invest!
But when these shades of time are chas'd away,
And darkness ends in everlasting day,
On what seraphic pinions shall we move,
And view the landscapes in the realms above?
There shall thy tongue in heav'nly murmurs flow,
And there my muse with heav'nly transport glow:
No more to tell of Damon's tender sighs,
Or rising radiance of Aurora's eyes,
For nobler themes demand a nobler strain,
And purer language on th' ethereal plain.
Cease, gentle muse! the solemn gloom of night
Now seals the fair creation from my sight.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A poem to a young African painter on seeing his works, in praise of painting as the art that makes thought visible.

Study notes by the editors

  1. S. M. and the occasion title; l. 1

    The initials are usually expanded to Scipio Moorhead, enslaved in the household of the Rev. John Moorhead, a Presbyterian minister in Boston; tradition holds that the minister's wife Sarah, herself a painter, taught him to draw. The identification goes back to handwritten marginal notes in a copy of Wheatley's book now at the American Antiquarian Society, and the poem is the main literary evidence that he painted at all. It is therefore one of the earliest poems in English by one Black artist addressed to another, and the speaker treats the young man as a fellow practitioner rather than a curiosity. Eric Slauter has questioned how much we really know about Moorhead and whether he painted the portrait of Wheatley used for her frontispiece; the identification of S. M. itself remains the standard reading.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; 'To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works', Anthology of Early American Literature (University of Virginia Library); Eric Slauter, 'Looking for Scipio Moorhead: An "African Painter" in Revolutionary North America', in Slave Portraiture in the Atlantic World, ed. Agnes Lugo-Ortiz and Angela Rosenthal (Cambridge University Press, 2013); Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989)

  2. The frontispiece portrait l. 3

    The 1773 Poems carried an engraved portrait of Wheatley seated at a writing desk, pen in hand, captioned as the servant of John Wheatley of Boston. Since the nineteenth century the original drawing or painting has often been attributed to Scipio Moorhead, partly because of this poem; the engraving bears no artist's name. Eric Slauter, weighing Moorhead's age, records of his sale and the styles of contemporary portraiture, doubts the attribution, and the Boston historian J. L. Bell has suggested another Black artist, Prince Demah. Readers should therefore not assume that the 'works' Wheatley admires include her own likeness, though it is an attractive possibility.

    'To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works', Anthology of Early American Literature (University of Virginia Library); Eric Slauter, 'Looking for Scipio Moorhead: An "African Painter" in Revolutionary North America', in Slave Portraiture in the Atlantic World, ed. Agnes Lugo-Ortiz and Angela Rosenthal (Cambridge University Press, 2013); Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (University of Georgia Press, 2011)

  3. pencil l. 3; l. 10

    In eighteenth-century English 'pencil' commonly meant a painter's fine brush of camel or sable hair (from Latin penicillus, 'little tail'), and by extension the artist's skill or style. Wheatley uses it twice, first for the brush that gave Moorhead's figures their 'beauties' and then in the wish that poetic fire may 'aid thy pencil'. The pairing of pencil and verse prepares the poem's central idea that painting and poetry are sister arts.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pencil, n.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  4. living characters l. 2

    'Character' could mean a letter or graphic sign (Greek charaktēr, a stamp or engraved mark) as well as a represented person. Wheatley's phrase thus makes painting a kind of writing that shows the 'deep intent' of the 'lab'ring bosom', the inner feeling of the subject. The idea that great painting makes figures seem to breathe belongs to a long classical topos; Virgil's Anchises speaks of others who will fashion 'breathing bronze' (Aeneid 6.847), and the next line's 'breathing figures' echoes the commonplace.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'character, n.'; Virgil, Aeneid 6.847, 'spirantia mollius aera'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  5. breathing figures l. 4

    The conceit that a painter or sculptor gives figures breath is a commonplace of Renaissance and neoclassical praise of art, descended from the Pygmalion story (Ovid, Metamorphoses 10) and from ekphrastic poetry. Wheatley gives it a theological edge in l. 6, where the paintings appear as 'A new creation', so that the artist's making mirrors divine creation. The figures 'learnt from thee to live', the verb crediting the young man with a teacher's and a creator's power.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.243-297 (Pygmalion); Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  6. A new creation l. 6

    On the aesthetic level the line describes the shock of delight at seeing an imagined world made visible. On the religious level, which Wheatley never lets go, 'new creation' recalls 2 Corinthians 5:17 ('if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature') and Revelation 21:5 ('Behold, I make all things new'). The second allusion anticipates the poem's turn toward the heavenly city in ll. 15-18, and its close, where night 'seals the fair creation from my sight' (l. 34), answers this line with the same rhyme-word.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Corinthians 5:17; Revelation 21:5; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  7. The sister arts ll. 9-10

    Neoclassical criticism, following Horace's 'ut pictura poesis' ('as is painting, so is poetry', Ars Poetica 361), treated painting and poetry as sister arts sharing a common 'fire' or inspiration; Dryden's ode to Anne Killigrew and Pope's Epistle to Mr Jervas, which Wheatley could have known, develop the same pairing. 'Conspire' here keeps its Latin sense of 'breathe together' (con + spirare): the two fires join forces. Readers often note that the wish creates an alliance of two young Black artists in the very forms of high culture from which Africans were thought to be excluded.

    Horace, Ars Poetica 361; Alexander Pope, 'Epistle to Mr Jervas, with Dryden's Translation of Fresnoy's Art of Painting' (1716); John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008); Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  8. 'thy verse' l. 10

    Taken literally, 'thy verse' means the young painter was also a poet, and the later lines (ll. 27-28) imagine both his 'tongue' and the speaker's muse singing in heaven. No poems by Scipio Moorhead survive, and nothing outside this poem shows that he wrote. Editors therefore leave open whether Wheatley is recording a fact about him, generously extending her own art to him, or using 'verse' in the sense of any expressive art. The ambiguity is worth noting because so much of the identification of S. M. rests on the poem itself.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989); Eric Slauter, 'Looking for Scipio Moorhead: An "African Painter" in Revolutionary North America', in Slave Portraiture in the Atlantic World, ed. Agnes Lugo-Ortiz and Angela Rosenthal (Cambridge University Press, 2013)

  9. seraphic l. 11; l. 25

    The seraphim are the six-winged beings around God's throne in Isaiah 6:2-3; Hebrew seraph is usually connected with a root meaning 'to burn', so the word suits the poem's language of 'fire'. A 'seraphic theme' is a religious subject, and Wheatley repeats the adjective in l. 25 for the 'pinions' or wings on which the soul will rise. The exhortation to choose sacred themes was conventional advice to young artists in the evangelical circles around Wheatley and her patron the Countess of Huntingdon.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 6:2-3; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seraphic, adj.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  10. Elate l. 14

    From Latin elatus, past participle of efferre, 'to carry out, lift up'. Wheatley uses the verb in its root sense: the soul is to be lifted toward 'the blissful wonders of the skies', while 'wishful eyes' look upward with longing. She uses the adjective 'Elate' in the same Latinate way in the Dartmouth poem, a reminder of her schooling in Latin and in Pope's Homer.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'elate, v.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  11. Celestial Salem ll. 15-18

    'Salem' is a biblical short name for Jerusalem (Psalm 76:2; Hebrews 7:2 glosses it as 'peace'). The details come from Revelation 21: the city has 'twelve gates' (21:12, Wheatley's 'twice six gates'), 'had no need of the sun' because God's glory lights it (21:23), and 'there shall be no night there' (21:25), hence 'crown'd with endless day'. The 'endless spring' adds a classical golden-age touch to the Christian vision. The move from earthly pictures to the city of God shows the poem's evangelical logic: art is praised as a step toward heaven.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 21:10-25; Psalm 76:2; Hebrews 7:2; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  12. Shades and day ll. 23-24

    On its surface the couplet is orthodox eschatology: time is a shadow, eternity is light (compare Revelation 22:5, 'there shall be no night there'). Some critics, among them John C. Shields, read Wheatley's frequent light/dark imagery as also touching on the Black speaker and addressee, who will be judged in heaven by the soul and not by colour, as in 'On Being Brought from Africa to America'. Others caution against over-reading conventional devotional language. Either way the couplet frames the two artists' shared destiny beyond the conditions of their earthly lives.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 22:5; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008); Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  13. Damon and Aurora ll. 29-30

    Damon is a conventional shepherd-name in pastoral from Virgil's eighth Eclogue onward; his 'tender sighs' stand for amorous verse. Aurora is the Roman goddess of dawn, whose rising radiance was a favourite descriptive subject; Wheatley herself wrote 'An Hymn to the Morning', which invokes Aurora, so the renunciation is partly of her own earlier manner. The rejection of secular subjects for sacred ones is itself a Miltonic and neoclassical convention, but it fits the poem's argument that heaven will provide a 'nobler strain' and 'purer language'.

    Virgil, Eclogue 8 (Damon's song); Phillis Wheatley, 'An Hymn to the Morning', in Poems (1773); Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  14. ethereal plain l. 32

    In ancient cosmology the aether (Greek aithēr) was the bright, pure air of the upper sky, above the murky air of earth. The 'ethereal plain' is a poetic diction for the heavens, familiar from Pope's Homer. Wheatley repeats it at the end of the Dartmouth poem for the path of the 'fiery coursers'. 'Purer language' there implies that heavenly speech will surpass even the best human verse.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ethereal, adj.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  15. Couplets and closing invocation ll. 33-34

    The poem is written in rhymed iambic pentameter couplets, the standard form for eighteenth-century public and devotional verse, which Wheatley learned largely from Pope's translations of Homer. Its closure is conventional (the poet bids the muse cease), but the final image, in which night 'seals the fair creation from my sight', turns the convention to the poem's theme: the speaker's vision of art and heaven ends, and she waits, like her addressee, for the 'everlasting day' of l. 24. The final rhyme repeats the 'sight' of l. 6, closing the poem's circle of seeing.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989); John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)
