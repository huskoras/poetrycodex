To the Right Honourable William, Earl of Dartmouth

by Phillis Wheatley c. 1753–1784

From Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773)

History & PoliticsSocial Commentaries

HAIL, happy day, when, smiling like the morn,
Fair Freedom rose New-England to adorn:
The northern clime beneath her genial ray,
Dartmouth, congratulates thy blissful sway:
Elate with hope her race no longer mourns,
Each soul expands, each grateful bosom burns,
While in thine hand with pleasure we behold
The silken reins, and Freedom's charms unfold.
Long lost to realms beneath the northern skies
She shines supreme, while hated faction dies:
Soon as appear'd the Goddess long desir'd,
Sick at the view, she languish'd and expir'd;
Thus from the splendors of the morning light
The owl in sadness seeks the caves of night.

No more, America, in mournful strain
Of wrongs, and grievance unredress'd complain,
No longer shalt thou dread the iron chain,
Which wanton Tyranny with lawless hand
Had made, and with it meant t' enslave the land.

Should you, my lord, while you peruse my song,
Wonder from whence my love of Freedom sprung,
Whence flow these wishes for the common good,
By feeling hearts alone best understood,
I, young in life, by seeming cruel fate
Was snatch'd from Afric's fancy'd happy seat:
What pangs excruciating must molest,
What sorrows labour in my parent's breast?
Steel'd was that soul and by no misery mov'd
That from a father seiz'd his babe belov'd:
Such, such my case. And can I then but pray
Others may never feel tyrannic sway?

For favours past, great Sir, our thanks are due,
And thee we ask thy favours to renew,
Since in thy pow'r, as in thy will before,
To sooth the griefs, which thou did'st once deplore.
May heav'nly grace the sacred sanction give
To all thy works, and thou for ever live
Not only on the wings of fleeting Fame,
Though praise immortal crowns the patriot's name,
But to conduct to heav'ns refulgent fane,
May fiery coursers sweep th' ethereal plain,
And bear thee upwards to that blest abode,
Where, like the prophet, thou shalt find thy God.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Addressed to the Earl of Dartmouth, Secretary of State for North America, it welcomes the hope of freedom in New England and recalls her own seizure from Africa.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Dartmouth's appointment, 1772 title; l. 1

    Dartmouth (1731-1801) succeeded the unpopular Earl of Hillsborough as Secretary of State for the American colonies on 27 August 1772. Colonists hoped he would be conciliatory, since as President of the Board of Trade in 1765-66 he had been associated with the ministry that repealed the Stamp Act. He was also an evangelical, a supporter of the Methodist movement and connected with the circle of the Countess of Huntingdon, Wheatley's patron; Dartmouth College was named for him. Wheatley sent the poem with a letter dated 10 October 1772, transmitted by the merchant Thomas Wooldridge, and revised it for the 1773 Poems.

    Wikipedia, 'William Legge, 2nd Earl of Dartmouth' (offices and dates, with references); Massachusetts Historical Society, Collections Online: copy of Phillis Wheatley's letter to the Earl of Dartmouth, 10 October 1772; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (University of Georgia Press, 2011)

  2. Freedom as goddess ll. 1-2

    Wheatley imagines Freedom as a sunrise, 'smiling like the morn', a figure close to Aurora. In colonial political language 'freedom' meant relief from parliamentary 'tyranny', and the poem uses it in that sense first. Its later stanzas reapply the word to the slave trade and to her own capture, so that the colonists' cry for liberty is turned back on them. Critics such as Carretta and Shields see this double application as the poem's central strategy.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008); The Norton Anthology of African American Literature, ed. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Nellie Y. McKay, 2nd ed. (Norton, 2004), headnotes and footnotes

  3. genial l. 3

    Latin genialis meant 'of generation or birth', and hence festive or fruitful. In eighteenth-century poetic usage 'genial' rays are those of the sun that make things grow. The 'northern clime' of New England, often described as cold, is imagined as warmed into life by the new minister's favour. The word links the political compliment to the sunrise image of the opening lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'genial, adj.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  4. The silken reins l. 8

    The image of government as a charioteer's reins is classical; Wheatley makes them 'silken' to signal mild, consensual rule. The softness contrasts pointedly with the 'iron chain' of l. 17, which the colonists feared from Parliament and which, in the next stanza, becomes literal for the enslaved. The chariot imagery returns at the poem's end with the 'fiery coursers' of Elijah, so that the poem begins and ends with horses and reins.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  5. hated faction ll. 10-12

    'Faction' in eighteenth-century politics meant a self-interested party acting against the public good. Wheatley does not name it. Editors generally take it as the ministerial interest identified with the Earl of Hillsborough, whose harsh American policy colonists resented; some readers take it more broadly as any partisan enemy of liberty. The personified faction sickens and dies 'at the view' of the goddess, a mock-epic touch, and the owl simile that follows treats it as a creature of darkness fleeing the dawn.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'faction, n.'

  6. The owl ll. 13-14

    The owl was a conventional emblem of darkness, ill omen and obscurantism, and its flight from daylight a familiar simile in Augustan verse. The couplet rounds off the first verse paragraph by returning to the sunrise of l. 1. It also contributes to the poem's pattern of light and darkness, which Wheatley later turns to her own transport from Africa.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  7. The triplet and the iron chain ll. 15-19

    This short paragraph breaks the run of couplets with a triplet, a Drydenian device used for emphasis. Its political metaphor was everywhere in the revolutionary press: colonists described parliamentary taxation as 'slavery' and 'chains'. 'Wanton' means unrestrained or capricious. Wheatley adopts the figure faithfully, but by placing it immediately before her account of being 'snatch'd' from Africa she makes the metaphor confront the fact, a juxtaposition critics regard as quietly pointed.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008); The Norton Anthology of African American Literature, ed. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Nellie Y. McKay, 2nd ed. (Norton, 2004), headnotes and footnotes

  8. The turn to herself ll. 20-23

    The address 'my lord' and the imagined question ('Wonder from whence...') set up the autobiographical passage that follows, the only place in the 1773 volume where Wheatley speaks directly of her capture. The appeal to 'feeling hearts' draws on the eighteenth-century culture of sensibility, in which the capacity to feel others' pain was a moral credential. The passage thus claims authority for the young African woman to speak about liberty to a minister of state.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Henry Louis Gates Jr., The Trials of Phillis Wheatley (Basic Civitas Books, 2003); John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  9. Afric's fancy'd happy seat l. 25

    'Afric' is the poetic form of Africa; 'seat' means dwelling place. 'Fancy'd' is ambiguous: it can mean 'supposed, merely imagined', so that the happiness of Africa is a European fiction or a child's memory, or 'loved, delighted in'. Coupled with 'seeming cruel fate', which hints that providence brought her to Christianity, the line can be read as cautiously keeping faith with her evangelical audience. Other readers stress that the line nonetheless names Africa as a home and her removal as a wrong.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989); Henry Louis Gates Jr., The Trials of Phillis Wheatley (Basic Civitas Books, 2003)

  10. excruciating; molest ll. 26-27

    'Excruciating', from Latin excruciare, 'to torment', built on crux, 'cross', carries a faint Passion overtone appropriate to a devotional poet. 'Molest' had the general sense 'to trouble, vex'. Strikingly the speaker imagines her parents' grief rather than her own,; the passage is often cited as a rare glimpse of the family she lost when she was taken as a child of about seven or eight.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'excruciating, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'molest, v.'; Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (University of Georgia Press, 2011)

  11. 'Such, such my case' ll. 28-31

    The 'steel'd' soul unmoved by misery is the slave trader who tore a child from her father. The repeated 'Such, such' is emphatic and the question that follows is a litotes: she cannot but pray that others be spared tyranny. Read alongside the preceding 'iron chain', the passage extends the colonists' language of political slavery to chattel slavery itself. Critics differ on how strong an antislavery statement this is: some, following Gates and Carretta, see it as a deliberate rebuke to slaveholding patriots; others note its tactful indirection and its framing as a personal prayer.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Henry Louis Gates Jr., The Trials of Phillis Wheatley (Basic Civitas Books, 2003); John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008); The Norton Anthology of African American Literature, ed. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Nellie Y. McKay, 2nd ed. (Norton, 2004), headnotes and footnotes

  12. 'Favours past' ll. 32-35

    The 'favours past' and the griefs 'which thou did'st once deplore' are usually taken to refer to Dartmouth's tenure as President of the Board of Trade in 1765-66, when the Rockingham ministry repealed the Stamp Act, a measure colonists celebrated. The plural 'our thanks' speaks for the colonists collectively; the poet now addresses him as a public spokesperson rather than as an individual sufferer.

    Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; Wikipedia, 'William Legge, 2nd Earl of Dartmouth' (offices and dates, with references); Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989)

  13. refulgent fane l. 40

    Both words are Latinate poetic diction: 'fane' from fanum, a consecrated place, and 'refulgent' from refulgere, to shine back. Heaven is imagined as a radiant temple to which Dartmouth will be conducted, a fitting destination for a man celebrated for his piety. The phrase shows the classical vocabulary Wheatley drew from Pope. The temple-and-chariot imagery of the last lines gives the poem a ceremonial, almost apotheotic close, of the kind Pope used in his panegyrics.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fane, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'refulgent, adj.'; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes

  14. The prophet Elijah ll. 41-43

    In 2 Kings 2:11 'there appeared a chariot of fire, and horses of fire' and Elijah 'went up by a whirlwind into heaven'. Wheatley's 'fiery coursers' sweeping 'th' ethereal plain' combine that scene with classical images of the sun's horses. The closing wish that Dartmouth gain immortality not only through 'fleeting Fame' but through heavenly reward is a standard Christian turn in panegyric, apt for an evangelical statesman. The horses also complete the poem's frame of reins and chariots begun in l. 8.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Kings 2:11; Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes; John C. Shields, Phillis Wheatley's Poetics of Liberation: Backgrounds and Contexts (University of Tennessee Press, 2008)

  15. Two versions whole poem

    Wheatley regularly revised her poems between manuscript, newspaper and book. The Massachusetts Historical Society holds a manuscript copy, not in Wheatley's hand, of her covering letter of 10 October 1772. Comparing the manuscript text with the printed one shows her revising the poem for publication. Students quoting the poem should note which version they are using; this archive gives the 1773 book text.

    Massachusetts Historical Society, Collections Online: copy of Phillis Wheatley's letter to the Earl of Dartmouth, 10 October 1772; Julian D. Mason Jr., ed., The Poems of Phillis Wheatley, rev. ed. (University of North Carolina Press, 1989); Phillis Wheatley, Complete Writings, ed. Vincent Carretta (Penguin Classics, 2001), explanatory notes
