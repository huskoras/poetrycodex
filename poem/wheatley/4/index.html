On Being Brought from Africa to America

by Phillis Wheatley c. 1753–1784

From Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773)

ReligionSocial Commentaries

'TWAS mercy brought me from my Pagan land,
Taught my benighted soul to understand
That there's a God, that there's a Saviour too:
Once I redemption neither sought nor knew,
Some view our sable race with scornful eye,
"Their colour is a diabolic die."
Remember, Christians, Negroes, black as Cain,
May be refin'd, and join th' angelic train.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

One of her best-known poems: eight lines that give thanks for her Christian faith and answer those who scorn the colour of her race.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Phillis Wheatley and the 1773 Poems title; l. 1

    John Wheatley's letter prefixed to the volume states that Phillis was brought from Africa in 1761, aged seven or eight, and learned English within sixteen months. She was named after the slave ship Phillis that carried her and after her owners. The book appeared in London in 1773, prefaced by an attestation, signed by eighteen Boston worthies including Governor Thomas Hutchinson and John Hancock, that the poems were really by 'an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa', a slave. The poem's title names the Middle Passage that the poem does not describe. Its opening word 'mercy' frames enslavement as providence, which has made it one of her most debated poems, since it can seem to condone slavery.

    Phillis Wheatley, Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (London, 1773), preface, letter and attestation (Project Gutenberg); Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (Athens, GA, 2011), ch. 1

  2. Pagan land l. 1

    The 1773 printing italicises 'Pagan', 'Saviour', 'Christians', 'Negros' and 'Cain', as RPO's transcription shows, so the key terms of the argument stand out. 'Pagan' (Latin paganus, villager, then heathen) is the standard word of evangelical missions. Wheatley was baptised at Boston's Old South Church in 1771. Sondra O'Neale, cited in the RPO notes, read her biblical imagery as a deliberate, subtle strategy; others take the phrase at face value as the conventional thanks of a convert. The line can be both.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'On Being Brought from Africa to America' (1773 text with italics; note on l. 1 citing O'Neale); Sondra O'Neale, 'A Slave's Subtle War: Phillis Wheatley's Use of Biblical Myth and Symbol', Early American Literature 21.2 (1986); Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (Athens, GA, 2011) (on her baptism at Old South Church, 1771)

  3. benighted l. 2

    'Benighted' literally means overtaken by darkness, as a traveller caught by nightfall, and figuratively in intellectual or moral darkness (OED). The light-dark metaphor of conversion is biblical ('The people which sat in darkness saw great light', Matthew 4:16). In a poem about colour, the word carries a charge: the soul, not the skin, was dark, and it has been 'taught' into light. The verb 'Taught' also matters, since the poem's existence displays the learning that the attestation had to vouch for.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'benighted, adj.'; Matthew 4:16 (King James Version)

  4. the turn at line 4 ll. 1-4

    The poem is eight lines of heroic couplets in the manner of Pope, the dominant model of Wheatley's verse. It divides in two: ll. 1-4 tell her own story (mercy, teaching, God, Saviour, redemption), ll. 5-8 address the reader about 'our sable race'. In the 1773 text, as RPO transcribes it, l. 4 ends with a full stop; the archive has a comma, which makes the move from personal testimony to public argument less marked. The pronoun shifts from 'me' and 'I' to 'our' and then to the imperative 'Remember'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'On Being Brought from Africa to America' (1773 text); Phillis Wheatley, Poems on Various Subjects (1773) (Project Gutenberg)

  5. sable race l. 5

    'Sable' is the heraldic term for black and a stock elevated word in eighteenth-century verse (sable night, sable weeds). Applied to Africans it was a polite periphrasis, and Wheatley uses it of herself and her people: 'our sable race'. The phrase 'scornful eye' describes white prejudice as a way of looking, and the next line quotes what such viewers say. The word choice sets her decorous poetic diction against the crude 'diabolic' judgement of others.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sable, adj. and n.2'; Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'sable'

  6. a diabolic die l. 6

    Wheatley puts the slur in quotation marks, so it is reported speech, not her view. 'Die' was a normal spelling of 'dye', colour; the spelling also brings in 'die' as a cast of fortune (the die is cast) and death. The RPO note summarises James A. Levernier's argument that the line puns on indigo dye and, with 'Cain' (cane) in l. 7, on sugar, two plantation crops of the slave economy; on that reading the poem quietly names the trade that brought her. Readers differ on how far such puns are intended, but the quotation marks at least distance her from the idea that colour is sin.

    Representative Poetry Online, note on l. 6 (summarising Levernier); James A. Levernier, 'Wheatley's On Being Brought from Africa to America', The Explicator 40.1 (1981); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dye, n.' (spelling die) and 'die, n.1'

  7. Remember, Christians, Negroes l. 7

    The usual reading takes 'Christians' as vocative: white Christians are told to remember that black people, though thought 'black as Cain', may be refined and saved. The commas allow a second reading, in which 'Christians' and 'Negros' stand together as subjects, so that Christians too are black as Cain, sinners equally in need of grace. Several critics favour this double reading, which makes the poem a rebuke to its readers. The italics of 1773 set the two nouns side by side. The imperative 'Remember' is sharp from an enslaved teenager, and reminds Christian readers of their own doctrine of universal redemption.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'On Being Brought from Africa to America' (1773 text and italics); Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (2011), ch. 1

  8. black as Cain l. 7

    Genesis 4:15 says only that 'the LORD set a mark upon Cain'. Folk and some learned traditions read the mark as blackness, and the related 'curse of Ham' (Genesis 9:25) was widely invoked in defence of African slavery. 'Black as Cain' was a common simile. Wheatley quotes the prejudice to dismantle it: whatever their colour, Africans 'May be refin'd'. Sondra O'Neale reads her use of such biblical myths as a deliberate strategy addressed to a white Christian audience.

    Genesis 4:15 and 9:25 (King James Version); Sondra O'Neale, 'A Slave's Subtle War: Phillis Wheatley's Use of Biblical Myth and Symbol', Early American Literature 21.2 (1986); Representative Poetry Online, note on l. 1 (citing O'Neale)

  9. refin'd l. 8

    The biblical image is of God as refiner: 'he is like a refiner's fire ... and he shall sit as a refiner and purifier of silver' (Malachi 3:2-3; compare Isaiah 48:10). Refinement removes dross, not colour, so the metaphor shifts the question from skin to soul. In the eighteenth century 'refined' also meant polished, cultivated, which suits a poem whose elegance is itself an argument. Levernier's reading of 'Cain' as cane makes 'refin'd' part of a sugar-refining pun, the product of slave labour.

    Malachi 3:2-3; Isaiah 48:10 (King James Version); Representative Poetry Online, note on l. 6 (Levernier)

  10. join th' angelic train l. 8

    A 'train' is a procession or retinue. The vision of the redeemed from 'all nations, and kindreds, and people' before the throne (Revelation 7:9) underlies the line. Critics divide over the poem's politics. Thomas Jefferson dismissed Wheatley in Notes on the State of Virginia (Query XIV), saying religion had produced her but not a poet, and later readers have criticised the poem's apparent gratitude for slavery. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Vincent Carretta, among others, read it instead as a careful argument that claims spiritual equality from white readers on their own terms. The last word 'train' answers 'race': heaven's company replaces the despised race.

    Revelation 7:9 (King James Version); Thomas Jefferson, Notes on the State of Virginia (1785), Query XIV (Avalon Project); Henry Louis Gates Jr., The Trials of Phillis Wheatley (New York, 2003)
