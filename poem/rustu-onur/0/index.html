Living

Hülasa

by Rüştü Onur 1920–1942

Living

Ben ölsem be anacığım
Nem var ki sana kalacak.
Ceketimi kasap alacak,
Pardösümü bakkal
Borcuma mahsuben...
Ya aşklarım
Ya şiirlerim nolacak
Ya sen ele güne karşı
Nasıl bakacaksın insan yüzüne.
Hülasa anacığım
Ne ambarda darım
Ne evde karım var.
Çıplak doğurdun beni
Çıplak gideceğim...

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hülasa ('In Short' / 'Summary') title; l. 10

    Rüştü Onur was born in Devrek (Zonguldak province) on 3 August 1920, fell ill with tuberculosis in 1938, left school to work as an assistant clerk in the finance revenue office at Zonguldak, and was treated at the Heybeliada sanatorium in 1941-42. He died in the night of 1-2 December 1942, a few weeks after the death of Mediha Sessiz, to whom he had become engaged that year (sources differ on where they met). His poems were gathered after his death by Salâh Birsel (1956). Critics place him among the poets influenced by the Garip movement, with its everyday language and loose free verse. Hülasa (Arabic khulāṣa, 'extract, summary') is used in speech as 'in short'; here it introduces the reckoning of a life that leaves nothing behind but debts. The poem is addressed to his mother.

    Kanat Atkaya, 'Bir kelebek rüyası: Rüştü Onur', Hürriyet; Tülay Gürler Kurtuluş, 'Bir koku var sende', Vatan, 2 March 2013; Kebire Sali, 'Rüştü Onur'un Şiirlerinde İzlekler', Hars Akademi 1/2 (2018), 184–200; Salâh Birsel (ed.), Rüştü Onur: Şiirleri, Mektupları, Ardından Yazılanlar (1956)

  2. be anacığım ('mother dear') l. 1

    Be is an informal interjection of spoken Turkish, adding familiarity or mild exasperation ('be adam!'). Anacığım joins ana (mother) to the diminutive -cık and the possessive -ım: 'my little mother, mother dear'. The diction belongs to the street and the home, not to literature, in the Garip manner. The conditional 'ölsem' (if I died) is gentle, almost hypothetical, though the poet was gravely ill.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Kebire Sali, 'Rüştü Onur'un Şiirlerinde İzlekler', Hars Akademi 1/2 (2018), 184–200

  3. nem var ki (what do I have?) l. 2

    'Nem var' is a spoken contraction of 'neyim var' (what do I have). The question begins the inventory that the poem then performs, of possessions that will go not to his mother but to creditors. Inheritance, a classic topic of wills and epitaphs, is reduced to nothing, as in Orhan Veli's 'Kitâbe-i Seng-i Mezar', whose dead man also has debts and no claims.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Tülay Gürler Kurtuluş, 'Bir koku var sende', Vatan, 2 March 2013

  4. mahsuben (set against, on account of) ll. 3–5

    Mahsuben (Arabic maḥsūban, 'counted, reckoned') is commercial and official language: 'borcuna mahsuben', set against one's account. Onur worked as a clerk in the finance revenue office at Zonguldak, and the term comes naturally. The kasap (butcher) and bakkal (grocer) are the neighbourhood tradesmen who gave credit to the poor; the jacket and pardösü (light overcoat, from French pardessus) are his only goods. The plain, comic precision of the list makes the poverty more painful.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Tülay Gürler Kurtuluş, 'Bir koku var sende', Vatan, 2 March 2013; Kanat Atkaya, 'Bir kelebek rüyası: Rüştü Onur', Hürriyet

  5. 'And my loves, and my poems?' ll. 6–7

    The question 'Ya aşklarım / Ya şiirlerim nolacak' (and what will become of my loves, my poems?) turns from material to spiritual goods. 'Nolacak' is the spoken contraction of 'ne olacak'. For a poet who died at twenty-two, and whose poems were collected only fourteen years later, the question is poignant. The two plural nouns are left without answer. The word 'Ya' (and what about...) begins this line and the next two (the third is 'Ya sen ele güne karşı'), a spoken pattern of anxious questioning.

    Salâh Birsel (ed.), Rüştü Onur: Şiirleri, Mektupları, Ardından Yazılanlar (1956); Kebire Sali, 'Rüştü Onur'un Şiirlerinde İzlekler', Hars Akademi 1/2 (2018), 184–200

  6. el gün (other people, the neighbours) l. 8

    'El gün' or 'el âlem' means 'strangers, the neighbours, people in general', the community whose judgement matters. 'Ele güne karşı' is 'in front of everyone'. 'İnsan yüzüne bakmak' (to look people in the face) is to keep one's honour. The son worries that his mother will be shamed by unpaid debts and perhaps by his unconventional life. The line catches the moral world of a small Anatolian town.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Tülay Gürler Kurtuluş, 'Bir koku var sende', Vatan, 2 March 2013

  7. 'Neither millet in the granary nor a wife at home' ll. 11–12

    Ambar is a granary or store (from Persian anbār), darı is millet, the poor man's grain. The rhyme 'darım / karım' gives the line the sound of a proverb or folk-song saying for someone with neither property nor family. Using a proverbial formula for the summary itself fits the title 'Hülasa': a life summed up in a saying. The negative 'Ne... ne...' (neither... nor) repeats the pattern of an inventory of absence.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  8. 'Naked you bore me, naked I shall go' ll. 13–14

    The idea that one is born naked and leaves the world with nothing is proverbial in many cultures, and in Turkish it is reinforced by the Muslim custom of burial in a simple shroud (kefen). Its most famous expression is Job 1:21 (KJV), 'Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither', echoed in Ecclesiastes 5:15. Onur's version addresses the mother directly: 'you bore me naked'. Whether he knew the biblical text is unknown; the saying was common property. The ellipsis at the end leaves the departure open.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 1:21; Ecclesiastes 5:15; Tülay Gürler Kurtuluş, 'Bir koku var sende', Vatan, 2 March 2013
