No Second Troy

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Green Helmet and Other Poems (1912)

LoveHistory & Politics

Why should I blame her that she filled my days
With misery, or that she would of late
Have taught to ignorant men most violent ways,
Or hurled the little streets upon the great,
Had they but courage equal to desire?
What could have made her peaceful with a mind
That nobleness made simple as a fire,
With beauty like a tightened bow, a kind
That is not natural in an age like this,
Being high and solitary and most stern?
Why, what could she have done being what she is?
Was there another Troy for her to burn?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Why should the poet blame the woman who filled his days with misery, taught ignorant men violence, and had a beauty 'like a tightened bow'? 'Was there another Troy for her to burn?'

Study notes by the editors

  1. Maud Gonne and the date l. 1

    The 'her' is Maud Gonne, the nationalist activist Yeats had loved since 1889. She is not named, but, as A. D. Nuttall remarks, every reader in 1910 knew who was meant. The poem appeared in The Green Helmet and Other Poems, first issued by the Cuala Press in 1910 and reissued in an enlarged Macmillan edition in 1912 (the archive's date). It belongs to the years after Gonne's marriage to John MacBride (1903) and their separation (1905), when Yeats renewed his friendship with her. The opening question assumes he has a right to blame her and then argues it away.

    A. D. Nuttall, British Academy lecture (PDF), on Yeats and 'No Second Troy'; David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  2. filled my days / With misery ll. 1-2

    The 'misery' refers to the long unrequited love: Gonne refused Yeats's proposals several times from 1891 onward, and in 1903 married the soldier John MacBride, a shock to Yeats. The line break after 'days' delays the noun, so that the sentence seems at first to praise her for filling his days, before it turns. The tone, however, is not complaint; the poem's questions refuse to blame her because of what she is.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024)

  3. taught to ignorant men most violent ways ll. 2-3

    Gonne was an inspiring public speaker and agitator: she campaigned against evictions in Donegal around 1890, led protests against Queen Victoria's Jubilee in Dublin in 1897, and supported violent resistance to British rule. 'Of late' points to her recent politics. Yeats, who had himself worked with her in the 1890s, now regards her influence on 'ignorant men' as misguided; the phrase reveals his growing aristocratic suspicion of the crowd, which would sharpen in later poems. 'Most violent' is emphatic: he does not soften what she did.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, I: The Apprentice Mage 1865-1914 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1997); David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024)

  4. hurled the little streets upon the great l. 4

    The 'little streets' are the poor quarters, the 'great' the wealthy avenues and those who live in them. By metonymy the streets stand for their inhabitants, and the violent verb 'hurled' makes the image physical, as if whole neighbourhoods were flung like missiles. The line pictures the revolution Gonne might have caused: a rising of the urban poor against the propertied classes. It is one of the most compressed and powerful lines in the poem.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024)

  5. Had they but courage equal to desire? l. 5

    This conditional clause qualifies the preceding lines: she 'would' have done all this, had the 'ignorant men' had courage to match their desire. The implication is double. It defends her (she cannot be blamed for a failure of others) and condemns the modern populace as timid. The first question of the poem ends here; the rest asks what could have tamed her, and answers that nothing could.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; A. D. Nuttall, British Academy lecture (PDF), on Yeats and 'No Second Troy'

  6. nobleness made simple as a fire ll. 6-7

    Gonne's 'mind' is made 'simple as a fire' by its 'nobleness': its very nobility gives it a fierce singleness of purpose. 'Simple' here means pure and undivided, not unintelligent. The image of fire is the first link in a chain that ends with Troy burning: she is herself a fire, and fire is what she would bring. The question 'What could have made her peaceful' implies that peace would have been unnatural to such a mind.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024)

  7. beauty like a tightened bow l. 8

    The simile makes beauty a weapon under tension. A strung or drawn bow is both graceful and deadly, and its curve suggests the line of a body. The image belongs to the heroic world: the bows of Apollo and Artemis, or the great bow of Odysseus that only he could string. Readers have also compared Heraclitus' figure of harmony as a tension of opposites, 'as of the bow and the lyre'; this is a suggestive analogy rather than a demonstrable source. The bow's tension contrasts with the slackness of 'an age like this'.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; A. D. Nuttall, British Academy lecture (PDF), on Yeats and 'No Second Troy'

  8. an age like this ll. 8-10

    Her 'kind' of beauty, 'high and solitary and most stern', is unnatural in the present age. The adjectives are aristocratic and heroic: 'high' (noble, lofty), 'solitary' (apart from the crowd), 'stern' (severe, unyielding). The sentence implies a critique of modernity as small, mercantile and timid, a recurrent Yeatsian theme. The problem is not that Gonne is wrong but that she has been born out of her time, a Helen in a world with no Troy.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; A. D. Nuttall, British Academy lecture (PDF), on Yeats and 'No Second Troy'

  9. being what she is l. 11

    The penultimate question turns from blame to fate: given her nature, she could not have acted otherwise. The colloquial 'Why,' at the start of the line gives the effect of a man arguing with himself and arriving at a conclusion. The line prepares the final question, which explains her nature by the myth of Helen. The logic is that of fate or tragedy: character is destiny, and a heroic nature placed in an unheroic setting must spend itself destructively. Yeats thereby turns personal grievance into something closer to admiration, and the poem, though it begins with blame, ends as praise.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; David Sutton, 'Week 628: No Second Troy, by W. B. Yeats' (2024)

  10. another Troy for her to burn l. 12

    In Greek myth Helen's flight with Paris caused the Trojan War, told in Homer's Iliad, and the burning of Troy. The best-known English treatment is Marlowe's Doctor Faustus: 'Was this the face that launched a thousand ships / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?' Yeats's final question echoes that form. It implies that Gonne, like Helen, had a beauty that was destined to cause destruction, and that she has acted as she has because there is no Troy for her to burn. The Green Helmet volume repeatedly presents Gonne as a new Helen, and the equation returns in later poems such as 'When Helen Lived' and 'A Woman Homer Sung'. The title states the answer: there is no second Troy.

    Christopher Marlowe, Doctor Faustus (1604 text), scene 13 (Helen speech); Project Gutenberg; Homer, Iliad, Book 3 (Helen on the walls of Troy), trans. A. T. Murray (Loeb, 1924), Perseus Digital Library; A. D. Nuttall, British Academy lecture (PDF), on Yeats and 'No Second Troy'

  11. Form: a twelve-line sonnet of questions ll. 1-12

    The poem has twelve lines of iambic pentameter, rhymed abab cdcd efef, like a Shakespearean sonnet without its closing couplet. It consists entirely of four rhetorical questions, the last line forming a question of its own. The missing couplet means there is no answer or resolution; the poem closes on a question. The syntax runs freely across line ends, giving the tone of argument. The form shows Yeats's new, plainer and more forceful style of the Green Helmet period, after the softer manner of his early work.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; CELT (University College Cork), Yeats, 'No Second Troy', electronic text
