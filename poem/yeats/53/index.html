Easter, 1916

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921)

History & PoliticsDeath & Dying

I have met them at close of day
Coming with vivid faces
From counter or desk among grey
Eighteenth-century houses.
I have passed with a nod of the head
Or polite meaningless words,
Or have lingered awhile and said
Polite meaningless words,
And thought before I had done
Of a mocking tale or a gibe
To please a companion
Around the fire at the club,
Being certain that they and I
But lived where motley is worn:
All changed, changed utterly:
A terrible beauty is born.

That woman’s days were spent
In ignorant good will,
Her nights in argument
Until her voice grew shrill.
What voice more sweet than hers
When young and beautiful,
She rode to harriers?
This man had kept a school
And rode our winged horse.
This other his helper and friend
Was coming into his force;
He might have won fame in the end,
So sensitive his nature seemed,
So daring and sweet his thought.
This other man I had dreamed
A drunken, vain-glorious lout.
He had done most bitter wrong
To some who are near my heart,
Yet I number him in the song;
He, too, has resigned his part
In the casual comedy;
He, too, has been changed in his turn,
Transformed utterly:
A terrible beauty is born.

Hearts with one purpose alone
Through summer and winter seem
Enchanted to a stone
To trouble the living stream.
The horse that comes from the road.
The rider, the birds that range
From cloud to tumbling cloud,
Minute by minute change;
A shadow of cloud on the stream
Changes minute by minute;
A horse-hoof slides on the brim,
And a horse plashes within it
Where long-legged moor-hens dive,
And hens to moor-cocks call.
Minute by minute they live:
The stone’s in the midst of all.

Too long a sacrifice
Can make a stone of the heart.
O when may it suffice?
That is heaven’s part, our part
To murmur name upon name.
As a mother names her child
When sleep at last has come
On limbs that had run wild.
What is it but nightfall?
No, no, not night but death;
Was it needless death after all?
For England may keep faith
For all that is done and said.
We know their dream; enough
To know they dreamed and are dead.
And what if excess of love
Bewildered them till they died?
I write it out in a verse--
MacDonagh and MacBride
And Connolly and Pearse
Now and in time to be,
Wherever green is worn.
Are changed, changed utterly:
A terrible beauty is born.

Codex Note

Yeats's meditation on the Dublin Rising and its executed leaders, whom he had met only as people from counters and desks in 'motley', and who are 'changed, changed utterly: / A terrible beauty is born'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and publication title, st. 1

    The Easter Rising began on Easter Monday, 24 April 1916, when Irish Volunteers and the Irish Citizen Army seized buildings in Dublin and proclaimed an Irish Republic; it was suppressed within a week, and fifteen leaders were executed in May (a sixteenth, Roger Casement, was hanged in August). Yeats began the poem soon after; he wrote to Lady Gregory on 11 May that he was trying to write a poem on the executed men, and the finished poem is dated 25 September 1916. Clement Shorter privately printed twenty-five copies for distribution among friends. It was first published in the New Statesman (23 October 1920) and collected in Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921).

    Modernism Lab, Yale University, 'Easter 1916' (Yeats to Lady Gregory, 11 May 1916, Letters ed. Wade, p. 613); UCD Special Collections, 'Easter 1916' exhibition (Shorter's private printing; New Statesman, Oct. 1920); New York Public Library, '“Easter, 1916” printed by Clement Shorter'; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications)

  2. Structure and numbers st. 1–4

    The poem's stanzas have sixteen, twenty-four, sixteen and twenty-four lines respectively. Commentators have pointed out that the numbers match the year 1916 and the date (24 April), with four stanzas for the fourth month. The lines are mostly three-stress, a short, conversational measure that gives the poem a restrained, sober tone. Stanzas 1, 2 and 4 end with the refrain; the third stanza, with its image of the stone, stands outside the refrain.

    'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  3. counter or desk l. 3

    The revolutionaries, seen before the Rising, were clerks, shop-assistants, teachers and minor officials, emerging 'at close of day' from 'counter or desk'. Yeats emphasises their ordinariness and his own condescension to them. The 'grey / Eighteenth-century houses' are the Georgian terraces of Dublin, the city of the Protestant Ascendancy, which Yeats later celebrated; 'grey' also suggests the dullness of ordinary life against their 'vivid faces'. The enjambment 'among grey / Eighteenth-century houses' spills the scene across the line.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  4. Polite meaningless words l. 6

    The repetition of 'Polite meaningless words' mimics the repetitive, automatic courtesy of casual encounters. The speaker admits his own disdain: he thought of 'a mocking tale or a gibe' to amuse a companion 'around the fire at the club', probably the Arts Club in Dublin which Yeats frequented. The confession places the speaker among those changed by the Rising: his own past attitude is exposed as shallow.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  5. motley l. 14

    'Motley' is the parti-coloured dress of the jester in Elizabethan drama, as in Jaques's 'Motley's the only wear' (As You Like It 2.7). Yeats implies that before the Rising both he and the rebels lived in a comic world where nothing was serious. The final stanza's 'Wherever green is worn' answers this line: the fool's motley has become the green of Irish nationalism. The 'casual comedy' of stanza 2 continues the theatrical metaphor.

    Shakespeare, As You Like It 2.7.12–43 (Jaques on the fool: 'Motley's the only wear'); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  6. A terrible beauty is born l. 16

    The refrain recurs at the close of three stanzas. Its oxymoron joins 'terrible', inspiring terror, with 'beauty', in a way often compared to Burke's sublime, and the verb 'is born' implies a new life emerging from violence. In his letter of 11 May 1916 to Lady Gregory, Yeats wrote that 'terrible beauty has been born again', after Maud Gonne's remark that tragic dignity had returned to Ireland. Critics differ on whether the refrain celebrates the rebels' transfiguration or registers anxious ambivalence about violence; the poem's questions in stanza 4 suggest both. 'All changed, changed utterly' insists on the totality of the transformation.

    Modernism Lab, Yale University, 'Easter 1916' (Yeats to Lady Gregory, 11 May 1916, Letters ed. Wade, p. 613); 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  7. That woman (Constance Markievicz) l. 17

    Constance Gore-Booth, of Lissadell, County Sligo, married the Polish Count Casimir Markievicz. She became a socialist and nationalist and was a second-in-command at St Stephen's Green in the Rising; her death sentence was commuted because she was a woman. Yeats contrasts her youthful beauty, when 'she rode to harriers' (hunted hares on horseback with a pack of harriers), with her later 'ignorant good will' and 'shrill' political voice. The judgement, coloured by Yeats's view that politics harmed women's beauty, recurs in 'In Memory of Eva Gore-Booth and Con Markiewicz' (written 1927, collected 1929).

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  8. rode to harriers l. 23

    A harrier is a hound used to hunt hares. To 'ride to harriers' (or 'to hounds') is to follow a hunt on horseback. The image recalls the Gore-Booths' Anglo-Irish gentry life at Lissadell, where Yeats knew Constance and her sister Eva in the 1890s. The question 'What voice more sweet than hers' sets the woman's earlier charm against her political shrillness, a contrast that later feminist critics have found condescending.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  9. This man (Patrick Pearse) l. 24

    Patrick (Pádraig) Pearse founded St Enda's (Scoil Éanna), a bilingual Irish school in Dublin, in 1908. He was a poet in Irish and English and an advocate of blood sacrifice for Irish freedom. He read the Proclamation of the Irish Republic outside the General Post Office on Easter Monday and was commander-in-chief of the rebel forces. He was executed by firing squad at Kilmainham Gaol on 3 May 1916. 'Rode our winged horse' means he wrote poetry (Pegasus being the poets' steed).

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); 'Patrick Pearse', Dictionary of Irish Biography (Royal Irish Academy)

  10. our winged horse l. 25

    Pegasus, the winged horse born from the blood of Medusa, struck the spring Hippocrene from Mount Helicon, the mountain of the Muses; later tradition made him the mount of poets and a symbol of poetic inspiration. 'Our' winged horse claims Pearse as a fellow poet. The understatement of the phrase ('rode') is typical of the stanza's mix of respect and reservation.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; Hesiod, Theogony 278–86 (Pegasus); Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.256–63 (Hippocrene)

  11. his helper and friend (Thomas MacDonagh) l. 26

    Thomas MacDonagh taught with Pearse at St Enda's and lectured in English at University College Dublin; his critical study Literature in Ireland was published posthumously in 1916. He was a signatory of the Proclamation and commanded the garrison at Jacob's biscuit factory. Yeats knew him through literary circles and thought him 'coming into his force': his talent was just maturing. The phrase 'So sensitive his nature seemed, / So daring and sweet his thought' is the most affectionate tribute in the stanza.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  12. This other man (John MacBride) l. 32

    John MacBride fought against the British in the Boer War with an Irish Brigade, married Maud Gonne in 1903, and separated from her in 1905 amid accusations of drunkenness and domestic abuse, which he denied. He joined the Rising almost by chance and was executed on 5 May 1916. 'He had done most bitter wrong / To some who are near my heart' refers to Gonne and her daughter Iseult. Yeats nonetheless numbers him 'in the song', a gesture of reluctant justice; Maud Gonne felt the poem unworthy of its subject, above all of MacBride.

    'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); Maud Gonne to W. B. Yeats, 3 Nov. [1916], Emory University; printed in The Gonne–Yeats Letters 1893–1938, ed. A. MacBride White and A. N. Jeffares (1992), pp. 383–84; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  13. the casual comedy l. 37

    The theatrical metaphor continues from 'motley': MacBride 'has resigned his part' (given up his role) in 'the casual comedy', the haphazard, unserious drama of everyday Dublin life. 'Casual' suggests both accident and lack of seriousness. Yeats, a man of the theatre as co-founder of the Abbey Theatre, uses the conceit to describe the transformation as a change of genre, from comedy to tragedy.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  14. Transformed utterly l. 39

    In stanzas 1 and 4 the line reads 'changed, changed utterly'; in stanza 2 Yeats writes 'Transformed utterly', with the preceding line 'He, too, has been changed in his turn'. The variation emphasises that even the man Yeats despised has undergone a complete metamorphosis through his sacrifice. 'Transformed' carries a hint of religious transfiguration, appropriate to an Easter poem about death and new birth.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications)

  15. Enchanted to a stone l. 43

    The third stanza turns from persons to a symbol. The hearts of those with 'one purpose alone' seem 'enchanted', as by a spell, into a stone that 'troubles' (disturbs) the 'living stream'. Around it, horse, rider, birds, clouds and moor-hens 'change' from minute to minute. The stone stands for fixed purpose, both heroic and inhuman: it gives the stream its form but cannot share its life. The image had a private dimension: In 1916 Yeats reportedly read the poem to Maud Gonne and urged her to put aside political fixity for the changing joy of life, and Gonne's later letter objected to its picture of the rebels' hearts as stone.

    Literariness.org, 'Analysis of W. B. Yeats's Easter, 1916' (Gonne's reply; Yeats's September 1916 letter); Maud Gonne to W. B. Yeats, 3 Nov. [1916], Emory University; printed in The Gonne–Yeats Letters 1893–1938, ed. A. MacBride White and A. N. Jeffares (1992), pp. 383–84; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications)

  16. moor-hens … moor-cocks ll. 53–54

    The moorhen (Gallinula chloropus) is a small water bird of ponds and streams. The 'hens' calling 'to moor-cocks' bring sexual and seasonal life into the stanza, an image of the living, mating, changing world. The repeated 'Minute by minute' measures the time the natural world lives in, against the stone's unchanging state. The stanza's natural imagery may draw on Yeats's experience of Coole and Galway rivers.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  17. Too long a sacrifice l. 57

    The fourth stanza opens with the poem's sharpest doubt: 'Too long a sacrifice / Can make a stone of the heart.' The sacrifice may be that of the revolutionaries, or the long political devotion of figures like Maud Gonne and Markievicz, or the centuries-long struggle of Ireland. Maud Gonne, in her letter of 3 November 1916, told Yeats bluntly that she did not like the poem and felt it unworthy of its subject. The question 'O when may it suffice?' asks when enough sacrifice will have been made; the answer is given to 'heaven', leaving 'our part' as mourning.

    Maud Gonne to W. B. Yeats, 3 Nov. [1916], Emory University; printed in The Gonne–Yeats Letters 1893–1938, ed. A. MacBride White and A. N. Jeffares (1992), pp. 383–84; Literariness.org, 'Analysis of W. B. Yeats's Easter, 1916' (Gonne's reply; Yeats's September 1916 letter); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  18. To murmur name upon name l. 61

    Yeats assigns the judgement of the sacrifice to heaven and gives the living the task of commemoration: to 'murmur name upon name', as in a litany or roll-call. The simile of a mother naming her child 'when sleep at last has come / On limbs that had run wild' makes the dead into children asleep after wild play, a tender but also infantilising image. The speaker immediately corrects himself: 'No, no, not night but death', rejecting the consoling euphemism.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications)

  19. England may keep faith l. 68

    The Government of Ireland Act 1914, granting Ireland limited self-government (Home Rule), received royal assent in September 1914, but its operation was suspended for the duration of the war. Yeats asks whether the Rising was 'needless death' since England might have kept its promise. The question hangs unanswered: the poem neither affirms nor denies the necessity of the rebellion. After 1916 Home Rule was in practice overtaken by the War of Independence, and the act never came into force in the south.

    'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); 'Government of Ireland Act 1914', Wikipedia (Home Rule Act, suspended by the Suspensory Act 1914); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  20. excess of love / Bewildered them ll. 72–73

    Yeats suggests that the rebels' love of Ireland was so excessive that it 'bewildered' them (led them astray, confused them) 'till they died'. The phrase may be read as tribute (their love surpassed reason) or as criticism (their love was misplaced, a romantic folly). It may also glance at Pearse's doctrine of blood sacrifice. The lines that follow ('I write it out in a verse') assert the poet's own role: whatever the judgement, he will record their names.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  21. MacDonagh and MacBride / And Connolly and Pearse ll. 75–76

    After withholding names throughout, Yeats now lists four of the executed men. Thomas MacDonagh, John MacBride and Patrick Pearse have been described in stanza 2; James Connolly (1868–1916), the labour leader and commander of the Irish Citizen Army, was so badly wounded that he was executed tied to a chair on 12 May 1916. The naming fulfils the promise to 'murmur name upon name'. The order (MacDonagh, MacBride, Connolly, Pearse) is shaped by rhyme ('Pearse / verse') rather than rank.

    'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'James Connolly', Dictionary of Irish Biography (Royal Irish Academy)

  22. Wherever green is worn l. 78

    Green is the traditional colour of Ireland and of Irish nationalism (the 'wearing of the green'). The line answers stanza 1's 'motley': the fool's costume has given way to the national colour. 'Now and in time to be' echoes liturgical formulas ('now and ever shall be'), giving the conclusion the weight of prayer. Yeats himself had reservations about green as a political symbol, which makes his use of it here more striking.

    'Easter, 1916', Wikipedia (structure, identifications); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem
