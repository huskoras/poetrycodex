Leda and the Swan

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Tower (1928)

Mythology & FolkloreHistory & Politics

A sudden blow: the great wings beating still
Above the staggering girl, her thighs caressed
By the dark webs, her nape caught in his bill,
He holds her helpless breast upon his breast.

How can those terrified vague fingers push
The feathered glory from her loosening thighs?
And how can body, laid in that white rush
But feel the strange heart beating where it lies?

A shudder in the loins engenders there
The broken wall, the burning roof and tower
And Agamemnon dead.
Being so caught up,
So mastered by the brute blood of the air,
Did she put on his knowledge with his power
Before the indifferent beak could let her drop?

Codex Note

A sonnet on Zeus's rape of Leda in the form of a swan, with the 'broken wall, the burning roof and tower' of Troy that followed. It ends by asking whether she took on his knowledge with his power.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Myth, date and origin title; ll. 1-2

    In Greek myth Zeus took the form of a swan to rape (in some versions seduce) Leda, wife of Tyndareus of Sparta; she bore eggs from which came Helen and Polydeuces, and in some accounts Clytemnestra and Castor. The poem, dated 1923, appeared in The Dial (June 1924), in To-morrow (August 1924), in The Cat and the Moon and Certain Poems (1924) and in The Tower (1928). Yeats's note says the editor of a political review (George Russell, AE, of the Irish Statesman) had asked him for a poem; he began one using Leda as a metaphor, but as he wrote, bird and lady took over the scene and politics fell away.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; 'Leda and the Swan', University of Virginia English Poetry anthology

  2. A sudden blow l. 1

    The opening phrase has no main verb, so the reader, like Leda, is struck before understanding. 'Blow' suggests both a physical strike and the beating of wings. The participles that follow ('beating', 'staggering', 'caressed') freeze the moment of action. Stallworthy's study of the drafts shows that earlier versions opened less abruptly; the final opening was a decisive revision. The colon after 'blow' then releases a rush of detail, as if the mind were catching up with the body.

    Jon Stallworthy, Between the Lines: Yeats's Poetry in the Making (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963); Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  3. staggering girl l. 2

    The word 'girl' stresses Leda's youth and vulnerability against the 'great wings'. 'Staggering' conveys physical shock and loss of balance. Feminist critics have emphasized how the poem's language registers the violence of rape ('helpless', 'terrified') while also aestheticizing it ('caressed', 'feathered glory'); this double register is at the heart of the debate over the poem. Cullingford discusses how its reception has shifted as readers named the act as rape.

    Elizabeth Butler Cullingford, Gender and History in Yeats's Love Poetry (Cambridge: CUP, 1993), ch. on 'Leda and the Swan'; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  4. dark webs l. 3

    The webs are the swan's feet, black against its white plumage. Pairing 'caressed' with 'dark webs' makes the act tender in word and repellent in image. 'Her nape caught in his bill' recalls how a mating swan grips the female's neck. Critics have connected the poem's composition with visual art on the subject, notably Michelangelo's lost Leda (known through copies) and classical reliefs that Yeats could have known in reproduction.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  5. feathered glory l. 6

    'Glory' carries the biblical sense of divine radiance (Hebrew kabod, Greek doxa), the visible splendour of God. Zeus's divinity appears here only as feathers and glory, power without human shape. The juxtaposition with 'terrified vague fingers' sets human helplessness against inhuman majesty. The rhetorical questions of the second quatrain imply that a mortal could not resist. 'Vague' fingers suggests not only weakness but dazed confusion: Leda cannot grasp, physically or mentally, what is happening to her.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. glory, n.; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  6. laid in that white rush l. 7

    Most readers take 'white rush' as the onrush of white feathers, the swan's body overwhelming her. Some critics note a possible pun on 'rush' as a reed, since Leda is often pictured by a river among reeds. The line asks how the 'body', made impersonal here, could fail to 'feel the strange heart beating where it lies', the divine heart now pressed against hers. 'Strange' means foreign, non-human.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  7. A shudder in the loins l. 9

    The sestet opens with the climax, the 'shudder in the loins' that 'engenders', conceives, the Trojan War and its aftermath. The phrase compresses causality: one physical moment produces centuries of history. The present tense ('engenders there') makes the future already present in the act. The shift from physical to historical in mid-sentence is one of the poem's most admired effects.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007)

  8. The fall of Troy l. 10

    Helen, born of Leda's union with Zeus, would be carried off by Paris, causing the Greek siege and sack of Troy. The three images (broken wall, burning roof, tower) compress the city's destruction as told in Homer's tradition and in Aeneid II. Some readers also hear in 'tower' a glance at Yeats's own tower, Thoor Ballylee, which gives the volume its title.

    Virgil, Aeneid, Book II; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  9. Agamemnon dead l. 11

    Agamemnon, king of Mycenae and commander of the Greek army, was killed on his homecoming by his wife Clytemnestra (in some versions also Leda's child) and her lover Aegisthus, as dramatized in Aeschylus' Agamemnon. Three words thus extend the consequences from Troy to the house of Atreus and its cycle of revenge. The short half-line, followed by a gap, gives a stunned pause: the sonnet's form breaks where history breaks.

    Aeschylus, Agamemnon; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  10. Form: a broken sonnet ll. 1-14; l. 11

    The poem is a sonnet rhyming abab cdcd efg efg: an octave built of two Shakespearean-style quatrains and a Petrarchan sestet. Line 11 is split, with 'Being so caught up' set down to the right after 'Agamemnon dead'. The visual break marks the turn from historical consequence back to Leda, and from statement to question. Yeats thus binds the sonnet, traditionally the form of love poetry, to violence, a choice that feminist critics find troubling and formalist critics regard as a triumph of controlled shock.

    Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007), chapter on the sonnet; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  11. Being so caught up, / So mastered ll. 11-12

    'Caught up' means both seized physically and rapt in spirit, as in Paul's account of a man 'caught up to the third heaven' (2 Corinthians 12:2) or of the faithful 'caught up' in the clouds (1 Thessalonians 4:17). 'So mastered' insists on subjection and domination. The double sense prepares the final question whether Leda, in being overpowered, also gained divine knowledge. The participles keep her grammatically passive throughout.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Corinthians 12:2; 1 Thessalonians 4:17; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; Elizabeth Butler Cullingford, Gender and History in Yeats's Love Poetry (Cambridge: CUP, 1993), ch. on 'Leda and the Swan'

  12. brute blood of the air l. 12

    The phrase fuses opposite realms: 'brute' (Latin brutus, dull, irrational) and 'blood' suggest animal violence, 'of the air' the celestial domain of Zeus, god of the sky. The alliteration on b makes the line heavy and forceful. It sums up the paradox of a god incarnate as a beast, both higher and lower than the human. The same doubleness is in 'mastered', which suggests both domination and the authority of a master over a servant.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. brute, adj.; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  13. Did she put on his knowledge with his power l. 13

    'Put on' echoes Paul's language of putting on immortality: 'this mortal must put on immortality' (1 Corinthians 15:53), as if Leda might be clothed in divinity. The question is unanswered. Some critics answer no, taking the 'indifferent beak' as proof of the god's careless use of her; others think she may have shared, for a moment, a vision of the history her body will produce. Feminist critics, notably Cullingford, ask whether the question romanticizes rape by imagining a gain for the victim; others argue that the poem exposes the violence of divine and historical power.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 15:53; Elizabeth Butler Cullingford, Gender and History in Yeats's Love Poetry (Cambridge: CUP, 1993), ch. on 'Leda and the Swan'; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  14. indifferent beak l. 14

    The adjective 'indifferent' reveals that the encounter, cosmic in its consequences, means nothing personal to the god. The verb 'drop' is casual and brutal. The rhyme 'up'/'drop' carries the poem from rapture to fall in a last physical motion. The poem ends on a question mark, leaving Leda's experience unknowable. Critics who read the poem as Yeats's myth of history note that the god, like history in A Vision, is impersonal: it uses individuals and discards them.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  15. Leda as a pagan Annunciation l. 9; whole poem

    In A Vision Yeats wrote that he imagined the annunciation that founded Greece as made to Leda, and recalled that a Spartan temple was said to have preserved one of her unhatched eggs; from one egg came Love and from the other War. Mary and the dove inaugurate the Christian era, Leda and the swan the classical one. The poem therefore stands as a counterpart to 'The Second Coming', which awaits the next annunciation. Yeats later wrote 'The Mother of God' on the Christian annunciation.

    W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937), Book V, 'Dove or Swan'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; W. B. Yeats, 'The Mother of God', The Winding Stair and Other Poems (1933)
