Arts & Sciences

Sailing to Byzantium

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Tower (1928)

Arts & SciencesTime & Brevity

I

That is no country for old men. The young
In one another’s arms, birds in the trees,
--Those dying generations--at their song,
The salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas,
Fish flesh or fowl, commend all summer long
Whatever is begotten born and dies.
Caught in that sensual music all neglect
Monuments of unaging intellect.

II

An aged man is but a paltry thing,
A tattered coat upon a stick, unless
Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing
For every tatter in its mortal dress,
Nor is there singing school but studying
Monuments of its own magnificence;
And therefore I have sailed the seas and come
To the holy city of Byzantium.

III

O sages standing in God’s holy fire
As in the gold mosaic of a wall,
Come from the holy fire, perne in a gyre,
And be the singing masters of my soul.
Consume my heart away; sick with desire
And fastened to a dying animal
It knows not what it is; and gather me
Into the artifice of eternity.

IV

Once out of nature I shall never take
My bodily form from any natural thing,
But such a form as Grecian goldsmiths make
Of hammered gold and gold enamelling
To keep a drowsy emperor awake;
Or set upon a golden bough to sing
To lords and ladies of Byzantium
Of what is past, or passing, or to come.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

'That is no country for old men.' An ageing poet leaves the sensual country of the young to sail to Byzantium, where he hopes to be gathered into the 'artifice of eternity' as a golden bird singing of what is past, passing and to come.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and the 'country' title; l. 1

    Yeats wrote the poem in 1926, aged sixty-one; it opens The Tower (1928). In a BBC broadcast of 1931 he said he was trying to write about the state of his soul, since it is right for an old man to make his soul, and that Byzantium, the centre of European civilization and the source of its spiritual philosophy, symbolized the search for the spiritual life. The demonstrative 'That' places the speaker already away from his 'country', which is Ireland and, more broadly, the world of nature and sensual youth. Cormac McCarthy took 'No Country for Old Men' as the title of his 2005 novel.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; 'Sailing to Byzantium', Wikipedia (composition; 1931 broadcast)

  2. Ottava rima stanza form; st. 2

    Each stanza has eight iambic pentameter lines rhyming abababcc, the Italian ottava rima of Ariosto and Tasso, used in English by Byron in Don Juan. Yeats made it his grave public form in 'Among School Children' and 'Coole Park and Ballylee, 1931'. He rhymes loosely ('young'/'song'/'long'; 'unless'/'dress'), letting the closing couplet carry each stanza's point: 'neglect'/'intellect', 'come'/'Byzantium', 'gather me'/'eternity', 'Byzantium'/'to come'. Each stanza is also a step in a journey: rejection, voyage, prayer, transformation.

    Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007), chapter on ottava rima; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  3. Those dying generations l. 3

    The dashes set off a judgement on the whole teeming scene: the lovers and birds belong to 'dying generations', each begetting the next only to die. 'Generations' carries both senses, the act of generating and a cohort of the living. Keats's 'Ode to a Nightingale' is a close source: 'Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird! / No hungry generations tread thee down'. Yeats reverses Keats: here the natural birds are mortal, and only the artificial golden bird of stanza IV can escape death.

    John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), st. 7; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  4. salmon-falls, mackerel-crowded seas l. 4

    Salmon climbing waterfalls to spawn and then die are an emblem of the urgency of procreation; in Irish lore the salmon is also the fish of wisdom, though here it is purely bodily. The 'mackerel-crowded seas' suggest a superabundant, swarming life. Commentators locate the images in the west of Ireland, with its salmon weirs and mackerel shoals. The crowding of compounds ('salmon-falls', 'mackerel-crowded') makes the line itself crowded.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  5. Fish, flesh, or fowl l. 5

    The phrase adapts the proverbial 'neither fish, flesh, nor fowl' (or 'nor good red herring'), used of something of no definite kind. Yeats turns it into a positive inventory of all living things, of water, land and air, which 'commend', praise or celebrate, the cycle of generation. The lack of commas in the archive's text, 'Fish flesh or fowl' and 'begotten born and dies', hurries the list along like the teeming life it names.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. fish, n.1 (phrase 'neither fish nor flesh'); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  6. begotten, born, and dies l. 6

    The three verbs compress the natural cycle, which the sensual world praises 'all summer long', a phrase that already hints at autumn. The line looks forward to the poem's last line, 'Of what is past, or passing, or to come', where another triad of time is sung by the golden bird from outside nature. Yeats repeatedly weighs begetting against art and age: compare 'Among School Children', where a mother is imagined seeing her son at sixty and asking whether he was worth the pang of his birth.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; W. B. Yeats, 'Among School Children', The Tower (1928), st. 5

  7. Monuments of unaging intellect l. 8

    'Monuments' (Latin monumentum, from monere, to remind) are lasting memorials: here works of art, philosophy and architecture, products of an intellect that, unlike the body, does not grow old. 'Unaging' is a pointed coinage against 'dying generations'. The phrase is picked up in stanza II, 'Monuments of its own magnificence', which the soul must study to learn its song. Critics note the old man's disdain for youth, and some read the stanza's lush sensuality as betraying his continued attachment to what he renounces.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  8. A tattered coat upon a stick l. 10

    The image is of a scarecrow, a coat hung on a stick: the old body is nothing but a frame with worn clothing. The 'tatter' is developed in l. 12, where each tear in the 'mortal dress' (the body as the soul's garment, a traditional Platonic and Pauline figure) is a reason for the soul to sing louder. Yeats used the scarecrow again in 'Among School Children', with its 'Old clothes upon old sticks to scare a bird'.

    W. B. Yeats, 'Among School Children', The Tower (1928), st. 4; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  9. Soul clap its hands and sing l. 11

    Gilchrist's Life of William Blake (1863) records that at the death of his brother Robert, Blake saw the released spirit ascend through the ceiling 'clapping its hands for joy'. Yeats, who co-edited Blake's works with Edwin Ellis (1893), knew the anecdote, and commentators cite it as the source. The difference is pointed: Blake's soul claps as it leaves the body, while Yeats's must learn to clap and sing while still 'fastened to a dying animal'. 'Unless' governs the whole clause: an old man is worthless unless the soul sings.

    Alexander Gilchrist, Life of William Blake (London, 1863), vol. 1, on the death of Robert Blake (1787); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  10. singing school l. 13

    The soul is not taught by nature; it learns its song only by studying the 'Monuments of its own magnificence', the masterpieces made by souls like itself. This motivates the voyage: Byzantium is where such monuments are. 'Singing school' prepares for the 'singing masters' of stanza III, the sages in the mosaic whom the speaker asks to teach his soul. 'And therefore' presents the journey as a logical consequence.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  11. Byzantium l. 16

    Byzantium, refounded by Constantine as Constantinople in 330, was the capital of the Christian Eastern Empire until 1453. In A Vision (1925) Yeats wrote that if he could be given a month of antiquity he would spend it in Byzantium a little before Justinian opened St Sophia, because there, perhaps as never before or since, religious, aesthetic and practical life were one, and artists worked almost impersonally. The 'holy city' is thus less a historical place than a symbol of art as an eternal, spiritual order. Yeats had seen Byzantine mosaics at Ravenna (1907) and in Sicily (1925).

    W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937), Book V, 'Dove or Swan'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  12. Sages in the gold mosaic ll. 17-18

    The 'sages' are figures in a Byzantine mosaic whose gold ground is imagined as 'God's holy fire'. Commentators point to the procession of martyrs in Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna, which Yeats saw in 1907 with Lady Gregory, and to the mosaics at Monreale and in the Cappella Palatina, Palermo, seen in 1925. The fire is purgatorial and refining: it burns away the natural self. The sages are asked to come out of the wall and become 'the singing masters of my soul'.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; 'Basilica of Sant'Apollinare Nuovo', Wikipedia (the martyrs' procession)

  13. perne in a gyre l. 19

    Yeats made a verb from 'pern', a word for a spool or bobbin on which thread is wound (related to dialect 'pirn'). In his note to 'Demon and Beast' (The Wild Swans at Coole, 1919) Yeats recalls seeing smoke from a 'pern mill' as a child in Sligo and being told that 'pern' was another name for the spool; the OED treats the verb as his adoption of an Irish dialect word. To 'perne in a gyre' is to spin round a spiral like thread unwinding, here descending from eternity into time to meet the speaker. The gyre is the same figure as in 'The Second Coming', the spiralling cone of Yeats's system, in which time and eternity interpenetrate. Some readers also hear 'pern', the honey-buzzard, circling.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. perne, v.; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937)

  14. Consume my heart away l. 21

    The heart is the seat of desire, 'sick with desire', and must be consumed by the refining fire, as metal is purified in a furnace, before the soul can be 'gathered' into art. The image of purgatorial fire anticipates Yeats's later 'Byzantium', with its 'flames begotten of flame'. The paradox, often noted, is that the poem's longing for an end to desire is itself passionate, and the stanza is the most intensely felt in the poem.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; W. B. Yeats, 'Byzantium', The Winding Stair and Other Poems (1933)

  15. fastened to a dying animal l. 22

    The blunt phrase makes the body an animal to which the heart is tied, an image with roots in Plato's picture of the soul bound and glued to the body (Phaedo) and in Christian ascetic writing. Its shock lies in the plain word 'animal' after the gold and fire of the stanza. The heart 'knows not what it is': confused by its double nature, spirit fastened to flesh, it cannot tell whether it belongs to time or to eternity.

    Plato, Phaedo 82e-83a (the soul bound to the body); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  16. artifice of eternity l. 24

    'Artifice' (Latin artificium, craftsmanship) means skilled making, so the speaker asks to be gathered into eternity as into a work of art. But the word can also mean contrivance or trickery, and critics have argued over whether it betrays a doubt about the eternity it seeks. Some read the line as Yeats's confidence that art alone is immortal; others, including Vendler, stress the tension between the artificial and the living that runs through the poem. 'Gather' has a tender sense, as of being gathered to one's fathers in death.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. artifice, n.; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007); Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  17. The golden bird ll. 27-29

    Yeats's note says he had read somewhere that in the Emperor's palace at Byzantium there was a tree made of gold and silver, and artificial birds that sang. Such automata are described by Liudprand of Cremona in his account of an embassy to Constantine VII (949), and Scott's Count Robert of Paris has also been suggested as Yeats's source. The bird is the opposite of Keats's nightingale and of the 'birds in the trees' of stanza I. When T. Sturge Moore objected that a golden bird is as much nature as a living one, Yeats took up the problem again in 'Byzantium'.

    Yeats's note on the poem, as quoted in Anthology of British Literature, Open Text BC; Liudprand of Cremona, Antapodosis, Book VI; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  18. To keep a drowsy emperor awake l. 29

    The golden bird's task, keeping an emperor awake, is curiously modest and courtly. Critics have noted the irony that the soul's highest aspiration ends in decorative entertainment. Others see in it Yeats's ideal of the poet in a hierarchical society, singing to 'lords and ladies' like a court poet, impersonal and traditional. The ambiguity matches the doubts many readers find in 'artifice'.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007)

  19. golden bough l. 30

    In Aeneid VI Aeneas must find and pluck a golden bough to pass alive into the underworld; it is a passport between the living and the dead. Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890; expanded 1906-15) made the phrase famous as a symbol of death and renewal in myth. The golden bird set on a golden bough thus sits at the frontier between time and eternity. The bough also matches the tree of gold and silver in Yeats's note.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.136-48; James George Frazer, The Golden Bough (1890); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  20. past, or passing, or to come l. 32

    The closing line sets a timeless singer against the time-bound triad of l. 6, 'begotten born and dies'. It recalls Blake's 'Introduction' to Songs of Experience, where the Bard 'Present, Past, & Future sees'. Critics note the irony that the eternal bird sings precisely of time, of the very generation the poem began by rejecting; this has been read either as art transfiguring nature, or as an admission that the poet cannot escape it.

    William Blake, 'Introduction', Songs of Experience (1794); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem
