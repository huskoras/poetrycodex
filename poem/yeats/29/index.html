History & Politics

September 1913

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From Responsibilities (1914)

History & PoliticsSocial Commentaries

What need you, being come to sense,
But fumble in a greasy till
And add the halfpence to the pence
And prayer to shivering prayer, until
You have dried the marrow from the bone;
For men were born to pray and save:
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.

Yet they were of a different kind
The names that stilled your childish play,
They have gone about the world like wind,
But little time had they to pray
For whom the hangman's rope was spun,
And what, God help us, could they save:
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.

Was it for this the wild geese spread
The grey wing upon every tide;
For this that all that blood was shed,
For this Edward Fitzgerald died,
And Robert Emmet and Wolfe Tone,
All that delirium of the brave;
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.

Yet could we turn the years again,
And call those exiles as they were,
In all their loneliness and pain
You'd cry 'some woman's yellow hair
Has maddened every mother's son':
They weighed so lightly what they gave,
But let them be, they're dead and gone,
They're with O'Leary in the grave.

Codex Note

A bitter poem on Dublin's shopkeepers 'fumbling in the greasy till' while the heroes of Irish nationalism, the Wild Geese, are dead. It insists that 'Romantic Ireland's dead and gone'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: the Lane gallery and Dublin in 1913 l. 1

    The poem attacks the Dublin middle class who opposed building a municipal gallery for Sir Hugh Lane's collection of modern French paintings. In his note of 1914 Yeats listed this as the third of three controversies (after the Parnell split and The Playboy riots) that showed Irish minds without culture, and named William Martin Murphy, organiser of the lock-out then in progress, as a leader of the opposition. The title it now bears gives the month of the Lockout, which had begun in August 1913, but the poem was not written about the strike. Reprinted as 'Romantic Ireland' in 1914, it took its present title only in the 1916 edition of Responsibilities. The opening question is scornful: 'being come to sense' mocks the prudence of people who think they have grown up.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg); 'September 1913 (poem)', Wikipedia (first printing and title history); 'Romantic Ireland's dead and gone', Century Ireland (RTE / Boston College), on the Irish Times printing of 8 September 1913

  2. fumble in a greasy till l. 2

    The till is a cash drawer; 'fumble' and 'greasy' make the getting of money furtive and dirty. Yeats associated this class with the Irish 'Paudeen', the name he gives the huckster in a neighbouring poem, and in his 1914 note he blames the rise of 'our new middle class' for the baseness he saw in Dublin's public quarrels. Critics have pointed out the social snobbery of the attack, made by a Protestant poet against Catholic tradesmen, and the poem is also read alongside the Lockout, though Yeats did not write it about the strike.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg); 'September 1913 (poem)', Wikipedia (first printing and title history)

  3. And prayer to shivering prayer l. 4

    The zeugma ('add the halfpence to the pence / And prayer to shivering prayer') puts money and prayer in the same ledger. 'Shivering' suggests fearful, cold devotion. Yeats's 1914 note complains that religious Ireland thinks of divine things 'as a round of duties separated from life', which is exactly the habit the line satirises. The phrase 'For men were born to pray and save' is the mercantile creed put in ironic aphorism.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  4. dried the marrow from the bone l. 5

    Marrow is the living substance inside bones and, proverbially, the core of strength and vigour. To dry it is to reduce life to a dead skeleton. The line marks the end of the first sentence's long movement and sets up the refrain's verdict that the romantic, self-sacrificing Ireland is dead. There may also be an echo of the biblical 'dry bones' (Ezekiel 37), a valley of bones without life, though Yeats's image is domestic and commercial rather than prophetic. The verb 'dried' makes the process slow and deliberate, the work of a lifetime of saving.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'marrow'; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  5. The refrain l. 7

    Each eight-line stanza of iambic tetrameter (rhymed ababcdcd) closes with the same couplet, a ballad device that gives the satire the cadence of a lament. In the last stanza the refrain changes to 'But let them be, they're dead and gone, / They're with O'Leary in the grave', shifting from the dead idea to the dead men. In a note dated July 1916 Yeats wrote that the refrain had seemed true in 1913 but that he 'did not foresee 1916': the Easter Rising proved romantic Ireland alive, a reversal he explored in 'Easter, 1916'.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg); 'September 1913 (poem)', Wikipedia (first printing and title history)

  6. O'Leary l. 8

    O'Leary was a leader of the Irish Republican Brotherhood, convicted in 1865 and imprisoned, then exiled; he returned to Dublin in 1885 and became the young Yeats's guide, lending him books of Irish poetry and modelling a nationalism of honour rather than self-interest. He died on 16 March 1907. For Yeats he embodied an aristocratic, disinterested patriotism, which the poem contrasts with shopkeeping prudence.

    'John O'Leary (Fenian)', Wikipedia, drawing on Alan O'Day's ODNB entry; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  7. The names that stilled your childish play l. 10

    The line recalls a time when the names of rebels were spoken with reverence at home, enough to stop children's games. The irony is that the same people, grown up and 'come to sense', now prize money and prayer. The heroes 'have gone about the world like wind', scattered in exile and death and as hard to hold as air.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  8. the hangman's rope l. 13

    The line imagines the rope spun in advance for the patriots, a fate they accepted. Unlike the shopkeepers, they had 'little time ... to pray', and nothing to 'save' in either sense (money or their lives). The play on 'save' links this stanza to the first, where men 'were born to pray and save'. The passive construction ('was spun') suggests a fate prepared by others, by the authorities who executed rebels, and accepted by the patriots as part of their calling.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  9. the wild geese l. 17

    After the defeat of the Jacobite cause in 1691 thousands of Irish soldiers sailed for France and later served in the armies of France, Spain and Austria; they were called the Wild Geese, and the name was extended to later Irish exiles in foreign service. Yeats's image ('spread / The grey wing upon every tide') makes them migrating birds. The question 'Was it for this ...?' is a classical rhetorical form of indignation, implying that their sacrifice has been betrayed.

    'Flight of the Wild Geese', Wikipedia; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  10. Edward Fitzgerald l. 20

    A son of the Duke of Leinster and a former British army officer, Lord Edward joined the United Irishmen and was a principal planner of the 1798 rising. He was wounded while resisting arrest in May 1798 and died in Newgate Prison, Dublin, on 4 June. His noble birth made him for Yeats a type of the aristocratic patriot, which matters to the poem's contrast between generous gentry and calculating tradesmen.

    'Lord Edward FitzGerald', Wikipedia; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  11. Robert Emmet l. 21

    Emmet's rising in Dublin collapsed in a night; he was captured, tried and hanged in Thomas Street. His speech from the dock, asking that no man write his epitaph until Ireland took her place among the nations, made him the most romantic of Irish martyrs, celebrated by Thomas Moore among others. He is one of the patriots for whom 'the hangman's rope was spun'.

    'Robert Emmet', Wikipedia; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  12. Wolfe Tone l. 21

    Tone, a Protestant Dublin lawyer, helped found the Society of United Irishmen in 1791 and sought French military aid; he sailed with French expeditions in 1796 and 1798. Captured in November 1798 and sentenced to death, he died of a self-inflicted wound before execution. With Fitzgerald and Emmet he completes a trio of mostly Protestant, eighteenth-century republican heroes, which suited Yeats's picture of a lost, generous Ireland.

    'Theobald Wolfe Tone', Wikipedia; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  13. All that delirium of the brave l. 22

    'Delirium' (Latin delirare, to go out of the furrow) is a fever of the mind. Yeats calls the rebels' courage a delirium partly in admiration and partly to anticipate how the prudent would describe it. The phrase looks forward to the last stanza, where the shopkeepers would say a woman's hair had 'maddened every mother's son'. The phrase also has an elegiac grandeur: the rebels of 1798 and 1803 failed, but their failure is given the dignity of passion rather than error.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'delirium'; W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg)

  14. some woman's yellow hair l. 28

    If the dead returned, says the poem, the modern Dubliner would explain their loneliness and pain as infatuation, as if all of them had been driven mad by 'some woman's yellow hair'. Readers differ about the force of the image. It can be the shopkeepers' cynical reduction of patriotism to romance; it can also allude to the tradition, which Yeats used in his play Cathleen ni Houlihan (1902), of Ireland figured as a woman for whom young men die; and some hear an echo of Helen of Troy, for whose beauty a war was fought. The irony is that the dismissive phrase is, in a sense, true.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats and Lady Gregory, Cathleen ni Houlihan (1902), 'Cathleen ni Houlihan', Wikipedia

  15. They weighed so lightly what they gave l. 30

    'Weighed' continues the commercial vocabulary of the first stanza: the shopkeepers weigh and count, the heroes gave without weighing. In his note of July 1916 Yeats quoted this line in praise of the leaders of the Easter Rising, who gave, 'in some cases without hope of success'. The line therefore turned out to describe heroes Yeats thought did not exist, and the poem's despair was overtaken within three years.

    W. B. Yeats, Responsibilities and Other Poems (London: Macmillan, 1916), text and Yeats's notes dated 1914 and July 1916 (Project Gutenberg); 'September 1913 (poem)', Wikipedia (first printing and title history)
