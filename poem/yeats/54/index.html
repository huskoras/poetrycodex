History & Politics

The Second Coming

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921)

History & PoliticsReligion

Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.

Surely some revelation is at hand;
Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
Troubles my sight: a waste of desert sand;
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Wind shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.' The poem describes the widening gyre of history and a rough beast slouching towards Bethlehem to be born. One of the most quoted poems of the twentieth century.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and context title; l. 1

    Yeats wrote the poem in January 1919, the date usually given by commentators. It appeared in The Dial (November 1920) and in The Nation (London), then in Michael Robartes and the Dancer (Cuala Press, dated 1920, issued early 1921). Its background is the end of the Great War, the Russian Revolution of 1917 and spreading violence in Ireland. Jon Stallworthy's study of the manuscripts shows that drafts referred more directly to contemporary events, including the Germans and Russia and the French Revolution, which Yeats cut, making the final poem a general vision rather than a commentary. According to Neil Mann, The Dial's first printing read 'thirty centuries' where later texts have 'twenty'. It draws on the system of history that Yeats and his wife George were developing through her automatic writing, later published as A Vision (1925).

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Jon Stallworthy, Between the Lines: Yeats's Poetry in the Making (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963), chapter on 'The Second Coming'; 'Michael Robartes and the Dancer', Wikipedia (publication); Neil Mann, 'The Second Coming: notes', YeatsVision.com

  2. gyre l. 1

    A gyre (Latin gyrus, Greek gyros, a circle or ring) is a spiral turning. In A Vision (1925) Yeats describes two interpenetrating cones, each with its apex in the other's base, and says the end of an age comes when one gyre reaches its greatest expansion and the other its greatest contraction. The Christian era, now nearly two thousand years old, is the widening gyre, its outward motion spinning apart. The falcon's widening circles are the visible image of that historical process. The word, which Yeats pronounced with a hard g, returns as 'perne in a gyre' in 'Sailing to Byzantium'.

    W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937); Neil Mann, 'The Second Coming: notes', YeatsVision.com; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  3. falcon and falconer l. 2

    In falconry a hawk is called back to the falconer's lure or voice. Here the bird has flown in ever-wider circles until the bond of command is broken, an image of a civilization drifting from its governing centre. Commentators have read the falconer variously as Christ, as reason or tradition, or simply as authority in general; Yeats leaves it open. The line also prepares for the 'desert birds' of l. 17: at the start a bird lost from its centre, at the end birds circling a new and terrible one.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007)

  4. Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold l. 3

    The line generalizes the falcon image: whatever held the world together has lost its pull. Its terse, monosyllabic statement has made it one of the most quoted lines of the century, borrowed by politicians and journalists in every crisis. Chinua Achebe took 'Things Fall Apart' as the title of his 1958 novel about the colonial destruction of Igbo society, with the poem's opening lines as epigraph; the borrowing turns Yeats's vision of the end of Christian Europe into an image of Africa's disruption by Europe.

    Chinua Achebe, Things Fall Apart (London: Heinemann, 1958), epigraph; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  5. Mere anarchy l. 4

    'Mere' keeps its older meaning (from Latin merus, pure, undiluted): sheer, absolute anarchy, not 'only' anarchy. 'Anarchy' (Greek anarchia, being without a ruler) suits the image of the falcon without a master. 'Loosed' suggests something formerly leashed or bound being let go, like a hunting animal or the destructive powers released in Revelation, where four angels 'were loosed' to slay a third part of men (9:14-15) and Satan 'must be loosed a little season' (20:3).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. mere, adj.2; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 9:14-15; 20:3; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  6. The blood-dimmed tide l. 5

    The tide 'dimmed' (darkened, clouded) by blood draws on biblical plague, especially the waters turned to blood in Exodus 7 and Revelation 8:8 and 16:3-4, and on the carnage of 1914-19. Some readers also hear Macbeth's 'multitudinous seas incarnadine'. The repetition of 'is loosed' joins the abstract anarchy and the physical flood in one movement. The tide then 'drowns' the 'ceremony of innocence', so that the image carries through the next line.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Exodus 7:20; Revelation 8:8; 16:3-4; William Shakespeare, Macbeth, 2.2; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  7. The ceremony of innocence l. 6

    Critics differ on what the 'ceremony of innocence' is. Some see a sacramental reference to baptism, whose water is now overwhelmed by a flood of blood. Others link it to Yeats's lifelong valuing of custom and ceremony as what preserves grace, as in 'A Prayer for My Daughter', written soon after and printed in the same volume, which asks how, except in custom and ceremony, innocence and beauty are born. That parallel suggests the phrase means the traditional, aristocratic forms of life that an age of revolution destroys. The two readings are not exclusive.

    W. B. Yeats, 'A Prayer for My Daughter', Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921), final stanza; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  8. The best and the worst ll. 7-8

    The couplet contrasts the irresolution of the thoughtful with the 'passionate intensity' of the violent: in Yeats's context perhaps the revolutionaries of Russia and Ireland, or the gunmen on all sides. Commentators point to a close anticipation in Shelley's Prometheus Unbound, where a Fury says that the good lack power and the powerful lack goodness (Act I, ll. 625-28); Yeats had read Shelley deeply since boyhood. The lines close the first section with an aphorism, after which the poem turns from public statement to private vision.

    Percy Bysshe Shelley, Prometheus Unbound (1820), Act I, ll. 625-28; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  9. The Second Coming ll. 9-10

    The title phrase refers to the expected return of Christ in glory (the Parousia), foretold in Matthew 24, where Jesus describes wars, false prophets and great tribulation as signs of his coming, and in Revelation. The speaker's 'Surely ... Surely' reads the present chaos as such a sign. But the revelation he receives inverts the doctrine: not Christ returning but a 'rough beast', closer to the Antichrist of 1 John 2:18 and the beast of Revelation 13. In Yeats's cyclical history each two-thousand-year era is begun by an annunciation, and the coming era will be the antithesis of the Christian one.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 24; 1 John 2:18; Revelation 13; W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  10. Spiritus Mundi l. 12

    Spiritus Mundi (Latin, 'the spirit of the world') is close to what Yeats elsewhere called Anima Mundi, the world-soul of Neoplatonic and Hermetic tradition, and to the 'Great Memory' of his essay 'Magic' (1901). In a note of 1921 he described it as a general storehouse of images which have ceased to be the property of any personality or spirit. The vision is therefore not merely private: it rises from a shared, impersonal memory, comparable to, though independent of, Jung's collective unconscious.

    Richard J. Finneran (ed.), The Poems of W. B. Yeats (Collected Works vol. 1; 2nd edn New York: Scribner, 1997), text and notes; W. B. Yeats, 'Magic' (1901), in Essays and Introductions (London: Macmillan, 1961); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  11. The sphinx-like shape l. 14

    The creature with 'lion body and the head of a man' recalls the Egyptian sphinx, the great image of pre-Christian, 'stony' antiquity. Its gaze, 'blank and pitiless as the sun', has no human feeling, and it is beginning to move its 'slow thighs', waking after long sleep. Yeats never names it a sphinx in the poem; Neil Mann links the image to the sphinx imagery of the Golden Dawn, the occult order Yeats belonged to, and in his system the new era will be violent and hierarchical, opposite to Christian love. The image is deliberately unexplained: we see only body, gaze and movement.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; Richard Ellmann, The Identity of Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1954); Neil Mann, 'The Second Coming: notes', YeatsVision.com

  12. indignant desert birds l. 17

    The shadows of desert birds, often taken as vultures, circle the moving shape. Their indignation, a human emotion transferred to them, registers that the order of nature is disturbed. They are the counterpart of the falcon of l. 2: then a bird lost from its centre, now birds circling a new and terrible one. Textual note: the archive's text reads 'Wind shadows', the reading of the Cuala Press Michael Robartes and the Dancer; later printings and the usual modern text read 'Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds'. The two readings change the image: wind-blown shadows versus shadows that whirl and stagger.

    Richard J. Finneran (ed.), The Poems of W. B. Yeats (Collected Works vol. 1; 2nd edn New York: Scribner, 1997), text and notes; Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach (eds), The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats (New York: Macmillan, 1957); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  13. twenty centuries of stony sleep l. 19

    In A Vision each great historical era lasts about two thousand years. The 'twenty centuries' are the Christian age that began with the birth of Christ; during them the beast, the spirit of the coming era, has slept like a stone image. The darkness that 'drops again' ends the vision, but the speaker now 'knows' the shape of history. The idea that each era breeds and is overturned by its opposite is central to Yeats's gyres.

    W. B. Yeats, A Vision (1925; rev. edn London: Macmillan, 1937); Neil Mann, 'The Second Coming: notes', YeatsVision.com; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  14. rocking cradle l. 20

    The cradle is Christ's at Bethlehem: the Incarnation that began the Christian era. Its 'rocking' is gentle, yet it has 'vexed to nightmare' the sleep of the pre-Christian world, so that the next birth will be a violent reply. The image prepares the final reversal, in which a beast rather than a child goes to Bethlehem 'to be born'. Yeats treated the same exchange of eras in 'Two Songs from a Play' and the play The Resurrection, where Christ's coming overturns the classical world.

    W. B. Yeats, 'Two Songs from a Play', The Tower (1928); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  15. what rough beast l. 21

    The poem ends with a question, not a prophecy: the speaker knows that a new era is coming but not 'what rough beast' it is. 'Rough' suggests both shaggy and brutal. 'Its hour come round at last' echoes the gyre turning to its appointed point, and also Christ's language about his 'hour' (John 2:4; 17:1). Critics debate whether Yeats feared or half-welcomed the beast: some read dread at coming totalitarian violence, and the poem has often been taken as prophetic of fascism; others hear the excitement of a believer in cyclical renewal.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 2:4; 17:1; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007)

  16. Slouches towards Bethlehem l. 22

    Bethlehem, the birthplace of Jesus (Matthew 2:1; Micah 5:2), becomes the destination of the new and opposite god. 'Slouches', a heavy, ungainly verb, contrasts brutally with the journey of the Magi under the star. The ending thus reverses the expectation of Christ's return raised at l. 10. Joan Didion took the line as the title of her essay collection Slouching Towards Bethlehem (1968), one sign of the poem's afterlife as a byword for cultural collapse.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 2:1; Micah 5:2; Joan Didion, Slouching Towards Bethlehem (New York: Farrar, Straus & Giroux, 1968); Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  17. Form: two-part blank verse whole poem

    The poem is in loose iambic pentameter, largely unrhymed, divided into an eight-line and a fourteen-line section. Some critics note that the second section has the length of a sonnet without its rhymes, a kind of broken or ghostly sonnet. The first part describes the world in the general present tense; the second shifts to the speaker's 'I' and a visionary moment, ended when 'The darkness drops again'. The final question leaves the poem open, in contrast with the confident 'Surely' that began the vision.

    Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2007); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem
