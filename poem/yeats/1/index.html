The Lake Isle of Innisfree

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Rose (1893)

NatureLiving

I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,
And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made:
Nine bean rows will I have there, a hive for the honey bee,
And live alone in the bee-loud glade.

And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,
Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;
There midnight's all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,
And evening full of the linnet's wings.

I will arise and go now, for always night and day
I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;
While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements gray,
I hear it in the deep heart's core.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The speaker will arise and go to Innisfree to build a cabin of clay and wattles with nine bean rows and a hive. Standing on the grey pavements, he hears the lake water 'in the deep heart's core'. Yeats's most popular lyric.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Fleet Street and Sligo title; ll. 9-12

    Yeats wrote the poem in London around 1888-90, when the family lived in Bedford Park and he was poor and homesick for Sligo. In Autobiographies he recalls walking down Fleet Street when the tinkle of a little fountain in a shop window, balancing a ball on its jet, brought back memories of lake water; from that sudden memory came the poem, which he called his first lyric with anything in its rhythm of his own music. Behind it lay a boyhood ambition, formed in Sligo, of living alone like Thoreau on the island. The poem first appeared in the National Observer (13 December 1890), was collected in The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics (1892), and later stood among the lyrics grouped as 'The Rose'. It became, somewhat to Yeats's own irritation, his most popular poem.

    W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree', The Victorian Web

  2. 'I will arise and go': the prodigal son l. 1

    The phrasing is biblical: in the parable of the prodigal son the young man, starving in a far country, says 'I will arise and go to my father' (Luke 15:18). The echo casts the London exile as a penitent wanderer longing for home and gives the plain wish an incantatory, scriptural weight. Yeats later judged this phrase harshly: in Autobiographies he says that a couple of years afterwards he could not have written the first line with its conventional archaism, 'Arise and go', nor the inversion in the last stanza, because he had come to believe he must use only the common syntax. The phrase returns at l. 9, so the poem is framed by a repeated vow rather than an action: the speaker never actually leaves the pavement.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 15:18; W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  3. Innisfree l. 1

    Innisfree (Irish Inis Fraoigh) is a small island at the eastern end of Lough Gill, near Sligo town, where Yeats spent much of his childhood with his mother's family, the Pollexfens. The name is generally glossed as 'heather island'. Yeats walked out to the lake as a boy and once spent a night in the woods on its shore. The real island is tiny and uninhabited; the poem makes it an imagined place of solitude, not a record of a stay. Its name also gives the poem an Irish sound, and readers have heard in it the word 'free', a refuge set against imperial London.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree', Wikipedia (location and Irish name); W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'

  4. clay and wattles l. 2

    Wattles (Old English watul) are rods or stakes interwoven with twigs or branches to make walls and fences; daubed with clay or mud they form the wattle-and-daub walls of the poorest rural dwellings. The detail is self-consciously primitive: the speaker means to build with his own hands, from materials of the island itself, the kind of cabin Thoreau built at Walden. The inversion 'a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made' is one of the archaisms Yeats later regretted. Its humble materials stand against the 'roadway' and 'pavements' of the final stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. wattle, n.1; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  5. Nine bean rows: Thoreau's Walden l. 3

    Thoreau's Walden (1854) has a chapter called 'The Bean-Field', describing the long rows of beans he hoed beside his cabin. Yeats's father read Walden to him, and in Autobiographies Yeats says he planned in his teens to live in imitation of Thoreau on Innisfree. The number nine has been read as a folk or magical number, suiting Yeats's interest in Irish lore and the occult, but it may simply mark a modest, manageable plot. The bean rows and the beehive make a self-sufficient economy of food and honey, the opposite of wage-labour in the city.

    Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854), ch. 7 'The Bean-Field'; W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  6. bee-loud glade l. 4

    A glade is an open space in a wood. 'Bee-loud' is a coinage in the manner of Keats's or Tennyson's compound epithets, making the place audible rather than visible: the island is known chiefly by its sounds (bees, cricket, linnet, lapping water). Each stanza's fourth line is shorter than the long lines before it, so the stanza closes on a settled, falling cadence, and here on a picture of the speaker living 'alone', the solitude he desires.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  7. Metre and sound st. 2; whole poem

    The poem has three quatrains rhyming abab. Lines 1-3 of each stanza are long lines of about six stresses with a strong caesura ('I will arise and go now, | and go to Innisfree'); the fourth line is shorter. Yeats called it the first lyric with anything in its rhythm of his own music, meaning a loosening of conventional metre as an escape from rhetoric. Repetition ('peace ... peace', 'dropping ... Dropping', 'I hear ... I hear') and the l- and s-sounds of the last stanza create the lulling effect that has made the poem much recited. Yeats's own recorded readings stress its incantatory quality.

    W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  8. peace comes dropping slow ll. 5-6

    The line makes an abstraction physical: peace falls drop by drop, like dew settling from the morning mist ('the veils of the morning') down to the ground where the cricket sings. The run-on into 'Dropping' at the start of l. 6 enacts the slow descent. The stanza then moves through the whole day, morning, midnight, noon and evening, so that peace becomes the island's continuous condition. The soft, misty phrasing belongs to the late Pre-Raphaelite and aesthetic manner of Yeats's early verse.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  9. veils of the morning l. 6

    The 'veils' are the mists that hang over a lake in early morning. The metaphor belongs to the Celtic Twilight vocabulary of Yeats's early verse, in which veils, mists and twilight mark a threshold between the visible world and an enchanted one. Here it stays largely naturalistic: peace drops from the mist to the grass, from the sky to the cricket on the ground.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; Richard Ellmann, The Identity of Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1954)

  10. cricket l. 6

    The cricket's chirping is a traditional emblem of homely, rural content, as in Milton's 'Il Penseroso' ('Save the cricket on the hearth'). Here the cricket sings in the grass, the lowest point of peace's descent from the morning mist. Like the bees and the linnet, it makes the island a place of small creatures and soft sounds rather than human company.

    John Milton, 'Il Penseroso' (1645), l. 82; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  11. midnight's all a glimmer l. 7

    The paradox of a bright midnight and a 'purple' noon reverses ordinary expectations: night glimmers with stars and their reflection in the lake, while noon is softened to a purple haze. The inversion suggests a landscape seen through feeling rather than observation, fitting a poem made from memory in a London street. The colloquial contraction 'midnight's all a glimmer' sits oddly but effectively beside the archaic diction elsewhere.

    Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  12. noon a purple glow l. 7

    Commentators usually take the 'purple glow' as the colour of heather in bloom, which fits the name Inis Fraoigh, 'heather island'. Others read it as the haze of a hot summer noon over water. Either way, colour is softened into a 'glow', in keeping with the poem's dreamy light. The stanza moves through dawn, midnight, noon and evening out of sequence, as memory rather than clock-time.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  13. linnet l. 8

    The linnet (Linaria cannabina) is a small brown finch of open country and gorse. In English verse it is often a figure of simple, natural song: Wordsworth's 'The Tables Turned' bids the reader 'hear the woodland linnet'. The stanza ends not on its song but on its 'wings', the soft whirr of birds settling at dusk. It completes the series of small creatures, bee, cricket, linnet, that people the island.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. linnet, n.; William Wordsworth, 'The Tables Turned' (1798), st. 3

  14. lake water lapping with low sounds l. 10

    The line is a celebrated piece of sound-painting: 'lake', 'lapping', 'low' and the long vowels of 'sounds by the shore' make the ear hear the water. The repetition of 'I hear' in ll. 10 and 12 stresses that the island is now present only as sound inside the speaker, not as sight. This is the memory of water that Yeats said the shop-window fountain in Fleet Street had suddenly brought back.

    W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem

  15. roadway and pavements gray l. 11

    The last stanza reveals that the speaker is in a city, London in the poem's origin, 'on the roadway, or on the pavements gray'. The grey of stone set against the 'purple glow' and the clay and wattles of the island gives the poem its structure of contrast between modern urban life and an imagined rural retreat. The postposed adjective, 'pavements gray', is a poetic inversion of the kind Yeats later disowned. Printings vary between 'gray' and 'grey'.

    Richard J. Finneran (ed.), The Poems of W. B. Yeats (Collected Works vol. 1; 2nd edn New York: Scribner, 1997), text and notes; W. B. Yeats, Autobiographies (London: Macmillan, 1955), 'Four Years: 1887-1891'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), note on this poem

  16. the deep heart's core l. 12

    'Heart's core' means the very centre of the heart; 'core' was popularly associated with Latin cor, heart, although its actual etymology is uncertain. The phrase echoes Hamlet's 'in my heart's core, ay, in my heart of heart' (Hamlet 3.2). Critics read the ending in two ways: as a confession that the island is an inner refuge that cannot be reached physically, or as an assertion that imagination's possession is the truest one. The three heavy stresses of 'deep heart's core' give the poem a firm close.

    William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. core, n.1; Daniel Albright (ed.), W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990; rev. 1994), notes to this poem
