The Wild Swans at Coole

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Wild Swans at Coole (1919)

NatureTime & Brevity

The trees are in their autumn beauty,
The woodland paths are dry,
Under the October twilight the water
Mirrors a still sky;
Upon the brimming water among the stones
Are nine and fifty swans.

The nineteenth Autumn has come upon me
Since I first made my count;
I saw, before I had well finished,
All suddenly mount
And scatter wheeling in great broken rings
Upon their clamorous wings.

I have looked upon those brilliant creatures,
And now my heart is sore.
All's changed since I, hearing at twilight,
The first time on this shore,
The bell-beat of their wings above my head,
Trod with a lighter tread.

Unwearied still, lover by lover,
They paddle in the cold,
Companionable streams or climb the air;
Their hearts have not grown old;
Passion or conquest, wander where they will,
Attend upon them still.

But now they drift on the still water
Mysterious, beautiful;
Among what rushes will they build,
By what lake's edge or pool
Delight men's eyes, when I awake some day
To find they have flown away?

Codex Note

Nineteen autumns after he first counted the swans at Coole Park, the poet counts nine and fifty again, still unwearied while his own heart is sore. He wonders in whose eyes they will delight when he wakes one day to find them gone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and context title, st. 1

    Coole Park, County Galway, was the estate of Yeats's friend and patron Augusta, Lady Gregory, where he spent summers from 1897 onward. The poem was written between 1916 and early 1917, first published in the Little Review in June 1917, and became the title poem of The Wild Swans at Coole (Cuala Press, 1917; Macmillan, 1919). Its context includes Yeats's sense of ageing (he was fifty-one), his renewed and rejected proposal to Maud Gonne in 1916, and his feeling for Iseult Gonne; critics such as Daniel Tobin also set it against the Irish unrest of the time. The swans of the lake are real, but the poem gives them symbolic weight.

    'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Wild Swans at Coole'; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003); W. B. Yeats, The Wild Swans at Coole (London: Macmillan, 1919), Project Gutenberg text

  2. Stanza form st. 1–5

    Each stanza has an unusual pattern of stresses: lines 1 and 3 roughly tetrameter, lines 2, 4 and 6 trimeter, line 5 pentameter, with rhymes abcbdd. The first stanza's closing couplet is a half-rhyme ('stones / swans'), a muted close, though later couplets ('rings / wings', 'head / tread') rhyme fully. The movement is meditative and conversational, with long sentences carried over the short lines, a style Yeats developed in this period as he moved from the ornate manner of his early verse to a more spare, spoken idiom.

    'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  3. October twilight l. 3

    The setting fuses time of year and time of day: October twilight, a season and hour of decline. The water 'mirrors a still sky': the stillness and reflection of the lake create an image of calm and of contemplation. 'Brimming' water (full to the edge) contrasts with the 'dry' paths, a fullness in nature against a sense of loss in the speaker. The images of autumn are traditional in the elegiac lyric, from Keats's 'To Autumn' to Shakespeare's Sonnet 73.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992)

  4. nine and fifty swans l. 6

    The archaic inverted numeral ('nine and fifty' for fifty-nine) gives the count a ballad-like, almost legendary tone. Many readers note that an odd number means one swan is unpaired, which casts the poet, unmarried and repeatedly refused by Maud Gonne, as the odd one out among the 'lover by lover' couples of stanza 4. Others caution against over-reading a number that may record a real count. The act of counting itself suggests an attempt to measure and possess what will escape.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Wild Swans at Coole'

  5. The nineteenth Autumn l. 7

    Yeats's first extended stay at Coole was in the summer and autumn of 1897, a time of emotional exhaustion after years of unrequited love for Maud Gonne; Lady Gregory nursed him back to health and became a lifelong collaborator. The 'nineteenth Autumn' sets the poem's time scheme: two moments compared, then and now. (Counting 1897 as the first autumn the nineteenth would fall in 1915, while the poem was written in 1916, so the figure is approximate or counted loosely.) 'Come upon me' presents age as something that has overtaken him from outside. The numbers (nineteen, fifty-nine) give the poem a sense of exact record.

    Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Wild Swans at Coole'; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  6. made my count l. 8

    In 1897 the speaker began to count the swans but they flew up before he had 'well finished'; the incompleteness of the count anticipates the poem's sense of the swans as elusive and beyond his control. The swans 'mount' and 'scatter wheeling in great broken rings': the circles anticipate Yeats's later symbol of the gyre, though that system was not developed until after his marriage in 1917 and A Vision (1925). The 'clamorous wings' introduce the sound of the swans, important in stanza 3.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992)

  7. great broken rings l. 11

    The rings are 'broken': the swans form circles that open and disperse. Some critics see a prefiguration of the 'widening gyre' of 'The Second Coming' (1920); others read it more simply as precise observation of birds taking off. 'Wheeling' suggests the circular flight of birds and the turning wheel of time, a theme that runs through the poem. The image of broken rings may also hint at broken attachments, the poet's own unfulfilled love.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992)

  8. my heart is sore l. 14

    The 'brilliant creatures' (shining, radiant) make the poet's heart 'sore', grieved and aching. 'All's changed' is a crucial phrase in 1916: the same year Yeats wrote 'Easter, 1916', with its refrain 'All changed, changed utterly'. The private change (his heart has aged, his loves have failed) and the public change of the Rising are both in the background. The stanza contrasts the 'lighter tread' of the younger man in 1897 with the present weight of age.

    R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem

  9. bell-beat l. 17

    Swans in flight make a loud, throbbing, musical sound with their wings, which Yeats renders with the compound 'bell-beat'. The alliteration suggests both regularity (beat) and resonance (bell), and perhaps also a bell tolling, a hint of mortality. The memory of hearing that sound for the first time 'above my head' is linked to the young man's 'lighter tread', so that the sound becomes a measure of the change in him.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  10. lover by lover l. 19

    Swans were long proverbial for fidelity, and the observation that they pair for life underlies the phrase 'lover by lover'. The adjective 'Unwearied' contrasts the swans with the weary poet. 'Companionable streams' is a transferred epithet: the swans are companionable, and so, in their eyes, is the water. The swans seem to escape time ('Their hearts have not grown old'), although individual swans die; Yeats treats them as a species or an idea, unchanging in a way the poet cannot be. The stanza ends with 'Passion or conquest' attending on them.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992)

  11. Passion or conquest l. 23

    'Attend upon' means accompany as servants: passion and conquest follow the swans as attendants follow nobility. 'Conquest' may refer to winning a mate (amorous conquest) and, more broadly, to the energy of achievement. The phrase defines what the poet feels he has lost: the capacity for passion and the success of conquest in love. 'Wander where they will' stresses the swans' freedom, against the speaker's own fixedness at the same lake after nineteen years.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915–1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  12. the still water l. 25

    The final stanza returns to the calm of stanza 1, with 'still water' echoing the 'still sky' and the 'Unwearied still' of stanza 4: Yeats exploits 'still' as both 'motionless' and 'yet, even now'. 'Mysterious, beautiful' isolates two adjectives on one line, suspending the movement. The swans' stillness becomes enigmatic, as the speaker imagines them building nests elsewhere, delighting other men's eyes in the future.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; 'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992)

  13. when I awake some day / To find they have flown away ll. 29–30

    The poem ends with a question. Readers propose several meanings: literal (the swans will one day migrate or move elsewhere); psychological (the poet will 'awake' to find that his capacity for wonder and passion has gone); or mortal (death as an awakening). Some critics read 'awake' as waking from a dream, so that the swans may be a vision that the poet will lose. The question form refuses closure, which suits the poem's theme of impermanence. The phrase 'Delight men's eyes' implies a future in which others, not he, will enjoy the beauty.

    'The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)', Wikipedia (dating, metre; citing Tobin, Yeats Eliot Review 11.3, 1992); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes on the poem; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'The Wild Swans at Coole'
