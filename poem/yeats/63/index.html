Time & Brevity

The Tower

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Tower (1928)

Time & BrevityArts & Sciences

I

What shall I do with this absurdity--
O heart, O troubled heart--this caricature,
Decrepit age that has been tied to me
As to a dog’s tail?
Never had I more
Excited, passionate, fantastical
Imagination, nor an ear and eye
That more expected the impossible--
No, not in boyhood when with rod and fly,
Or the humbler worm, I climbed Ben Bulben’s back
And had the livelong summer day to spend.
It seems that I must bid the Muse go pack,
Choose Plato and Plotinus for a friend
Until imagination, ear and eye,
Can be content with argument and deal
In abstract things; or be derided by
A sort of battered kettle at the heel.

II

I pace upon the battlements and stare
On the foundations of a house, or where
Tree, like a sooty finger, starts from the earth;
And send imagination forth
Under the day’s declining beam, and call
Images and memories
From ruin or from ancient trees,
For I would ask a question of them all.

Beyond that ridge lived Mrs. French, and once
When every silver candlestick or sconce
Lit up the dark mahogany and the wine,
A serving man that could divine
That most respected lady’s every wish,
Ran and with the garden shears
Clipped an insolent farmer’s ears
And brought them in a little covered dish.

Some few remembered still when I was young
A peasant girl commended by a song,
Who’d lived somewhere upon that rocky place,
And praised the colour of her face,
And had the greater joy in praising her,
Remembering that, if walked she there,
Farmers jostled at the fair
So great a glory did the song confer.

And certain men, being maddened by those rhymes,
Or else by toasting her a score of times,
Rose from the table and declared it right
To test their fancy by their sight;
But they mistook the brightness of the moon
For the prosaic light of day--
Music had driven their wits astray--
And one was drowned in the great bog of Cloone.

Strange, but the man who made the song was blind,
Yet, now I have considered it, I find
That nothing strange; the tragedy began
With Homer that was a blind man,
And Helen has all living hearts betrayed.
O may the moon and sunlight seem
One inextricable beam,
For if I triumph I must make men mad.

And I myself created Hanrahan
And drove him drunk or sober through the dawn
From somewhere in the neighbouring cottages.
Caught by an old man’s juggleries
He stumbled, tumbled, fumbled to and fro
And had but broken knees for hire
And horrible splendour of desire;
I thought it all out twenty years ago:

Good fellows shuffled cards in an old bawn;
And when that ancient ruffian’s turn was on
He so bewitched the cards under his thumb
That all, but the one card, became
A pack of hounds and not a pack of cards,
And that he changed into a hare.
Hanrahan rose in frenzy there
And followed up those baying creatures towards--

O towards I have forgotten what--enough!
I must recall a man that neither love
Nor music nor an enemy’s clipped ear
Could, he was so harried, cheer;
A figure that has grown so fabulous
There’s not a neighbour left to say
When he finished his dog’s day:
An ancient bankrupt master of this house.

Before that ruin came, for centuries,
Rough men-at-arms, cross-gartered to the knees
Or shod in iron, climbed the narrow stairs,
And certain men-at-arms there were
Whose images, in the Great Memory stored,
Come with loud cry and panting breast
To break upon a sleeper’s rest
While their great wooden dice beat on the board.

As I would question all, come all who can;
Come old, necessitous, half-mounted man;
And bring beauty’s blind rambling celebrant;
The red man the juggler sent
Through God-forsaken meadows; Mrs. French,
Gifted with so fine an ear;
The man drowned in a bog’s mire,
When mocking muses chose the country wench.

Did all old men and women, rich and poor,
Who trod upon these rocks or passed this door,
Whether in public or in secret rage
As I do now against old age?
But I have found an answer in those eyes
That are impatient to be gone;
Go therefore; but leave Hanrahan
For I need all his mighty memories.

Old lecher with a love on every wind
Bring up out of that deep considering mind
All that you have discovered in the grave,
For it is certain that you have
Reckoned up every unforeknown, unseeing
Plunge, lured by a softening eye,
Or by a touch or a sigh,
Into the labyrinth of another’s being;

Does the imagination dwell the most
Upon a woman won or woman lost?
If on the lost, admit you turned aside
From a great labyrinth out of pride,
Cowardice, some silly over-subtle thought
Or anything called conscience once;
And that if memory recur, the sun’s
Under eclipse and the day blotted out.

III

It is time that I wrote my will;
I choose upstanding men,
That climb the streams until
The fountain leap, and at dawn
Drop their cast at the side
Of dripping stone; I declare
They shall inherit my pride,
The pride of people that were
Bound neither to Cause nor to State,
Neither to slaves that were spat on,
Nor to the tyrants that spat,
The people of Burke and of Grattan
That gave, though free to refuse--
Pride, like that of the morn,
When the headlong light is loose,
Or that of the fabulous horn,
Or that of the sudden shower
When all streams are dry,
Or that of the hour
When the swan must fix his eye
Upon a fading gleam,
Float out upon a long
Last reach of glittering stream
And there sing his last song.
And I declare my faith;
I mock Plotinus’ thought
And cry in Plato’s teeth,
Death and life were not
Till man made up the whole,
Made lock, stock and barrel
Out of his bitter soul,
Aye, sun and moon and star, all,
And further add to that
That, being dead, we rise,
Dream and so create
Translunar Paradise.
I have prepared my peace
With learned Italian things
And the proud stones of Greece,
Poet’s imaginings
And memories of love,
Memories of the words of women,
All those things whereof
Man makes a superhuman,
Mirror-resembling dream.

As at the loophole there,
The daws chatter and scream,
And drop twigs layer upon layer.
When they have mounted up,
The mother bird will rest
On their hollow top,
And so warm her wild nest.

I leave both faith and pride
To young upstanding men
Climbing the mountain side,
That under bursting dawn
They may drop a fly;
Being of that metal made
Till it was broken by
This sedentary trade.

Now shall I make my soul
Compelling it to study
In a learned school
Till the wreck of body
Slow decay of blood,
Testy delirium
Or dull decrepitude,
Or what worse evil come--
The death of friends, or death
Of every brilliant eye
That made a catch in the breath--
Seem but the clouds of the sky
When the horizon fades;
Or a bird’s sleepy cry
Among the deepening shades.

Codex Note

Title poem of the 1928 collection, in three parts and set at Thoor Ballylee. Yeats faces old age against the strength of the imagination, and in the last part writes his 'will', leaving his pride and faith to the young.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening: old age as absurdity Part I, ll. 1-4

    Yeats wrote 'The Tower' at Thoor Ballylee, the Norman tower near Gort, Co. Galway, that he had bought in 1917; in the 1928 volume it is dated 1926, and Jeffares places its drafting in 1925, when Yeats turned sixty. Part I sets out the quarrel the whole poem tries to settle: the body is 'decrepit', yet the imagination has never been more 'excited, passionate, fantastical'. The comparison of age to something tied to a dog's tail is deliberately undignified, and it returns at the end of the stanza as 'a sort of battered kettle at the heel', the tin can that boys tie to a dog. The poem as a whole moves from complaint (I), through a summoning of local ghosts to question them (II), to a testament (III).

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'

  2. Ben Bulben Part I, l. 9

    Ben Bulben (Irish Binn Ghulbain) dominates the country around Sligo town, where Yeats spent long childhood summers with his mother's family, the Pollexfens. Recalling himself fishing its streams 'with rod and fly, / Or the humbler worm', he sets the boy's unlimited summer day against the old man's limited time. The mountain was to become Yeats's emblem of home and of the legendary west: his late poem 'Under Ben Bulben' (1938) names it in its title and ends with the instructions for his grave at nearby Drumcliff. The fishing image also prepares the will in Part III, where the heirs are men who climb the streams at dawn and 'drop their cast'.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; W. B. Yeats, Reveries over Childhood and Youth (1916), on the Sligo years

  3. Plato and Plotinus Part I, l. 12

    Plato (c. 427-347 BC) and the Neoplatonist Plotinus (AD 204/5-270) represent the life of argument and 'abstract things' that age seems to impose: both taught that true reality lies beyond the senses, so that the body and its passions are a prison to escape. Yeats had been reading Stephen MacKenna's translation of Plotinus's Enneads (1917-30). Part I accepts this programme only grudgingly ('It seems that I must'), and Part III reverses it: 'I mock Plotinus' thought / And cry in Plato's teeth'. Yeats's own note later admitted that Plotinus himself had said something close to Yeats's position, that soul is the maker of all things.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; Plotinus, The Enneads, trans. Stephen MacKenna (London: Medici Society, 1917-30), V.1.2; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  4. battered kettle at the heel Part I, l. 16

    The image completes the comparison at l. 4, where age is tied to the poet 'as to a dog's tail'. Tying a kettle or tin can to a dog's tail was a common boys' cruelty, and the noise made the dog ridiculous as it ran. Yeats offers two options, both humiliating: either give up imagination for philosophy, or be mocked by the clattering presence of his own decrepitude. The colloquial bathos is typical of the later Yeats, who mixes grand rhetoric with plain and even comic diction; compare the 'tattered coat upon a stick' of 'Sailing to Byzantium', written in the same period.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'

  5. Mrs. French Part II, l. 9

    Yeats's note identifies her as a Mrs French who lived at Peterswell in the eighteenth century and was related to Sir Jonah Barrington, the Irish judge and memoirist (1760-1834), who told the story of the ears and the trouble it caused in his Personal Sketches of His Own Times (1827-32). The anecdote shows the old Ascendancy world of candlesticks, mahogany and wine and its casual violence: the servant 'divines' an unspoken wish and acts on it. Yeats tells it with a cool, almost comic precision ('a little covered dish'). When she returns in the roll-call she is 'Gifted with so fine an ear', a grim pun on the clipped ears.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; Sir Jonah Barrington, Personal Sketches of His Own Times (London, 1827-32); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  6. the peasant girl (Mary Hynes) Part II, ll. 17-24

    Yeats's note names the beauty and the poet as Mary Hynes and Raftery. Antoine Ó Raifteirí (c. 1779-1835), a blind itinerant poet of Co. Mayo and Galway, made an Irish song in praise of Mary Hynes of Ballylee. Yeats had collected stories of both from old people near Gort in the 1890s and printed them in 'Dust Hath Closed Helen's Eye' in The Celtic Twilight, with local memories of her beauty. The stanza shows how song gives glory: farmers 'jostled at the fair' just to see her. The country girl thus becomes a local Helen, and the next stanzas extend the parallel.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; W. B. Yeats, The Celtic Twilight (1893; rev. 1902), 'Dust Hath Closed Helen's Eye'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  7. the bog of Cloone Part II, l. 32

    The story is in The Celtic Twilight ('Dust Hath Closed Helen's Eye'): Yeats records that men drinking and talking of Mary Hynes set out to see her, and that one was drowned in the bog of Cloone. In the poem the men 'mistook the brightness of the moon / For the prosaic light of day'. Moon and sun are a key pair in Yeats: moonlight stands for imagination and subjective vision, daylight for practical, objective fact. The drowned man is the first of the poem's victims of imagination, and his fate sharpens the question of whether poetry's power to 'make men mad' is a triumph or a curse.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; W. B. Yeats, The Celtic Twilight (1893; rev. 1902), 'Dust Hath Closed Helen's Eye'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  8. Homer and Helen Part II, ll. 33-37

    Ancient tradition held that Homer was blind (the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, 172, describes its singer as a blind man of Chios). Raftery, also blind, praised a beauty who brought men to ruin; Homer sang of Helen, whose flight with Paris brought Greeks and Trojans to ten years of war. Yeats finds 'nothing strange' in the blind poet's power over sight, because the tragedy of imagination began with Homer. 'Helen has all living hearts betrayed' recalls Yeats's lifelong identification of Maud Gonne with Helen ('No Second Troy', 'The Rose of the World'). In Iliad 3 the Trojan elders, seeing Helen on the walls, admit that men might well suffer for such a woman; Yeats's Celtic Twilight cites the scene in the same connection.

    Homer, Iliad 3.146-60 (the elders on the wall); Homeric Hymn to Apollo 172; W. B. Yeats, The Celtic Twilight (1893; rev. 1902), 'Dust Hath Closed Helen's Eye'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  9. 'O may the moon and sunlight seem / One inextricable beam' Part II, ll. 38-40

    The lines turn the drowned man's mistake into a wish. If the poet triumphs, his songs will be as powerful as Raftery's and so as dangerous; Yeats prays that the moonlight of vision and the sunlight of the prosaic world might become 'one inextricable beam', so that imagination does not lead men astray but becomes their reality. Readers divide on the tone: some hear a proud acceptance of the poet's maddening power, others a troubled awareness of its cost. The sun/moon opposition belongs to the system Yeats was building in A Vision (1925), where solar and lunar stand for objective and subjective natures.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'; W. B. Yeats, A Vision (London: privately printed, 1925)

  10. Red Hanrahan Part II, ll. 41-56

    Owen Hanrahan the Red is a hedge-schoolmaster and poet modelled partly on the eighteenth-century Munster poet Eoghan Rua Ó Súilleabháin. Yeats wrote his stories in the 1890s ('twenty years ago', roughly) for The Secret Rose (1897) and rewrote them with Lady Gregory as Stories of Red Hanrahan (1904). Yeats's note says the chase of the phantom hare and hounds comes from that book: in a barn an old man bewitches a pack of cards, which turn into hounds and a hare, and Hanrahan runs after them and is lost to his love. Including his own invention among real local figures shows that the creatures of imagination are as real to the poet as history.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; W. B. Yeats, Stories of Red Hanrahan (Dundrum: Dun Emer Press, 1904); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  11. bawn Part II, l. 49

    The OED derives bawn from Irish bódhún (cow-fort), a fortified enclosure for cattle, and in Irish English it came to mean the yard or outbuilding attached to a house. Yeats uses the Irish word for the barn where, in the Hanrahan story, the 'good fellows shuffled cards'. The rhyme of 'bawn' with 'on' is an approximate one, typical of the half-rhymes in Part II's eight-line stanzas, rhymed aabbcddc. The stanza ends on a dash in mid-sentence ('towards--'), and the next stanza breaks off: Yeats theatrically 'forgets' where Hanrahan was going.

    Oxford English Dictionary; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  12. the ancient bankrupt master Part II, ll. 57-64

    Yeats's note says the bankrupt man lived at the castle about a hundred years earlier; one legend held that he could leave only on Sundays, when debtors could not be arrested, and another that he hid in the secret passage. He is the figure of the house itself in decline, so 'harried' that none of the poem's earlier pleasures (love, music, the 'enemy's clipped ear') could please him. 'When he finished his dog's day' picks up the dog of Part I. He returns in the roll-call as the 'old, necessitous, half-mounted man', poor and only half a gentleman.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  13. cross-gartered Part II, l. 66

    Before the bankrupt master, 'for centuries', the tower was a fighting house of the de Burgos, and its men-at-arms climbed the narrow stair either with cross-gartered legs or 'shod in iron'. The word is best known from Twelfth Night, where Malvolio is tricked into appearing 'cross-gartered' in yellow stockings (II.v, III.iv), already an old-fashioned style. Yeats's note adds that the ghosts had been seen playing dice in what was now his bedroom: the 'great wooden dice' beat on the board and break a sleeper's rest.

    William Shakespeare, Twelfth Night, II.v and III.iv; W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; Oxford English Dictionary

  14. the Great Memory Part II, l. 69

    Yeats believed, from his studies in Neoplatonism, theosophy and spiritualism, that individual memories belong to one great memory of nature or of the race. In the essay 'Magic' (1901) he set out the doctrine that 'the borders of our memories are shifting' and that our minds can be evoked by symbols to reveal a single great mind and memory; in Per Amica Silentia Lunae (1918) he called it Anima Mundi, the soul of the world, drawing on Henry More. The ghosts of the men-at-arms are therefore not mere fancies: their images are 'stored' and can come back with 'loud cry and panting breast'.

    W. B. Yeats, 'Magic', in Ideas of Good and Evil (London, 1903); W. B. Yeats, Per Amica Silentia Lunae (London: Macmillan, 1918), 'Anima Mundi'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  15. The roll-call of ghosts Part II, ll. 73-80

    This stanza gathers the cast: the 'half-mounted man' (the bankrupt master), 'beauty's blind rambling celebrant' (Raftery), 'the red man the juggler sent' (Red Hanrahan, sent off by the old conjuror), Mrs French with her 'fine ear', the drowned man, and the 'country wench' (Mary Hynes) whom 'mocking muses' chose for praise. The epithets compress whole stories into a phrase, and the formal summons recalls the Homeric nekuia, Odysseus calling up the dead in Odyssey 11. The question, in the next stanza, is whether all old men and women have raged against age as he does; their eyes, 'impatient to be gone', answer yes, and he dismisses all but Hanrahan.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'; Homer, Odyssey 11 (the summoning of the dead)

  16. half-mounted Part II, l. 74

    Jonah Barrington, the source of the Mrs French story, divided the Irish gentry of his day into three ranks, of which the lowest were the 'half-mounted gentlemen', men of small means who kept up some appearance of gentility. Yeats applies the label to the bankrupt master: 'old, necessitous' (needy) and only half-mounted, a gentleman in decay. The term fits the poem's interest in a social order that is passing, and in Part III Yeats will claim the pride of the Anglo-Irish Protestant tradition at its best.

    Sir Jonah Barrington, Personal Sketches of His Own Times (London, 1827-32), on 'half-mounted gentlemen'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  17. 'Old lecher' and the labyrinth Part II, ll. 89-104

    Hanrahan, the poet-wanderer with 'a love on every wind', has explored 'the labyrinth of another's being', the maze of erotic intimacy. Yeats asks which haunts the imagination more, the woman won or the woman lost, and answers his own question with self-accusation: if the lost, one must admit having turned aside from the labyrinth out of pride, cowardice, over-subtle thought or 'conscience'. Most critics read this as Yeats's confession about Maud Gonne, whom he courted for decades without success, and perhaps about his hesitations before marriage; memory of that refusal eclipses the sun. The labyrinth, from the Cretan maze of Daedalus, recurs in Yeats as an image of the soul's winding path.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'; Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.152-68 (the labyrinth of Daedalus)

  18. The will (Part III) Part III, ll. 1-24

    After the long eight-line stanzas of Part II, Part III runs in brisk lines of three stresses, loosely rhymed, as if the speaker had reached a resolution. The conceit is a legal will. The heirs are 'upstanding men' who fish mountain streams at dawn, recalling the boy on Ben Bulben in Part I: Yeats leaves his inheritance to the active, physical life his 'sedentary trade' has broken in him. The pride he bequeaths is then defined by a series of natural images: the morning, the 'fabulous horn', the sudden shower, the swan's last song.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'

  19. Burke and Grattan Part III, ll. 9-13

    Edmund Burke (1729-97), political philosopher, and Henry Grattan (1746-1820), leader of the Irish Parliament that won legislative independence in 1782, were heroes of the Anglo-Irish Protestant tradition that Yeats increasingly championed in the 1920s. In his Senate speech on divorce (11 June 1925) he used almost the same phrase: 'We are the people of Burke; we are the people of Grattan'. The pride he describes belongs to people bound 'neither to Cause nor to State', neither to the oppressed nor to oppressors, and who 'gave, though free to refuse': an aristocratic ideal of free generosity. Critics have debated how far this idealises the Ascendancy.

    The Senate Speeches of W. B. Yeats, ed. Donald R. Pearce (Bloomington: Indiana UP, 1960), speech of 11 June 1925; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  20. the fabulous horn Part III, l. 16

    Yeats does not say which horn he means, and commentators have not agreed. One reading takes it as the cornucopia, the horn of the goat Amalthea that overflowed with plenty, matching the image of free giving in 'gave, though free to refuse'. Another hears a hero's horn, such as Roland's, or the horn of Fionn and the Fianna in Irish legend, which fits the heroic sense of pride and the sounds of dawn and hunting around it. 'Fabulous' means belonging to fable or legend. Students should note that the image works by sound and suggestion more than by a single reference.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'; Ovid, Fasti 5.115-28 (the horn of Amalthea)

  21. the swan's last song Part III, ll. 19-24

    The belief that swans sing most beautifully just before they die is ancient: in Plato's Phaedo Socrates says swans sing at death not from grief but from joy at going to their god. Yeats makes the dying swan the last image of pride, floating out on a 'glittering stream' to sing. His own note confesses an unconscious debt to 'one of the loveliest lyrics of our time', Thomas Sturge Moore's 'The Dying Swan', which he had often recited on an American lecture tour. Swans are central symbols in Yeats ('The Wild Swans at Coole', 'Leda and the Swan'), and the image points to the poet's own final song.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; Plato, Phaedo 84e-85b; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  22. 'I mock Plotinus' thought' Part III, ll. 25-36

    Reversing Part I, Yeats rejects the Neoplatonic doctrine that the material world flows down from a higher reality. Instead, 'Death and life were not / Till man made up the whole': the human imagination creates everything, 'sun and moon and star', and even after death 'we rise, / Dream and so create / Translunar Paradise'. His note admits he forgot that Plotinus himself wrote that soul is 'the author of all living things' and 'the maker of the sun' (Enneads V.1.2). The quarrel is therefore more with a popular, transcendental reading of Plato and Plotinus than with Plotinus himself, as several critics observe.

    W. B. Yeats, The Tower (London: Macmillan, 1928), author's Notes to 'The Tower'; Plotinus, The Enneads, trans. Stephen MacKenna, V.1.2; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  23. Translunar Paradise Part III, l. 36

    In ancient and medieval cosmology the region below the moon (sublunary) was the realm of change and decay, and what lay beyond the moon was changeless and divine. 'Translunar' (OED: beyond the moon; also used figuratively of the ethereal) here names the heaven the dead create by dreaming, in keeping with the doctrine of the dreaming-back of souls in A Vision. Michael Drayton had used 'translunary' of Marlowe's imagination ('brave translunary things'), and Yeats's paradise likewise belongs to the imagination. The line completes the faith: man makes not only this world but the next.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Michael Drayton, 'To Henry Reynolds, of Poets and Poesy' (1627); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'

  24. the daws' nest Part III, ll. 46-53

    Jackdaws (daws) nest in old towers and chimneys. At the arrow-slit or 'loophole' of the tower they pile twig on twig until a mother bird can rest on the 'hollow top' and warm her nest. The image comes in the middle of the will as an emblem of how a tradition is built: by slow accumulation, layer on layer, until it makes a shelter for new life. It also answers the poet's own 'peace', built from 'learned Italian things' and 'the proud stones of Greece'. Compare 'The Stare's Nest by My Window' in 'Meditations in Time of Civil War', where bees build in the loosening masonry of the same tower.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'

  25. 'Now shall I make my soul' Part III, ll. 62-76

    'To make one's soul' is an old devotional expression, used in Irish and Catholic speech, for preparing oneself for death. Yeats gives it a secular sense: he will compel his soul to study 'in a learned school', recalling the 'singing school' of 'Sailing to Byzantium', until bodily decay, delirium, decrepitude and even the death of friends seem no more than clouds at a fading horizon. The ending modulates from the defiant tone of Part I to quiet acceptance, closing on 'a bird's sleepy cry / Among the deepening shades', where 'shades' means both evening shadows and the ghosts of the dead.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to 'The Tower'; Daniel Albright, ed., W. B. Yeats: The Poems (London: Everyman/Dent, 1990), notes to 'The Tower'; Oxford English Dictionary
