The Stolen Child

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Wanderings of Oisin and Other Poems (1889)

Mythology & FolkloreNature

Where dips the rocky highland
Of Sleuth Wood in the lake,
There lies a leafy island
Where flapping herons wake
The drowsy water rats;
There we've hid our faery vats,
Full of berries,
And of reddest stolen cherries.
Come away, O human child!
To the waters and the wild
With a faery, hand in hand,
For the world's more full of weeping than you can understand.

Where the wave of moonlight glosses
The dim gray sands with light,
Far off by furthest Rosses
We foot it all the night,
Weaving olden dances,
Mingling hands and mingling glances
Till the moon has taken flight;
To and fro we leap
And chase the frothy bubbles,
While the world is full of troubles
And is anxious in its sleep.
Come away, O human child!
To the waters and the wild
With a faery, hand in hand,
For the world's more full of weeping than you can understand.

Where the wandering water gushes
From the hills above Glen-Car,
In pools among the rushes
That scarce could bathe a star,
We seek for slumbering trout
And whispering in their ears
Give them unquiet dreams;
Leaning softly out
From ferns that drop their tears
Over the young streams,
Come away, O human child!
To the waters and the wild
With a faery, hand in hand,
For the world's more full of weeping than you can understand.

Away with us he's going,
The solemn-eyed:
He'll hear no more the lowing
Of the calves on the warm hillside
Or the kettle on the hob
Sing peace into his breast,
Or see the brown mice bob
Round and round the oatmeal-chest.
For he comes, the human child,
To the waters and the wild
With a faery, hand in hand,
From a world more full of weeping than he can understand.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The faeries call a human child away to the waters and the wild, since 'the world's more full of weeping than you can understand'. The refrain follows each stanza.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its date and its Sligo setting l. 1

    Yeats was twenty-one when he wrote it, and it became one of his first poems to be widely liked; it was reprinted in Poems and Ballads of Young Ireland (1888) and placed among the changeling stories of his anthology Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (1888). His note to the first printing says that all the places named lie round about Sligo, where he spent much of his childhood with his mother's family, the Pollexfens. Each of the first three stanzas maps a real spot (Sleuth Wood, the Rosses, Glencar) on to faery activity, so the supernatural world is given an exact local geography. The speakers throughout are the faeries themselves; the human voice never answers.

    Yeats's note to the first printing of 'The Stolen Child', Irish Monthly (December 1886), as quoted in Lapham's Quarterly; Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach, eds., The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats (New York: Macmillan, 1957), entry for the poem (first printings and variants); W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg)

  2. Sleuth Wood l. 2

    The wood is Slish Wood, which runs along the steep south side of Lough Gill, the lake in which Yeats also set 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree'. Lough Gill holds some twenty small islands, so the 'leafy island' need not be any particular one, though local guides like to identify it. The spelling 'Sleuth' is probably an approximation of local pronunciation, and it suits the metre; it also lends the place a faintly furtive sound appropriate to hiding stolen goods. The opening phrase 'Where dips the rocky highland' inverts normal word order, giving the poem a ballad-like, storytelling start.

    'Lough Gill', Wikipedia (Slish Wood and the lake's islands)

  3. faery vats ... stolen cherries l. 6

    Vats are large vessels, usually for brewing or dyeing; here they hold berries, perhaps fermenting into faery wine. The detail of 'reddest stolen cherries' is light-hearted, but it introduces the idea that the faeries take what belongs to humans, which the title and the final stanza develop into the theft of a child. Irish folk belief made the 'good people' habitual thieves of milk, butter and the goodness of crops, and Yeats's anthology of 1888 records many such tales. The playful petty theft therefore prepares the reader for a much graver one.

    W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  4. The refrain l. 9

    Each stanza builds through short, rapid lines describing the faeries' haunts and ends in the same summons. The refrain's final line ('For the world's more full of weeping than you can understand') is much longer and slower than anything around it, and its weight shifts the poem from enchantment to sorrow: the faeries' best argument is not their own delight but the misery of the human world. In the last stanza Yeats alters the refrain, changing the summons into a report ('For he comes ... From a world more full of weeping than he can understand'), so that the invitation has been accepted and the child is spoken of, not to.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, Poems (London: T. Fisher Unwin, 'Every Irishman's Library' issue), text and Yeats's preface on the dates of Crossways (1889) and The Rose (1892) (Project Gutenberg)

  5. the world's more full of weeping l. 12

    The line can be read two ways. Spoken by the faeries it is a seduction, offering release from grief; read against the last stanza it becomes ironic, since the human world the child leaves turns out to hold the warm, homely things the faery world lacks. Many readers see here a pattern that runs through Yeats's early poetry, the longing to flee the real world into a dream or faery realm, together with an awareness of what that flight costs. The phrasing 'than you can understand' also stresses that a child cannot weigh the bargain: the choice is made for him.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg)

  6. glosses l. 13

    'Gloss' as a verb here means to give a shining surface to something (from the noun 'gloss', lustre), not to comment on a text. The waves catch moonlight and spread it over 'the dim gray sands', a picture of the strand at Rosses. The sound of the stanza (glosses / Rosses, dances / glances) is deliberately incantatory, with internal and feminine rhymes that imitate the circling dance it describes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gloss'; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  7. furthest Rosses l. 15

    Yeats spent summer holidays at Rosses Point with his Middleton cousins. In his note to the first printing he explained that the Rosses was a celebrated faery place, with a small point of rocks where anyone who fell asleep risked waking 'silly', because the faeries had carried off his soul. That belief bears directly on the poem: to be taken by the faeries is to lose one's wits or soul while the body remains, which is the logic of the changeling. Yeats returned to the place in 'Drumcliff and Rosses' in The Celtic Twilight.

    Yeats's note to the first printing of 'The Stolen Child', Irish Monthly (December 1886), as quoted in Lapham's Quarterly; 'Rosses Point', Wikipedia

  8. foot it l. 16

    'To foot it' means to dance (the 'it' is idiomatic, as in 'trip it'). The phrase has a strong literary pedigree in faery and spirit contexts: Ariel's song in The Tempest invites the spirits to 'Foot it featly here and there' (I.ii), and Milton's L'Allegro asks Mirth to 'trip it as ye go'. Yeats's faeries dance 'olden dances' all night, the traditional pastime of the trooping faeries, who in Irish lore dance in rings on strands and hills.

    William Shakespeare, The Tempest, I.ii, Ariel's song 'Come unto these yellow sands' (Folger Shakespeare); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'foot'; W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg)

  9. the world ... anxious in its sleep l. 22

    The contrast between faery play and human anxiety is the poem's central opposition. The faeries 'leap / And chase the frothy bubbles', a deliberately trivial activity, while the world is 'anxious in its sleep'. The faeries' freedom from care is attractive but also weightless; they have no troubles because they have no responsibilities or affections, a point the last stanza makes by listing the human attachments the child will lose.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  10. Glen-Car l. 29

    Glencar Lough lies in a steep valley under the Dartry mountains, and its waterfall, fed by streams from the hills, is one of the best-known beauty spots of the district. Yeats's 'wandering water' that 'gushes / From the hills above Glen-Car' is that cascade. The pools 'That scarce could bathe a star' are so small that they could hold only the reflection of a single star, a delicate image of scale that makes the faeries tiny presences in a vast landscape.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  11. Give them unquiet dreams l. 34

    This is the most unsettling detail in the faeries' self-portrait. They seek out 'slumbering trout' only to disturb them, the same thing they do to the 'world ... anxious in its sleep'. Read closely, the stanza shows the faeries as mischievous and even cruel, so their promise of escape from weeping is not entirely to be trusted. The image of ferns 'that drop their tears / Over the young streams' quietly reintroduces weeping into the faery world itself.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg)

  12. The changeling belief l. 42

    Yeats's introduction to the 'Changelings' section of his 1888 anthology explains that the faeries carry mortals off to their country, most often children, leaving a sickly faery or a bewitched stock that seems to pine and die. He printed 'The Stolen Child' in that very section. The final stanza enacts the abduction: the voice switches from second person ('you') to third ('he'), as if the child has already passed out of human hearing. The poem never says the child is unhappy, which is what makes it eerie.

    W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  13. The solemn-eyed l. 43

    The phrase is the only description of the child in the poem. 'Solemn-eyed' suggests the gravity of very young children, but in the context of changeling lore it can also suggest a child who already seems 'away', marked for the other world. Giving the epithet a line to itself, after the jaunty 'Away with us he's going', slows the stanza at the moment the theft happens.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, ed., Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (London: Walter Scott, 1888), section 'Changelings' (Project Gutenberg)

  14. the kettle on the hob ... the oatmeal-chest l. 46

    The last stanza lists what the child will never again hear or see: calves lowing on the hillside, the kettle singing on the hob (the ledge beside a hearth where pans were kept warm), brown mice running round the oatmeal-chest (the bin of meal for porridge). These small domestic details are the warmest in the poem, and their placement after three stanzas of faery glamour reverses the poem's argument: the 'world ... full of weeping' also contains 'peace' sung 'into his breast'. Many critics take this as the poem's real centre, a sign that even the young Yeats felt the price of escape.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hob'

  15. The altered refrain l. 50

    Yeats changes three things in the final refrain. 'Come away' becomes 'For he comes'; 'the world's' becomes 'a world', distancing it; and 'you' becomes 'he'. The grammar records the success of the faeries' call. The result is a quiet shock, since after three stanzas of persuasion the reader learns that the child's choice has already been made, without his ever speaking.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, Poems (London: T. Fisher Unwin, 'Every Irishman's Library' issue), text and Yeats's preface on the dates of Crossways (1889) and The Rose (1892) (Project Gutenberg)
