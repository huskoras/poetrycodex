Love

When You Are Old

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Rose (1893)

LoveTime & Brevity

When you are old and gray and full of sleep,
And nodding by the fire, take down this book,
And slowly read, and dream of the soft look
Your eyes had once, and of their shadows deep;

How many loved your moments of glad grace,
And loved your beauty will love false or true;
But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you,
And loved the sorrows of your changing face.

And bending down beside the glowing bars
Murmur, a little sadly, how love fled
And paced upon the mountains overhead
And hid his face amid a crowd of stars.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

An address to a woman who will one day be old and grey beside the fire, asking her to remember that one man loved 'the pilgrim soul in you'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ronsard's 'Quand vous serez bien vieille' l. 1

    Ronsard's sonnet in the second book of the Sonnets pour Helene pictures Helene in old age, sitting by the fire at candlelight spinning, reciting his verses and marvelling that Ronsard praised her when she was beautiful; it ends with the advice to gather life's roses today. Yeats keeps the fireside scene and the book of poems but drops both the boast that the poet's verse makes the lady famous and the carpe diem conclusion. Where Ronsard's speaker threatens her with regret, Yeats's is gentler and sadder, asking her only to remember one man's kind of love.

    Pierre de Ronsard, Sonnets pour Helene, II (1578), 'Quand vous serez bien vieille' (Wikisource); W. B. Yeats, Poems (London: T. Fisher Unwin, 'Every Irishman's Library' issue), text and Yeats's preface on the dates of Crossways (1889) and The Rose (1892) (Project Gutenberg)

  2. Occasion: Maud Gonne, 1891 l. 1

    Yeats met Maud Gonne in January 1889 and fell in love with her; in 1891 he proposed for the first time and was refused. The poem belongs to that year and was first published in The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics (1892), whose lyrics were later grouped as The Rose. It imagines a future in which the beautiful young woman is old and sleepy by the fire, and the image of the woman 'full of sleep' is the poem's first move: her present vitality is seen from a distance of decades.

    W. B. Yeats, Poems (London: T. Fisher Unwin, 'Every Irishman's Library' issue), text and Yeats's preface on the dates of Crossways (1889) and The Rose (1892) (Project Gutenberg); Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach, eds., The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats (New York: Macmillan, 1957), entry for the poem (first printings and variants)

  3. this book l. 2

    As in Ronsard, the poem includes itself in its scene: the old woman takes down 'this book' and reads. The device makes the poem a message sent forward in time. It also lets Yeats imply a quiet reproach, since the book records a love she did not return, without stating it. The slow reading ('slowly read, and dream') sets the meditative pace of the whole poem.

    Pierre de Ronsard, Sonnets pour Helene, II (1578), 'Quand vous serez bien vieille' (Wikisource); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  4. Form: three quatrains l. 4

    Ronsard wrote a sonnet; Yeats shortens the form to twelve lines, three quatrains rhymed abba cddc effe. The envelope rhyme gives each stanza a closed, self-contained movement, and the poem is a single extended instruction ('take down ... read ... dream ... Murmur') stretched across all three. Missing the expected two lines of a sonnet, the poem ends on an image rather than an epigram, appropriately for a love that has fled.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); Pierre de Ronsard, Sonnets pour Helene, II (1578), 'Quand vous serez bien vieille' (Wikisource)

  5. moments of glad grace l. 5

    'Grace' here means charm and elegance of movement, with a faint religious undertone that the later 'pilgrim soul' picks up. The word 'moments' matters: the many admirers loved fleeting qualities, while the one man loved something lasting. The alliteration of 'glad grace' gives the line a lightness that the next stanza's 'sorrows' will counter. The adjective 'glad' also characterises the young woman as joyous, which makes the later picture of her 'a little sadly' murmuring by the fire the more poignant.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grace'; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  6. 'will love' or 'with love'? l. 6

    Yeats's own collected texts (for example the 1908 Collected Works) print 'And loved your beauty with love false or true': many men loved her beauty, some sincerely and some not. The reading 'will love', found in the archive's source edition, makes no grammatical sense and appears to be a misprint or transcription error. Students should quote 'with'. The line's real point, the contrast between love of beauty and love of the soul, depends on 'with love', which balances the many lovers against the 'one man' of the next line.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, Poems (London: T. Fisher Unwin, 'Every Irishman's Library' issue), text and Yeats's preface on the dates of Crossways (1889) and The Rose (1892) (Project Gutenberg); Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach, eds., The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats (New York: Macmillan, 1957), entry for the poem (first printings and variants)

  7. the pilgrim soul l. 7

    A pilgrim travels toward a holy place, and Christian writing commonly treats the soul as a pilgrim on earth ('strangers and pilgrims', 1 Peter 2:11). Applied to Maud Gonne, the phrase suggests her restlessness and her devotion to causes, especially Irish nationalism. It is the poem's central claim: other men loved what changes (beauty, grace), the speaker loved what journeys and endures. The phrase has become one of Yeats's most quoted.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Peter 2:11; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  8. the sorrows of your changing face l. 8

    The line answers 'loved your beauty' with a love that includes ageing and sorrow, which is why it suits a poem addressed to the woman in old age. 'Changing' looks both back to her shifting expressions and forward to the old face of the first stanza. The quatrain thus moves from beauty to soul to sorrow, a progression from surface to depth.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  9. the glowing bars l. 9

    The bars are those of the grate that holds the coals. The detail returns the poem to the fireside of the first line and frames the poem's movement: she reads by the fire, looks up into memory, and then bends to the fire again. The glow of the bars is answered by the stars of the last line, a shift from domestic to cosmic light.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bar'; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  10. Murmur, a little sadly l. 10

    The verb continues the sentence begun with 'take down this book', so the whole poem is a set of instructions to the future woman. 'A little sadly' is an understatement typical of the poem's restraint: the speaker does not ask her to regret bitterly, only to feel a slight sorrow. Some readers hear a reproach beneath the restraint, others a generous release.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  11. Love fled ... hid his face amid a crowd of stars l. 10

    The image recalls the Petrarchan god of Love who retreats and hides: in Petrarch's Rime 140, translated by Wyatt ('The longe love') and Surrey ('Love that doth reign'), Love, rebuked by the lady, flees back into the heart and hides there, no longer showing himself ('ivi s'asconde, et non appar piu fore'). Yeats sends Love not inward but upward, so the hiding becomes a sublime dispersal. Readers differ over whether 'love' means the speaker's love, which has withdrawn into the distance, or Love itself, which the woman let go. Either way the ending turns a personal loss into a cosmic image, and 'hid his face' also echoes the biblical idiom for divine withdrawal.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere (Rerum vulgarium fragmenta) 140, 'Amor, che nel penser mio vive et regna', l. 11 (Wikisource); The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 13:1; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)
