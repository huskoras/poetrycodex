The Song of Wandering Aengus

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Wind Among the Reeds (1899)

Mythology & FolkloreLove

I went out to the hazel wood,
Because a fire was in my head,
And cut and peeled a hazel wand,
And hooked a berry to a thread;
And when white moths were on the wing,
And moth-like stars were flickering out,
I dropped the berry in a stream
And caught a little silver trout.

When I had laid it on the floor
I went to blow the fire a-flame,
But something rustled on the floor,
And someone called me by my name:
It had become a glimmering girl
With apple blossom in her hair
Who called me by my name and ran
And faded through the brightening air.

Though I am old with wandering
Through hollow lands and hilly lands,
I will find out where she has gone,
And kiss her lips and take her hands;
And walk among long dappled grass,
And pluck till time and times are done,
The silver apples of the moon,
The golden apples of the sun.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A wanderer catches a silver trout from a hazel stream, which becomes a glimmering girl who calls his name and vanishes. He grows old looking for her and the 'silver apples of the moon, the golden apples of the sun'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Aengus and the poem's origin l. 1

    Oengus mac ind Oc, one of the Tuatha De Danann ('the Tribes of the goddess Danu', as Yeats puts it), is a god of love and youth. In the Old Irish tale Aislinge Oenguso (The Dream of Oengus) he falls ill for love of a girl seen in a dream and searches Ireland for her, finding that she takes the shape of a swan in alternate years. Yeats's poem, first printed in The Sketch (4 August 1897) as 'A Mad Song' and retitled in The Wind Among the Reeds (1899), gives the god the same pattern of a vision and a lifelong quest. Yeats's own note says the poem was suggested by a Greek folk song, though he 'certainly thought' of Ireland and its spirits while writing.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg); 'The Song of Wandering Aengus', Wikipedia (first printing in The Sketch, 4 August 1897, as 'A Mad Song'); Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach, eds., The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats (New York: Macmillan, 1957), entry for the poem (first printings and variants)

  2. the hazel wood l. 1

    In a note on 'The Secret Rose' in The Wind Among the Reeds, Yeats says he put the Tree of Life into his 'Song of Mongan' 'under its common Irish form of a hazel'. Irish tradition also makes the hazel the tree of wisdom: in the tales of Fionn, hazelnuts falling into a sacred pool feed the salmon of knowledge. The speaker going out to a hazel wood is thus entering a place of vision and otherworldly knowledge. The setting also prepares the transformation of a fish, since hazel, water and fish belong together in this lore.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  3. a fire was in my head l. 2

    The phrase gives the speaker's motive for going out: an inner burning that cannot be quenched at home. The original title 'A Mad Song' suggests derangement, while the god of love's identity suggests passion; for Yeats the two are close, since visionary and erotic longing are fused in this volume. The 'fire' is answered later by the literal fire he tries to 'blow ... a-flame' at home, where the miracle happens.

    'The Song of Wandering Aengus', Wikipedia (first printing in The Sketch, 4 August 1897, as 'A Mad Song'); W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg)

  4. a hazel wand l. 3

    At the literal level he makes a rough rod from a hazel switch, strips the bark, and baits it with a berry on a thread, a boy's way of fishing. But hazel wands are also the traditional rods of diviners and magicians. In his note to 'Mongan laments the Change that has come upon him and his Beloved', Yeats wrote that the man in that poem who has a hazel wand may have been Aengus, Master of Love, which confirms that he associated the hazel wand with this god.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  5. white moths ... moth-like stars l. 6

    The repetition ties earth and sky together: the white moths of the hedgerow and the faint early stars are seen as the same kind of thing. 'Flickering out' here means flickering into view, coming out as stars do at nightfall, though it carries a hint of going out too. The hour between day and night is the classic time for meetings with the otherworld in Irish lore.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  6. a little silver trout l. 8

    Yeats's note to this poem begins by explaining that the Tribes of the goddess Danu can take every shape, and that those in the waters often take the shape of fish, adding Galway testimony about beings that come to boats as fishes or as beautiful women. The 'silver' colour anticipates the 'silver apples of the moon' at the end, so the trout already belongs to the lunar world the speaker will spend his life seeking.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg)

  7. a-flame l. 10

    'A-flame' is the old construction with 'a-' meaning 'in' or 'on', giving the line a folk-song flavour. The homely act of reviving the hearth fire contrasts with the uncanny rustle on the floor. The speaker is turning from the fish to the fire when the transformation happens, as if the otherworld comes when one looks away. The setting is a cottage with an earthen or flagged floor where the fish is laid, a deliberately humble scene for a divine visitation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aflame'; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  8. a glimmering girl l. 13

    'Glimmering' suggests a faint, unsteady light, appropriate to a being half of this world. Her calling the speaker by name is a mark of the otherworld lover, who knows the mortal before he knows her. She then runs and 'faded through the brightening air', vanishing at dawn, as faery visitants traditionally do. Yeats's note gives Galway testimony, including that of an old man cutting a hedge near Gort who told of seeing a girl picking nuts, with brown hair hanging over her shoulders, before she vanished, folklore that clearly fed the poem.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg); 'The Song of Wandering Aengus', Wikipedia (first printing in The Sketch, 4 August 1897, as 'A Mad Song')

  9. apple blossom in her hair l. 14

    The detail anticipates 'the silver apples of the moon': the girl comes from the orchard of the otherworld, and apple trees and branches are central to the Irish otherworld of the voyage tales. Biographical critics also connect the image with Maud Gonne, whom Yeats repeatedly pictured among blossoms: in 'The Arrow' (In the Seven Woods, 1903) she is 'Blossom pale' and pulls down 'the pale blossom / At the moth hour', the same dusk hour of moths as this poem's opening stanza. Yeats's later memoirs also describe her complexion as like apple blossom. The connection enriches the poem but is not needed to read it.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); Sheila O'Malley, 'Maud Gonne: "The Pilgrim Soul"' (quoting Yeats's recollection of her 'complexion like the blossom of apples')

  10. old with wandering l. 17

    The third stanza jumps over years. 'Hollow lands and hilly lands' is a ballad-like doublet covering the whole country (valleys and hills), and the voice is that of the god who, in the old tale, searches all Ireland for his dream-woman. The vow is confident ('I will find out where she has gone'), but the poem never shows the finding, and the future tense gives the ending its quality of unfulfilled desire.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); 'The Song of Wandering Aengus', Wikipedia (first printing in The Sketch, 4 August 1897, as 'A Mad Song')

  11. long dappled grass l. 21

    'Dappled' means marked with patches of different shades, here the light of sun and moon falling through leaves, or flowers scattered in the grass. The word brings together the light and dark that the final pair of lines will unite as moon and sun. Gerard Manley Hopkins used 'dappled' famously in 'Pied Beauty', but for Yeats the word belongs to the romantic vocabulary of the 1890s, softening the landscape of the otherworld. Walking 'among long dappled grass' is an image of leisure without end, the opposite of the weary 'wandering' of the stanza's opening.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dappled'; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  12. time and times l. 22

    The phrase recalls Daniel 7:25 and Revelation 12:14, where 'a time, and times, and the dividing of time' (or 'half a time') measures the period before the end of days. Yeats uses it to mean forever, to the end of time itself. Placed beside the gathering of apples, it turns the lovers' reunion into an eternal, apocalyptic act. The phrase also sounds like folk-tale repetition, so the biblical echo is absorbed into the poem's song-like manner.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Daniel 7:25; Revelation 12:14; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  13. The silver apples of the moon, / The golden apples of the sun l. 23

    Golden apples recall the apples of the Hesperides in Greek myth and the golden fruit of many otherworld tales; silver apples recall the silver branch of the Irish otherworld. Pairing moon and sun suggests a union of opposites (night and day, female and male, the imaginative and the active), the reconciliation that runs through Yeats's symbolism. The two-line close, with its parallel syntax, has made these lines among the best known in Yeats.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg)

  14. Form l. 18

    The short four-beat lines and plain syntax ('And ... And ... And') give the poem the feel of a folk song, which suits Yeats's claim that it was suggested by a Greek folk song. The stanzas move through three times: the night of the catch, the transformation at home, and the lifelong search. Rhymes fall mainly on the even lines, and the final couplet of parallel lines on moon and sun gives the close a refrain-like finality.

    W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); W. B. Yeats, The Wind Among the Reeds (4th edn, London: Elkin Mathews, 1903), Yeats's 'Notes' (Project Gutenberg)
