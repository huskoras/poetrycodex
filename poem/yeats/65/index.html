Time & Brevity

Among School Children

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Tower (1928)

Time & BrevityArts & Sciences

I

I walk through the long schoolroom questioning,
A kind old nun in a white hood replies;
The children learn to cipher and to sing,
To study reading-books and history,
To cut and sew, be neat in everything
In the best modern way--the children’s eyes
In momentary wonder stare upon
A sixty year old smiling public man.

II

I dream of a Ledæan body, bent
Above a sinking fire, a tale that she
Told of a harsh reproof, or trivial event
That changed some childish day to tragedy--
Told, and it seemed that our two natures blent
Into a sphere from youthful sympathy,
Or else, to alter Plato’s parable,
Into the yolk and white of the one shell.

III

And thinking of that fit of grief or rage
I look upon one child or t’other there
And wonder if she stood so at that age--
For even daughters of the swan can share
Something of every paddler’s heritage--
And had that colour upon cheek or hair
And thereupon my heart is driven wild:
She stands before me as a living child.

IV

Her present image floats in to the mind--
Did quattrocento finger fashion it
Hollow of cheek as though it drank the wind
And took a mass of shadows for its meat?
And I though never of Ledæan kind
Had pretty plumage once--enough of that,
Better to smile on all that smile, and show
There is a comfortable kind of old scarecrow.

V

What youthful mother, a shape upon her lap
Honey of generation had betrayed,
And that must sleep, shriek, struggle to escape
As recollection or the drug decide,
Would think her son, did she but see that shape
With sixty or more winters on its head,
A compensation for the pang of his birth,
Or the uncertainty of his setting forth?

VI

Plato thought nature but a spume that plays
Upon a ghostly paradigm of things;
Solider Aristotle played the taws
Upon the bottom of a king of kings;
World-famous golden-thighed Pythagoras
Fingered upon a fiddle stick or strings
What a star sang and careless Muses heard:
Old clothes upon old sticks to scare a bird.

VII

Both nuns and mothers worship images,
But those the candles light are not as those
That animate a mother’s reveries,
But keep a marble or a bronze repose.
And yet they too break hearts--O Presences
That passion, piety or affection knows,
And that all heavenly glory symbolise--
O self-born mockers of man’s enterprise;

VIII

Labour is blossoming or dancing where
The body is not bruised to pleasure soul,
Nor beauty born out of its own despair,
Nor blear-eyed wisdom out of midnight oil.
O chestnut tree, great rooted blossomer,
Are you the leaf, the blossom or the bole?
O body swayed to music, O brightening glance,
How can we know the dancer from the dance?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A senator visits a school and remembers the beloved of his youth, and sees how age ruins ideals of form. The poem ends with the famous question: 'How can we know the dancer from the dance?'

Study notes by the editors

  1. The occasion st. I, l. 1

    Yeats sat in the Irish Free State Senate from 1922 to 1928 and took an interest in education. In February 1926 he inspected St Otteran's School in Waterford, run by the Sisters of Mercy on Montessori principles, which he admired. A diary entry of March 1926 sketches the theme: schoolchildren and the thought that life will waste them. He finished the poem by June, and it was published in The Tower (1928). The opening line is a deliberately prosaic report: the speaker is a public man on an official tour. The poem moves from this setting into memory, philosophy and finally a visionary image of unity.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford UP, 2003); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of W. B. Yeats's Among School Children', Interesting Literature

  2. Ottava rima st. I, l. 1

    Each stanza has eight iambic pentameter lines rhyming abababcc, often with slant rhymes (questioning/sing/everything; replies/history/eyes). Ottava rima came from Italian romance epic (Boccaccio, Ariosto, Tasso) and was used in English by Byron for Don Juan. Yeats adopted it in the 1920s for his weightiest poems ('Sailing to Byzantium', 'Coole Park and Ballylee, 1931'). Its closing couplet gives each stanza a summarising turn, as at the end of stanza IV, where the couplet shrinks the speaker into a scarecrow, and of stanza VI, where it does the same to the philosophers.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); Helen Vendler, Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (Harvard UP, 2007)

  3. A kind old nun st. I, l. 2

    St Otteran's was run by the Sisters of Mercy. The nun who 'replies' to the visiting senator is courteous and practical, and the curriculum she lists (ciphering, singing, reading, history, sewing) is set out in a flat catalogue. 'In the best modern way' alludes to the Montessori method, the progressive approach Yeats admired, and the phrase may also carry a faint irony. Nuns return in stanza VII as worshippers of images, paired with mothers.

    R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford UP, 2003); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of W. B. Yeats's Among School Children', Interesting Literature

  4. A sixty year old smiling public man st. I, l. 8

    Yeats turned sixty in June 1925, so the self-description is exact. The line is notably self-ironic: 'smiling public man' sums up the role of the visiting senator, polite and professional. The children stare in 'momentary wonder', so the dignitary is a passing curiosity to them. The gap between public role and private reflection drives the poem: the next stanza goes straight into memory.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  5. a Ledaean body st. II, l. 1

    In Greek myth Zeus, in the form of a swan, raped or seduced Leda, who bore Helen (and in some versions Clytemnestra and the Dioscuri). Yeats had written 'Leda and the Swan' (1923) shortly before. For decades he had identified Maud Gonne, the revolutionary he loved, with Helen ('No Second Troy', 1910). A 'Ledaean body' is therefore a body of Helen-like beauty, Maud Gonne's, which he remembers bent over a fire telling a story of a childish grief. The swan imagery returns in stanza III ('daughters of the swan') and stanza IV ('Ledaean kind', 'plumage').

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); W. B. Yeats, 'Leda and the Swan' (1923); 'No Second Troy' (1910)

  6. Plato's parable ... yolk and white st. II, l. 8

    In Plato's Symposium, Aristophanes tells how the first humans were round double creatures that Zeus split in two; ever since, each half longs for its other, and love is that longing for wholeness. Yeats remembers his and Maud Gonne's sympathy as a moment in which their natures 'blent / Into a sphere'. Then he alters the parable: they were like yolk and white within a single egg, not two halves of one being. The egg also recalls the swan myth, since in some versions Helen was born from an egg. The image is of unity, yet it contains difference.

    Plato, Symposium 189c-193d (Aristophanes' speech); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  7. daughters of the swan ... every paddler's heritage st. III, l. 4

    Helen and her like are 'daughters of the swan'. Yeats reflects that even they share 'every paddler's heritage', the nature of common waterfowl such as ducks, which paddle. So as a child Maud Gonne may have looked like one of these ordinary schoolgirls. The thought makes him see her vividly ('She stands before me as a living child'), and his heart is 'driven wild'. The bird imagery joins the mythic and the everyday.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  8. quattrocento finger st. IV, l. 2

    Quattrocento (literally 'four hundred') is the Italian name for the 1400s, the early Renaissance of painters such as Botticelli and Mantegna. Maud Gonne's 'present image', the woman of about sixty, gaunt and hollow-cheeked, looks as if an artist of that period had shaped it. The images of drinking the wind and feeding on shadows suggest asceticism and spiritual hunger, and perhaps also Gonne's life of political austerity. Jeffares notes the contrast with the radiant beauty of her youth.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford UP, 2003)

  9. pretty plumage st. IV, l. 6

    Continuing the bird imagery, the poet admits that he was never a swan but had some attractiveness ('pretty plumage') when young. 'Enough of that' breaks off the self-pity with characteristic abruptness. The stanza's couplet then reverts to the public role of stanza I: better to smile on those who smile and show that he is a comfortable old scarecrow. The tone shifts abruptly from passion to self-mockery.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  10. old scarecrow st. IV, l. 8

    The scarecrow, old clothes stretched on a stick frame, is Yeats's recurrent image for the aged body: in 'Sailing to Byzantium' (1926) an old man is 'a tattered coat upon a stick'. Here the scarecrow is 'comfortable', resigned and amiable, which is how the smiling public man presents himself. The image returns at the end of stanza VI ('Old clothes upon old sticks to scare a bird'), where even the great philosophers turn out to be scarecrows.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); W. B. Yeats, 'Sailing to Byzantium' (The Tower, 1928), l. 10

  11. Honey of generation st. V, l. 2

    Yeats's note to The Tower says he took 'honey of generation' from Porphyry's essay on the Cave of the Nymphs (a Neoplatonic allegory of Odyssey 13). In Porphyry, honey symbolises the pleasure that draws souls down into generation, that is, into bodily birth. Yeats adds that Porphyry does not warrant treating it as the 'drug' that destroys the soul's memory of its pre-natal freedom. The infant is a soul 'betrayed' into birth by that honey, and it sleeps, shrieks and struggles as memory of its former state or the drug of forgetting prevails. The idea resembles Wordsworth's Platonic 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality'.

    The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats, ed. Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach (Macmillan, 1957), with Yeats's notes to The Tower; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); Porphyry, On the Cave of the Nymphs, trans. Thomas Taylor (1788)

  12. The mother's question st. V, l. 1

    Stanza V is one long rhetorical question. Would a young mother, holding her baby, think the old man he will become ('sixty or more winters on its head') a fair return for the pangs of childbirth or the anxieties of sending him into the world? The implied answer is no. The stanza universalises the poet's ageing: every scarecrow was once a baby on a mother's lap. Yeats's own mother had died in 1900, and the poem gives no direct biographical reference.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); The Variorum Edition of the Poems of W. B. Yeats, ed. Peter Allt and Russell K. Alspach (Macmillan, 1957), with Yeats's notes to The Tower

  13. Plato ... a ghostly paradigm of things st. VI, l. 1

    Plato taught that the visible world is a copy of eternal Forms or Ideas; in the Timaeus the maker shapes the cosmos after an eternal paradeigma (pattern or model). Yeats makes nature mere 'spume', foam playing on a ghostly pattern. The stanza runs through three philosophers as types: Plato the idealist, Aristotle the empiricist and teacher, and Pythagoras the mystic mathematician. All three are then dismissed in the last line as scarecrows.

    Plato, Timaeus 28a-29b; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  14. played the taws / Upon the bottom of a king of kings st. VI, l. 3

    A tawse (plural taws) is a leather strap with split ends, used in Scottish schools to beat pupils (the word comes from Scots). Aristotle was tutor to the young Alexander of Macedon, later 'king of kings', from about 343 BCE, as Plutarch relates. Yeats imagines the philosopher spanking the future conqueror, a comic, earthy image fitting a poem set in a school. 'Solider' contrasts Aristotle's empirical philosophy with Plato's ghostly paradigms.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'taws, tawse'; Plutarch, Life of Alexander 7-8; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  15. golden-thighed Pythagoras st. VI, l. 5

    Pythagoras of Samos (sixth century BCE) was said in ancient legend to have a golden thigh, a sign of divinity (Diogenes Laertius, Lives 8.11; also Iamblichus and Plutarch). The Pythagoreans discovered numerical ratios in musical intervals by measuring strings, and held that the planets made a music of the spheres. Yeats pictures him fingering a fiddle-stick or strings to work out what 'a star sang'. The 'careless Muses' heard the music without needing to calculate it.

    Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 8.11; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  16. Old clothes upon old sticks to scare a bird st. VI, l. 8

    The last line of stanza VI cuts the three great thinkers down to the scarecrow image of stanza IV. Whatever their philosophies, they grew old like everyone else. 'To scare a bird' may hint ironically that their thought frightens away living things (such as the swan-like beauty of the earlier stanzas). The line's plain monosyllables after the polysyllabic grandeur ('World-famous golden-thighed Pythagoras') make the deflation sharper.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  17. Both nuns and mothers worship images st. VII, l. 1

    Stanza VII pairs the nuns of stanza I with the mother of stanza V. Nuns worship images of marble or bronze in candlelight, still and unchanging. Mothers 'worship' the images in their reveries, their idealised hopes for their children. The religious statues keep their repose, unlike the mother's images, which reality will betray, 'and yet they too break hearts'. Any ideal image, sacred or domestic, remains beyond the human life that serves it.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); Frank Kermode, Romantic Image (Routledge, 1957)

  18. O Presences ... self-born mockers st. VII, l. 5

    The capitalised 'Presences' are the ideal forms that passion (the lover), piety (the nun) and affection (the mother) worship. They symbolise 'all heavenly glory', but they are 'self-born', existing independently of the people who serve them, and they 'mock' human enterprise because no actual life can match them. Critics debate their status: Platonic forms, Neoplatonic daimons, or psychological projections. The stanza runs on, through a semicolon, into stanza VIII, so the apostrophe opens directly onto the poem's final vision.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); Frank Kermode, Romantic Image (1957)

  19. Labour is blossoming or dancing st. VIII, l. 1

    The last stanza defines an ideal state: labour becomes blossoming or dancing when the body is not 'bruised to pleasure soul' (as in asceticism), when beauty is not born from despair, and when wisdom is not got by 'blear-eyed' study by 'midnight oil'. 'Labour' means both work and childbirth, and so answers the mother's pangs of stanza V. The ideal is Yeats's 'Unity of Being', the harmony of body and mind. The negations reject the nun, the mother, the scholar and the lover of the earlier stanzas.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984); Cleanth Brooks, 'Yeats's Great Rooted Blossomer', in The Well Wrought Urn (Harcourt, 1947)

  20. O chestnut tree, great rooted blossomer st. VIII, l. 5

    The chestnut tree is apostrophised as a 'great rooted blossomer': it is rooted in earth and it flowers. The question 'Are you the leaf, the blossom or the bole?' (bole = trunk) cannot be answered, because the tree is the living whole, not any part. Cleanth Brooks took the phrase as the title of his New Critical essay, reading the tree as an emblem of the poem's own organic unity. The image answers the scarecrow of earlier stanzas, a dead frame of sticks, with a living structure.

    Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn (1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bole'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (Macmillan / Stanford UP, 1984)

  21. How can we know the dancer from the dance? st. VIII, l. 8

    In the dancer, body and art, performer and form, are fused in movement: one cannot tell where the person ends and the dance begins. Most readers, including Brooks and Frank Kermode (who made the dancer central to his account of the Romantic Image), take the question as rhetorical. We cannot know them apart, and this unity is the poem's resolution. Paul de Man, in Allegories of Reading (1979), argued that the line can also be read literally, as a real and urgent question about how to distinguish them, and that grammar alone cannot decide between the two readings. The crux has become a classic test case in literary theory.

    Frank Kermode, Romantic Image (1957); Paul de Man, 'Semiology and Rhetoric', in Allegories of Reading (Yale UP, 1979); Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn (1947)
