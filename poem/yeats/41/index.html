Death & Dying

An Irish Airman Foresees His Death

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From The Wild Swans at Coole (1919)

Death & DyingHistory & Politics

I know that I shall meet my fate
Somewhere among the clouds above;
Those that I fight I do not hate
Those that I guard I do not love;
My country is Kiltartan Cross,
My countrymen Kiltartan's poor,
No likely end could bring them loss
Or leave them happier than before.
Nor law, nor duty bade me fight,
Nor public man, nor angry crowds,
A lonely impulse of delight
Drove to this tumult in the clouds;
I balanced all, brought all to mind,
The years to come seemed waste of breath,
A waste of breath the years behind
In balance with this life, this death.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A pilot says he fights for neither his country nor the English, but 'a lonely impulse of delight' drove him to the tumult in the clouds. He balances 'this life, this death' against the years behind and ahead.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Robert Gregory and the date l. 1

    The airman is Robert Gregory (1881-1918), the only child of Yeats's patron and collaborator Augusta, Lady Gregory, of Coole Park, Co. Galway. A painter who had studied at the Slade, he served first in the Connaught Rangers and then in the Royal Flying Corps, won the Military Cross, and was killed over northern Italy on 23 January 1918 while serving with 66 Squadron. The usual account is that he was shot down in error by an Italian pilot, but the cause is disputed and other accounts suggest an accident. Yeats wrote the poem in 1918 and published it in the Macmillan edition of The Wild Swans at Coole (1919), having withheld it during the war. It is a dramatic monologue: Gregory speaks before his death.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); Commonwealth War Graves Commission, casualty record: Major R. Gregory, Royal Flying Corps, 66th Sqdn., d. 23 January 1918; Great War Forum, 'Major Robert Gregory RFC: KIA, mistaken ID or the flu?' (discussion of official record and alternatives)

  2. among the clouds l. 2

    Military aviation was only a decade old when the war began, and the airman was quickly romanticised as a lone knight of the air, set apart from the mass slaughter of the trenches. The vague 'Somewhere' expresses the pilot's uncertainty of place and time, but not of fate: 'I know that I shall meet my fate'. The clouds return at l. 12 in 'this tumult in the clouds', so the setting frames the poem.

    RAF100 Schools Project, 'Major William Robert Gregory MC'; Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003)

  3. Those that I fight ... Those that I guard ll. 3-4

    Two balanced lines deny the usual motives of war. He does not hate 'Those that I fight', the Central Powers, nor love 'Those that I guard', the British and their allies. For an Irishman after the 1916 Rising, fighting for the British Empire was a fraught choice, and the Gregory family's Anglo-Irish position made it more complex. The parallel syntax and inversion are typical of the poem's measured style, and they introduce the theme of balance that dominates its close.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003); Connaught Rangers Association, 'An Irish Airman Foresees his Death'

  4. Kiltartan Cross l. 5

    Kiltartan is a townland and parish near Gort in Co. Galway, beside the Gregory estate at Coole; Kiltartan Cross is a crossroads there. Lady Gregory had made the name familiar in her books drawn from local speech and lore, such as The Kiltartan History Book (1909). By naming this tiny place as his 'country', the airman rejects national allegiance in favour of a local, almost feudal attachment. The line has been read as an expression of Anglo-Irish identity, rooted in a particular estate and its people.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003)

  5. Kiltartan's poor ll. 6-8

    The airman's 'countrymen' are 'Kiltartan's poor', the tenant farmers and labourers around the Gregory estate. The phrase reflects the paternal relation between the landed family and its people, which Yeats idealised. 'No likely end' of the war 'could bring them loss / Or leave them happier than before': whichever side wins, life in Kiltartan will continue as it was. The sentence denies the political claims of both British and Irish nationalist propaganda about the war's meaning for Ireland.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003); Connaught Rangers Association, 'An Irish Airman Foresees his Death'

  6. Nor law, nor duty l. 9

    Military conscription was introduced in Britain in 1916 but not in Ireland, where the attempt to extend it in 1918 provoked a political crisis and was abandoned. Gregory, then in his thirties, volunteered in 1915. 'Nor law' therefore has a literal truth. 'Nor duty' denies the patriotic or moral obligation urged in recruiting propaganda. The anaphora of 'Nor ... nor ... Nor ... nor' clears away every conventional motive in turn before the true one is named.

    Connaught Rangers Association, 'An Irish Airman Foresees his Death'; Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003)

  7. public man ... angry crowds l. 10

    A 'public man' is a politician or public figure: the recruiting speeches of the war, made in Ireland by both British and Irish Parliamentary Party leaders, are dismissed. 'Angry crowds' are the patriotic mobs and demonstrations of the war years. The pair recalls Yeats's suspicion of the crowd in 'No Second Troy'. The airman's choice is private, not political. The line also has a personal edge: Yeats had watched Irish politics divide bitterly over the war, and Gregory's refusal of every public motive leaves only the private impulse named in the next line.

    R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003); Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003)

  8. A lonely impulse of delight l. 11

    The poem's centre is this phrase. The impulse is 'lonely', private and individual, and its object is 'delight', the exhilaration of flight and combat. Yeats presents the choice as a free, heroic act, like that of the Renaissance gentleman he praised in Gregory elsewhere. Critics are divided. Some admire the poem as a refusal of propaganda and a celebration of individual freedom; others find the motive self-regarding, an aesthete's choice of an intense death while others died in the trenches. Readers also note the Romantic ring of 'impulse' and 'delight', words used by Wordsworth. The phrase has become one of Yeats's most quoted.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  9. this tumult in the clouds l. 12

    'Tumult' suggests noise, confusion and violent movement: the air battle seen from inside. The phrase picks up 'among the clouds' from l. 2. The demonstrative 'this' places the speaker in the present moment of combat or just before it, giving the poem its dramatic immediacy. The verb 'Drove' (l. 12) makes the impulse a force acting on him, even though it is his own.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  10. Balance and chiasmus ll. 13-16

    The final four lines enact the act of balancing they describe. 'The years to come seemed waste of breath, / A waste of breath the years behind' is a chiasmus, a mirror-like reversal of word order, which places the present moment at the fulcrum. The last line weighs both the past and the future against 'this life, this death', the single heroic instant. The poem is in four iambic tetrameter quatrains rhyming alternately, a tight form that gives the monologue the authority of a decision. The exact symmetry has been praised as a formal image of calm resolution.

    Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  11. this life, this death; the other Gregory poems l. 16

    The final phrase treats life and death as one: the moment of flight is both the culmination of his life and his death. Yeats wrote several poems for Gregory. 'In Memory of Major Robert Gregory', printed beside this one in The Wild Swans at Coole (1919), praises him as soldier, scholar and horseman and calls him 'Our Sidney and our perfect man', likening him to Sir Philip Sidney, the Elizabethan courtier-poet who died of a battle wound. 'Shepherd and Goatherd' is a pastoral elegy for him, and 'Reprisals' (written 1920, published later) invokes him against the violence of the Black and Tans in Ireland.

    W. B. Yeats, The Wild Swans at Coole (London: Macmillan, 1919), Project Gutenberg text; Wikipedia, 'An Irish Airman Foresees His Death' (citing Pierce 2000 and R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, vol. 2, 2003); R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, II: The Arch-Poet 1915-1939 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003)
