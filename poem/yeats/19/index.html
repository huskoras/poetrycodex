Adam's Curse

by W. B. Yeats 1865–1939

From In the Seven Woods (1903)

Arts & SciencesLove

We sat together at one summer's end
That beautiful mild woman your close friend
And you and I, and talked of poetry.

I said 'a line will take us hours maybe,
Yet if it does not seem a moment's thought
Our stitching and unstitching has been naught.
Better go down upon your marrow bones
And scrub a kitchen pavement, or break stones
Like an old pauper in all kinds of weather;
For to articulate sweet sounds together
Is to work harder than all these and yet
Be thought an idler by the noisy set
Of bankers, schoolmasters, and clergymen
The martyrs call the world.'

That woman then
Murmured with her young voice, for whose mild sake
There's many a one shall find out all heartache
In finding that it's young and mild and low.
'There is one thing that all we women know
Although we never heard of it at school,
That we must labour to be beautiful.'

I said, 'It's certain there is no fine thing
Since Adam's fall but needs much labouring.
There have been lovers who thought love should be
So much compounded of high courtesy
That they would sigh and quote with learned looks
Precedents out of beautiful old books;
Yet now it seems an idle trade enough.'

We sat grown quiet at the name of love.
We saw the last embers of daylight die
And in the trembling blue-green of the sky
A moon, worn as if it had been a shell
Washed by time's waters as they rose and fell
About the stars and broke in days and years.

I had a thought for no one's but your ears;
That you were beautiful and that I strove
To love you in the old high way of love;
That it had all seemed happy, and yet we'd grown
As weary hearted as that hollow moon.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

At summer's end the poet talks poetry with a beautiful woman and her friend. He says that nothing fine is made without labour, 'a line will take us hours maybe', and the conversation turns sadly to love's fading.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date ll. 1-3

    The poem recalls an evening on which Yeats talked with Maud Gonne ('you') and her sister Kathleen Pilcher ('That beautiful mild woman your close friend'). It was published in the Monthly Review in December 1902 and collected in In the Seven Woods (Dun Emer Press, 1903), and was written just before Gonne's marriage to John MacBride in February 1903, a blow Yeats felt deeply. The poem is usually taken to draw on a real meeting of the three; Yeats alters the situation slightly, turning the sister into Gonne's 'close friend'.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  2. Couplets and conversation st. 1-3

    The poem is in pentameter couplets, but Yeats loosens them: rhymes are sometimes full (thought/naught, bones/stones), sometimes partial (enough/love, school/beautiful), and sentences run on across line and paragraph breaks. The first verse paragraph runs fourteen lines, suggesting a sonnet, and ends on a short line ('The martyrs call the world') completed by the start of the next ('That woman then'). The effect enacts the poem's argument: what looks like casual speech is the result of labour. The movement from argument to silence ('We sat grown quiet') to private confession gives the poem a three-part structure.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  3. a moment's thought ll. 4-6

    The poet's first claim is that labour must be concealed: a line 'will take us hours maybe', but if it does not 'seem a moment's thought' the work is wasted. This is the classical ideal that art should conceal art (Latin ars est celare artem) and the Renaissance courtier's sprezzatura, the studied ease described in Castiglione's Book of the Courtier, a work Yeats came to admire. 'Us' makes the poet speak for poets in general. The idea underlies the whole poem: the beauty of verse, of women, and of courtly love are all achieved through hidden effort.

    Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  4. stitching and unstitching l. 6

    The poet compares revising a line to sewing and unpicking a seam, a domestic and traditionally female craft. The image prepares Kathleen's reply that women too 'must labour to be beautiful'. Readers may also hear Penelope in the Odyssey, who weaves by day and unpicks by night, though the phrase does not require the allusion. 'Naught', nothing, is a slightly archaic word that gives the line weight.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  5. go down upon your marrow bones l. 7

    'Marrow bones' is a jocular or colloquial term for the knees; 'to go down on one's marrow-bones' means to kneel, as here to scrub a kitchen floor. The OED records the phrase in English from the early modern period. The homely idiom brings physical drudgery into a poem about refined art, and the comparison argues that verse is harder than manual labour. 'Pavement' here means a paved floor.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  6. break stones / Like an old pauper ll. 8-9

    Breaking stones for roads was the archetypal hard labour of the poor: workhouses and relief schemes in Britain and Ireland set able-bodied paupers to it as a 'labour test'. The 'old pauper in all kinds of weather' is the lowest image of toil the poet can find, and verse is said to be harder still. The comparison is partly playful exaggeration, part of the poem's 'mock-serious' tone noted by critics, and partly serious about a poet's unseen effort.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  7. articulate l. 10

    From Latin articulus, a small joint, 'articulate' means to join, and then to utter distinctly. Here both senses work: to write verse is to fit 'sweet sounds together' as a craftsman joins pieces, and so to make them speak. The phrase is itself a line in which sound is carefully composed. It sums up the poet's labour in a single elevated verb after the plain list of scrubbing and stone-breaking.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  8. The martyrs call the world ll. 12-14

    The 'noisy set / Of bankers, schoolmasters, and clergymen' are the respectable and practical people who think the poet an 'idler'. Yeats calls them what the martyrs call 'the world', using the New Testament sense of the world as the sphere of worldliness opposed to the spirit (as in 1 John 2:15, 'Love not the world'). The poet thus implies that artists are martyrs, suffering for a higher calling; the Wikipedia summary notes that the speaker aligns himself with the martyrs and speaks from a victim's standpoint. The inclusion of 'clergymen' is a sly twist: the clergy themselves belong to 'the world'. The joke is half serious.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; 1 John 2:15 (KJV)

  9. That beautiful mild woman l. 2

    The 'beautiful mild woman' is usually identified as Kathleen Pilcher (née Gonne), Maud's younger sister. The poem calls her 'your close friend', not sister, a small alteration of fact. Later the poet says that many 'shall find out all heartache' for her 'mild sake', in finding her voice 'young and mild and low', a phrase that recalls King Lear's praise of Cordelia's voice, 'ever soft, / Gentle, and low' (5.3), a possible echo. Kathleen's mildness contrasts with the fierce Maud of later poems such as 'No Second Troy'.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  10. we must labour to be beautiful ll. 19-21

    Kathleen answers that 'all we women know', though no school teaches it, that beauty must be laboured for. Her words widen the poem's argument: the labour of art is matched by the labour, often unacknowledged, of being beautiful. Elizabeth Butler Cullingford's essay 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats' examines how the poem links poetic and feminine labour, and how both are hidden behind an appearance of ease. The poem gives women a voice in the debate but also casts beauty as a performance, which some feminist readers have questioned.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  11. Adam's fall; the title ll. 22-23

    The title and these lines allude to Genesis 3:17-19, where God curses the ground for Adam's sake: 'in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread'. Before the Fall, work and delight were one; afterwards, every good thing must be laboured for. The poet generalises the curse to art, beauty and love. Wikipedia's summary notes that the title evokes the Fall and the separation of work from pleasure. 'Labouring' rhymes with 'thing', giving the couplet the finality of a maxim; it is one of the most quoted statements of Yeats's aesthetic of hard-won ease.

    Genesis 3:17-19 (KJV); Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  12. high courtesy; beautiful old books ll. 24-27

    The 'lovers who thought love should be / So much compounded of high courtesy' are the courtly lovers of medieval romance and troubadour lyric, for whom love was an art with rules, refined manners and devotion to an unattainable lady. They 'quote with learned looks / Precedents out of beautiful old books', as a lawyer cites cases. Yeats had modelled his own long devotion to Gonne on this ideal, which is why the 'old high way of love' returns in the poem's last section. 'Compounded' means made up of, as of ingredients.

    Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'; A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem

  13. an idle trade enough l. 28

    'Idle trade' is a near-oxymoron, a trade being a working occupation. The phrase echoes the 'idler' of l. 12: what the worldly think of poets, the poet now thinks of the courtly lovers, and by implication of his own devotion. 'Enough' here means 'sufficiently, quite', an idiomatic intensifier. The line marks the turn from argument to feeling, and the next line (l. 29), 'We sat grown quiet at the name of love', shows that the topic has touched the speakers too closely.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1)

  14. A moon, worn as if it had been a shell ll. 32-34

    In the dying light appears a moon 'worn as if it had been a shell / Washed by time's waters'. The thin crescent looks like a sea-shell eroded by the waves; the 'waters' are those of time, rising and falling about the stars and breaking 'in days and years', as waves break on a shore. The image transfers the theme of labour and wear from art and beauty to the cosmos: even the moon is worn down. The 'trembling blue-green' of the sky gives one of Yeats's most delicate colour effects. The moon will return in the last line (l. 39) as 'that hollow moon'.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; Jahan Ramazani, Richard Ellmann and Robert O'Clair, eds, The Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1 (New York: Norton, 2003), notes to 'Adam's Curse'

  15. the old high way of love ll. 36-38

    The last paragraph is a confession meant 'for no one's but your ears', that is, for Maud Gonne. 'The old high way of love' is the courtly ideal of the previous paragraph, now applied to Yeats's own devotion. He had proposed marriage to Gonne several times from 1891 onward and been refused; within weeks of the poem's publication she married MacBride. The sentence moves in three clauses ('That you were beautiful ... That I strove ... That it had all seemed happy'), each a little sadder, and ends with the image of the moon.

    Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1); A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; R. F. Foster, W. B. Yeats: A Life, I: The Apprentice Mage 1865-1914 (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1997)

  16. that hollow moon l. 39

    The worn, shell-like moon of ll. 32-34 now becomes 'hollow', an image of the lovers' hearts emptied by long effort. The final couplet's slant rhyme ('grown' / 'moon') leaves the poem slightly unresolved, in keeping with a love that has not been answered. The moon was already one of Yeats's favourite symbols and would later become central to his system in A Vision (1925). Here it serves a simple, poignant purpose: even beauty and love, like the moon, wear away.

    A. Norman Jeffares, A New Commentary on the Poems of W. B. Yeats (London: Macmillan, 1984), notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'Adam's Curse (poem)' (citing Cullingford, 'Labour and Memory in the Love Poetry of W. B. Yeats', and the Norton Anthology of Modern and Contemporary Poetry, 3rd ed., vol. 1)
