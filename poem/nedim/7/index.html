The Realm of Sufferance Thou'st Laid Waste

by Nedim c. 1681–1730

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1905) · From A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (1905)

LoveHistory & Politics

The realm of sufferance thou'st laid waste; Hulagu Khan art hight, Paynim,
Have ruth! thou'st set the world aflame; art thou a burning light, Paynim?

A maiden's winsome air, thine air; a youthful gallant's tone, thy tone;
A torment thou! I wist not, art thou youth or maiden bright, Paynim?

What meaning bears that wede of flame-hued satin o'er thy shoulders thrown?
Art thou the soul-consuming gleam of grace and love-delight, Paynim?

Ah, what may be those secret, secret sighs, and what that rended spare?
Art thou, e'en thou, the wailing lover of some wanton wight, Paynim?

A many say to thee 'My Life;' another many say 'My Lore;'
What art thou? Art thou Life, or art thou Love? declare aright, Paynim.

The flame-hued wine hath flushed thee, dear, and set thy sweetest face aglow;
Art thou become the lamp wherewith the topers' circle is dight, Paynim?

O wherefore in the burnished mirror lookest thou so oft, my fair?
Art thou likewise astound of thine own dazzling beauty's sight, Paynim?

I've heard a Paynim hath made thrall of hapless and forlorn Nedim;
Art thou that foe unto the Faith, that adversary of right, Paynim?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A ghazel that calls the beloved 'Paynim' and compares her cruelty to that of Hulagu Khan, the Mongol conqueror whom Gibb's notes describe as a byword for a bloody tyrant.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: Nedîm's 'kâfir' ghazel couplet 1

    Nedîm (Ahmed, d. 1730) was a scholar-official and the favourite poet of the court of Ahmed III and the grand vizier Nevşehirli Damad İbrahim Paşa, in the period later called the Lâle Devri (Tulip Era, 1718-30). His style, called 'Nedîmâne', is known for ease, wit, local Istanbul colour and pleasure-loving themes. The opening line reads 'Tahammül mülkünü yıktın Hülâgû Hân mısın kâfir' ('You have destroyed the realm of endurance: are you Hülâgû Khan, infidel?'). Gibb gives this 1905 version as Ghazel [327]; the archive also holds his shorter 1882 version (as 'The Realm of Patience Thou'st Laid Waste'), which omits three couplets.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), Ghazel [327], pp. 42-43, with notes; Muhsin Macit, 'Nedîm', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Kemal Sılay, Nedim and the Poetics of the Ottoman Court: Medieval Inheritance and the Need for Change (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1994); TDV contributor, 'Lâle Devri', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  2. The refrain 'Paynim': 'are you ..., infidel?' end of every couplet

    The original's redif is 'mısın kâfir', 'are you ..., infidel?', so the ghazel is a string of questions to the beloved. Gibb's 'Paynim' is an archaic English word for a pagan or heathen (OED, from Old French). Calling the beloved an infidel is a convention of Persian and Turkish poetry: the beloved is cruel and 'unbelieving' because without mercy, and is often imagined as a Christian or other non-Muslim youth, which adds a frisson. The poem scans in the hezec metre, mefâîlün four times to the line ('Ta-ham-mül mül-kü-nü yık-tın...'), a smooth, songlike measure.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), pp. 42-43; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'paynim'; TDV contributor, 'Hezec', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  3. Hülâgû Khan l. 1

    Gibb's note: Hülâgû Khan, grandson of Chinggis Khan, commanded the Mongol horde which devastated Persia, captured and sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph, al-Musta'sim, in 656/1258; the name came to mean a cruel and bloodthirsty tyrant, 'as we might say an Attila'. The 'realm of sufferance' (tahammül mülkü) is the poet's capacity to endure, laid waste as a country is by an invader. 'Art hight' is archaic English for 'art called' (OED, s.v. 'hight'). The hyperbole is comic as well as grand.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 42, n. 3; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 232, p. 259; TDV contributor, 'Hülâgû', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hight'

  4. The beloved as fire l. 2

    Fire is the element of love in the poetry: the beloved's cheek is a flame, the lover a moth or a burnt heart. Nedîm develops the motif across the ghazel: the flame-coloured satin of couplet 3, the 'soul-consuming gleam', the flame-hued wine and the lamp of the drinkers' circle in couplet 6. 'Have ruth!', 'have pity!' (OED, s.v. 'ruth'), renders the cry of mercy with which lovers address the cruel beloved.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 42; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ruth'

  5. 'Are you a girl or a boy?' ll. 3-4

    In Turkish the couplet runs 'Kız oğlan nâzı nâzın şehlevend âvâzı âvâzın / Belâsın ben de bilmem kız mısın oğlan mısın kâfir'. Gibb's 'youthful gallant' renders şehlevend, a dashing, swaggering young man. The gender of the beloved in Ottoman poetry is often unmarked, since Turkish has no grammatical gender, and the beloved was frequently imagined as a youth; Andrews and Kalpaklı discuss this culture of beloveds at length. Nedîm makes the ambiguity the explicit subject. One modern columnist confesses that this couplet still puzzles him.

    Walter G. Andrews and Mehmet Kalpaklı, The Age of Beloveds: Love and the Beloved in Early-Modern Ottoman and European Culture and Society (Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2005); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 42; Yusuf Ziya Cömert, 'Nedim'in en büyük marifeti', Karar (newspaper column; quotes the couplet); Kemal Sılay, Nedim and the Poetics of the Ottoman Court: Medieval Inheritance and the Need for Change (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1994)

  6. The flame-coloured satin robe l. 5

    Gibb's 'wede' is archaic English for clothing (the 'weeds' of 'widow's weeds'; OED, s.v. 'weed'). Fine dress and the colours of silk and satin are a feature of Nedîm's Istanbul world, which the TDV article describes as full of scenes from the city's pleasure grounds and fashionable life. The red robe connects with the fire images: the question is whether the beloved is the 'soul-consuming' flash of grace itself. Gibb's 1882 version omits this couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weed'; Muhsin Macit, 'Nedîm', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 42

  7. Secret sighs and a torn collar l. 7

    The doubled 'secret, secret' imitates Turkish reduplication (gizli gizli), a colloquial touch typical of Nedîm. 'Spare' is an old word for the slit or opening in a gown (OED, s.v. 'spare', n.), and tearing one's collar or garment open was the conventional sign of a lover's despair. The joke of the couplet is the reversal of roles: the cruel beloved shows the symptoms of a lover, the 'wailing lover of some wanton wight'. Jealousy of rivals is a stock theme, but here it is turned against the beloved.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spare'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 43

  8. Cân and cânân: Life or Love? ll. 9-10

    The pun depends on the Persian jân ('life, soul') and jânân ('beloved'), which differ by a single syllable; both are common endearments. Gibb's 'Life' and 'Love' imitate the near-rhyme in English. The question form of the refrain (is it Life, or Love, O infidel?) makes the couplet a playful riddle. The archive text reads 'My Lore', apparently a misprint: Gibb's 1905 printing reads 'My Love', and the next line ('Art thou Life, or art thou Love?') confirms it.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 43; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  9. The lamp of the drinking party ll. 11-12

    The wine party (bezm) is the central scene of Nedîm's poetry, and the beloved is often the cupbearer or the 'candle' of the gathering (şem'-i meclis), around which the lovers, like moths, are drawn. 'Toper' is an old word for a hard drinker; 'dight' means adorned or arrayed. The couplet joins three motifs of the poem, fire, wine and gaze. Silay's study of Nedîm discusses how he renews such inherited motifs with a lighter, more worldly touch.

    Kemal Sılay, Nedim and the Poetics of the Ottoman Court: Medieval Inheritance and the Need for Change (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1994); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 43; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dight'

  10. The beloved at the mirror ll. 13-14

    Mirrors in the period were of polished metal, hence 'burnished'. The beloved's vanity before the glass is a favourite comic scene, and 'astound' (astonished) makes the beloved, for once, the stunned admirer, the role normally played by the lover. In mystical poetry the mirror is a weighty image, since creation was said to be the mirror in which God contemplates His own beauty; Nedîm's poem is worldly, but readers trained in the tradition would catch the echo.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 43; TDV contributor, 'Kenz-i Mahfî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  11. The last couplet: the infidel who enslaved Nedîm ll. 15-16

    The final couplet (makta') names the poet. In Turkish it reads 'Nedîm-i zârı bir kâfir esîr etmiş işitmiştim / Sen ol cellâd-ı dîn ol düşmen-i îmân mısın kâfir', where cellâd-ı dîn means 'executioner of religion'. The joke is that the Muslim poet speaks of himself as a captive taken by an infidel, as if in a frontier war, and then asks the beloved, as an innocent question, whether he or she is the culprit. Gibb's 1905 text has 'forlore' and 'adversaire', archaisms that the archive text modernizes to 'forlorn' and 'adversary'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. IV (London: Luzac, 1905), p. 43; Yusuf Ziya Cömert, 'Nedim'in en büyük marifeti', Karar (quotes the couplet); Kemal Sılay, Nedim and the Poetics of the Ottoman Court: Medieval Inheritance and the Need for Change (Bloomington: Indiana University Turkish Studies, 1994)
