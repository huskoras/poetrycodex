Religion

Vpon the Infant Martyrs.

by Richard Crashaw 1613–1649

Religion

TO see both blended in one flood.
The Mothers Milke, the Childrens blood,
Makes me doubt if Heaven will gather,
Roses hence, or Lillies rather.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Holy Innocents and the epigram ll. 1-4

    Herod, learning of a newborn 'King of the Jews', had all the boys of Bethlehem aged two and under killed (Matthew 2:16-18). The Church honours them as the first martyrs, the Holy Innocents, on 28 December. Crashaw's English sacred epigrams were printed in Steps to the Temple (1646, enlarged 1648); they grow out of his Latin Epigrammatum Sacrorum Liber (Cambridge, 1634), a book of short witty meditations on gospel scenes in the manner of continental Jesuit epigrammatists, ultimately modelled on Martial's compression. This quatrain is a characteristic example: a single paradox, a startling image, and a turn in the last line.

    Matthew 2:16-18 (KJV); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams; George Walton Williams, ed., The Complete Poetry of Richard Crashaw (New York, 1970), notes to the epigrams; Austin Warren, Richard Crashaw: A Study in Baroque Sensibility (Louisiana State University Press, 1939)

  2. both blended in one flood l. 1

    The 'flood' is literal and hyperbolic at once: the children were killed while nursing, so their mothers' milk and their own blood ran together. Mingled liquids are Crashaw's signature image, from the tears and blood of his poems on Christ's wounds to the milk and wine of other epigrams, and critics of the 'baroque' Crashaw, from Austin Warren onward, have seen in them a sensibility shaped by Counter-Reformation devotional art. Readers have long been divided between finding such images powerful and finding them grotesque.

    Austin Warren, Richard Crashaw: A Study in Baroque Sensibility (Louisiana State University Press, 1939); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams; George Walton Williams, ed., The Complete Poetry of Richard Crashaw (New York, 1970), notes to the epigrams

  3. The Mothers Milke, the Childrens blood l. 2

    The balanced line sets the mother's white milk against the child's red blood, preparing the colour-riddle of the conclusion. The scene recalls the grief of Rachel 'weeping for her children' which Matthew quotes from Jeremiah (Matthew 2:18; Jeremiah 31:15), though Crashaw, unlike many treatments of the Massacre, does not dwell on lament: the mothers are present only through their milk. The red-and-white pairing is also a commonplace of love poetry (the beloved's roses and lilies), here turned to sacred use.

    Matthew 2:18; Jeremiah 31:15 (KJV); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams; Austin Warren, Richard Crashaw: A Study in Baroque Sensibility (Louisiana State University Press, 1939)

  4. Metre, 'doubt' and the epigram's turn ll. 3-4

    The quatrain is in four-stress couplets; the second couplet has feminine (two-syllable) rhymes, 'gather' / 'rather', which give the ending a light, lilting fall. 'Doubt' is used in its older sense of being undecided rather than disbelieving (OED, doubt v.). The speaker pretends not to know which flower heaven will pick, and this pretended uncertainty is the epigram's point (acumen): the infants are equally martyrs and innocents, so either answer is right.

    OED, 'doubt, v.'; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams; George Walton Williams, ed., The Complete Poetry of Richard Crashaw (New York, 1970), notes to the epigrams

  5. Heaven will gather l. 3

    The image of heaven gathering flowers recalls the Song of Songs, where the beloved goes down to his garden 'to feed in the gardens, and to gather lilies' (Song of Solomon 6:2), a text read allegorically as Christ gathering souls. It also draws on the liturgical tradition of the Innocents as 'flowers of the martyrs': Prudentius's hymn, used in the breviary for their feast, hails them as 'flores martyrum' cut down like budding roses by a whirlwind. The infants' deaths thus become a harvest rather than a destruction.

    Song of Solomon 6:2 (KJV); Prudentius, Cathemerinon 12 (Hymnus Epiphaniae), source of the breviary hymn 'Salvete flores martyrum'; text and translations at CPDL; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams

  6. Roses l. 4

    In Christian symbolism the red rose stands for martyrdom and the white lily for virginity or purity, a pairing found in medieval and Renaissance art and hymnody. The children's blood qualifies them as roses, the earliest martyrs, in line with Prudentius's 'Salvete flores martyrum'. 'Hence' means 'from here', i.e. from this scene of slaughter; the Folger first-line index records a manuscript version (Bodleian MS Tanner 465) reading 'Roses here'.

    Prudentius, Cathemerinon 12 (Hymnus Epiphaniae), source of the breviary hymn 'Salvete flores martyrum'; text and translations at CPDL; Folger Shakespeare Library, Union First Line Index of English Verse, entry for 'To see both blended in one flood' (Bodleian MS Tanner 465); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams

  7. Lillies rather l. 4

    The infants' milk-white innocence makes them lilies. The Book of Common Prayer's Epistle for Innocents' Day is Revelation 14:1-5, on those who 'were not defiled' and 'are virgins', 'the firstfruits unto God and to the Lamb', so that the Innocents were liturgically associated with virginal purity as well as martyrdom. Crashaw's 'rather' poses an apparent choice, but the point is that both are true at once; the poem's wit lies in refusing to choose. Some readers find the image's sweetness at odds with its violence; others see the transfiguration of horror into beauty as exactly the poem's devotional purpose.

    Revelation 14:1-5 (KJV), the Epistle for Innocents' Day in the Book of Common Prayer; Austin Warren, Richard Crashaw: A Study in Baroque Sensibility (Louisiana State University Press, 1939); George Walton Williams, ed., The Complete Poetry of Richard Crashaw (New York, 1970), notes to the epigrams

  8. Crashaw's sensibility l. 2

    Crashaw, son of a Puritan divine, became a Roman Catholic in exile and died at Loreto in 1649. His fondness for physical, liquid images of sacred suffering was long held against him by English critics who found it un-English or in bad taste. Twentieth-century critics such as Austin Warren and Mario Praz reread him in the context of European baroque art and Jesuit devotion, where such images are designed to move the affections. More recent scholarship has explored the gendered and bodily dimensions of his imagery. The quatrain's calm conversion of blood into flowers shows why he provokes both admiration and unease.

    Austin Warren, Richard Crashaw: A Study in Baroque Sensibility (Louisiana State University Press, 1939); Mario Praz, The Flaming Heart (New York, 1958); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poems English, Latin and Greek of Richard Crashaw, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on the Divine Epigrams
