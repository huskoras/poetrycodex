Cædmon's Hymn: Northumbrian Version (Old English)

by Cædmon fl. c. 657–680

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. E. V. K. Dobbie, vol. VI (1942)

Religion

Nu scylun hergan hefaenricaes uard,
metudæs maecti end his modgidanc,
uerc uuldurfadur, sue he uundra gihuaes,
eci dryctin, or astelidæ.
He aerist scop aelda barnum
heben til hrofe, haleg scepen;
tha middungeard moncynnæs uard,
eci dryctin, æfter tiadæ
firum foldu, frea allmectig.

Codex Note

Old English text as in ASPR; hand-keyed e-text via sacred-texts.com.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Bede's story of Cædmon l. 1

    In the Historia ecclesiastica (IV.24), finished in 731, Bede tells how Cædmon, a layman at the double monastery of Whitby ruled by Abbess Hild (657-680), used to leave the feast whenever the harp came round to him. One night in the cattle-shed a figure in a dream told him to 'sing the beginning of creatures'. He then sang verses he had never heard. Bede gives the hymn only in a Latin paraphrase and warns that this is the sense and not the order of the words, since poetry cannot be translated word for word without losing its beauty. The English text survives as an addition in Bede manuscripts. Cædmon was then taught scripture and turned it into vernacular verse. The story is often read as the moment the native verse tradition was taken over for Christian use.

    Bede, Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum IV.24 (Latin text); D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes

  2. Nu scylun – who must praise? l. 1

    'Scylun' is the present plural of 'sculan', 'must, ought'. The West Saxon versions read 'Nu we sculon herigean', but the early Northumbrian copies have no 'we'. Two readings follow. The first takes the subject as understood, 'Now [we] must praise the Guardian of heaven's kingdom', which Old English sometimes allows. The second takes 'uerc uuldurfadur' (l. 3) as the subject: 'Now the work of the Father of glory must praise...', with 'uerc' as nominative and not accusative. Mitchell and others discuss the grammar, and O'Donnell sets out how the different witnesses treat the line. Bede's Latin, 'Nunc laudare debemus', has a first-person plural, which supports the first reading. 'Hergan' is the infinitive 'praise' (West Saxon 'herian').

    D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; Bede, Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum IV.24 (Latin text); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. herian

  3. Manuscripts and spelling l. 1

    Dobbie's ASPR text of the Northumbrian version follows the Moore Bede (Cambridge University Library, MS Kk.5.16), written in Northumbria a few years after Bede's death. The hymn is added on the last page. The St Petersburg Bede (National Library of Russia, MS lat. Q.v.I.18) has a very similar text with small differences of spelling. These are among the earliest surviving records of English verse. The spelling is early Northumbrian: 'u/uu' for 'w' ('uard', 'uuldurfadur'), 'ae/æ' for later 'e/æ' ('hefaenricaes'), 'd' or 'th' where later texts write 'ð/þ' ('tha'), and 'ct' for 'ht' ('dryctin', 'maecti'). 'Hefaenricaes uard' is West Saxon 'heofonrices weard', 'the Guardian of the kingdom of heaven'. 'Weard' is a guardian, keeper or warden.

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; Wikipedia, 'Moore Bede'

  4. metud – the Measurer l. 2

    'Metod' is related to 'metan', 'to measure', and is usually glossed 'Creator, ordainer, God'. In poetry it may once have meant something nearer 'fate' or 'that which measures out', and it is used both for God and, in heroic verse, in phrases close to destiny. Critics such as Mitchell and Robinson note how the hymn reuses traditional poetic words for a Christian God. 'Maecti' (West Saxon 'meahte') is accusative, 'might, power'. In the poem it is one of the three objects of praise along with 'modgidanc' and possibly 'uerc'. The genitive '-æs' is early Northumbrian for later '-es'. The line alliterates on 'm': metudæs / maecti / modgidanc.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. meotod; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005)

  5. modgidanc – the mind's purpose l. 2

    'Modgeþanc' combines 'mod' ('mind, spirit, heart, courage') and 'geþanc' ('thought, purpose'), so 'thought of the mind, purpose, intention'. Applied to God it means the plan behind creation, the design the poem goes on to describe. In this early spelling the prefix 'ge-' appears as 'gi-' and 'þ' as 'd' in 'gidanc'. The line's three objects, might, design, and possibly work, give a summary of God's attributes before the narrative of creation starts in l. 5. 'End' is the Northumbrian form of 'and'.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. modgeþanc; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005)

  6. uerc uuldurfadur – the work of the Glory-Father l. 3

    'Wuldor' means 'glory, splendour, heaven', and '-fæder' means 'father'. The compound is a typical poetic name for God. Its genitive here is 'uuldurfadur', without an ending, an old form for nouns of relationship. 'Uerc' (West Saxon 'weorc') can be accusative, 'the work of the Father of glory', parallel to 'maecti' and 'modgidanc'. It can also be nominative and the subject of 'scylun'. See the note on the opening crux. On the first reading, God's power, purpose and work are the things praised. On the second, creation itself is called on to praise its Creator, as in Psalm 148. The line alliterates on 'w' (here spelled 'u/uu'): uerc / uuldurfadur / uundra.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. wuldorfæder; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 148

  7. uundra gihuaes ... or astelidæ – the beginning of every wonder l. 3

    'Sue' is Northumbrian for West Saxon 'swa', 'as, how'. 'Uundra gihuaes' is 'of each of wonders', that is, 'of every wonder', with the genitive plural 'wundra' governed by 'gehwæs', the genitive of 'gehwa', 'each'. 'Or' (neuter) is 'beginning, origin'. 'Astelidæ' is the preterite of 'astellan', 'to establish, set up, institute'. The clause recalls Genesis 1:1, 'In the beginning God created...', and Bede's Latin, 'auctor omnium miraculorum'. The idea is that God set going the origin of all marvels, and the next lines make this specific: heaven, then earth.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. astellan; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. or; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 1:1

  8. eci dryctin – formula and variation l. 4

    'Eci' (West Saxon 'ece') means 'eternal', and 'dryctin' (West Saxon 'dryhten') means 'lord'. Originally 'dryhten' was the lord of a 'dryht', a war-band, and it became the usual poetic word for God. Nine short lines name God in many ways: 'hefaenricaes uard', 'metud', 'uuldurfadur', 'eci dryctin' (twice), 'haleg scepen', 'moncynnæs uard', 'frea allmectig'. Repeating the same idea in different words, often in apposition, is variation, the central stylistic habit of Old English verse. F. P. Magoun's 1953 article used Cædmon's Hymn as a test case for the theory that Old English poetry was composed orally from traditional formulas. Later critics have argued that the formulas may already have been reshaped for a Christian subject, perhaps by Cædmon himself.

    F. P. Magoun, Jr, 'Oral-Formulaic Character of Anglo-Saxon Narrative Poetry', Speculum 28 (1953); B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. dryhten

  9. aelda barnum – for the children of men l. 5

    'Aelda' is the genitive plural of 'ælde/ielde', 'men, people', a poetic word. 'Barnum' (West Saxon 'bearnum') is the dative plural of 'bearn', 'child'. The hymn's surviving copies divide into versions by dialect and by this variant. Some read 'aelda barnum' ('for the children of men'), others 'eorðan bearnum' ('for the children of earth'). O'Donnell and earlier editors use the variant to group the manuscripts. 'Aerist scop' means 'first created'. 'Scop', the preterite of 'scieppan', 'to create, shape', is spelled the same as the noun 'scop', 'poet'. Critics note the fitting accident that the hymn credits its first poet's God with being the first maker.

    D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. ælde; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  10. heben til hrofe – heaven as a roof l. 6

    'Heben' (West Saxon 'heofon') is 'heaven, sky'. 'Til' is a northern preposition meaning 'to, for, as', which also appears in Old Norse, and 'hrof' is 'roof'. Hence 'heaven as a roof'. The order follows Genesis 1, where the firmament comes before dry land. The image of a roofed house, the world as a hall built by God, belongs to the same imaginative world as the 'heal' or hall of heroic poetry. Compare the creation song sung in Heorot in Beowulf (ll. 90-98). The West Saxon versions read 'heofon to hrofe'. The spelling 'b' in 'heben' for later 'f' is an early form.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. hrof; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005); The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 1:6

  11. haleg scepen – holy Creator l. 6

    'Scepen' is a short form of the agent noun 'scyppend', 'shaper, creator', from 'scieppan'. The half-line repeats the act just stated: he created ('scop') as the Creator ('scepen'). This links verb and epithet, a small example of the hymn's dense patterning. 'Haleg' (West Saxon 'halig') means 'holy'. Bede's Latin paraphrase leaves this epithet out and keeps only the narrative: heaven as roof, then the earth. The line alliterates on 'h': heben / hrofe / haleg. The sequence of epithets shows the variation discussed in the note on 'eci dryctin'.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. scyppend; Bede, Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum IV.24 (Latin text); B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005)

  12. middungeard – middle-earth l. 7

    'Middangeard' (here 'middungeard') means 'the middle enclosure', the earth as the dwelling of men. The second element, 'geard', is 'enclosure, yard'. The word has relatives across the Germanic languages: Gothic 'midjungards', Old Norse 'Miðgarðr', Old Saxon 'middilgard'. It is common in Old English poetry and prose for 'the world'. 'Tha' is Northumbrian 'þa', 'then'. 'Æfter tiadæ' means 'afterwards made/prepared': 'tiadæ' is the preterite of a verb 'teon' ('to make, arrange, ordain'), and translators vary between 'adorned', 'ordained' and 'created'. 'Firum foldu' is 'the earth (for) men', with 'firum' the dative plural of the poetic 'firas', 'men'. 'Frea allmectig' ('Lord almighty') closes the poem with alliteration on 'f': firum / foldu / frea.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. middangeard; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. firas; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; D. P. O'Donnell, Caedmon's Hymn: A Multimedia Study, Edition and Archive (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 2005)
