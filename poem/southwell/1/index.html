The Burning Babe

by Robert Southwell 1561–1595

Religion

As I in hoary winter’s night
Stood shivering in the snow,
Surprised I was with sudden heat
Which made my heart to glow;
And lifting up a fearful eye
To view what fire was near,
A pretty babe all burning bright
Did in the air appear;
Who, scorchèd with excessive heat,
Such floods of tears did shed,
As though His floods should quench His flames,
Which with His tears were bred:
‘Alas!’ quoth He, ‘but newly born
In fiery heats I fry,
Yet none approach to warm their hearts
Or feel my fire but I!

‘My faultless breast the furnace is;
The fuel, wounding thorns;
Love is the fire, and sighs the smoke;
The ashes, shames and scorns;
The fuel Justice layeth on,
And Mercy blows the coals,
The metal in this furnace wrought
Are men’s defilèd souls:
For which, as now on fire I am
To work them to their good,
So will I melt into a bath,
To wash them in my blood.’
With this He vanish’d out of sight
And swiftly shrunk away,
And straight I callèd unto mind
That it was Christmas Day.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Southwell and the poem ll. 1-2

    Robert Southwell (1561-95), an English Jesuit, worked secretly as a priest in England from 1586, was arrested in 1592, tortured and hanged at Tyburn on 21 February 1595; he was canonised in 1970. His poems circulated in manuscript among Catholics and were printed after his death, 'The Burning Babe' first in the 1602 edition of 'Saint Peters Complaint'. Ben Jonson told William Drummond that if he had written 'The Burning Babe' he would have been content to destroy many of his own poems. 'Hoary' means white with frost.

    Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts (CELM), Robert Southwell (first printing of 'The Burning Babe' in Saint Peters Complaint, 1602); James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967); Ben Jonson, Conversations with William Drummond of Hawthornden (1619)

  2. Metre and vision form ll. 1-4

    The poem is in fourteeners, long lines of seven stresses rhymed in couplets, here broken into alternating lines of eight and six syllables (the common metre of hymns and ballads). The plain, almost ballad-like movement carries a dense chain of paradoxes. The narrative frame is that of a vision: the speaker stands in the snow, is surprised by sudden heat, sees, hears a speech and, when the vision vanishes, understands. The heat that makes the heart 'glow' anticipates the moral of the poem, that the Christ-child's love should kindle human hearts.

    James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967); A. B. Grosart (ed.), The Complete Poems of Robert Southwell (1872)

  3. The burning babe ll. 7-8

    The vision of a child burning but not consumed recalls the burning bush in which God appeared to Moses (Exodus 3:2), a traditional type of the Virgin's conception, and God as 'a consuming fire' (Hebrews 12:29; Deuteronomy 4:24). 'Pretty' meant fine or comely rather than merely charming. The fire is the fire of divine love, and the infant's suffering looks forward to the Passion. The image is deliberately startling.

    The Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:2; The Bible (KJV), Hebrews 12:29; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  4. Tears and flames ll. 9-12

    The child weeps floods of tears, as though they could quench his flames, yet the tears are themselves produced by the heat. Fire and water, burning and weeping, were the stock oxymorons of Petrarchan love poetry ('I burn and freeze'). Southwell deliberately converted secular love conventions to sacred subjects, as he says in the prefatory letter to his poems, and here the lover's sighs and tears become Christ's. 'Excessive' carries its root sense, going beyond measure.

    James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967); A. B. Grosart (ed.), The Complete Poems of Robert Southwell (1872)

  5. fry l. 14

    In the sixteenth century 'fry' could mean simply to burn, to be scorched or to suffer intense heat, of passion as well as of fire (OED 'fry', v.). The newborn Christ declares that he burns from the moment of his birth, so that the Nativity already contains the Passion. The verb is thus intense rather than comic, and the paradox of a newborn suffering in fire prepares for the furnace allegory that follows. The present tense makes the suffering immediate.

    Oxford English Dictionary; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  6. None come to warm themselves ll. 15-16

    The lament that no one approaches to warm their hearts at his fire recalls John 1:11 and the image, at the Nativity, of the inn with no room. The disciples on the road to Emmaus later say 'Did not our heart burn within us?' (Luke 24:32). In Southwell's England, where Catholic worship was illegal, the reproach could also be heard as addressed to a nation that had turned away from the old faith. The speaker himself, warmed at the start, is the exception.

    The Bible (KJV), John 1:11; The Bible (KJV), Luke 24:32

  7. The furnace conceit ll. 17-20

    The child's speech develops a sustained allegory, a conceit of the kind later associated with the metaphysical poets. His sinless ('faultless') breast is the furnace, the fuel is 'wounding thorns' (the crown of thorns), love is the fire, sighs the smoke, and the 'shames and scorns' of the Passion are the ashes. The furnace recalls the refiner's fire of Malachi 3:2-3 and 'the furnace of affliction' (Isaiah 48:10). Each element is carefully assigned, in a manner like an emblem with its explanation.

    The Bible (KJV), Malachi 3:2-3; The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 48:10; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  8. The thorns l. 18

    The 'wounding thorns' are the crown of thorns placed on Christ's head before the crucifixion (Matthew 27:29; John 19:2). Making them the fuel of the furnace means that Christ's suffering nourishes the fire of his love. The anticipation of the Passion in the Nativity was common in devotional art and poetry, where the infant is shown with emblems of the cross.

    The Bible (KJV), Matthew 27:29; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  9. Love the fire l. 19

    The line reuses the commonplace of the love lyric, the lover's burning heart and smoking sighs, and gives it to Christ, whose love for humanity is the true fire. Compare Romeo's 'Love is a smoke made with the fume of sighs' (Romeo and Juliet 1.1). Southwell's project of rescuing poetry from profane love for divine subjects is visible here. The smoke of sighs is the visible sign of the invisible fire.

    William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 1.1 (Folger Shakespeare); James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  10. Justice and Mercy ll. 21-22

    Justice lays on the fuel (the demands of divine justice that require sacrifice) while Mercy blows the coals (fans the fire of love). The pairing recalls the medieval allegory of the Four Daughters of God, Mercy, Truth, Justice and Peace, based on Psalm 85:10 ('Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other'), in which the virtues debate humanity's fate and are reconciled by the Incarnation. Here both work together in the same furnace.

    The Bible (KJV), Psalm 85:10; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  11. The metal refined ll. 23-24

    The furnace is now a smelter or forge, and the 'metal ... wrought' (worked, refined) is 'men's defiled souls'. The image draws on Malachi 3:3, where God 'shall sit as a refiner and purifier of silver', purifying his people. Christ's suffering is the means of their purification: he burns 'To work them to their good'. The process of refining by fire was a familiar image of spiritual purification in devotional writing.

    The Bible (KJV), Malachi 3:3; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  12. The bath of blood ll. 27-28

    The final metamorphosis: fire becomes liquid, a bath in which the child will 'wash them in my blood'. The idea derives from Revelation 1:5 ('washed us from our sins in his own blood') and 7:14 ('washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb'). Readers often hear an allusion to baptism and the Eucharist, the sacraments by which Catholics believed Christ's blood was applied to the soul. The logic of the poem moves from fire to water to blood.

    The Bible (KJV), Revelation 1:5; The Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:14; James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967)

  13. Christmas Day ll. 29-32

    The vision 'vanish'd out of sight / And swiftly shrunk away', and the speaker 'straight' (immediately) recalls 'That it was Christmas Day'. The ending is a quiet recognition that turns the poem into a meditation on the feast: the newborn child is already the suffering Saviour, and Christmas means sacrifice. The plain last line can be read as anticlimax or, more often, as deliberate understatement. The poem sits beside Southwell's companion Nativity poems such as 'New Prince, New Pomp'.

    James H. McDonald and Nancy Pollard Brown (eds), The Poems of Robert Southwell, S.J. (Oxford, 1967); Ben Jonson, Conversations with William Drummond of Hawthornden (1619); A. B. Grosart (ed.), The Complete Poems of Robert Southwell (1872)
