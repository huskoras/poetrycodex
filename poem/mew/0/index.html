The Farmer's Bride

by Charlotte Mew 1869–1928

From The Farmer's Bride (1921 edition)

RelationshipsLove

Three Summers since I chose a maid,
Too young maybe--but more’s to do
At harvest-time than bide and woo.
When us was wed she turned afraid
Of love and me and all things human;
Like the shut of a winter’s day.
Her smile went out, and ’twasn’t a woman--
More like a little frightened fay.
One night, in the Fall, she runned away.

“Out ’mong the sheep, her be,” they said,
’Should properly have been abed;
But sure enough she wasn’t there
Lying awake with her wide brown stare.
So over seven-acre field and up-along across the down
We chased her, flying like a hare
Before our lanterns. To Church-Town
All in a shiver and a scare
We caught her, fetched her home at last
And turned the key upon her, fast.

She does the work about the house
As well as most, but like a mouse:
Happy enough to chat and play
With birds and rabbits and such as they,
So long as men-folk keep away.
“Not near, not near!” her eyes beseech
When one of us comes within reach.
The women say that beasts in stall
Look round like children at her call.
I’ve hardly heard her speak at all.

Shy as a leveret, swift as he,
Straight and slight as a young larch tree,
Sweet as the first wild violets, she,
To her wild self. But what to me?
The short days shorten and the oaks are brown,
The blue smoke rises to the low grey sky,
One leaf in the still air falls slowly down,
A magpie’s spotted feathers lie
On the black earth spread white with rime,
The berries redden up to Christmas-time.
What’s Christmas-time without there be
Some other in the house than we!

She sleeps up in the attic there
Alone, poor maid. ’Tis but a stair
Betwixt us. Oh! my God! the down,
The soft young down of her, the brown,
The brown of her--her eyes, her hair, her hair!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A farmer's own account of the young wife who, 'afraid of love and me and all things human', ran from him, was fetched home and locked up, and now sleeps alone in the attic. The poem ends in his helpless longing. The title poem of Mew's book.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and voice l. 1

    The poem made Mew's name when H. W. Massingham printed it in The Nation in 1912. Harold Monro's Poetry Bookshop issued The Farmer's Bride as a small book in 1916; the 1921 edition added eleven poems (published in America as Saturday Market). Thomas Hardy, who noticed the book in 1918 and befriended her, was foremost among her admirers. The poem is a dramatic monologue: the farmer tells his story without understanding all it reveals, a form Mew shared with Browning and Hardy. 'Three Summers since' places the marriage three years back, and 'chose' shows that the bride had little say.

    Charlotte Mew, Collected Poems and Prose, ed. Val Warner (Carcanet/Virago, 1981); The Farmer's Bride (poem), Wikipedia; New York Review of Books, 17 December 2020, review of Charlotte Mew: Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus; Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021)

  2. Metre and rhyme l. 2

    Mew's stanzas are irregular (9, 10, 10, 12 and 5 lines) and rhyme in couplets and cross-rhymes as the speaker's thought dictates. The base is a loose four-stress line close to speech. In stanza 4 the lines swell ('The short days shorten and the oaks are brown') into long, slow lines of landscape, before the final stanza's broken exclamations. Editors such as Warner and Copus note Mew's use of line length and indentation as a kind of score for the speaking voice.

    Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes; Charlotte Mew, Collected Poems and Prose, ed. Val Warner (Carcanet/Virago, 1981)

  3. 'bide and woo' l. 3

    'Bide' (to wait, stay) is old and survives in dialect. The farmer's excuse, that there is more to do at harvest than courting, is offered as common sense, but it tells us the girl was married without being won. The admission 'Too young maybe' is the first sign of the speaker's half-conscious guilt. It also places the poem in the farming year that governs the whole story, from harvest to Christmas.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bide'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  4. Dialect grammar l. 4

    In south-western English dialects object pronouns often serve as subjects ('us was', 'her be'), and strong verbs can take regular endings ('runned'). The dialect recalls the Wessex voices of Hardy and William Barnes. The dialect is used sparingly, enough to mark the speaker as a countryman without parody. Readers should note that the dialect marks class and place, not stupidity: the farmer's landscape writing in stanza 4 is among the finest in the poem.

    Wikipedia, 'The Farmer's Bride'; Joseph Wright, The English Dialect Grammar (Oxford, 1905); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  5. 'afraid of love' l. 5

    The line is the key to her character. Critics divide on emphasis: some read a portrait of sexual fear in a child-bride; feminist readers stress that she was married before she was ready and then imprisoned; others connect the poem with Mew's own fears and her sense of sexual difference, which Fitzgerald and Copus discuss biographically. The farmer reports the fear without understanding it.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); Penelope Fitzgerald, Charlotte Mew and Her Friends (Collins, 1984); New York Review of Books, 17 December 2020, review of Charlotte Mew: Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus

  6. 'the shut of a winter's day' l. 6

    The 'shut' of the day, its shutting-in at evening, is an old expression recorded in the OED and in dialect. The simile ties the bride to the seasons, as the whole poem does: she withdraws as daylight fails, and stanza 4 is set in the shortening days before Christmas. 'Her smile went out' continues the image of a light put out.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shut'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  7. fay l. 8

    'Fay' is a literary and archaic word for a fairy. The farmer says she was not a woman but something from the other world. Folklore of changelings and fairy brides who cannot live among mortals lies behind the image, and it anticipates the animal similes (hare, mouse, leveret) that follow. The farmer reaches for fairy-tale language because he has no other words for what has happened to his marriage, and the word quietly removes his wife from the human world in which he could claim her.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fay'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  8. 'runned' l. 9

    The regularised past tense ('runned' for 'ran') belongs to country speech. 'In the Fall' (autumn) is old English usage, now more common in America. Indenting the line sets the event apart: the turning point of the story is told in one short, flat line. The short, flat sentence after the long build-up of the stanza works almost like a news report, and the second stanza then tells the chase in vivid detail, as if the farmer were reliving it.

    Joseph Wright, The English Dialect Grammar (Oxford, 1905); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  9. 'up-along across the down' l. 14

    'Up-along' and 'down-along' are south-western expressions for going up or down the road or hill. The 'down' is open upland pasture (Old English dun, 'hill'), the landscape of Wessex. The word returns at the end in a different sense, 'the soft young down of her', so that the land she fled across and her body are joined in one word.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'down'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  10. The hunted hare l. 15

    Hares are hunted and coursed; the image makes the men a hunting party and the bride their quarry. The farmer does not see the cruelty in his own comparison, and the dramatic irony is strong. The animal images (hare, mouse, leveret) consistently make her a wild creature, a view both affectionate and dehumanising. The lanterns place the chase at night, which makes the image of quarry and pursuers stronger still.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  11. Church-Town l. 16

    In Cornish and western usage a 'churchtown' is the hamlet round a parish church, which need not be the largest place in the parish. The chase runs across the farm's fields and the down to the church village. The capitals make it a proper name, typical of the speaker's local world. Reaching the village also means reaching the church where they were married, a quiet irony in the geography of the chase.

    'Churchtown, Cornwall', Wikipedia (place-name usage); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  12. Imprisonment l. 19

    'Fast' means securely. The rhyme last/fast closes the stanza like the door. Readers often compare the locked-up wife with the madwoman in the attic of Jane Eyre, and the final stanza places her 'up in the attic'. Mew's own family knew confinement: two of her siblings spent their lives in mental institutions, which Fitzgerald and Copus treat as background to her poems of shut-in women.

    Penelope Fitzgerald, Charlotte Mew and Her Friends (Collins, 1984); Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021)

  13. 'Not near, not near!' l. 25

    The bride never speaks in the poem: her plea is in her eyes, and the farmer says 'I've hardly heard her speak at all'. She is at ease with birds and rabbits but terrified of 'men-folk'. The phrase is set in quotation marks, as if her look had a voice, which shows how closely the farmer watches her. Her silence, and the farmer's fluent report of it, is the central asymmetry of the monologue.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  14. Beasts at her call l. 27

    The report, from 'the women', gives her a kind of power over animals, like a saint or a fairy woman. Some readers also hear a faint suggestion of the animals of the Nativity stable, which would prepare for the 'Christmas-time' of stanza 4, though this is an association rather than a certain allusion. The image also continues the contrast between her ease with creatures and her terror of men.

    Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes; Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021)

  15. leveret l. 30

    The OED defines a leveret as a hare in its first year. The stanza opens with a run of similes in praise (leveret, larch, violets) and then turns: 'To her wild self. But what to me?' Here the farmer's praise of her beauty changes into a sense of his own exclusion, the emotional turn of the poem. The young larch and the first wild violets keep the praise in the language of the countryside, the only vocabulary of beauty the farmer has.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'leveret'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  16. The turn l. 33

    The two short sentences break the flowing similes. 'To her wild self' admits that she is complete in her own wildness; 'But what to me?' is the farmer's first open self-pity. Many readers find here the poem's balance of sympathy: we are moved both by her refusal and by his longing. A single rhyme (he/tree/she/me) binds the four lines together.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  17. The winter landscape l. 34

    The lines lengthen to five and six beats, and the stanza becomes a still life of approaching winter that expresses the farmer's desolation without stating it. A magpie's scattered feathers on the black earth hint at a small death. The berries 'redden up to Christmas-time', and Christmas turns his thoughts to a household with 'some other' in it. The landscape becomes a language for feeling he cannot otherwise put into words.

    Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes; Charlotte Mew, Collected Poems and Prose, ed. Val Warner (Carcanet/Virago, 1981)

  18. rime l. 38

    The OED gives 'rime' as hoarfrost, from Old English hrim. The black earth spread white with frost echoes the black-and-white plumage of the magpie in the line above, a careful piece of colour design within the stanza. The whiteness of frost also continues the poem's movement into winter, and the contrast of black and white anticipates the closing contrast of the brown of her eyes and hair against the farmer's darkening mood.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rime'; Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes

  19. The wish for a child l. 41

    The line is delicately indirect: 'some other' may mean a child, or simply company. Either way, the marriage has never become a family. The ungrammatical 'without there be' is dialect for 'unless there is'. The question, closed with an exclamation mark, is the farmer's most direct statement of loneliness before the confession of desire in the final stanza. Christmas, the feast of a child's birth, sharpens the sense of absence.

    Charlotte Mew, Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus (Faber, 2019), notes; Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021)

  20. The attic and the stair l. 42

    ''Tis but a stair / Betwixt us' measures the gap exactly: so near, so impossible. The archaic 'betwixt' belongs to the farmer's speech. The attic recalls Bertha Mason's confinement in Jane Eyre, and the line suggests both the farmer's restraint and the risk that he may not go on restraining himself; critics have read the ending as tender and as menacing.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); New York Review of Books, 17 December 2020, review of Charlotte Mew: Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus

  21. The ending l. 46

    'Down' now means the fine soft hair of her skin, recalling the down they chased her across; 'brown' repeats her 'wide brown stare' and the brown oaks. The repetitions and broken syntax show desire overwhelming speech. Some readers feel sympathy for an inarticulate, frustrated man; others hear violence about to cross the stair. A reviewer of Copus's biography calls the poem 'a brilliant piece of imaginative ventriloquism' (London Grip), in which Mew voices a man's longing for a woman.

    Julia Copus, This Rare Spirit: A Life of Charlotte Mew (Faber, 2021); London Grip, review of Copus, This Rare Spirit (2022); New York Review of Books, 17 December 2020, review of Charlotte Mew: Selected Poetry and Prose, ed. Julia Copus; Penelope Fitzgerald, Charlotte Mew and Her Friends (Collins, 1984)
