Religion

Recessional

by Rudyard Kipling 1865–1936

From The Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912)

Religion

God of our fathers, known of old —
Lord of our far-flung battle-line —
Beneath whose awful Hand we hold
Dominion over palm and pine —
Lord God of Hosts, be with us yet,
Lest we forget, lest we forget!

The tumult and the shouting dies —
The captains and the kings depart —
Still stands Thine ancient sacrifice,
An humble and a contrite heart.
Lord God of Hosts, be with us yet,
Lest we forget, lest we forget!

Far-call'd our navies melt away —
On dune and headland sinks the fire —
Lo, all our pomp of yesterday
Is one with Nineveh and Tyre!
Judge of the Nations, spare us yet,
Lest we forget, lest we forget!

If, drunk with sight of power, we loose
Wild tongues that have not Thee in awe-
Such boasting as the Gentiles use
Or lesser breeds without the Law —
Lord God of Hosts, be with us yet,
Lest we forget, lest we forget!

For heathen heart that puts her trust
In reeking tube and iron shard —
All valiant dust that builds on dust,
And guarding calls not Thee to guard —
For frantic boast and foolish word,
Thy Mercy on Thy People, Lord!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and occasion l. 1

    A 'recessional' is the hymn sung at the end of a service as the clergy and choir process out, the opposite of a processional. Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee (sixty years' reign) was celebrated in June 1897 with a procession in London of colonial premiers and troops from across the Empire and a vast naval review at Spithead. Kipling wrote the poem as the celebrations ended; it was printed in The Times on 17 July 1897, and he took no payment. Instead of triumph it offers a penitential warning that empire is transient and accountable to God. It became a much-sung hymn, used for instance at the burial of the Unknown Warrior in 1920. The opening 'God of our fathers' is a biblical formula (e.g. Exodus 3:15; Deuteronomy 26:7).

    Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Wikipedia, 'Recessional (poem)'; Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:15

  2. Hymn form and refrain ll. 5-6

    The poem is written as a hymn: five stanzas of iambic tetrameter rhyming ababcc, the measure of many English hymns, and it was later sung to tunes such as 'Melita'. Each of the first four stanzas closes with a couplet refrain; in stanza 3 'Lord God of Hosts' becomes 'Judge of the Nations, spare us yet'. In the last stanza the refrain disappears and the poem ends with a direct prayer, 'Thy Mercy on Thy People, Lord!' The early printings ended with 'Amen', which Kipling dropped in the Sussex Edition, where he added the subtitle '(After Queen Victoria's Jubilee)'. The dashes that end many of the lines give the stanzas the cumulative, breathless syntax of invocation.

    Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Wikipedia, 'Recessional (poem)'

  3. far-flung battle-line l. 2

    The phrase pictures the British Empire's dispersed military frontier (India's North-West Frontier, Africa, the seas) as a single 'battle-line', with God as its commander. The Diamond Jubilee had displayed this reach, with troops from many colonies marching in London. The phrase entered common usage. Note that the poem acknowledges imperial power as God-given while immediately subordinating it to his 'awful Hand'.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  4. awful l. 3

    'Awful' keeps its older sense: inspiring awe, reverential fear. The 'Hand' of God is a frequent biblical metonymy for divine power (e.g. Deuteronomy 26:8, 'the LORD brought us forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand'). Britain holds dominion only 'beneath' that hand, a posture of subordination that sets the tone for the poem's penitence. Compare 'Wild tongues that have not Thee in awe' in stanza 4.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'awful'; Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 26:8

  5. Dominion over palm and pine l. 4

    The alliterating pair is a synecdoche for the Empire's geographical range, from the palms of India, Africa and the Caribbean to the pine forests of Canada. 'Dominion' recalls God's grant to humanity to 'have dominion' over the earth (Genesis 1:26), implying that rule is a trust from God. It also had a political sense: Canada had become the 'Dominion of Canada' in 1867, and the word was later used for the self-governing colonies generally.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:26; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dominion'

  6. Lord God of Hosts l. 5

    'Lord God of Hosts' translates the Hebrew title of God as lord of 'hosts' (tseva'ot), the armies of heaven and of Israel, as in Psalm 80:4 and Amos. The English Te Deum has 'Lord God of Sabaoth'. The title suits a poem about military power: Britain's armies and navies are not ultimately her own, and God is the true commander of the 'battle-line'. 'Be with us yet' asks that God remain with the nation still, a prayer more anxious than confident.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 80:4; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  7. Lest we forget l. 6

    The refrain comes from Moses' warning to Israel that in prosperity they might forget the God who freed them: 'Then beware lest thou forget the LORD, which brought thee forth out of the land of Egypt' (Deuteronomy 6:12; compare 8:11-14). The object of forgetting in the poem is God and the humility owed to him, not, as later usage suggests, the dead. After the First World War 'Lest we forget' was detached from its context and inscribed on war memorials across the Empire and is still spoken at Remembrance and Anzac Day ceremonies, where the hymn is sung.

    Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 6:12; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Wikipedia, 'Recessional (poem)'

  8. The tumult and the shouting dies l. 7

    The Jubilee's celebrations are over. Kipling's words echo the war-horse of Job 39:25, which 'smelleth the battle afar off, the thunder of the captains, and the shouting', hence also 'The captains and the kings depart' in the next line, which refers to the departing colonial leaders, foreign royalty and troops. The singular verb 'dies' with a double subject treats 'the tumult and the shouting' as one event. The line has become proverbial for the ending of any brief glory.

    Bible (KJV), Job 39:25; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes

  9. Thine ancient sacrifice, / An humble and a contrite heart ll. 9-10

    Psalm 51, David's psalm of repentance, says that God does not desire burnt offerings: 'The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise' (51:17). Kipling's 'humble and a contrite heart' blends this with Isaiah 57:15, where God dwells 'with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit'. 'Contrite' (Latin contritus, crushed) means penitent. The 'ancient sacrifice' that 'still stands' when the pageant is gone is inward humility, the one offering that outlasts pomp. Psalm 51 is a standard penitential psalm, so the stanza turns the Jubilee hymn into a confession.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 51:17; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 57:15; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  10. Far-call'd our navies melt away l. 13

    On 26 June 1897 a great naval review was held at Spithead, off Portsmouth, with some 165 Royal Navy ships on display; Kipling saw it. 'Far-call'd' means summoned to far-off stations: the warships dispersed after the review. 'Melt away' describes ships vanishing over the horizon, but it also hints at a more permanent disappearance of power, preparing for the comparison with Nineveh and Tyre two lines later. The shift is characteristic of the poem: a literal detail of the celebrations becomes an emblem of transience.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  11. On dune and headland sinks the fire l. 14

    Chains of beacon bonfires were lit across Britain and the Empire on the night of the Jubilee, 22 June 1897, on hilltops, dunes and headlands, as in older times of national celebration or alarm (as in the Armada beacons). The sinking fire is both literal and symbolic: the blaze of national glory dies down. Alliterating 'dune' and 'headland' covers the island's coast, matching the global 'palm and pine' of stanza 1.

    Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (Hodder & Stoughton, 1914), on 'Recessional'

  12. Nineveh and Tyre l. 16

    Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian empire, was destroyed by Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC; the prophets Nahum and Zephaniah foretold its fall ('Nineveh is laid waste', Nahum 3:7; Zephaniah 2:13). Tyre, the rich Phoenician sea-power, was the subject of Ezekiel's prophecy of ruin (Ezekiel 26-28) and was taken by Alexander the Great in 332 BC. Both stood in the Bible for wealth, commerce and arrogance brought low, a pointed comparison for a commercial and maritime empire. 'Our pomp of yesterday' may echo Psalm 90:4, where a thousand years in God's sight are 'but as yesterday'. Allen Drury later took a novel title, Come Nineveh, Come Tyre (1973), from this line.

    Bible (KJV), Nahum 3:7; Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 26:3; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes

  13. Judge of the Nations l. 17

    After the vision of fallen empires the refrain changes: God is now the 'Judge of the Nations', who weighs peoples as he judged Nineveh and Tyre, and the plea becomes 'spare us yet'. The title recalls Abraham's question, 'Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?' (Genesis 18:25), and the Psalms' God who 'shall judge the world with righteousness, and the people with his truth' (Psalm 96:13). The variation shows the poem moving from confident prayer for presence to fear of judgement.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 18:25; Bible (KJV), Psalms 96:13; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  14. drunk with sight of power ... Wild tongues ll. 19-20

    Stanza 4 names the sin the poem fears: pride. 'Drunk with sight of power' figures the imperial spectacle of the Jubilee as an intoxicant, and 'Wild tongues that have not Thee in awe' are boastful voices, journalists, politicians, crowds, that forget God. The word 'jingoism', from a music-hall song of 1878 ('We don't want to fight but by Jingo if we do'), named exactly this kind of bellicose patriotism. Kipling, often thought of as its laureate, here condemns it, which is one reason Orwell argued the poem had been misread.

    Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); George Orwell, 'Rudyard Kipling' (Horizon, February 1942), Orwell Foundation

  15. Such boasting as the Gentiles use / Or lesser breeds without the Law ll. 21-22

    The 'Gentiles' are, in biblical usage, the nations other than Israel; 'without the Law' echoes Romans 2:14, 'the Gentiles, which have not the law'. Kipling casts Britain as a chosen people bound by God's law, and other nations as boasting outsiders. Many readers have heard 'lesser breeds' as contempt for non-white colonised peoples, consistent with the racial hierarchy of 'The White Man's Burden' (1899). Orwell, defending the poem in 1942, insisted that the phrase almost certainly means the Germans, 'without the Law' in the sense of lawless, not powerless. Both readings remain current: the line condemns boasting while itself drawing on a vocabulary of racial and national rank, and many critics see that tension as central to Kipling.

    Bible (KJV), Romans 2:14; George Orwell, 'Rudyard Kipling' (Horizon, February 1942), Orwell Foundation; Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  16. heathen heart that puts her trust l. 25

    The final stanza turns the charge of heathenism against Britain itself: any heart, British or not, is 'heathen' if it trusts in arms. Psalm 20:7 sets the pattern: 'Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of the LORD our God' (note 'remember', the opposite of the refrain's 'forget'). The feminine 'her' treats the nation or soul in the traditional way. Hamer reads the stanza as a direct attack on jingoism.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 20:7; Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  17. reeking tube and iron shard l. 26

    'Reeking tube' is a deliberately unheroic periphrasis for a gun barrel, smoking and stinking after firing; 'iron shard' is a fragment of shell or shrapnel. By naming weapons as mere tubes and shards, Kipling deflates the naval and military spectacle of the Jubilee: these are things, not gods, and to trust in them is idolatry. The phrase anticipates the new industrial warfare of the Boer War (1899-1902) and the First World War.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shard'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reek'; Thomas Pinney, ed., The Cambridge Edition of the Poems of Rudyard Kipling (Cambridge UP, 2013), 'Recessional'

  18. valiant dust that builds on dust ll. 27-28

    'Dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return' (Genesis 3:19): brave soldiers are still mortal clay, and the empires they build are built on dust. Shakespeare's Beatrice uses the same ironic phrase when she scorns the idea of being 'overmastered with a piece of valiant dust', a man (Much Ado About Nothing 2.1). The next line, 'guarding calls not Thee to guard', recalls Psalm 127:1: 'Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the LORD keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain'. The psalm supplies both the building and the guarding of Kipling's lines.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Bible (KJV), Psalms 127:1; Shakespeare, Much Ado About Nothing 2.1 (Folger Shakespeare); Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914)

  19. Thy Mercy on Thy People l. 30

    The last couplet breaks the pattern of the refrain: instead of 'Lest we forget', the poem ends by asking mercy 'For frantic boast and foolish word'. The structure of the final stanza is a confession: 'For [these sins] ... Thy Mercy'. It resembles the liturgical 'Lord, have mercy upon us' (Kyrie eleison), and 'Thy People' again presents the nation as a covenant people like Israel. In the Kipling Society notes Hadas reads the poem as a whole as a prayer for mercy, a penitential psalm in the manner of the Miserere. The early texts closed with 'Amen'.

    Kipling Society, 'Recessional', notes by Mary Hamer (with D. Hadas and D. Weiss), drawing on Ralph Durand, A Handbook to the Poetry of Rudyard Kipling (1914); Representative Poetry Online (ed. Ian Lancashire), 'Recessional', notes
