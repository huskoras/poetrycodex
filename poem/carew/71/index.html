Death & Dying

An Elegie upon the death of Doctor Donne, Deane of Pauls.

by Thomas Carew 1595–1640

Death & Dying

CAn we not force from widowed Poetrie
Now thou art dead (Great Donne) one Elegie,
To crowne thy Hearse? Why yet did we not trust,
Though with unkneaded dow-bak'd prose, thy dust,
Such as th'uncizard Lect'rer from the flower
Of fading Rhet'rique, short-liv'd as his houre,
Drie as the sand that measures it, might lay
Vpon the ashes, on the Funerall day?
Have we nor tune, nor voyce? didst thou dispence
Through all our languge both the words and sence?
'Tis a sad truth The Pulpit may her plaine,
And sober Christian precepts still retaine,
Doctrines it may, and wholsome uses, frame,
Grave Homilies, and Lectures, but the flame
Of thy brave soule, that shot such heat, and light,
As burnt our Earth, and made our darknesse brighs,
Committed holy rapes upon the will,
Did through the eye the melting heart distill.
And the deepe knowledge, of darke truths, so teach
As sence might judge where fancy could not reach,
Must be desir'd for ever. So the fire
That fills with spirit and heate the Delphique Quire,
Which kindled first by thy Promethean breath
Glow'd here a while, lyes quencht now in thy death.
The Moses garden, with Pedantique weedes
O're-spread, was purg'd by thee, the lazie seeds
Of servile imitation throwne away,
And fresh invention planted; thou did'st pay
The debts of our penurious banquerout Age:
Licentious thefts, that make poetique rage
A mimique furie, when our soules must be
Possest, or with Anacrcons extasie,
Or Pindars, not their owne, the subtle cheate
Of flie exchanges, and the jugling seate
Of two-edg'd words, or whatsoever wrong
By ours was done the Greeke or Latine tongue,
Thouhast redeem'd, and Opened us a Mine
Of rich and pregnant fancie, drawne a line
Of Masculine expression, which had good
Old Orpheus seene, or all the ancient brood
Our superstitious fooles admire, and hold
Their Leade more precious then thy burnisht gold,
Thou hadst beene their Exchequer, and no more,
They each in others dung had search'd for Ore.
Thou shalt yeeld no precedence, but of Time,
And the blind fate of Language, whose tun'd chime
More charmes the outward sense; yet thou mayst claime
From so great disadvantage, greater fame,
Since to the awe of thy imperious wit
Our troublesome language bends, made only fit
With her tough thick-rib'd hoopes, to gird about
Thy Gyant fancie, which had prov'd too stout
For their soft melting phrases. As in time
They had the start, so did they cull the prime
Buds of invention many a hundred yeare,
And left the rifled fields, besides the seate
To touch their harvest, yet from those bare lands
Of what was onely thine, thy onely hands
(And that their smallest worke) have gleaned more
Then all those times, and Tongues, could reape before.

But thou art gone, and thy strickt lawes will be
Too hard for Libertines in Poetrie,
They will recall the goodly exil'd ttaine
Of Gods, and Goddesses, which in thy just raigne
Was banisht nobler Poems; now, with these,
The silenc'd tales i'th' Metamorphoses
Shall stuffe their lines, and swell the windie page
Till verse resin'd by thee, in this last Age
Turne Ballad-rime, or those old Idols be
Ador'd againe with new Apostasie.

Oh! pardon me that breake with untun'd Verse
The reverend silence, that attends thy Hearse,
Whose solemne, awfull Murmurs, were to thee
More then these rude lines, a loude Elegie,
That did proclaime in a dumbe Eloquence
The death of all the Arts, whose influence
Growne feeble, in these panting numbers lyes
Gasping short-winded accents, and so dyes.
So doth the swiftly-tuning wheele, not stand
In th'instant we withdraw the moving hand,
But some short time retaine a saint weake course,
By vertue of the first impulsive force;
And so, whilst I cast on thy funerall stile,
Thy crowne of Bayes, oh let it crack awhile
And spit disdaine, till the devouring flashes
Suck all the moysture up, then turne to ashes,

I will not draw the envy, to engrosse
All thy perfections, or weepe all the losse,
Those are too numerous for one Elegie,
And this too great to be exprest by me.
Let others carve the rest; it shall suffize,
I on thy Grave this Epitaph incize.
Here lyes a King, that rul'd as he thought fit
The Vniversall Monarchie of wit,
Here lyes two Flamens, and both those the best.
Apollo's first, at last the true God's Priest.

Study notes by the editors

  1. An Elegy upon the Death of Doctor Donne title; ll. 1-3

    John Donne, poet and from 1621 Dean of St Paul's, died on 31 March 1631. Thomas Carew (1595-1640), courtier and poet, wrote this elegy, which was printed with other tributes in the first edition of Donne's Poems (1633) and again in Carew's own Poems (1640). Poetry is 'widowed' because Donne, her husband, is dead; the opening question complains that no adequate elegy has yet appeared. The poem is one of the first critical assessments of Donne's 'wit' and his reform of English verse, and is frequently quoted in histories of the 'metaphysical' style.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  2. unkneaded dough-baked prose ll. 3-4

    'Dough-baked' meant imperfectly baked, still doughy, and figuratively dull or half-witted (OED, dough-baked, a.). 'Unkneaded' adds that the material was never worked into shape. Carew asks why the poets have not trusted Donne's dust even to such inferior offerings as the prose of funeral sermons, implying that poets have been even more silent than preachers. The homely baking image, typical of Donne's own mixed diction, is set against the elevated elegiac occasion.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  3. the unscissored lecturer l. 5

    RPO glosses 'unscissor'd' as having uncut hair. A 'lecturer' was a preacher, often funded by a parish or town to give sermons in addition to the incumbent's services; such lectureships were frequently held by Puritans. Readers therefore sometimes take the jibe as aimed at a Puritan type, though the word itself proves only untidiness. The 'flower / Of fading Rhet'rique' is the flowery but perishable ornament of his style. The archive spelling 'uncizard' preserves an early form.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium

  4. the pulpit hourglass l. 7

    Early modern pulpits often had an hourglass fixed to them so that preacher and congregation could see the sermon's length, normally an hour (hence 'short-liv'd as his houre'). RPO identifies the sand as the hourglass's. The simile neatly unites dryness of style with the passing of time, and contrasts the measured, mechanical sermon with Donne's 'flame'. The image also hints at mortality, appropriate to a funeral sermon.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Izaak Walton, The Life of Dr. John Donne (1640; rev. 1658, 1675)

  5. what the Pulpit retains ll. 11-21

    The sentence is long and the archive lacks a comma after 'truth'. 'Her plaine, / And sober Christian precepts' are the plain moral teaching that the pulpit can keep without Donne. Carew's point is that doctrine, 'wholsome uses', homilies and lectures remain available to any preacher, whereas the 'flame' of Donne's soul, which moved the hearer's will through the eye and heart, has died with him. The passage is often quoted in discussions of how Donne's preaching and his poetry belong together.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  6. doctrines and uses l. 13

    William Perkins's influential manual of preaching (Latin 1592, English 1607) taught preachers to open the biblical text, collect doctrines from it, and apply them in 'uses', practical applications to the hearers' lives; the method became standard, especially among Puritans (OED, use, n., sense of application of doctrine in a sermon). 'Homilies' recalls the official Books of Homilies, and 'Lectures' were sermons outside the regular service (RPO). Carew lists the routine products of the pulpit to set off the power of Donne's preaching.

    William Perkins, The Arte of Prophecying (Latin 1592; English 1607), on dividing a sermon into doctrine and 'use'; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  7. the flame of Donne's soul ll. 14-16

    Heat and light are traditional figures of zeal and illumination, and Pentecostal fire (Acts 2:3) of inspired preaching. 'Brave' means splendid, excellent. The archive's 'brighs' should read 'bright' (as in the Vincent text), restoring the rhyme with 'light'. Walton, who heard Donne preach, describes his sermons in similar terms of ardour, weeping and persuasion. The fire imagery runs throughout the elegy, through 'Promethean breath' to the burning laurel at the end.

    Izaak Walton, The Life of Dr. John Donne (1640; rev. 1658, 1675); Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611

  8. holy rapes upon the will l. 17

    'Rape' kept its Latin sense of seizure and carrying off (rapere), as in 'rapture', but the sexual sense was also present. The bold oxymoron echoes Donne's Holy Sonnet 'Batter my heart', which ends by asking God to 'ravish' him, since only so can he be chaste. Carew implies that Donne's sermons did to their hearers what Donne prayed God would do to him. The following line, 'through the eye the melting heart distill', uses alchemical language: the heart is melted and drawn off in tears.

    John Donne, Holy Sonnet 'Batter my heart, three person'd God', ll. 13-14, in Grierson (1912), vol. 1; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  9. dark truths and fancy ll. 19-21

    'Sence' here means the bodily senses or plain understanding, and 'fancy' the imagination. Carew's claim is that Donne made difficult mysteries apprehensible, the reverse of what one might expect: he did not merely imagine beyond sense, but made the senses judge where even imagination failed. Later critics such as T. S. Eliot made a similar point about Donne's unified sensibility, his ability to feel thought. 'Must be desir'd for ever' (l. 21) closes the long sentence: this power is lost for good.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633)

  10. Delphic choir; Promethean breath ll. 22-24

    Delphi was Apollo's chief oracle, and Apollo was god of poetry; the Delphic 'quire' (choir) is therefore the poets. Prometheus stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity (Hesiod, Theogony and Works and Days), and in some versions also shaped humans from clay. Donne is thus a culture-hero who brought new fire to English poetry; now that fire 'lyes quencht' in his death. The claim that Donne originated a poetic school is one of the elegy's influential judgements.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Hesiod, Theogony 561-69 and Works and Days 47-58 (Prometheus' theft of fire); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  11. the Muses' garden ll. 25-28

    The 1633 text and modern editions read 'Muses' garden', the garden of poetry; 'Moses' here is a mistake in the archive's source. Donne 'purg'd' it of 'Pedantique weedes' (learned but lifeless borrowings) and 'the lazie seeds / Of servile imitation', replacing them with 'fresh invention' (in rhetoric, the finding of matter and argument). Carew's attack on 'servile imitation' is pointed, since Renaissance poetics, including Jonson's, prized the imitation of classical models. Here originality becomes the highest praise.

    Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  12. the bankrupt age ll. 28-29

    'Banquerout' is an early form of 'bankrupt', from Italian banca rotta, the broken bench of a failed money-changer (OED, bankrupt). The financial metaphor runs through the passage: the age lives by 'thefts', Donne opens 'a Mine' of riches (l. 37), his gold is set against others' lead (l. 42), and he could have been the ancients' 'Exchequer' (l. 43). The economics of borrowing and originality is the elegy's central critical argument.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  13. Anacreon and Pindar ll. 30-33

    Anacreon (6th century BC) wrote light lyrics of wine and love, much imitated in the Renaissance from the pseudo-Anacreontic Anacreontea; Pindar (5th century BC) wrote lofty odes for athletic victors. RPO notes that both were considered great masters of the Greek ode. 'Poetique rage' is the Platonic furor poeticus, the divine madness of inspiration; Carew argues that imitators merely mimic it, being possessed by a borrowed ecstasy 'not their owne'. The archive spelling 'Anacrcons' is a typographical error.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium

  14. sly exchanges and two-edged words ll. 33-36

    RPO glosses the wrong done to Greek and Latin (l. 36) as 'by borrowing Greek and Latin words'. The 'subtle cheate / Of slie exchanges' (the archive's 'flie' is a misreading of the long s in 'slie') suggests passing off foreign words as English, while 'jugling seate' (juggling feat, a conjuring trick) and 'two-edg'd words' point to ambiguous coinages that cut both ways. 'Whatsoever wrong / By ours was done the Greeke or Latine tongue' sums up this as plunder of the classics, which Donne has 'redeem'd' (in the financial sense of buying back a pledge).

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  15. masculine expression ll. 37-39

    'Masculine' in seventeenth-century criticism described a vigorous, compressed, argumentative style opposed to smooth, 'soft' or 'effeminate' sweetness (compare 'soft melting phrases', l. 53). The term belongs to the vocabulary of 'strong lines' in which Donne's admirers praised him. Modern critics have examined the gendered assumptions behind it. 'Pregnant fancie' (fertile imagination) mixes the metaphor oddly but tellingly with 'Masculine', and 'drawne a line' plays on a line of verse, a line of descent and a line drawn as a standard.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  16. Orpheus and the ancient brood ll. 39-42

    Orpheus, the legendary Thracian singer whose music moved stones and trees and who descended to the underworld for Eurydice, was the archetype of the poet (RPO). 'All the ancient brood / Our superstitious fooles admire' are the classical poets venerated by slavish modern imitators, who 'hold / Their Leade more precious then thy burnisht gold'. Carew here inverts the usual Renaissance hierarchy, ranking the modern Donne above the ancients: a bold move in the quarrel of ancients and moderns.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Ovid, Metamorphoses, Latin text

  17. Exchequer; ore in dung ll. 43-44

    The Exchequer was the royal treasury and revenue court of England. The second line alludes to the story, preserved in the Suetonius-Donatus Life of Virgil, that when asked why he was reading Ennius, Virgil said he was gathering gold from Ennius' dung: i.e. salvaging fine things from a rough predecessor. Carew turns the anecdote into a jibe: the ancient poets borrowed from each other's leavings, whereas Donne mined his own gold.

    Aelius Donatus, Life of Virgil (the anecdote that Virgil, reading Ennius, said he was gathering gold from Ennius' dung); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  18. the fate of language ll. 45-48

    The ancients precede him only in time, and in the 'blind fate' that gave them languages whose 'tun'd chime' (musical sound, perhaps quantitative metre) charms the 'outward sense' (the ear) more than English. Carew argues that overcoming such a disadvantage earns Donne greater fame. The passage is an early example of the self-conscious comparison of English with the classical languages that runs from the sixteenth-century defences of the vernacular to Dryden.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633)

  19. the hooped language and giant fancy ll. 49-52

    The image is of a cooper's barrel: the language's 'tough thick-rib'd hoopes' gird or bind Donne's 'Gyant fancie' (huge imagination), which would have burst the 'soft melting phrases' of Greek and Latin. 'Imperious' (commanding, masterful) keeps up the elegy's language of rule that culminates in the epitaph's 'King'. The lines themselves enact Donne's style, with knotted, consonantal monosyllables and strong enjambment.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  20. rifled fields and gleaning ll. 52-60

    'Cull the prime / Buds' means select the best blossoms; 'rifled fields' are plundered ones. Gleaning was the right of the poor to gather what the reapers left (Ruth 2; Leviticus 19:9-10). Carew's paradox is that Donne, coming late to exhausted ground, found more with his 'onely hands' (his hands alone, 'and that their smallest worke') than all earlier ages and languages reaped. The phrase 'besides the seate / To touch their harvest' is difficult; the sense seems to be that the ancients left the fields stripped, with only the bare ground remaining.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium

  21. libertines in poetry ll. 61-62

    A 'libertine' was a freethinker in religion or a dissolute person; here, a poet who takes liberties with form and matter. The phrase carries irony, since Carew himself was known for libertine love poetry such as 'A Rapture'. The 'strickt lawes' and 'just raigne' cast Donne as a lawgiver-king whose death will bring anarchy, preparing for the epitaph's 'Monarchie of wit'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  22. the exiled train of gods ll. 63-67

    Donne's poetry does indeed largely avoid the mythological machinery common in Elizabethan verse (Venus, Cupid, Ovidian narratives such as Marlowe's Hero and Leander). Carew imagines that machinery as a 'goodly' (handsome) 'traine' of exiled deities waiting to return. RPO notes that Ovid's Metamorphoses, with its lavish tales of gods and goddesses, strongly influenced sixteenth-century poetry. 'Silenc'd tales' and 'windie page' scorn such narrative as empty and inflated. The archive 'ttaine' is a typographical error for 'traine'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Ovid, Metamorphoses, Latin text; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  23. ballad-rhyme and apostasy ll. 68-70

    The archive's 'resin'd' misreads the long s of 'refin'd'. 'Ballad-rime' means the crude rhymes of popular broadside ballads, the lowest kind of verse in courtly eyes. 'Idols' and 'Apostasie' (abandonment of the true faith) apply religious language to literary history: the return of pagan gods in poetry is a relapse into idolatry, a joke made sharper by Donne's having become a priest. 'This last Age' may suggest the last age of the world, as well as the most recent.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  24. the spinning wheel ll. 79-82

    The standard text reads 'swiftly-turning wheel' (the archive's 'tuning' omits a letter, and 'saint' in l. 81 is a misprint for 'faint'). The image may be a potter's or spinning wheel. 'Impulsive' means impelling, driving forward (RPO); the idea of a moving body retaining an impressed force derives from the scholastic theory of impetus. Carew's modesty topos makes his own elegy a dying motion of Donne's art: the arts' 'influence', grown feeble, 'gasps' in his 'panting numbers' (metres).

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  25. the reverend silence ll. 71-78

    The apology is a conventional elegiac humility formula, but the paradox of a silence more eloquent than verse ('a loude Elegie', 'dumbe Eloquence') is in Donne's own manner. The 'death of all the Arts' expresses the extravagant claim that Donne's death leaves eloquence itself expiring; 'panting numbers' and 'Gasping short-winded accents' imitate the dying breath in irregular rhythm. Compare the opening question about whether poetry can produce 'one Elegie'.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633)

  26. the crackling bay crown ll. 83-86

    Bay (laurel) was sacred to Apollo and the crown of poets. In Roman practice laurel was burned in rituals, and its crackling in the flame was read as an omen (Tibullus 2.5.81-84; compare the laurel in Virgil's Eclogue 8.82-83). Carew imagines the crown on the pyre crackling as if it disdains the fire, i.e. the poetic fame of Donne resists destruction until the flames consume its moisture. The archive's 'funerall stile' is apparently an error for 'funerall pile' (pyre); the ancient image of cremation is a literary fiction, since Donne was buried in St Paul's.

    Tibullus 2.5.81-84 (the laurel that crackles well in sacred flames as a good omen), Latin text; Virgil, Eclogue 8.82-83 (laurel burned in a love charm), Latin text; Arthur Vincent, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew (London: Routledge, n.d. [1899]), text as reproduced on Luminarium

  27. engross ll. 87-90

    RPO glosses 'engross' as to draw up a complete list, the legal sense of writing a document out in large fair hand (OED, engross, v.). The word also meant to monopolise, to buy up the whole supply, which fits 'draw the envy': he will not seize all the praise for himself. 'Let others carve the rest' anticipates the epitaph by imagining tributes cut in stone; 'incize' (l. 92) means cut or engrave.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  28. the universal monarchy of wit ll. 93-94

    'Universal monarchy' was a political term for the world empire attributed to the ambitions of Spain and the Habsburgs; Carew applies it to Donne's absolute dominion over wit (intelligence, invention, ingenious conceit). 'Rul'd as he thought fit' suggests the absolutist language of Stuart kingship as well as Donne's freedom from rules. The couplet has fixed Donne's reputation as the founder and sovereign of a school of 'wit', a view later developed by Dryden and Samuel Johnson in his account of the 'metaphysical poets'.

    Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy; H. J. C. Grierson, ed., The Poems of John Donne (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), vol. 1, 'Elegies upon the Author' (Carew's elegy reprinted from 1633); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633)

  29. two Flamens ll. 95-96

    The flamines were Roman priests each dedicated to one deity, such as the Flamen Dialis of Jupiter (RPO notes that flamens were Roman priests). Carew's conclusion divides Donne's career into the secular poet ('Apollo's first') and the ordained divine (he took orders in 1615 and became Dean of St Paul's in 1621). RPO reads the line as alluding to the move from secular to religious poetry. The neat division, echoed by Walton's Life, has been both influential and disputed, since Donne's 'two' vocations overlapped.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), Carew, 'An Elegy upon the Death of the Dean of St. Paul's', ed. N. J. Endicott, from Donne's Poems (1633); Izaak Walton, The Life of Dr. John Donne (1640; rev. 1658, 1675); Rhodes Dunlap, ed., The Poems of Thomas Carew, with his Masque Coelum Britannicum (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), commentary on the Donne elegy

  30. couplets and enjambment whole poem

    The elegy is in iambic pentameter couplets, but the sense runs on across couplets and lines (e.g. ll. 11-21, one sentence), with caesuras in unexpected places, unlike the end-stopped couplets later perfected by Waller and Dryden. This 'masculine' roughness enacts the style the poem describes. Carew, often counted among the 'Cavalier' poets and influenced by Jonson, here shows his debt to Donne. Critics often read the elegy as a pivotal text between Donne's generation and the Restoration's critical vocabulary.
