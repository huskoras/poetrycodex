Love

Upon Julia's Clothes.

by Robert Herrick 1591–1674

From Hesperides (1648)

Love

WHen as in silks my Julia goes, (flowes
Ther, then (me thinks) how sweetly
That liquefaction of her clothes.
Next, when I cast mine eyes and see
That brave Vibration each way free;
O how that glittering taketh me!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Upon Julia's Clothes l. 1

    Hesperides (1648) contains many poems to Julia, the most frequent of Herrick's mistresses, who is generally taken to be a literary creation rather than a real woman. This six-line poem, one of the most anthologised in the book, describes first the flowing of her silk dress as she walks and then its shimmering when it moves. 'Whenas' means simply 'when'. The poem is often read as a study in how desire attaches to surfaces and motion rather than to the body itself.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  2. (flowes / Ther, then ll. 1-2

    In the archive's text the word 'flowes' has slipped to the end of line 1 and 'Then' has become 'Ther', apparently an error in transcription, since Herrick's rhyme scheme needs 'flows' at the end of line 2 to rhyme with 'goes' and 'clothes'. Modern editions print: 'Whenas in silks my Julia goes, / Then, then (methinks) how sweetly flows / That liquefaction of her clothes.' The repeated 'Then, then' imitates the speaker's excited attention. Students quoting the poem should use the standard lineation.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Hesperides (1648), original edition

  3. silks l. 1

    Silk was an expensive imported luxury in the seventeenth century and the mark of court dress. Its qualities, weight, drape and glitter, are what the poem attends to: silk flows like liquid when the wearer walks and catches the light when she turns. Herrick's poetry is full of loving attention to clothes, as in 'Delight in Disorder' and 'Julia's Petticoat'.

    The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Robert Herrick, 'Delight in Disorder' and 'Julia's Petticoat', Hesperides (1648)

  4. liquefaction l. 3

    'Liquefaction', from Latin liquefacere (to make liquid), was a learned word of natural philosophy and alchemy, used of melting metals and fluids. Its sudden appearance in a light lyric is the poem's most admired effect: the long Latinate word slows the line and makes the silk seem to melt and flow. Some readers hear a sexual undertone in the speaker's own 'melting'. The word's syllables also sound liquid, with l and q.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'liquefaction'; The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  5. brave Vibration l. 5

    'Brave' in the seventeenth century often meant fine, splendid, showily dressed, rather than courageous. 'Vibration', from Latin vibrare (to shake, brandish), was another learned word, used of oscillating motion; the second stanza matches the first's 'liquefaction' with it. 'Each way free' describes the dress swinging freely in every direction as she moves. The pairing of two scientific terms makes the poem a small experiment in observing light and motion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brave'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vibration'; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  6. that glittering taketh me l. 6

    The final line moves from observation to confession. 'Taketh' means captivates; the same verb ends a line of Jonson's 'Still to be neat' ('Such sweet neglect more taketh me'), and Herrick, a self-declared follower of Jonson, may be echoing it. The glitter is the play of light on silk as it moves; critics note that the speaker is enchanted by appearances, which suits Herrick's poetics of surface. The exclamation 'O' is the only overtly emotional word in the poem.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Ben Jonson, 'Still to be neat', Epicoene 1.1

  7. Form: two triplets ll. 1-6

    The poem's structure is as neat as its subject. Each three-line stanza rhymes on one sound, and each moves from a temporal clause ('When as ... Then'; 'Next, when ...') to a response. The first triplet presents flowing, the second glittering; the first is about sound and motion, the second about light. The triplet rhyme gives the poem a ringing, self-contained shape.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  8. Julia l. 1

    Julia appears in dozens of poems in Hesperides, alongside Corinna, Anthea, Electra and others. Biographers have searched for a real original without success, and most editors treat her as a literary figure in the tradition of the Roman elegists' mistresses (Catullus' Lesbia, Ovid's Corinna). The possessive 'my Julia' gives the poem its tone of intimate admiration. Herrick's Julia poems focus repeatedly on particular details, her breath, her lips, her petticoat, her clothes.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  9. me thinks l. 2

    'Methinks' (from Old English me thyncth, it seems to me) is an impersonal verb. Set in brackets, it qualifies the claim that the clothes flow: this is how it seems to the watching lover. The small hesitation makes the poem as much about the act of looking as about the dress, and readers have noted that the speaker never describes Julia herself, only the effect of her clothes on his eyes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'methinks'; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary
