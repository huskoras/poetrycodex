Love

Corinna's going a Maying.

by Robert Herrick 1591–1674

From Hesperides (1648)

Love

GEt up, get up for shame, the Blooming Morne
Upon her wings presents the god unshorne.
See how Aurora throwes her faire
Fresh-quilted colours through the aire:
Get up, sweet-Slug-a-bed, and see
The Dew-bespangling Herbe and Tree.
Each Flower has wept, and bow'd toward the East,
Above an houre since; yet you not drest,
Nay! not so much as out of bed?
When all the Birds have Mattens seyd,
And sung their thankfull Hymnes: 'tis sin,
Nay, profanation to keep in,
When as a thousand Virgins on this day,
Spring, sooner then the Lark, to fetch in May.

Rise; and put on your Foliage, and be seene
To come forth, like the Spring-time, fresh and greene;
And sweet as Flora. Take no care
For Jewels for your Gowne, or Haire:
Feare not; the leaves will strew
Gemms in abundance upon you:
Besides, the childhood of the Day has kept,
Against you come, some Orient Pearls unwept:
Come, and receive them while the light
Hangs on the Dew-locks of the night:
And Titan on the Eastern hill
Retires himselfe, or else stands still
Till you come forth. Wash, dresse, be briefe in praying:
Few Beads are best, when once we goe a Maying.

Come, my Corinna, come; and comming, marke
How each field turns a street; each street a Parke
Made green, and trimm'd with trees: see how
Devotion gives each House a Bough,
Or Branch: Each Porch, each doore, ere this,
An Arke a Tabernacle is
Made up of white-thorn neatly enterwove;
As if here were those cooler shades of love.
Can such delights be in the street,
And open fields, and we not see't?
Come, we'll abroad; and let's obay
The Proclamation made for May:
And sin no more, as we have done, by staying;
But my Corinna, come, let's goe a Maying.

There's not a budding Boy, or Girle, this day,
But is got up, and gone to bring in May.
A deale of Youth, ere this, is come
Back, and with White-thorn laden home.
Some have dispatcht their Cakes and Creame,
Before that we have left to dreame:
And some have wept, and woo'd, and plighted Troth,
And chose their Ptiest, ere we can cast off sloth:
Many a green-gown has been given;
Many a kisse, both odde and even:
Many a glance too has been sent
From out the eye, Loves Firmament:
Many a jest told of the Keyes betraying
This night, and Locks pickt, yet w'are not a Maying.

Come, let us goe, while we are in our prime;
And take the harmlesse follie of the time.
We shall grow old a pace, and die
Before we know our liberty.
Our life is short; and our dayes run
As fast away as do's the Sunne:
And as a vapour, or a drop of raine
Once lost, can ne'r be found againe:
So when or you or I are made
A fable, song, or fleeting shade;
All love, all liking, all delight
Lies drown'd with us in endlesse night.
Then while time serves, and we are but decaying;
Come, my Corinna, come, let's goe a Maying.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Corinna's going a-Maying title; l. 1

    On May Day young people went out at dawn to the woods and fields to gather hawthorn ('May') and green boughs, decorated houses and streets, and danced round maypoles; the custom was attacked by Puritan moralists such as Philip Stubbes (1583) for its sexual licence. Herrick's poem, five fourteen-line stanzas, the last four ending with 'a Maying', joins this English folk festival to the classical carpe diem. 'Corinna' is the name of Ovid's mistress in the Amores, which sets the poem in the Latin erotic tradition. Cleanth Brooks's influential reading treats it as a poem whose 'statement' cannot be separated from its play of pagan and Christian vocabularies.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Ronald Hutton, The Rise and Fall of Merry England: The Ritual Year 1400-1700 (Oxford UP, 1994)

  2. the god unshorn l. 2

    Classical poets called Apollo 'intonsus', unshorn, because his long hair symbolised eternal youth (Horace, Odes 1.21.2, 'intonsum ... Cynthium'; Tibullus 1.4.37). The sun's rays are his flowing locks. The 'Blooming Morne' carries him 'upon her wings', a personification of dawn. Opening with the pagan sun-god sets up the poem's pervasive mixture of classical mythology, English folk custom and Christian religious vocabulary.

    Horace, Odes 1.21.2 ('intonsum ... Cynthium', the unshorn Apollo), Latin text; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Alfred Pollard, ed., Robert Herrick: The Hesperides and Noble Numbers, 2 vols (London: Lawrence & Bullen, 1891; rev. 1898), notes (Project Gutenberg #22421)

  3. Aurora; fresh-quilted colours ll. 3-4

    Aurora (Greek Eos) is the rosy-fingered dawn of Homer. 'Fresh-quilted colours' has been read in two ways: as colours quilted together like the patches of a quilt or bedspread (an image that suits a poem about getting out of bed), or as colours newly 'quilled' or layered. The quilt sense suits the bedroom setting best. The airy, sewn texture of the dawn sky contrasts with the 'Slug-a-bed' who stays under her own covers.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  4. slug-a-bed l. 5

    'Sluggard' and 'slug-a-bed' (OED, slug-a-bed, n.) were common terms of reproof for a late riser; Shakespeare uses 'slug-a-bed' in Romeo and Juliet 4.5.2, when the Nurse tries to wake Juliet. Herrick sweetens it. The tone throughout the first stanza is mock-sermonising: 'for shame', 'tis sin, Nay, profanation', a parody of the preacher's rebuke that reverses Puritan values by making it sinful not to join the festivities.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  5. flowers bowing to the East ll. 7-8

    The dew on the petals is figured as tears, the flowers' bending heads as bows of devotion. Facing east was traditional in Christian prayer and church orientation, because Christ's return was associated with the rising sun (and the Laudian church emphasised the eastern altar). Nature thus performs a liturgy while Corinna lies abed. The worship is ambiguous: it is directed to the sun-god Apollo as much as to the Christian God, which is the poem's characteristic blend.

    Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  6. matins ll. 10-12

    Matins (Latin matutinae, morning hours) was the night or dawn office of the medieval Church, and in the Book of Common Prayer the name of Morning Prayer. The birds' songs become 'thankfull Hymnes'. The escalation 'tis sin, / Nay, profanation' wittily uses church language to argue for a festival that Puritans condemned as itself sinful. On Brooks's reading the religious terms are not simply ironic: they suggest that May-day joy is a natural form of worship.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary

  7. fetch in May ll. 13-14

    'Bringing in the May' was the dawn excursion to gather hawthorn blossom, green boughs and flowers, carried back to decorate doors, windows and the maypole. Hawthorn was itself called 'may' because it blooms in that month. The lark, traditionally the earliest bird, is outdone by the virgins. Herrick's 'a thousand' is festive hyperbole; the whole community is imagined as taking part.

    Ronald Hutton, The Rise and Fall of Merry England: The Ritual Year 1400-1700 (Oxford UP, 1994); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Philip Stubbes, The Anatomie of Abuses (1583), the attack on May games and maypoles

  8. Flora l. 17

    Ovid gives Flora's story in Fasti 5, where she explains that she was the nymph Chloris, married to the west wind Zephyrus, and describes her games. The Floralia fell at the turn of April and May and were proverbial for their licence, so the comparison links the English May festival to its pagan Roman ancestor. Puritan critics made exactly this link to condemn May games as heathen. Corinna is to dress in 'Foliage' and come forth like spring itself.

    Ovid, Fasti 5.183-378 (Flora and the Floralia), Latin text; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  9. orient pearls ll. 19-24

    'Orient' was applied to pearls of the finest lustre, originally those from the Indian Ocean, and then to any shining, precious, or dawn-like thing (OED, orient, a.). The 'childhood of the Day' (early morning) has saved some dewdrops unshed, to adorn her instead of jewels; the leaves will 'strew Gemms' upon her. The 'Dew-locks of the night' make the night a figure whose hair is still beaded with dew. These conceits turn a practical argument (you need not dress up) into a lavish compliment.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'

  10. Titan stands still ll. 25-27

    Roman poets (e.g. Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.10) call the sun 'Titan', since Helios was a child of the Titan Hyperion. The suggestion that the sun 'Retires himselfe, or else stands still' until Corinna appears may echo Joshua 10:12-13, where the sun stood still in the midst of heaven at Joshua's word, another fusion of classical and biblical. The conceit reverses the conventional aubade, in which lovers ask the sun to delay its rising (as in Donne's 'The Sun Rising').

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  11. beads ll. 27-28

    The word 'bead' originally meant 'prayer' (Old English gebed); the sense passed to the rosary beads used to count prayers (OED, bead, n.). Telling the beads was a Catholic practice, so the line has provoked comment from a Protestant clergyman: it may simply be a lightly archaic, pastoral term for prayer, or a provocative glance at old religion, part of the poem's nostalgic conservatism. Brooks treats the phrase as typical of the poem's tactful balance between pagan holiday and Christian observance. Either way, the advice is witty: the briefest prayers suit a May morning.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'

  12. field turns street, street turns park ll. 29-31

    The chiasmus-like exchange of field and street reflects the May custom of bringing the countryside into town. 'Parke' (an enclosed area of woodland and grass) emphasises how the decoration of houses with green branches turns streets into woods. The second stanza shifts from bedroom to village, and its imagery is communal rather than private, part of the poem's movement from persuasion of one woman to celebration of a whole society's festival.

    Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Ronald Hutton, The Rise and Fall of Merry England: The Ritual Year 1400-1700 (Oxford UP, 1994)

  13. Ark and Tabernacle ll. 32-35

    In the Old Testament the Ark of the Covenant was kept in the Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary (Exodus 25-26); and at the Feast of Tabernacles Israel was commanded to take 'boughs of goodly trees' and dwell in booths (Leviticus 23:40-42). The leafy porches thus become sacred shelters, and 'Devotion' (l. 32) gives each house its bough. The next line adds a pagan-erotic turn: the shades are 'cooler shades of love', bowers for lovers. Brooks treats this mingling as the poem's method; Marcus links it to the Laudian defence of festive ceremonial as a kind of worship.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'

  14. white-thorn l. 35

    Whitethorn (Crataegus) is the common hawthorn, so named for its white flowers (OED, whitethorn, n.). It was the characteristic plant of May Day, and the flowering of hawthorn marked the start of summer. Folklore surrounded it with taboos as well as festive uses. 'Enterwove' (interwoven) makes the decoration a deliberate craft, linking the folk festival with the poem's own skilled weaving of themes.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ronald Hutton, The Rise and Fall of Merry England: The Ritual Year 1400-1700 (Oxford UP, 1994); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary

  15. the Proclamation made for May ll. 39-40

    James I's Declaration of Sports (1618), reissued by Charles I in 1633, permitted lawful recreations after Sunday service, explicitly including May games, Whitsun ales, morris dances and maypoles. Puritan magistrates and clergy opposed it, and Parliament had it publicly burned in 1643. Leah Marcus argues that Herrick's phrase invokes this royal policy, so that obeying the 'Proclamation' is a loyal and religious duty, and the poem becomes a political as well as an erotic invitation. Other readers take the phrase more generally as nature's or custom's decree, but Marcus's reading has been influential in recent criticism.

    James I, The King's Majesty's Declaration to His Subjects Concerning Lawful Sports (the 'Book of Sports', 1618; reissued by Charles I, 1633); summary in 'Declaration of Sports', Wikipedia; Leah S. Marcus, 'Herrick's Hesperides and the "Proclamation made for May"', Studies in Philology; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'

  16. chose their priest ll. 49-50

    To plight one's troth is to pledge faith in betrothal, the phrase of the marriage service. 'Chose their Priest' means they have arranged who will marry them. The archive's spelling 'Ptiest' is a typographical error for 'Priest'. The stanza's pleasures move from innocent (cakes and cream) to courtship (weeping, wooing, betrothal) to the more equivocal 'green-gown' and picked locks; the marriage reference legitimates the love-making within Christian custom, a balance critics have found characteristic of Herrick.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'

  17. green-gown l. 51

    The phrase was a standard early modern euphemism for a tumble on the grass, often implying the loss of virginity (OED, green gown, n.). It appears in ballads and other May-day verse. Herrick's casual 'many a' makes it part of the festival's routine, and the following lines continue the innuendo with kisses 'odde and even' (in any number) and glances sent from 'the eye, Loves Firmament' (the eye as the sky in which love's stars shine).

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  18. keys betraying, locks picked ll. 55-56

    The lines suggest doors unlocked and chaperones evaded on May Eve, when young people spent the night out of doors; keys and locks carry obvious sexual double meanings. The stanza's refrain twist ('yet w'are not a Maying') renews the urgency: while everyone else has had these adventures, the speaker and Corinna are still at home. Puritan writers such as Stubbes had complained that few maidens returned from the May-night woods undefiled, and Herrick answers that charge with good humour.

    Philip Stubbes, The Anatomie of Abuses (1583), the attack on May games and maypoles; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Ronald Hutton, The Rise and Fall of Merry England: The Ritual Year 1400-1700 (Oxford UP, 1994)

  19. harmless folly ll. 57-58

    'In our prime' means in youth, and perhaps also at the 'prime', the first hour of the day (the canonical hour of Prime). 'Harmlesse follie' defends the festival against moralists: it is folly, but harmless. From here the poem shifts register into the solemn tradition of the classical invitation to love, where the brevity of life justifies present pleasure. The festive comedy of the earlier stanzas now sounds against a darker background, which many critics regard as the poem's emotional climax.

    Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Catullus 5 ('Vivamus, mea Lesbia'), ll. 4-6, Latin text

  20. vapour, raindrop ll. 63-64

    James 4:14 asks 'what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away'; and 2 Samuel 14:14 compares mortals to water spilt on the ground that cannot be gathered up. The Wisdom of Solomon (2:1-9) gives the reasoning of the ungodly, who say that life is short, that we shall be as though we had never been, and therefore 'let us crown ourselves with rosebuds'. Some critics note the irony that Herrick's carpe diem argument echoes that condemned passage; others take the biblical images straightforwardly as reminders of mortality.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  21. fable, song, shade ll. 65-66

    Horace, Odes 4.7.16, says that after death we are dust and shadow ('pulvis et umbra sumus'); 'shade' is the classical word for a ghost in the underworld. 'Fable' and 'song' imply that the lovers survive only as stories, perhaps in poems such as this one: Herrick's Hesperides often reflects on poetry as the one kind of survival. The tricolon moves from the most substantial (a story) to the least (a fading shadow).

    Horace, Odes 4.7.16 ('pulvis et umbra sumus'), Latin text; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  22. endless night ll. 67-68

    Catullus 5.4-6 says that suns may set and return, but once our brief light has set we must sleep one perpetual night ('nox est perpetua una dormienda'). Herrick's 'endlesse night', after the sun image of l. 62, translates that thought. For a Christian priest the line is strikingly pagan in its finality, and critics debate whether it is a conventional classical gesture or a genuine moment of doubt. The final refrain then answers the darkness with the festival's invitation.

    Catullus 5 ('Vivamus, mea Lesbia'), ll. 4-6, Latin text; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  23. stanza and refrain st. 1-5

    Each stanza is fourteen lines of rhyming couplets with the same broad shape: long pentameter lines open and close it, with shorter tetrameter lines (and an occasional trimeter) between. The first stanza ends on the rhyme day/May ('to fetch in May'); stanzas 2 to 5 close on the feminine '-aying' (praying/Maying, staying/Maying, betraying/Maying, decaying/Maying), and the final line varies a refrain ('let's goe a Maying'). The movement of the stanzas is a progress from bedroom (1), to dressing (2), to street (3), to village and fields (4), to the meditation on mortality (5).

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Cleanth Brooks, The Well Wrought Urn: Studies in the Structure of Poetry (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947), ch. 4, 'What Does Poetry Communicate?'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary
