The Argument of his Book.

by Robert Herrick 1591–1674

From Hesperides (1648)

Arts & Sciences

I sing of brooks, of blossoms, birds and bowers,
Of April, May, of June and July-flowers;
I sing of May-poles, hock-carts, wassails, wakes,
Of bridegrooms, brides and of their bridal cakes;
I write of youth, of love, and have access
By these to sing of cleanly wantonness;
I sing of dews, of rains, and piece by piece
Of balm, of oil, of spice and ambergris;
I sing of times trans-shifting, and I write
How roses first came red and lilies white;
I write of groves, of twilights, and I sing
The Court of Mab, and of the Fairy King;
I write of hell; I sing (and ever shall)
Of heaven, and hope to have it after all.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Argument of his Book l. 1

    'Argument' here means a summary of the contents, as in the prose arguments prefixed to books of an epic. This sonnet-length poem stands first in Hesperides and catalogues the themes of the 1,100-odd poems that follow: country nature, the seasons, rural festivals, love, weddings, fairy lore, and finally religion. 'I sing' is the epic formula of Virgil's 'arma virumque cano', applied playfully to small subjects. The poem is in fourteen lines of rhymed couplets, a sonnet in length though not in form.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Virgil, Aeneid 1.1 ('Arma virumque cano')

  2. July-flowers l. 2

    'July-flower' was a popular form of 'gillyflower', the clove pink or carnation, derived from French girofle (clove). Herrick's spelling lets the word complete the list of months (April, May, June, July) while naming a flower. The line thus moves from the calendar to the garden in one word. The flower catalogue of lines 1-2, with its alliteration of b in 'brooks, ... blossoms, birds and bowers', sets the tone of pastoral delight that much of Hesperides sustains.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gillyflower'; The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  3. May-poles, hock-carts, wassails, wakes l. 3

    Maypoles were raised for May Day dancing; the hock-cart carried the last load of the harvest home amid feasting (Herrick has a poem 'The Hock-cart, or Harvest Home'); wassails were the drinking and singing at Christmas and Twelfth Night; wakes were parish festivals on the feast of the church's patron saint. In the 1630s and 1640s these customs were politically charged: the Stuart Book of Sports (1618, reissued 1633) defended them against Puritan opposition. Leah Marcus's The Politics of Mirth (1986) reads Herrick's celebration of them as royalist and Laudian.

    The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Chicago, 1986); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hock-cart'

  4. bridegrooms, brides l. 4

    Herrick wrote a number of marriage poems, among them 'A Nuptial Song, or Epithalamy, on Sir Clipseby Crew and his Lady', and the bridal cake and its rites recur in his verse. The line places weddings among the communal festivals of the previous line, as part of a world of ceremony and custom. Bride-cake was broken over the bride's head or shared among the guests in folk custom, and Herrick's wedding poems take pleasure in such rites. Like the festivals, they belong to the rhythm of the rural year.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary

  5. cleanly wantonness l. 6

    'Wantonness' means playfulness, frolic or amorousness, often with a sense of lewdness; 'cleanly' means pure, decent, neat. The phrase claims that his erotic verse is harmless, a claim he repeats in the book's closing poem, 'The Pillar of Fame', with the line 'Jocund his Muse was, but his Life was chast', which has classical precedents in Ovid and Martial. Critics differ on how far to take it at face value. Marcus reads such phrases in the light of the Stuart defence of 'lawful recreations' in the Book of Sports.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wantonness'; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth (1986)

  6. ambergris l. 8

    Ambergris (from French ambre gris, grey amber) is a substance formed in the intestines of sperm whales, found floating at sea or washed ashore, and highly valued in perfumery and cookery. With balm, oil and spice, it belongs to the language of scent and anointing that runs through Herrick's love poems and his religious verse alike. The list 'piece by piece' suggests the small, separate pieces that make up his book.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ambergris'; The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  7. times trans-shifting l. 9

    'Trans-shifting' appears to be a rare word, perhaps Herrick's own coinage, meaning shifting across, changing. The main sense is the passage of time and its transformations, a theme of 'To the Virgins' and 'Corinna's going a Maying'. Published in 1648, after the Civil War had overturned the world of Herrick's festivals and he had been ejected from his Devon living, the phrase also invites a political reading of 'times' as historical change. Editors gloss it both ways.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trans-shifting'; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  8. How roses first came red l. 10

    Herrick wrote several short myths explaining the origins of colours and flowers, in the manner of Ovid's Metamorphoses: 'How Roses came red', 'How Lilies came white', 'How Primroses came green' and others. Such playful aetiologies, often involving a nymph or Venus, are typical of Hesperides' small-scale classicism. The line shows Herrick's taste for the tiny myth, a whole metamorphosis told in a few couplets, and for the colours red and white, which run through his love poetry as the colours of the beloved's cheek.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; Ovid, Metamorphoses (model for flower metamorphoses, e.g. 10.162-219, Hyacinthus)

  9. The Court of Mab ... the Fairy King l. 12

    Queen Mab is the fairy midwife of Mercutio's speech in Romeo and Juliet 1.4; the Fairy King is most probably Oberon, known from A Midsummer Night's Dream and Jonson's masque Oberon (1611). Herrick wrote 'Oberon's Feast', 'Oberon's Palace' and related miniature poems, part of a fashion for fairy verse in the circle of his friends. The tininess of fairy life suits the delicate scale of much of Hesperides.

    The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary; Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet 1.4 (Queen Mab); Robert Herrick, 'Oberon's Feast' and 'Oberon's Palace', Hesperides (1648)

  10. hell ... heaven ll. 13-14

    Hesperides was published together with His Noble Numbers, Herrick's sacred poems, and the Argument ends by moving from earth to the last things. The parenthesis 'and ever shall' implies singing praise eternally in heaven. The final half-line, 'and hope to have it after all', is disarmingly casual: after a book of pleasures, the priest-poet hopes for salvation all the same. Critics enjoy the double sense of 'after all', both 'at the end' and 'in spite of everything'.

    The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick, ed. L. C. Martin (Oxford, 1956), commentary; The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, ed. Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary
