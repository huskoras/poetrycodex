Time & Brevity

To the Virgins, to make much of Time.

by Robert Herrick 1591–1674

From Hesperides (1648)

Time & Brevity

GAther ye Rose-buds while ye may,
Old Time is still a flying:
And this same flower that smiles to day,
To morrow will be dying.

The glorious Lamp of Heaven, the Sun,
The higher he's a getting;
The sooner will his Race be run,
And neerer he's to Setting.

That Age is best, which is the first,
When Youth and Blood are warmer;
But being spent, the worse, and worst
Times, still succeed the former.

Then be not coy, but use your time;
And while ye may, goe marry:
For having lost but once your prime,
You may for ever tarry.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, publication and setting l. 1

    The poem appeared in Herrick's Hesperides (1648) and became one of the most popular songs of the century through William Lawes's setting, published in John Playford's Select Musicall Ayres and Dialogues (1652). The title addresses unmarried young women and urges them to 'make much of', make the most of, their time. The carpe diem theme derives from Horace (Odes 1.11) but the imagery of roses comes from late Latin poetry and the Bible. Herrick, a royalist clergyman ejected from his Devon parish in 1647, published the volume in London in the year of the second civil war.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To Virgins, to Make Much of Time', ed. N. J. Endicott (text of Hesperides, 1648); Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds, The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary on H-208; Wikipedia, 'Gather ye rosebuds while ye may'

  2. Rose-buds l. 1

    In the Apocryphal Wisdom of Solomon 2:8 the ungodly say 'Let us crown ourselves with rosebuds, before they be withered', in a speech the book condemns. The late Latin poem De rosis nascentibus, ascribed to Ausonius or to Virgil, ends 'collige, virgo, rosas', gather roses, girl, while the flower and your youth are new. Renaissance versions include Tasso (Gerusalemme liberata 16.14-15) and Spenser's adaptation, 'Gather therefore the Rose, whilest yet is prime' (Faerie Queene 2.12.75), sung in the Bower of Bliss. Herrick's source tradition is therefore ambiguous: pagan pleasure, the voice of the ungodly, and Spenser's seductive song. His poem redirects it toward marriage.

    KJV (1611) Apocrypha, Wisdom of Solomon 2:6-8; Wikipedia, 'Gather ye rosebuds while ye may' (on De rosis nascentibus); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene 2.12.74-75; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds, The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary on H-208

  3. still a flying l. 2

    'Still' in its older sense means constantly. 'A flying' is the archaic construction 'a-' (from 'on') plus verbal noun, as in 'a-hunting', giving a colloquial, song-like lightness also heard in 'a getting' (l. 6). Time's flight is proverbial (Latin tempus fugit, from Virgil, Georgics 3.284, 'fugit inreparabile tempus'), and 'Old Time' evokes the emblematic figure of Father Time with his wings and scythe. The feminine rhyme 'flying/dying' gives the even lines a falling cadence.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'still', adv.; 'a-', prep.1; Virgil, Georgics 3.284 (Latin Library); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  4. Stanza and rhyme st. 1

    The stanza is a version of common or hymn measure (the RPO entry calls it a variant hymnal measure), rhymed abab. The odd lines are iambic tetrameter with masculine endings (may/day), the even lines trimeter with an extra unstressed syllable (flying/dying, getting/setting, warmer/former, marry/tarry). The feminine endings give each stanza a lilting, falling close well suited to song, which helps explain the popularity of Lawes's setting. The argument advances one step per stanza: flower, sun, ages of life, conclusion ('Then').

    Representative Poetry Online, 'To Virgins, to Make Much of Time', ed. N. J. Endicott (text of Hesperides, 1648); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds, The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary on H-208

  5. the glorious Lamp of Heaven ll. 5-8

    Psalm 19:4-5 describes the sun as a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, rejoicing 'as a strong man to run a race', a nuptial image that suits the poem's marriage theme. Herrick's paradox is that the sun's rise toward noon also brings it closer to setting: maturity contains decline. 'Race' means both the course run and a lifespan. The second stanza moves the argument from the single flower to the daily cycle of the cosmos.

    KJV, Psalm 19:4-6; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  6. That Age is best ll. 9-12

    Ovid's account of the four ages of the world (Metamorphoses 1.89-150) begins 'aurea prima sata est aetas', golden was the first age, followed by silver, bronze and iron. Herrick applies the declining sequence to a human life: 'worse, and worst / Times, still succeed the former'. 'Youth and Blood are warmer' draws on humoral physiology, in which youth is hot and moist and age cold and dry. The run-on from 'worst' to 'Times' enacts the succession.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.89-150 (Latin Library); L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds, The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary on H-208

  7. coy l. 13

    'Coy' (from Old French coi, quiet, from Latin quietus) meant shy, reserved, or affectedly reluctant to respond to wooing. The word belongs to the carpe diem genre; Marvell's 'To His Coy Mistress', composed in the same mid-century decades but not printed until 1681, uses it in its title. Herrick addresses not one mistress but all virgins, giving the poem a general, almost homiletic voice.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'coy', adj.; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary

  8. goe marry l. 14

    The carpe diem tradition often argues for love outside marriage (Catullus 5; Spenser's Bower of Bliss). Herrick's final stanza turns the argument toward marriage, giving the poem a socially respectable conclusion compatible with his clerical office. Critics such as Leah Marcus have related Hesperides to the Caroline defence of traditional festivity and fertility rituals against Puritan disapproval; others read the poem's insistence on loss as more melancholy than its jaunty rhythm suggests.

    Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds, The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford, 2013), commentary on H-208; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Chicago, 1986); Representative Poetry Online, 'To Virgins, to Make Much of Time', ed. N. J. Endicott (text of Hesperides, 1648)

  9. prime ... tarry ll. 15-16

    'Prime' means the first and best period, the spring of life (Latin primus; compare Spenser's 'whilest yet is prime'). Lose it 'but once' and the chance does not return. 'Tarry' means to linger or wait, and here to be left waiting for a husband who never comes. The closing line has an ominous undertone: 'for ever' turns from marriage prospects toward death, completing the movement of the poem from the dying flower to the setting sun.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'prime', n.1; 'tarry', v.; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford, 1956), commentary
