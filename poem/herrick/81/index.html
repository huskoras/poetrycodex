Living

Delight in Disorder.

by Robert Herrick 1591–1674

From Hesperides (1648)

Living

A Sweet disorder in the dresse
Kindles in cloathes a wantonnesse:
A Lawne about the shoulders thrown
Into a fine distraction:
An erring Lace, which here and there
Enthralls the Crimson Stomacher:
A Cuffe neglectfull, and thereby
Ribbands to flow confusedly:
A winning wave (deserving Note)
In the tempestuous petticote:
A carelesse shooe-string, in whose tye
I see a wilde civility:
Doe more bewitch me, then when Art
Is too precise in every part.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Delight in Disorder title; l. 1

    Robert Herrick (1591-1674), vicar of Dean Prior in Devon and a self-declared 'son of Ben', printed this among the secular poems of Hesperides (1648). It is a reply to and variation on the song in Jonson's Epicoene (1609), 1.1, which ends by preferring 'sweet neglect' to the 'adulteries of art'. Behind both lies Ovid's advice that artful negligence suits many women (Ars Amatoria 3.153). Herrick's poem is also a miniature poetics: it describes, and enacts, a style in which slight irregularity is more pleasing than rigid correctness, an aesthetic of graceful negligence akin to Castiglione's 'sprezzatura'.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Ben Jonson, Epicoene, or The Silent Woman (1609), 1.1, the song 'Still to be neat' (Project Gutenberg #4011); Ovid, Ars Amatoria 3.133-68 (on women's hair and dress; 'neglecta decet multas coma', 3.153), Latin text

  2. wantonness l. 2

    'Wanton' meant undisciplined or unruly (originally of children), playful, luxuriant in growth, and sexually loose (OED, wanton, a.). Herrick exploits the whole range: the clothes become lively and capricious, and they also excite desire. 'Kindles' adds a hint of fire and passion. A feature of the poem is that the clothes, not the woman, are the grammatical subjects throughout: the garments act, wander and bewitch, and the wearer is never directly described.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  3. lawn l. 3

    Lawn was a very fine linen or cotton fabric, said to be named after Laon in France, used for scarves, ruffs and the sleeves of bishops (OED, lawn, n.1). The garment is 'thrown' carelessly about the shoulders into a 'fine distraction'. The rhyme of 'thrown' with 'distraction' is a deliberately imperfect, stretched rhyme (the final syllable of distraction must be stressed), an example of the poem's own 'disorder' at the level of sound.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Alfred Pollard, ed., Robert Herrick: The Hesperides and Noble Numbers, 2 vols (London: Lawrence & Bullen, 1891; rev. 1898), notes (Project Gutenberg #22421)

  4. distraction l. 4

    From Latin distrahere, 'to draw in different directions', distraction could mean physical disorder or division as well as mental perplexity or madness (OED, distraction, n.). The scarf is literally in disarray; but the word also suggests the beholder's agitation and the wearer's absent-mindedness. 'Fine' qualifies it: the disorder is elegant. Herrick's oxymorons accumulate through the poem (sweet disorder, fine distraction, wild civility), each pairing a word of control with a word of looseness.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary

  5. erring lace; stomacher ll. 5-6

    'Erring' keeps its Latin sense of 'wandering' (errare), with a moral overtone of straying; the lace (a cord or decorative braid) wanders across the dress. The stomacher was a stiff, often embroidered, triangular panel worn over the chest and abdomen beneath the lacing of a bodice (OED, stomacher, n.). 'Enthralls' means captures or binds, from 'thrall', a slave: the lace literally ties the stomacher, and metaphorically enslaves the onlooker. The crimson colour adds a touch of passion.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Alfred Pollard, ed., Robert Herrick: The Hesperides and Noble Numbers, 2 vols (London: Lawrence & Bullen, 1891; rev. 1898), notes (Project Gutenberg #22421)

  6. cuff and ribbons ll. 7-8

    'Neglectful' takes up Jonson's 'sweet neglect' from Epicoene. The detail is precise: in the period sleeves and cuffs were tied with ribbons ('ribbands'), and a loosened cuff lets them trail. The four-syllable adverb 'confusedly' fills half of line 8, and its trailing unstressed syllables let the line itself seem to flow loose like the ribbons. The catalogue of garments works like a slow look down and across the figure, from shoulders and breast to wrists and, finally, feet.

    Ben Jonson, Epicoene, or The Silent Woman (1609), 1.1, the song 'Still to be neat' (Project Gutenberg #4011); Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  7. winning wave; tempestuous petticoat ll. 9-10

    The petticoat was an underskirt, often displayed beneath an open gown. Its 'wave' is both a fold in the fabric and an ocean wave, extended in 'tempestuous', which turns the swirling skirt into a stormy sea. 'Winning' means attractive, winning hearts. The parenthesis 'deserving Note' is self-conscious: the wave deserves notice, and perhaps a note of music or a note in verse. As with 'thrown / distraction', the rhyme 'Note / petticote' is slightly off-key.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  8. careless shoestring ll. 11-12

    Shoes were fastened with ribbon or string ties, often decorative. Ending the list at the feet, Herrick finds even here the poem's paradox. 'Careless' in the period meant free from care as well as negligent, so the shoestring suggests ease and naturalness. Some critics have noted an erotic undercurrent in the sequence of loosened ties, laces and ribbons, as if the clothes were beginning to come undone.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  9. wild civility l. 12

    'Civility' meant civilised behaviour, courtesy, ordered society (Latin civilitas, the quality of a citizen); 'wild' means untamed, natural. The phrase sums up the poem's ideal of an art that does not suppress nature, an aesthetic shared with the Renaissance garden and with the debate on art and nature (as in Polixenes' speech in The Winter's Tale). Herrick's whole volume plays with such tensions between licence and order, and Leah Marcus has read the Hesperides as a whole in relation to the Laudian defence of ceremony and festive custom.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary

  10. syntax: the delayed verb l. 13

    The structure is periodic: a catalogue of noun phrases held in suspense until 'Doe more bewitch me'. The plural verb gathers all the details into one effect, and 'bewitch' (to cast a spell on, to charm) completes the language of enchantment and enslavement begun in 'Kindles' and 'Enthralls'. The suspended syntax is itself a kind of graceful irregularity: the reader must hold the list open, like the loose ties of the clothes, before it is fastened by the verb.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  11. too precise l. 14

    'Precise' meant exact, strict, scrupulous; but 'precise' and 'precisian' were common labels for the Puritans, who were mocked for scrupulosity in religion and dress (OED, precise, a., and precisian, n.). Herrick, a royalist and Laudian clergyman ejected from his living in 1647, may intend a sly political-religious joke: careful disorder in dress, like ceremony in worship, is preferred to Puritan rigidity. Not all critics accept the allusion, and the line works perfectly well as purely aesthetic. Compare Jonson's song, which rejects the 'adulteries of art', a harsher phrase than Herrick's mild 'too precise'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Leah S. Marcus, The Politics of Mirth: Jonson, Herrick, Milton, Marvell, and the Defense of Old Holiday Pastimes (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1986), ch. 5, 'Churchman among the Maypoles'; Ben Jonson, Epicoene, or The Silent Woman (1609), 1.1, the song 'Still to be neat' (Project Gutenberg #4011)

  12. metre and rhyme whole poem

    The poem has seven couplets, fourteen lines in all, which has led some critics to call it a 'disordered sonnet'. The metre is mostly regular iambic tetrameter, but the rhymes include several imperfect pairs (thrown/distraction, Note/petticote, and arguably tye/civility), where the second word must be stressed on a weak final syllable. These calculated roughnesses illustrate the poem's thesis: the poem is itself a 'sweet disorder', exactly controlled. 'Then' in l. 13 is the normal early spelling of 'than'.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Poetical Works of Robert Herrick (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), commentary; Tom Cain and Ruth Connolly, eds., The Complete Poetry of Robert Herrick, 2 vols (Oxford UP, 2013), commentary
