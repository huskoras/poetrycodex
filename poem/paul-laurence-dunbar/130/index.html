Sympathy

by Paul Laurence Dunbar 1872–1906

Social Commentaries

I know what the caged bird feels, alas!
When the sun is bright on the upland slopes;
When the wind stirs soft through the springing grass,
And the river flows like a stream of glass;
When the first bird sings and the first bud opes,
And the faint perfume from its chalice steals--
I know what the caged bird feels!

I know why the caged bird beats his wing
Till its blood is red on the cruel bars;
For he must fly back to his perch and cling
When he fain would be on the bough a-swing;
And a pain still throbs in the old, old scars
And they pulse again with a keener sting--
I know why he beats his wing!

I know why the caged bird sings, ah me,
When his wing is bruised and his bosom sore,--
When he beats his bars and he would be free;
It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart's deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings--
I know why the caged bird sings!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sympathy: title and occasion l. 1

    Dunbar (1872-1906), the son of formerly enslaved parents, took a post as an attendant at the Library of Congress in Washington on 30 September 1897; the work among the stacks, in summer heat, was unhealthy and confining. In 1914 his widow, Alice Dunbar Nelson, wrote that the iron grating of the book stacks suggested to him the bars of a bird's cage. The poem appeared in Lyrics of the Hearthside (1899). The title, from Greek sympatheia, 'feeling with', claims the speaker's suffering is the bird's: he does not pity it from outside but knows it from within. The bird has been read as figuring the condition of Black Americans after slavery, and of the artist constrained by a racist culture.

    Library of Congress blog, '"Caged Bird" Inspired by the Library of Congress' (Feb. 2020), quoting Alice Dunbar Nelson (1914); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; 'Sympathy (poem)', summary of critical reception (Braxton, Leonard, Wooley, Burns)

  2. the refrain 'I know ...' ll. 1, 7, 8, 14, 15, 21

    The refrain frames every stanza and develops through the poem: 'what the caged bird feels' (st. 1), 'why the caged bird beats his wing' (st. 2), 'why the caged bird sings' (st. 3). The progression moves from feeling to action to art, and the final stanza's revelation is that singing is a form of the same struggle as beating against the bars. The insistent first person 'I know' asserts experience rather than observation, which gives the poem its moral authority. Maya Angelou took the title of her autobiography, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings (1969), from the final line.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; 'Sympathy (poem)', summary of critical reception (Braxton, Leonard, Wooley, Burns); Maya Angelou, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings (Random House, 1969)

  3. Stanza and rhyme st. 1-3

    The first stanza rhymes abaabcc (alas/slopes/grass/glass/opes/steals/feels), as does the third (me/sore/free/glee/core/flings/sings); the second rhymes abaabaa, because the c-rhyme is replaced by the -ing of 'wing' (wing/bars/cling/swing/scars/sting/wing). The b-lines are indented, and the closing line is a shortened echo of the first. The lines are mostly four-stress, mixing iambic and anapaestic feet ('When the WIND stirs SOFT through the SPRINGing GRASS'), a lilting movement that suits song and also suggests the bird's beating. Keith Leonard has noted that the poem follows Standard English norms, unlike Dunbar's dialect verse, and links its celebration of nature to the Romantic poets, while arguing that it also betrays Dunbar's social anxieties.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; 'Sympathy (poem)', summary of critical reception (Braxton, Leonard, Wooley, Burns)

  4. upland l. 2

    'Upland' denotes higher ground, away from towns and rivers, and in poetic diction it suggests freedom and fresh air. The first stanza is a catalogue of a spring day seen from inside the cage: sun, wind, grass, river, the first song and first bud. The pain lies in the contrast between the bird's confinement and the visible, unreachable world. The springtime setting may also hint at the hopes of Emancipation and Reconstruction, which by the 1890s had been betrayed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'upland, n. and adj.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  5. springing l. 3

    To 'spring' is to rise or sprout, and the participle describes young grass shooting up in spring. The word also suggests leaping and elasticity, qualities denied the caged bird. The repeated 'When' clauses accumulate the season's freedoms, each one a reproach to the cage. The participle also keeps the scene in motion: the grass is still growing, the wind still stirring, the river still flowing, while the bird alone is fixed in place. Dunbar's nature catalogue thus works by contrast rather than celebration.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'springing, adj.'

  6. a stream of glass l. 4

    The simile pictures a slow, transparent river, smooth as glass. Some readers have noticed the irony: glass, like the bars of a cage, is a barrier through which one can see without passing; the transparency of the world outside makes the bird's exclusion sharper. The figure is a conventional poetic one (compare Milton's 'glassy, cool, translucent wave' in Comus, l. 861), and its conventionality is part of the stanza's deliberate idyllic surface.

    John Milton, A Mask (Comus) (1634), l. 861; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  7. opes l. 5

    'Ope' is an archaic and poetic short form of 'open', common in Romantic and Victorian verse ('ope their golden eyes', Cymbeline 2.3). Dunbar's standard-English poems often use such literary diction, consciously placing himself in the line of English lyric. The 'first bird sings and the first bud opes' marks the very beginning of spring, the season of renewal the caged bird cannot share.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ope, v.'; William Shakespeare, Cymbeline, 2.3.22

  8. chalice l. 6

    A chalice is a drinking cup, especially the cup of wine in the Christian Eucharist, and by extension a cup-shaped flower (Latin calix also gives 'calyx'). The religious overtone anticipates the poem's close, in which the bird's song becomes a prayer to Heaven. 'Steals' means slips quietly away, and the scent escaping from the flower is a free movement the bird cannot imitate.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chalice, n.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  9. blood on the cruel bars l. 9

    The second stanza moves from longing to struggle. The bird's beating of its wing against the bars until 'its blood is red' is the poem's most violent image, and the transferred epithet 'cruel' gives the bars a will of their own. The image belongs to a long tradition of caged birds as figures of the captive spirit, from Sterne's starling in A Sentimental Journey (1768), whose 'I can't get out' made Yorick reflect on slavery and imprisonment, to many nineteenth-century anti-slavery poems. Dunbar gives the tradition a first-person, racial urgency.

    Laurence Sterne, A Sentimental Journey through France and Italy (1768), 'The Passport. The Hotel at Paris' (the starling); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  10. fain l. 11

    'Fain' (Old English faegen, 'glad') survives chiefly in the archaic phrase 'would fain', meaning 'would gladly' or 'wishes to'. The line contrasts the forced return to the perch, where the bird must 'cling', with the swinging bough where it longs to be. 'A-swing' uses the old prefix a- (from 'on'), as in 'a-hunting', for an ongoing action; the swaying bough is free motion, the perch is fixed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fain, adv.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'a-, prefix'

  11. the old, old scars l. 12

    The doubled 'old, old' suggests wounds received long before, which 'pulse again with a keener sting' at each new effort. Read historically, the old scars are those of slavery, from which Dunbar's parents had escaped or been freed (his father, Joshua Dunbar, escaped slavery and served in the Union army), and the new sting is the racism of the 1890s, the decade of segregation laws, disfranchisement and lynching. Read more personally, they are the frustrations of an artist who felt confined by the expectation that he write dialect verse. The poem supports both readings; Christine Wooley argues that Dunbar identifies with the bird but shifts attention, in the last stanza, to the song itself.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; 'Sympathy (poem)', summary of critical reception (Braxton, Leonard, Wooley, Burns)

  12. carol l. 18

    A carol was originally a song accompanying a ring-dance (Old French carole), then any joyful song, especially at Christmas. The final stanza corrects a misreading: listeners assume the caged bird sings because it is happy, but its song is a prayer and a plea. Critics have connected this with the minstrel stereotype of the 'happy' singing slave and with white readers' reception of Black art as entertainment; it also bears on Dunbar's own reputation for cheerful dialect poems, which he felt obscured his serious verse (compare 'We Wear the Mask', 1895).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'carol, n.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  13. a prayer ... a plea ll. 19-20

    The climax redefines the song as prayer and plea, directed 'upward to Heaven'. The vertical movement contrasts with the horizontal bars: the bird cannot fly, but its voice can rise. The language echoes the spirituals, in which song itself was a coded petition for deliverance, and gives the poem a religious resolution without any assurance that the prayer will be answered. 'Flings' recalls the beating wing: singing is a form of struggle, not consolation.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (UP of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; 'Sympathy (poem)', summary of critical reception (Braxton, Leonard, Wooley, Burns)
