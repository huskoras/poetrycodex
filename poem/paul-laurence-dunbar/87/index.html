We Wear the Mask

by Paul Laurence Dunbar 1872–1906

Social Commentaries

We wear the mask that grins and lies,
It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,--
This debt we pay to human guile;
With torn and bleeding hearts we smile,
And mouth with myriad subtleties.

Why should the world be over-wise,
In counting all our tears and sighs?
Nay, let them only see us, while
We wear the mask.

We smile, but, O great Christ, our cries
To thee from tortured souls arise.
We sing, but oh the clay is vile
Beneath our feet, and long the mile;
But let the world dream otherwise,
We wear the mask!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication l. 1

    Dunbar (1872-1906), son of formerly enslaved parents in Dayton, Ohio, published the poem in Majors and Minors (1895), among the 'majors' in standard English, and reprinted it in Lyrics of Lowly Life (1896), whose introduction by W. D. Howells made him nationally famous. The 1890s saw the spread of segregation laws, the Supreme Court's Plessy v. Ferguson decision (1896) and frequent lynchings. The poem never names race, but the 'we' has always been read as Black Americans. Critics often call it his finest poem; Revell's judgement to that effect is widely quoted.

    Paul Laurence Dunbar, Majors and Minors (Toledo: Hadley & Hadley, 1895) [first printing]; Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York, 1896); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; "We Wear the Mask", Wikipedia (publication, form, reception); Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  2. the mask l. 9

    The mask 'grins and lies': it presents the cheerful face that white America expected, notably in minstrel shows, where performers in blackface caricatured Black people as carefree and comic. Dunbar's own career depended partly on dialect verse that white audiences enjoyed in this spirit, which gives the poem a personal edge. The mask is both a protection and a burden, a 'debt'. The image anticipates W. E. B. Du Bois's 'double-consciousness' and 'veil' (1903), the sense of 'always looking at one's self through the eyes of others'.

    W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 1, "Of Our Spiritual Strivings"; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  3. hides our cheeks and shades our eyes l. 2

    Cheeks and eyes are where emotion is visible (blushing, weeping). 'Shades' means both covers and darkens, making the eyes unreadable. The double dash at the line's end in this text marks a pause before the explanation in the next line. The physical detail roots the abstract mask in the body, and prepares for 'tears and sighs' in stanza 2. The rhyme 'eyes' with 'lies' links seeing and deception, the theme of the whole poem.

    Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York, 1896); Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  4. This debt we pay to human guile l. 3

    'Guile' is craft or deceit. Two readings are common. In one, the 'guile' is that of the oppressor: a deceitful, hostile society exacts the mask as tribute, as a debtor must pay. In the other, the guile is the wearers' own, the cunning that survival requires, so that the 'debt' is owed to human nature in general. The word 'human' universalises the line, and some critics stress that the poem speaks for all who conceal suffering, while most read its primary reference as racial. The economic metaphor ('debt', 'pay') may also recall the unpaid labour of slavery.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'guile, n.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979); "We Wear the Mask", Wikipedia (publication, form, reception)

  5. mouth with myriad subtleties l. 5

    To 'mouth' words is to speak them without meaning them, or with exaggerated expression, as an actor might. 'Myriad subtleties' are countless small evasions, ironies and double meanings. Critics see here Dunbar's own art: the coded speech of the trickster tradition in African American folklore and of the spirituals, in which a surface meaning hides another. The polished Latinate diction itself is a kind of mask, a display of mastery in the standard literary language.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mouth, v.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'myriad, n. and adj.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction

  6. over-wise l. 6

    'Over-wise' means too knowing, or knowing more than is proper. The question is ironic: the world has no wish to count 'our tears and sighs', and the speaker will not give it the chance. Concealment becomes a form of dignity and self-protection rather than only submission. 'Nay, let them only see us, while / We wear the mask' grants the world only the surface.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'overwise, adj.'; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction

  7. O great Christ, our cries l. 10

    Behind the mask the 'cries' rise 'from tortured souls' to Christ, the suffering God who knows what the world refuses to see. The appeal recalls the African American spirituals, which voiced suffering and longing for freedom in biblical terms, and the Psalms of lament ('Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O Lord', Psalm 130:1). The apostrophe is the one moment the mask slips, and it slips only before God.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 130:1; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  8. the clay is vile l. 12

    Read literally, the singer walks on foul, sticky ground along a long road ('long the mile'), an image of hard labour and endurance. Clay is also the stuff of the human body in Genesis and in Isaiah's 'we are the clay, and thou our potter' (Isaiah 64:8), and the Psalmist thanks God for bringing him 'out of the miry clay' (Psalm 40:2). 'Vile' means base, worthless, or morally degraded. Some readers take 'clay' as the conditions of oppression, others as the world's or the body's fallen state; both fit the prayer of the stanza.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 40:2; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 64:8; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vile, adj.'

  9. let the world dream otherwise l. 14

    The phrase turns the tables: it is the world, not the oppressed, who lives in illusion. The tone is bitter and defiant. Whether the decision to keep the mask is a strategy of survival or a tragic resignation has been debated; the exclamation mark on the last refrain (after a full stop in stanza 2) suggests a shift from statement to proud, perhaps anguished, declaration.

    Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; "We Wear the Mask", Wikipedia (publication, form, reception)

  10. The rondeau l. 12

    The rondeau, revived by Victorian poets such as Austin Dobson and Swinburne, has three stanzas (5, 4 and 6 lines here), only two rhyme sounds ('-ies/-ise' and '-ile'), and a refrain taken from the opening words. Dunbar's rhyme scheme is aabba aabR aabbaR (R = refrain). Choosing so artificial and European a form is itself significant: the poem shows mastery of the high literary tradition while its tight constraint mirrors the restraint of the mask. 'We sing' alludes to the stereotype of the singing slave and to Dunbar's own songs.

    "We Wear the Mask", Wikipedia (publication, form, reception); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rondeau, n.'

  11. The refrain l. 15

    In a rondeau the refrain (rentrement) is not counted in the rhyme scheme and is shorter than the other lines; here it is indented in the archive's text. Each return gives the phrase new weight: first a plain statement (stanza 2), then, after the prayer and the 'vile' clay, an exclamation (stanza 3). The repetition enacts the poem's subject: the mask is put on again and again.

    "We Wear the Mask", Wikipedia (publication, form, reception); Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York, 1896)

  12. Reception: Howells, dialect and the mask l. 4

    In his 1896 introduction Howells hailed Dunbar as the first man of 'pure African blood' to feel Black life aesthetically and express it lyrically, but valued chiefly the dialect pieces. Dunbar came to resent this; in 'The Poet' (1903) he complained that the world praised only 'a jingle in a broken tongue'. Critics therefore often read 'We Wear the Mask' also as a statement about Dunbar's own position as an artist, smiling for his audience. Later writers, among them Maya Angelou and Ralph Ellison, took up the idea of the mask in African American literature.

    Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York, 1896); Paul Laurence Dunbar, "The Poet", Lyrics of Love and Laughter (1903); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  13. Double consciousness l. 4

    Eight years after Dunbar's poem, Du Bois described the Black American's 'two-ness', 'two warring ideals in one dark body', and the 'veil' through which he sees and is seen. Dunbar's contrast of grinning mask and 'torn and bleeding hearts' dramatises the same division. Du Bois's analysis is public domain (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903) and is the standard companion text for the poem in teaching.

    W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 1, "Of Our Spiritual Strivings"; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction
