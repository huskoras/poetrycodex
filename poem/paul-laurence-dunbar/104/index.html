When Malindy Sings

by Paul Laurence Dunbar 1872–1906

Living

G'way an' quit dat noise, Miss Lucy--
Put dat music book away;
What's de use to keep on tryin'?
Ef you practise twell you 're gray,
You cain't sta't no notes a-flyin'
Lak de ones dat rants and rings
F'om de kitchen to be big woods
When Malindy sings.

You ain't got de nachel o'gans
Fu' to make de soun' come right,
You ain't got de tu'ns an' twistin's
Fu' to make it sweet an' light.
Tell you one thing now, Miss Lucy,
An' I 'm tellin' you fu' true,
When hit comes to raal right singin',
'T ain't no easy thing to do.

Easy 'nough fu' folks to hollah,
Lookin' at de lines an' dots,
When dey ain't no one kin sence it,
An' de chune comes in, in spots;
But fu' real melojous music,
Dat jes' strikes yo' hea't and clings,
Jes' you stan' an' listen wif me
When Malindy sings.

Ain't you nevah hyeahd Malindy?
Blessed soul, tek up de cross!
Look hyeah, ain't you jokin', honey?
Well, you don't know whut you los'.
Y' ought to hyeah dat gal a-wa'blin',
Robins, la'ks, an' all dem things,
Heish dey moufs an' hides dey faces
When Malindy sings.

Fiddlin' man jes' stop his fiddlin',
Lay his fiddle on de she'f;
Mockin'-bird quit tryin' to whistle,
'Cause he jes' so shamed hisse'f.
Folks a-playin' on de banjo
Draps dey fingahs on de strings--
Bless yo' soul--fu'gits to move em,
When Malindy sings.

She jes' spreads huh mouf and hollahs,
"Come to Jesus," twell you hyeah
Sinnahs' tremblin' steps and voices,
Timid-lak a-drawin' neah;
Den she tu'ns to "Rock of Ages,"
Simply to de cross she clings,
An' you fin' yo' teahs a-drappin'
When Malindy sings.

Who dat says dat humble praises
Wif de Master nevah counts?
Heish yo' mouf, I hyeah dat music,
Ez hit rises up an' mounts--
Floatin' by de hills an' valleys,
Way above dis buryin' sod,
Ez hit makes its way in glory
To de very gates of God!

Oh, hit's sweetah dan de music
Of an edicated band;
An' hit's dearah dan de battle's
Song o' triumph in de lan'.
It seems holier dan evenin'
When de solemn chu'ch bell rings,
Ez I sit an' ca'mly listen
While Malindy sings.

Towsah, stop dat ba'kin', hyeah me!
Mandy, mek dat chile keep still;
Don't you hyeah de echoes callin'
F'om de valley to de hill?
Let me listen, I can hyeah it,
Th'oo de bresh of angels' wings,
Sof an' sweet, "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot,"
Ez Malindy sings.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and speaker l. 1

    Dunbar divided Majors and Minors into poems in standard English ('Majors') and dialect pieces ('Minors'); this poem belongs to the second group and was reprinted in Lyrics of Lowly Life, the book W. D. Howells introduced. The speaker is an older Black woman or man, probably a servant, addressing 'Miss Lucy', the young white mistress practising from a music book. The poem's comedy depends on this inversion: the servant instructs the mistress in what real singing is. Accounts of Dunbar's life connect the poem with his mother Matilda, who sang hymns and spirituals at home.

    EBSCO Research Starters, 'When Malindy Sings' by Paul Laurence Dunbar (publication and background); Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York: Dodd, Mead, 1896); text as in The Complete Poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar (1913), Project Gutenberg; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  2. Dialect and its controversy ll. 3-4

    Spellings such as 'twell' (till), 'sta't' (start), 'lak' (like) and 'nachel' (natural) imitate pronunciation. Howells praised Dunbar chiefly for such poems, and the white market preferred them, which frustrated the poet; in 'The Poet' he complained that the world praised only 'a jingle in a broken tongue'. James Weldon Johnson later argued that dialect had become an instrument with 'but two full stops, humor and pathos'. Since the 1980s critics such as Gates and Braxton have read poems like this one as affectionate and skilful renderings of vernacular culture that also turn the 'plantation tradition' against itself.

    James Weldon Johnson, ed., The Book of American Negro Poetry (Harcourt, Brace, 1922), preface (on dialect); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Henry Louis Gates Jr., Figures in Black: Words, Signs, and the 'Racial' Self (Oxford University Press, 1987), on Dunbar and dialect; Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York: Dodd, Mead, 1896); text as in The Complete Poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar (1913), Project Gutenberg

  3. kitchen to big woods l. 7

    The kitchen fixes Malindy's social place as a cook or house servant, the big woods the reach of her voice. 'Be' is evidently a misprint for 'de' (the) in this text, as the dialect elsewhere always uses 'de'; this archive reproduces its source. The line's geography, from house to wilderness, anticipates the later stanzas in which the voice rises from earth to 'de very gates of God'.

    Paul Laurence Dunbar, Lyrics of Lowly Life, with an introduction by W. D. Howells (New York: Dodd, Mead, 1896); text as in The Complete Poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar (1913), Project Gutenberg; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  4. Stanza and refrain l. 8 and passim

    Each stanza alternates lines of four trochaic feet with shorter, catalectic lines, and most close on the refrain, a device of song and ballad that suits a poem about singing. The variations at the end ('While Malindy sings', 'Ez Malindy sings') mark the shift from argument to rapt listening. The refrain's rhyme, 'sings' with 'rings', 'strings', 'clings', 'wings', ties the stanzas together.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  5. Natural gifts versus training ll. 9-12

    'Nachel o'gans' are natural vocal organs; 'tu'ns an' twistin's' are the melodic turns, slides and ornaments characteristic of African American sacred singing, which cannot be learned from notation. The opposition of nature and art is old (it appears in the eighteenth-century praise of 'native woodnotes'), but here it has a racial and social edge, since the 'natural' singer is a servant and the trained one her mistress. Some critics warn that the poem risks the stereotype of Black musicality as instinct; others stress that it attributes real artistry and spiritual authority to Malindy.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Henry Louis Gates Jr., Figures in Black: Words, Signs, and the 'Racial' Self (Oxford University Press, 1987), on Dunbar and dialect; W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 14, 'Of the Sorrow Songs'

  6. Lines and dots l. 18

    The speaker describes written music from outside, as an unfamiliar code of 'lines an' dots'. Singing from the page produces only 'hollah' and a tune that 'comes in, in spots'. The joke sets literate, European musical culture against oral tradition. Readers have seen an irony here: Dunbar, a highly literate poet writing in print for a mainly white readership, praises the unwritten art he can only imitate on the page.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  7. sence l. 19

    No one listening can make out what is being sung from the book. 'Chune' in the next line is the dialect form of 'tune', and 'melojous' (l. 21) of 'melodious'. The comic misspellings carry the speaker's confidence and humour. The speaker's point is that notation-bound singing has no meaning a listener can feel; real music, as the next lines say, 'strikes yo' hea't and clings'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sense, v.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  8. 'Tek up de cross' l. 26

    Christ says 'If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me' (Matthew 16:24). The speaker uses the phrase as a pious interjection of astonishment that Miss Lucy has never heard Malindy. It also prepares the hymn 'Rock of Ages' in the sixth stanza, where Malindy 'to de cross she clings'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 16:24; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  9. Birds silenced ll. 30-31

    The hyperbole is a pastoral convention: a singer so fine that nature listens, as birds and beasts listened to Orpheus. The larks are a literary inheritance (Shelley's skylark), the robin and mockingbird American. The mockingbird, famous for imitating other birds, 'quit tryin' to whistle' because even imitation fails, a joke that also bears on Miss Lucy's attempts to copy what she cannot feel.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.86-105 and 11.1-2 (Orpheus charms trees, beasts and birds); Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  10. Fiddle and banjo ll. 33-38

    Fiddle and banjo were the instruments of Black dance music in the nineteenth-century South, and the banjo, an instrument of African origin, had been appropriated by blackface minstrel shows. The players stop, fingers dropped on the strings. Their silence moves the poem from the secular world toward the hymns and spiritual of the following stanzas. The comic details, the fiddle laid on the shelf and fingers dropped on the strings, show the musicians' astonishment.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979); Dena J. Epstein, Sinful Tunes and Spirituals: Black Folk Music to the Civil War (University of Illinois Press, 1977)

  11. 'Come to Jesus' and 'Rock of Ages' ll. 42-46

    'Come to Jesus' was the refrain of a popular nineteenth-century revival song of invitation, sung while sinners came forward to the 'mourners' bench', which explains the 'Sinnahs' tremblin' steps' drawing near. 'Rock of Ages' by the Anglican clergyman Augustus Toplady, first published in 1776, includes the line 'Simply to Thy cross I cling', which Dunbar adapts as 'Simply to de cross she clings'. Malindy's repertoire thus moves from the camp meeting to the standard hymnal and, in the last stanza, to the spiritual.

    Augustus M. Toplady, 'Rock of Ages', Gospel Magazine (1776), text at Hymnary.org; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  12. Humble praises ll. 49-50

    'Master' here means God, though the word inevitably recalls the slave-master, a doubleness that gives the stanza its force. Against anyone who scorns humble praise, the speaker hears the music rise 'above dis buryin' sod', the earth of graves and of mortal life, to heaven. The stanza is the poem's spiritual claim: an uneducated servant's voice reaches God more surely than trained performance.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 14, 'Of the Sorrow Songs'

  13. The gates of God l. 56

    The image combines the ascent of prayer (Psalm 141:2, 'Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense') with the gates of the heavenly city (Revelation 21:12-21). It is the high point of the poem and the only stanza without the refrain, as if the voice has left earthly measures behind. The rising movement also echoes the spirituals' constant imagery of ascent, ladders and chariots, which the final stanza makes explicit.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 21:12-21; Psalm 141:2; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  14. Better than the band ll. 57-60

    The comparison draws on late nineteenth-century popular culture, in which uniformed brass bands and patriotic 'battle' songs from the Civil War were widely admired. The scale of value is spiritual: Malindy's music is 'holier dan evenin'' when the church bell rings. The repeated comparisons ('sweetah', 'dearah', 'holier') give the stanza a sermon-like rhetoric. The implied contrast is with secular, institutional music.

    Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes; Peter Revell, Paul Laurence Dunbar (Twayne, 1979)

  15. Towsah and Mandy ll. 65-66

    The speaker silences the dog and the child to listen: the domestic scene returns just before the climax. 'Towser' was a stock name for a large dog in English and American usage. The interruptions give the poem a dramatic, spoken quality, and they show the speaker's world as a crowded household rather than the parlour of Miss Lucy. 'Ba'kin'' is barking.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'towser, n.'; Joanne M. Braxton, ed., The Collected Poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar (University Press of Virginia, 1993), introduction and notes

  16. 'Swing Low, Sweet Chariot' l. 71

    The spiritual draws on Elijah's ascent in 'a chariot of fire' (2 Kings 2:11) and imagines angels coming to carry the singer home. The Fisk Jubilee Singers' concert tours from 1871 brought spirituals to American and European audiences, and the singers helped to popularise this one. Du Bois called such songs 'the sorrow songs', the music of slavery's hope. Ending the poem with the spiritual, heard 'Th'oo de bresh of angels' wings', makes Malindy's song the vehicle of the ascent it describes.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Kings 2:11; W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 14, 'Of the Sorrow Songs'; Andrew Ward, Dark Midnight When I Rise: The Story of the Fisk Jubilee Singers (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2000)
