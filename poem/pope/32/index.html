Epistle to Mrs Teresa Blount, on Her Leaving the Town after the Coronation

by Alexander Pope 1688–1744

Relationships

As some fond virgin, whom her mother's care
Drags from the town to wholesome country air,
Just when she learns to roll a melting eye,
And hear a spark, yet think no danger nigh;
From the dear man unwilling she must sever,
Yet takes one kiss before she parts for ever:
Thus from the world fair Zephalinda flew,
Saw others happy, and with sighs withdrew;
Not that their pleasures caused her discontent,
She sigh'd not that they staid, but that she went.

She went to plain-work, and to purling brooks,
Old-fashion'd halls, dull aunts, and croaking rooks:
She went from opera, park, assembly, play,
To morning-walks, and prayers three hours a-day:
To part her time 'twixt reading and bohea,
To muse, and spill her solitary tea;
Or o'er cold coffee trifle with the spoon,
Count the slow clock, and dine exact at noon;
Divert her eyes with pictures in the fire,
Hum half a tune, tell stories to the 'squire;
Up to her godly garret after seven,
There starve and pray, for that's the way to heaven.

Some 'squire, perhaps, you take delight to rack;
Whose game is whist, whose treat, a toast in sack;
Who visits with a gun, presents you birds,
Then gives a smacking buss, and cries--No words!
Or with his hound comes hallooing from the stable,
Makes love with nods, and knees beneath a table;
Whose laughs are hearty, though his jests are coarse,
And loves you best of all things--but his horse.

In some fair evening, on your elbow laid,
You dream of triumphs in the rural shade;
In pensive thought recall the fancied scene,
See coronations rise on every green;
Before you pass the imaginary sights
Of lords, and earls, and dukes, and garter'd knights,
While the spread fan o'ershades your closing eyes;
Then give one flirt, and all the vision flies.
Thus vanish sceptres, coronets, and balls,
And leave you in lone woods, or empty walls!

So when your slave, at some dear idle time,
(Not plagued with headaches, or the want of rhyme)
Stands in the streets, abstracted from the crew,
And while he seems to study, thinks of you;
Just when his fancy paints your sprightly eyes,
Or sees the blush of soft Parthenia rise,
Gay pats my shoulder, and you vanish quite,
Streets, chairs, and coxcombs rush upon my sight;
Vex'd to be still in town, I knit my brow,
Look sour, and hum a tune, as you do now.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion, date and addressee title; l. 1

    George I was crowned at Westminster on 20 October 1714, and the festivities brought the Catholic gentry, briefly, into London society. Teresa Blount (1688-1759) and her sister Martha, Pope's close friends from Mapledurham near Reading, had to return to the country afterwards. The poem was first printed in Pope's Works of 1717 as 'To the same, on her leaving the Town after the Coronation', following the epistle that accompanied a gift of Voiture's works; 'the same' is Miss Blount. The addressee is generally identified as Teresa, since 'Zephalinda' was her coterie name, and the archive's title names her; her sister Martha is the likelier 'Parthenia' of l. 46. The poem's teasing affection has been read in the light of Pope's shifting feelings for both sisters.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; The Works of Mr. Alexander Pope (London, 1717): 'To the same, on her leaving the Town after the Coronation' (Wikisource transcription); 'Teresa Blount', Wikipedia (the Epistle addressed to Teresa; her sister Martha missed the coronation through illness); Valerie Rumbold, Women's Place in Pope's World (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1989), on Teresa and Martha Blount

  2. Mock-epic simile ll. 1-8

    Homer and Virgil introduce a comparison with 'as when' and close it with 'so' or 'thus'. Pope uses the same frame on a tiny scale: a girl dragged from a flirtation by her mother is the vehicle, and Zephalinda's departure is the tenor. The comedy lies in the disproportion, a technique Pope was perfecting at the same time in The Rape of the Lock (1714). 'The world' here means fashionable society, the beau monde, as opposed to the country. Note too that the simile is itself a small story with a kiss 'before she parts for ever', so that the exaggerated pathos of 'for ever' is gently mocked.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'world'

  3. a melting eye l. 3

    'Melting' in the sense 'tender, languishing, softened by love' was a standard term of amatory verse and drama in Pope's day, and to 'roll' the eyes was the conventional stage gesture of a woman in love. The phrase is therefore slightly ironic: the girl has learned the outward signs of feeling as a social skill, just as she is learning to 'hear a spark' without noticing the risk. Pope's Eloisa to Abelard (1717) uses the same vocabulary seriously; here it belongs to the light satire of manners.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'melting'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  4. spark l. 4

    The OED records 'spark' in the sense of an elegant or foppish young man, often a lover, from the late sixteenth century; it was common in Restoration comedy. To 'hear a spark' is to listen to his addresses, and 'yet think no danger nigh' points to the mother's reason for removing her daughter to 'wholesome country air'. The moral language (danger, wholesome) is put in the mother's mouth and teased; the poem's sympathy lies with the girl's regret.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spark'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  5. Zephalinda l. 7

    Coterie names drawn from French heroic romance were fashionable among young women and their admirers. In 1713 the sisters corresponded with James Moore Smythe, author of the comedy The Rival Modes, in which he figures as 'Alexis', Teresa as 'Zephalinda' and Martha as 'Parthenissa'. Pope adopts the name to give the epistle the air of a romance heroine's lament while using an in-joke the addressee would recognise. The romance name also fits the poem's theme: the lady's imagination keeps turning her country life into a scene from fiction.

    'Martha Blount', Wikipedia (the 1713 names Alexis, Zephalinda and Parthenissa in the circle of James Moore Smythe); Valerie Rumbold, Women's Place in Pope's World (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1989), on Teresa and Martha Blount; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town

  6. Antithesis and the couplet ll. 9-10

    'Staid' is the usual eighteenth-century spelling of 'stayed'. The couplet is a model of the Augustan heroic couplet, closed and pointed, with the antithesis divided at the caesura. Its wit is a kind act of defence: Zephalinda is cleared of envy, but in a way that confesses how much she wanted to remain. The phrase 'she went' is then picked up as an anaphora in the next paragraph ('She went to ... She went from'), turning the regret into a comic itinerary of rural boredom.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stay'

  7. plain-work; purling brooks l. 11

    The OED defines 'plain work' as plain needlework, the hemming and seaming of household linen, as distinct from decorative work. Pairing it with 'purling brooks' (murmuring streams) yokes domestic drudgery with the pastoral clichés of country poetry, and the line ends with 'croaking rooks', the noisy birds of rookeries near old country houses. Pope himself mocked 'purling streams' as a cliché of bad pastoral; here the phrase measures the gap between the poetic idea of the country and its reality for a young woman.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'plain work'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'purling'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town

  8. Pleasures of the town l. 13

    Italian opera had become a craze in London from about 1705, and Handel's Rinaldo (1711) sealed its fashion. The 'park' is Hyde Park or St James's Park, where the fashionable walked and drove; an 'assembly' was an evening gathering of polite company for cards, conversation and dancing. The line moves in four quick beats, and the next line answers with the country day: 'morning-walks, and prayers three hours a-day', the long devotions of a pious Catholic household. The two lists set London's evening pleasures against rural morning duties.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'assembly'

  9. bohea l. 15

    The OED records 'bohea' from the early eighteenth century as the name of a black tea from the Wuyi hills of Fujian province, at first a fine grade and later a cheap one. Tea was still a luxury in 1714 and associated with women's sociability; to drink it alone, as here, empties it of its social purpose. The couplet's rhyme 'bohea / tea' follows the period pronunciation, in which 'tea' was often sounded 'tay', as in The Rape of the Lock's rhyme of 'tea' with 'obey'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bohea'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  10. Solitary tea and cold coffee ll. 16-17

    The comedy of these lines is bathos: 'To muse' is a dignified, poetic activity, and it is followed at once by the clumsiness of spilling the tea. Coffee left to go cold and a spoon used only to 'trifle' show time that has nothing to fill it, and the next line's 'Count the slow clock' makes the theme explicit. The detail of dining 'exact at noon' marks the country timetable, earlier and more regular than the fashionable London hour for dinner, which was moving later in the afternoon in this period. Pope's eye for domestic minutiae anticipates the novel of manners.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (New Haven: Yale UP, 1985), on the Blount sisters

  11. pictures in the fire l. 19

    Looking for faces and scenes in the fire was a proverbial idle amusement. It is a small instance of fancy, and it looks forward to the third paragraph, where Zephalinda dreams of the coronation in 'imaginary sights'. 'Hum half a tune' and 'tell stories to the 'squire' complete the picture of a mind half-occupied; the poem ends with the poet himself humming a tune, so the motif returns in the last line.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  12. starve l. 22

    The OED records the older sense of 'starve', to die or suffer from cold, which survived in dialect long after the hunger sense dominated. A 'godly garret' is a cold attic bedroom given a mock-pious epithet, and bedtime 'after seven' is absurdly early by town standards. The couplet closes the paragraph with a comic aphorism ('for that's the way to heaven') that glances at the Blounts' devout Catholic household without real hostility; Pope, himself a Catholic, is mocking rural austerity rather than religion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'starve'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  13. The country squire ll. 23-30

    The apostrophe in 'squire marks it as an abbreviation of 'esquire'. The country squire, hard-drinking, hunting and boorish, was a stock figure in Restoration and early eighteenth-century comedy and in periodical essays such as the Spectator's portrait of Sir Roger de Coverley (a gentler version). 'Rack' means to torture, as on the rack, and is the language of romance: the lady's only power in the country is to torment her clumsy admirer. The portrait is built of telling details: he courts with dead game, nods and knee-pressing under the table.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rack'

  14. whist; a toast in sack l. 24

    In 1714 whist was considered an unfashionable game of country houses and servants' halls; it became fashionable later in the century. 'Sack' was the general name for dry white wines imported from Spain and the Canaries, Falstaff's drink. A 'toast in sack' may be the drinking of her health, or a piece of toast soaked in wine, a common old-fashioned dish; editors note both possibilities, and either way the 'treat' is unrefined. The comic point is that the squire's pleasures are as old-fashioned as the country halls in l. 12.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sack'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'whist'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town

  15. smacking buss; No words! l. 26

    The OED records 'buss' as a rough or playful kiss, a low word by Pope's time; 'smacking' adds the sound. 'No words!' is a colloquial injunction to secrecy. The country lover's kiss answers the 'one kiss' of the opening simile (l. 6), but crude where the other was sentimental. The squire 'visits with a gun' and brings 'birds' (game he has shot) as love-tokens, the sporting gentleman's courtship.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'buss'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  16. Anticlimax l. 30

    The dash before the last two words sets up the anticlimax, a favourite device of Pope's satire (compare the 'Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux' of The Rape of the Lock). The pointed final line sums up the rural suitor's priorities and the fate of a woman of wit in the country. Readers have noted that the joke is affectionate rather than cruel, and that it gives Zephalinda the power to mock her suitor that she lacks in the life she has been sent back to.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; John Butt, ed., The Poems of Alexander Pope: A One-Volume Edition of the Twickenham Text (London: Methuen, 1963)

  17. The day-dream of coronations ll. 31-40

    This paragraph is a miniature of the 'vision' passages of romance and of Pope's own Rape of the Lock. 'Triumphs' are pageants or processions, and 'garter'd knights' are Knights of the Garter in their insignia, who walked in the coronation procession. 'Coronets' are the small crowns worn by peers at a coronation. The vision rises 'on every green', so the country is briefly transformed into court, and then 'all the vision flies'. The close, 'lone woods, or empty walls', is gently mock-tragic, the forsaken romance heroine in her solitude.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'triumph'

  18. flirt l. 38

    The OED gives 'flirt' first as a sudden jerk or quick movement, and records 'a flirt of a fan' among its uses; the fan was a woman's instrument of social expression. The verb 'to flirt' in the sense of trifling amorously was also coming into use, and Pope enjoys the ambiguity: the gesture that dispels the vision is itself the coquette's gesture. In the Spectator no. 102 (1711) Addison satirised an 'academy' for the 'exercise of the fan', including the 'flutter' of the fan, a good gloss on the cultural meaning of the gesture.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flirt'; Joseph Addison, The Spectator, no. 102 (27 June 1711), on the exercise of the fan; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town

  19. The poet's turn ll. 41-44

    The last paragraph answers the 'As ... Thus' of the opening with a second 'So when': the poet is in town and dreaming of the country lady, as she is in the country dreaming of the town. 'Slave' is the conventional servant of the lover in courtly and romance convention. The parenthesis 'Not plagued with headaches, or the want of rhyme' is self-mockery: Pope suffered from chronic headaches, and the 'want of rhyme' is the poet's occupational complaint. 'Abstracted from the crew' means withdrawn in thought from the crowd.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (New Haven: Yale UP, 1985), on the Blount sisters; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  20. Parthenia l. 46

    The name derives from Greek parthenos, 'maiden'. In the 1713 names of the Moore Smythe circle Martha was 'Parthenissa', and editors commonly take 'Parthenia' as Martha, so that the poet's day-dream includes both sisters. The 'blush' fits the name's suggestion of modesty. Since Martha was to become Pope's lifelong companion, biographers have found the line suggestive, though in 1714 it is still part of a playful compliment to both women; Rumbold and Mack discuss the shifting relations of Pope with the two sisters.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; 'Martha Blount', Wikipedia; Valerie Rumbold, Women's Place in Pope's World (Cambridge: Cambridge UP, 1989), on Teresa and Martha Blount; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (New Haven: Yale UP, 1985), on the Blount sisters

  21. Gay l. 47

    Gay, the future author of The Beggar's Opera (1728), was a fellow member with Pope of the Scriblerus Club, formed in 1714. His poem Trivia, or the Art of Walking the Streets of London (1716) is a mock-georgic of the city's streets, and the line's crowd of 'Streets, chairs, and coxcombs' is just the world of that poem. The tap on the shoulder works like the lady's flirt of the fan: a small physical gesture dispels the vision and returns the dreamer to where he is.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (New Haven: Yale UP, 1985), on the Blount sisters; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem

  22. chairs; coxcombs l. 48

    The sedan chair, a covered seat on poles carried by two men, was the usual way for the well-to-do to move through crowded London streets in the early eighteenth century. 'Coxcomb' (from the jester's cap shaped like a cock's comb) had come to mean a conceited, showy fool, a fop. The line is the urban counterpart of the rural list in l. 12 ('Old-fashion'd halls, dull aunts, and croaking rooks'), each of three items: the town is no more satisfying to the poet than the country is to the lady.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chair'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'coxcomb'; Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town

  23. The symmetrical close ll. 49-50

    The final couplet closes the poem's design. Zephalinda in the country longs for town; the poet in town longs for the country because she is there. 'Hum a tune' recalls l. 20, and the present tense 'as you do now' imagines her reading the poem with exactly that expression. The joke turns the satire back on its author, so that the poem's mockery of the lady becomes a shared joke about the restlessness of desire. Critics have admired the poem as one of Pope's most graceful light pieces, combining the satire of manners with intimacy.

    Norman Ault and John Butt, eds., Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition of the Poems of Alexander Pope, vol. VI (London: Methuen, 1954), notes to the Epistle to Miss Blount on her leaving the Town; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (New Haven: Yale UP, 1985), on the Blount sisters; Pat Rogers, ed., Alexander Pope: The Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 2006), notes to the poem
