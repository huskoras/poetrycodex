Living

Ode on Solitude

by Alexander Pope 1688–1744

Living

1 Happy the man, whose wish and care
A few paternal acres bound,
Content to breathe his native air
In his own ground.

2 Whose herds with milk, whose fields with bread,
Whose flocks supply him with attire,
Whose trees in summer yield him shade,
In winter fire.

3 Blest, who can unconcern'dly find
Hours, days, and years slide soft away,
In health of body, peace of mind,
Quiet by day;

4 Sound sleep by night; study and ease,
Together mix'd; sweet recreation;
And innocence, which most does please,
With meditation.

5 Thus let me live, unseen, unknown,
Thus unlamented let me die,
Steal from the world, and not a stone
Tell where I lie.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ode on Solitude: date title; st. 1

    The ode exists in several versions. Pope sent one to his friend Henry Cromwell in a letter of 17 July 1709; a version appeared in 1717 (Poems on Several Occasions), and Pope himself described it as written at about twelve years old. That early dating rests on Pope's word alone, and since he liked to stress his precocity and the poem exists in revised versions, it should be held with some caution. Whatever its date, it is a young poet's exercise in a classical genre, and it reflects the Pope family's retired life at Binfield in Windsor Forest after Catholic laws pushed them out of London.

    Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'; Pope's letter to Henry Cromwell, 17 July 1709 (index of Pope's correspondence, Warburg Institute); Representative Poetry Online, 'Solitude: An Ode', text from Pope's Own Miscellany (Nonsuch Press, 1935), a reprint of Poems on Several Occasions (1717); Wikipedia, 'Ode on Solitude'

  2. Happy the man st. 1, l. 1

    Horace's second Epode begins 'Beatus ille qui procul negotiis, / ut prisca gens mortalium, / paterna rura bobus exercet suis' ('Happy the man who, far from business, like the early race of mortals, works his ancestral fields with his own oxen'). Pope's 'paternal acres' renders 'paterna rura'. Horace's poem has a famous sting: the speaker turns out to be a moneylender who praises country life and then returns to usury. Pope keeps the ideal without the irony. The 'beatus ille' tradition was hugely popular in seventeenth-century English verse (Jonson, Cowley, Herrick).

    Horace, Epodes 2 (Latin text); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'; Maren-Sofie Rostvig, The Happy Man: Studies in the Metamorphoses of a Classical Ideal, 2 vols (Oslo, 1954-58)

  3. stanza form sts. 1-5

    The pattern of three longer lines and one short 'tail' has sometimes been compared to the Sapphic stanza (three hendecasyllables and an adonic), used by Horace in several odes, though the likeness is loose and the stanza is ordinary English quatrain-making. The short fourth line makes each stanza settle like a cadence, and the effect suits a poem about contentment and limits. The rhyme is ABAB. In the last stanza the short line becomes an epitaph: 'Tell where I lie.'

    Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'; Representative Poetry Online, 'Horatian ode' (form note)

  4. paternal acres st. 1, l. 2

    The syntax is compressed: the man's wishes and cares are bounded by a few acres he has inherited. 'Bound' is both verb (limit) and suggestion of a boundary. Pope's father, a retired linen merchant, had bought the small estate at Binfield around 1700; as Catholics the family faced penal laws and double taxation, which gives the ideal of a self-sufficient, unambitious life a quiet personal context. 'Native air' adds health and rootedness.

    Horace, Epodes 2.3 (Latin text); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'; Maynard Mack, Alexander Pope: A Life (Yale University Press, 1985), on Binfield

  5. Whose herds ... In winter fire st. 2

    The anaphora of 'whose' builds the list of the farm's gifts across the year. The economy described is pre-commercial, the classical ideal of autarkeia (self-sufficiency) praised by Horace and Virgil (Georgics 2.458-540, 'O fortunatos nimium'). The final short line 'In winter fire' completes the seasonal cycle and turns the trees from summer shade to winter firewood, a neat economy of a single source.

    Virgil, Georgics 2.458-74 (Latin text); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'

  6. Blest st. 3, l. 1

    The repetition marks the poem's second movement: after the farm's material gifts (sts. 1-2) come its inner rewards, health, peace, sleep and study (sts. 3-4). Latin beatus covers worldly good fortune and, in Christian Latin, spiritual blessedness, as in the Vulgate Beatitudes ('Beati pauperes spiritu'). Pope's English uses two words for the two senses, moving from 'happy' to the more religious 'blest', which suits a poet raised a Catholic. The construction 'Blest, who' (without 'is he') is a compressed classical idiom.

    Horace, Epodes 2.1 (Latin text); Matthew 5:3 (KJV); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'

  7. unconcern'dly st. 3, l. 1

    The word (four syllables, un-con-cern'd-ly) means 'without concern or disquiet' (OED). The stanza borrows from the philosophic retirement tradition: 'health of body, peace of mind' renders the classical ideal of mens sana in corpore sano (Juvenal 10.356) and Epicurean ataraxia. The sliding 'Hours, days, and years' is a rising sequence that makes time's passage both smooth and quick. The verb 'slide soft away' gives time the motion of a gentle stream rather than a rush.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'unconcernedly, adv.'; Juvenal, Satire 10.356 (Latin text); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'

  8. recreation ... meditation st. 4, ll. 2, 4

    In older pronunciation the suffix -tion could count as two syllables, so 'recreation' and 'meditation' make a full feminine rhyme. 'Recreation' meant refreshment of body and mind by pleasant occupation. The stanza mixes 'study and ease', the Horatian ideal of otium spent in reading and thought. 'Innocence, which most does please' gives the moral centre: the retired life is valued because it is blameless.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'recreation, n.1'; Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'

  9. unseen, unknown ... unlamented st. 5, ll. 1-2

    The chorus in Seneca's Thyestes (391-403) wishes to live and die quietly, unknown, and warns that death weighs heavily on one too well known to all but unknown to himself. Cowley translated these lines in his essay 'Of Obscurity'. Horace too praised the 'fallentis semita vitae', the path of a life that escapes notice (Epistles 1.18.103). The young Pope's wish is ironic in hindsight: he became the most famous poet of his age, and a stone at Twickenham does tell where he lies.

    Seneca, Thyestes 391-403 (Latin text); Horace, Epistles 1.18.103 (Latin text); Abraham Cowley, 'Of Obscurity', Essays (1668); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'

  10. Steal from the world st. 5, l. 3

    'Steal' in the intransitive sense of moving secretly or imperceptibly (OED, steal, v., sense of going stealthily). The quiet death mirrors the quiet life, and the verb gives the ending its tact: departure without notice. Compare Gray's later Elegy (1751), whose villagers keep 'the noiseless tenor of their way'; Pope's ode belongs to the same tradition of praise for obscure lives.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'steal, v.1'; Thomas Gray, Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), l. 76

  11. not a stone / Tell where I lie st. 5, ll. 3-4

    Classical and Renaissance poets often promised immortality through verse (Horace, Odes 3.30, 'exegi monumentum'). Pope inverts the convention: he wishes for no monument. The paradox is that the ode is itself a verbal tombstone spoken in the first person. 'Lie' carries the formula of epitaphs ('here lies'). The final short line, the most abrupt in the poem, enacts the silence asked for.

    Horace, Odes 3.30 (Latin text); Norman Ault and John Butt (eds.), Alexander Pope: Minor Poems, Twickenham Edition vol. 6 (Methuen, 1954), notes to 'Ode on Solitude'
