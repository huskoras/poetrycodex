Love

V. Oh Turkish maid of Shiraz! in thy hand

by Hafiz c. 1315–1390

Translated by Gertrude Lowthian Bell (1897) · From Poems from the Divan of Hafiz (1897)

Love

Oh Turkish maid of Shiraz! in thy hand
If thou’lt take my heart, for the mole on thy cheek
I would barter Bokhara and Samarkand.
Bring, Cup-bearer, all that is left of thy wine!
In the Garden of Paradise vainly thou’lt seek
The lip of the fountain of Ruknabad,
And the bowers of Mosalla where roses twine.

They have filled the city with blood and broil,
Those soft-voiced Lulis for whom we sigh;
As Turkish robbers fall on the spoil,
They have robbed and plundered the peace of my heart.
Dowered is my mistress, a beggar am I;
What shall I bring her? a beautiful face
Needs nor jewel nor mole nor the tiring-maid’s art.

Brave tales of singers and wine relate,
The key to the Hidden ’twere vain to seek;
No wisdom of ours has unlocked that gate,
And locked to our wisdom it still shall be.
But of Joseph’s beauty the lute shall speak;
And the minstrel knows that Zuleika came forth,
Love parting the curtains of modesty.

When thou spokest ill of thy servant ’twas well—
God pardon thee! for thy words were sweet;
Not unwelcomed the bitterest answer fell
From lips where the ruby and sugar lay.
But, fair Love, let good counsel direct thy feet;
Far dearer to youth than dear life itself
Are the warnings of one grown wise—and grey!

The song is sung and the pearl is strung;
Come hither, oh Hafiz, and sing again!
And the listening Heavens above thee hung
Shall loose o’er thy verse the Pleiades’ chain.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The 'Shirazi Turk' and Bell's translation ll. 1-3

    Hafiz of Shiraz (c. 1315-1390) wrote this ghazal of about nine couplets; its first line names the beloved as 'that Turk of Shiraz'. In Persian lyric 'Turk' means a beautiful, fair-skinned, imperious beloved, because Turkish soldiers and slaves were proverbial for their looks and their cruelty; the word need not mean that the person was literally Turkish, and the Persian does not mark gender. Bell chose 'maid', as William Jones did in 1771 ('Sweet maid, if thou would'st charm my sight'), usually counted the first English version of a poem by Hafiz, made by way of a Latin rendering. Bell (1868-1926), later famous as traveller, archaeologist and political officer in the Middle East, published these translations in 1897, after a visit to Persia. The ghazal has been read both literally and mystically: Bell herself thought the mystical interpretation added little to its value.

    Gertrude Lowthian Bell, trans., Poems from the Divan of Hafiz (London: Heinemann, 1897), poem V; 'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); William Jones, 'A Persian Song of Hafiz', in A Grammar of the Persian Language (London, 1771); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  2. The beauty-spot l. 2

    In the Persian the mole is hindu, an epithet meaning black or dark (Indians being darker-skinned than Persians), which also sets an image of a dark 'Hindu' slave on the face of a 'Turk', so that the line plays on peoples and empires as well as features. Bell keeps 'the mole on thy cheek' and loses the ethnic wordplay. The single black spot on a fair cheek is one of the fixed beauties of Persian and Ottoman lyric; Sufi readers made it the point of divine unity, a single dot from which the world unfolds. The extravagance of trading two great cities for one mole is the joke of the couplet.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Leonard Lewisohn, ed., Hafiz and the Religion of Love in Classical Persian Poetry (London: I. B. Tauris, 2010)

  3. Bukhara and Samarkand, and the Timur anecdote l. 3

    Bukhara and Samarkand, in present-day Uzbekistan, were the richest cities of Central Asia. The story that Timur (Tamerlane), after taking Shiraz, summoned Hafiz and asked how he dared give away Timur's own cities for a mole, and that the poet answered that such prodigality was why he was now so poor, first appears in Shuja' Shirazi's Anis al-nas (1426) and was elaborated by Dawlatshah Samarqandi in his biographies of poets (completed 1486-87). It is almost certainly legend; the anecdote nevertheless shows how early the couplet became proverbial. Some scholars date the poem after about 1370, when Timur began to make Samarkand famous; others reject that inference.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); Dawlatshah Samarqandi, Tadhkirat al-shu'ara (1486-87), notice of Hafiz; A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  4. Cup-bearer (saqi) l. 4

    The saqi pours wine at the majlis, the evening gathering of friends with music and poetry, and is usually a handsome youth. 'All that is left of thy wine' renders mey-i baqi, a pun in the Persian, since baqi means both 'remaining' and 'everlasting': the wine left in the jar and the wine of eternity. The couplet then argues that even Paradise lacks the pleasures of Shiraz. Mystical commentators, such as the Ottoman Sudi (d. c. 1600), whose Turkish commentary on Hafiz was long standard, discuss whether wine and saqi are to be taken spiritually; Hafiz's ambiguity (iham) allows both.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Leonard Lewisohn, ed., Hafiz and the Religion of Love in Classical Persian Poetry (London: I. B. Tauris, 2010)

  5. Ruknabad l. 6

    The Ruknabad was a stream fed by an underground water-channel (qanat) north of Shiraz, traditionally said to have been built by the Buyid ruler Rukn al-Dawla in the tenth century, from whom it took its name. Hafiz praises it in several ghazals. The boast here is pointed: the Qur'anic Paradise is a garden of flowing rivers, yet the poet claims that Paradise lacks the banks of the Ruknabad. Bell's 'lip of the fountain' translates kinar, 'bank' or 'edge', but 'lip' also recalls the beloved's lips and the rim of the cup, a characteristic Hafizian overlap of landscape and body.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Gertrude Lowthian Bell, trans., Poems from the Divan of Hafiz (London: Heinemann, 1897), poem V

  6. Mosalla (Musalla) l. 7

    A musalla is literally an open-air place of prayer, used for the great festival prayers outside a city; in Shiraz it became the name of a district of gardens on the northern edge of the city, beside the Ruknabad, and Hafiz's tomb, the Hafiziyya, stands in that area. The word carries an irony that Persian readers enjoy: the poet prefers the rose-walks of a place named for prayer to the gardens of Paradise. Bell's 'bowers ... where roses twine' expands the Persian gulgasht, 'rose-walk' or 'promenade among roses'.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  7. Lulis l. 9

    Luli is the Persian word for the Romani or Gypsy people and, by extension, for itinerant musicians and dancers, figures of attraction and disorder. Hafiz calls them shukh-u shirinkar, 'saucy and sweet-working', and shahrashub, 'city-disturbing': they throw the town into turmoil as a raid would. Bell keeps the Persian word, explaining it only by 'soft-voiced'. Iraj Bashiri proposed an astronomical reading, taking the lulis as Venus and other planets, which has not won wide agreement.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); Gertrude Lowthian Bell, trans., Poems from the Divan of Hafiz (London: Heinemann, 1897), poem V

  8. 'As Turkish robbers fall on the spoil' ll. 10-11

    The Persian says that the lulis have taken patience from the heart 'as Turks take the tray of plunder' (khwan-i yaghma). Herman Bicknell, the Victorian translator of Hafiz, explained the 'feast of plunder' as a military custom of Turkestan, a banquet at which the dishes were set out to be seized, so the image is festive as well as violent; it also returns to the opening 'Turk', closing the first movement of the poem. Bell's 'Turkish robbers fall on the spoil' gives the sense of plunder but loses the feast. The rhyme of the Persian, -a ra, runs through the whole ghazal; Bell does not attempt the monorhyme.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Herman Bicknell, trans., Hafiz of Shiraz: Selections from his Poems (London: Trubner, 1875)

  9. The self-sufficient beloved ll. 12-14

    The Persian says the friend's beauty is independent (mustaghni) of 'our incomplete love': what need has a lovely face of powder, colour, mole and painted line? Bell turns the idea into social terms, 'Dowered is my mistress, a beggar am I', which keeps the inequality but shifts it to wealth. In mystical reading the couplet states the absolute self-sufficiency of God (al-Ghani, 'the Rich', is a divine name in the Qur'an), who has no need of human love. A 'tiring-maid' is a lady's maid who dresses her hair and attire (OED, tire v.2, tiring-woman): Bell's archaism for the cosmetics of the original.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, 'tiring-woman, n.'

  10. Bell's rearrangement of the couplets ll. 15-18

    The Persian ghazal is a chain of couplets (bayts) on one rhyme, each fairly self-contained, and manuscripts of Hafiz differ in the order and number of couplets; in one survey of ten manuscripts of this poem only two give the standard text, and the middle couplets (vv. 6-8) vary most. The poem has been a test case in the debate about the unity of the ghazal (Arberry, Rehder, Hillmann, Pritchett). Bell turns the couplets into stanzas of her own design but keeps the usual order, so that the riddle couplet, 'no one has solved and no one will solve this enigma by wisdom', stands before Joseph and Zuleika and the minstrel's song of Joseph answers the riddle. Her 'key to the Hidden' and 'locked' expand the single Persian image of 'opening' (gushudan) the riddle.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Gertrude Lowthian Bell, trans., Poems from the Divan of Hafiz (London: Heinemann, 1897), poem V

  11. Joseph and Zuleika ll. 19-21

    In Sura 12 of the Qur'an and in Genesis 39, Joseph is desired by his Egyptian master's wife; Islamic tradition names her Zulaykha. Persian poets, above all Jami in his Yusuf u Zulaykha (1483), made her a sympathetic figure, the lover overwhelmed by divine beauty, and the Sufis read Joseph as the manifestation of God's beauty. Hafiz's couplet says, 'from that ever-increasing beauty of Joseph I learned that love draws Zulaykha out from the curtain of chastity.' Bell makes the lute and the minstrel tell the tale, linking it with her previous stanza, a connection not in the Persian.

    Qur'an 12:23-32; Genesis 39:7-20 (KJV); 'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others)

  12. Ruby and sugar ll. 22-25

    The Persian couplet means: 'You spoke ill of me, and I am content; God forgive you, you spoke well. A bitter answer becomes the ruby lip that chews sugar.' Ruby (la'l) stands for red lips and sugar (shakkar) for sweet speech; the joke is that bitterness from such lips is a kind of sweetness. Wikipedia's survey notes that a group of manuscripts gives a different version of this couplet. Bell's 'God pardon thee!' keeps the playful blessing, a mock-pious formula, and her 'Not unwelcomed' captures the lover's masochistic pleasure in rebuke.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  13. The counsel of the wise elder ll. 26-28

    The 'old man' (pir) of the Persian is both an elderly adviser and, in Sufi vocabulary, the spiritual master. Here the aging poet advises the young beloved, perhaps to accept his love, a reversal of the usual ghazal in which the lover is rebuked. Rehder has suggested that this couplet, or the fourth, may be spurious, but most editors keep all nine. Bell's 'wise—and grey!', with its dash, makes a wry self-portrait that the Persian only implies.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  14. 'The song is sung and the pearl is strung' l. 29

    Persian criticism often compares a poem to pearls threaded on a string, each couplet a pearl. In the last couplet Hafiz addresses himself: 'You have made a ghazal and pierced pearls; come and sing it sweetly, Hafiz.' William Jones ended his 1771 version with the line 'like orient pearls at random strung', a phrase that became shorthand for the Western view of the ghazal as a set of loose couplets; scholars since Arberry have argued for subtler kinds of unity. Bell renders gufti and sufti with an English internal rhyme, 'sung ... strung'.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); William Jones, 'A Persian Song of Hafiz', in A Grammar of the Persian Language (London, 1771); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes

  15. The signature (takhallus) l. 30

    Persian, Ottoman and Urdu ghazals normally name the poet in the last couplet, using a pen-name (takhallus). Hafiz, 'one who has memorised', was the title for someone who knew the whole Qur'an by heart, which Shams al-Din Muhammad of Shiraz reputedly did. The signature often marks a shift of voice, the poet stepping outside the poem to praise or address himself. Compare the final couplets of the Fuzuli and Yunus Emre poems in this archive, where the poet's name is used to qualify or even deny the poem's speech.

    A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Leonard Lewisohn, ed., Hafiz and the Religion of Love in Classical Persian Poetry (London: I. B. Tauris, 2010)

  16. The necklace of the Pleiades ll. 31-32

    Persian poets saw the Pleiades (Surayya) as a necklace or a cluster of pearls; the image occurs before Hafiz, for instance in the preface of Sa'di's Gulistan. Hafiz's line says that heaven 'scatters the necklace of the Pleiades' over his verse, as listeners showered coins and gems (nisar) over a fine singer or a bride. Bicknell read it as heaven flinging largesse in delight, and Arberry judged the treatment of the closing theme here scarcely surpassed for beauty in the whole Divan. The pearls of the poem (l. 29) are answered by the pearls of the sky.

    'Shirazi Turk', Wikipedia (survey of the text, manuscripts and readings, citing Arberry, Hillmann, Rehder, Bashiri and others); A. J. Arberry, Fifty Poems of Hafiz (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1947), text, translations and notes; Sa'di, Gulistan, preface
