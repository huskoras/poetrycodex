Time & Brevity

Terkîb-i Bend-i Nâ-Tamâm

by Recaizade Mahmut Ekrem 1847–1914

From Nağme-i Seher (1288 AH / c. 1871)

Time & Brevity

Nedir menâfi'i emrse rûzenin bilmem
Bu dâr-ı pür-miheni cilvegâh eder âdem

Ne hazzı var meselâ ayş u nûş kılsa kişi
Degil mi âhiri nîş-i belâ vü zahm-ı elem

Bu oldı deydene-i rûzgâr-ı bed-girdâr
Ki her meserretin elbet sonu olur mâtem
Firîb-i hurde-i ikbâl-i dehr olur mukbil
Sanır ki ola cihân içre dâ'imâ hurrem

Döner mi çerh-i felek her zamân murâd üzre
Anıda bir gün eder iktizâ-yı devr-i dijem

Cihânda hâsılı yoktur hevesçe kâm almış
Gerek gedâ-yı muhakkar gerek şeh-i efham

Verir mi ârif olan kabz u bast-ı dehre vücûd
Olur mı nâfile hâtır-rübûde-i âlem

Hakîkat üzre tasavvur olunsa dünyânın
Çıkar vücûdu adem zevk u lütfu renciş ü gam

Degil degil bu fenâ bezmi câygâh-ı huzûr
Hazer ki olmayasın dil-harâb-ı câm-ı gurûr

Bu âlemin ki yogu vârı cümle yeksândır
Bu sırra vâkıf olan hâl-i dehre handândır

Eden hakîkat-i eşyâya iktisâb-ı vukuf
Seferber oldıgına âleme peşîmândır

Garîb-i gülşen-i âlâmdır bu bâg-ı fenâ
Gül-i şüküftesi hem-hâlet-i mugaylandır

Viren bu âlem-i sûrînin âl ü nakşına dil
Soñunda pâ-zede-i mihnet-i firâvândır
Bu sûrgehte olan bir nefes sürûr ile şâd
Hezâr sâle gam-ı mâtem ile giryândır

Emîn olur mu kişi dest-bürd-i kahrından
Felek ki eski cefâkâr-ı bezm-i imkândır

Muhâl ise ta'accüb intizâm-ı kevn ü fesâd
Esâs-ı dehr cihân olalı perîşândır

Olan bekasına ka'il bu kârgâh-ı gamın
Fenâ-yı nefsine esbâb arar harîfândır

Yazık bu mertebe re'yi sakîm olan dûna
Ki hande-bahş ola akvâli merd-i mecnûna

Yoluñ şaşırma tarîk-ı savâb ara her gâh
K'ider bu bâdiye çok rehrevânını gümrâh

Serâbı âb sınırsın hayâli ayn-ı misâl
Bu vechile seni bir gün kılar fütâde-i çâh

Ne çerh u bahtı zahîr et ne mâle ol magrûr
Ne eyle kendiñi dilhaste-i tahassür-i câh

Ne kâr-ı sa'bıñ olursa verir kolaylıgını
Bütün umûrda dergâh-ı hakkı eyle penâh

Açar suhûletile kufl-ı genc-i eltâfı
Medenk-i âh gibi zûr-ı bang-i yâ allâh
Kalaydı iş bize vay idi hâl-i zârımıza
Olurdu acz ile hep kârımız tazallüm-i vâh

Veren Hudâ mı degildir bu fikr ü aklı bize
Cebîn-i hamdi hemân eyle sûde-i dergâh

Tahayyür eyle Hudâ’nın kemâl-i kudretine
Edip semâ vü zemîne basîret üzre nigâh

Gör añla cilve-i esrâr-ı sun’-ı mevlâ’yı
Cihâna akl-ı kasîrince verme ma’nâyı

Study notes by the editors

  1. Terkîb-i Bend-i Nâ-Tamâm: an 'unfinished' terkib-i bend title; whole poem

    In a terkîb-i bend each stanza (bend, hâne) is a run of couplets on one rhyme, closed by a linking couplet (vâsıta) with a new rhyme that changes from stanza to stanza; in the tercî-i bend the linking couplet repeats. Here there are three bends: on -em (âdem, elem, mâtem...), closed by 'Degil degil bu fenâ bezmi...'; on -ândır, closed by 'Yazık bu mertebe...'; and on -âh, closed by 'Gör añla...'. Uzun notes that terkib-i bends often have seven to nine couplets per stanza and that elegy is their chief subject; this one, like many, turns the elegiac mood on the world itself. 'Nâ-tamâm' (Persian nā-tamām) means 'incomplete', which suggests that the young poet left it unfinished.

    Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Terciibend', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 40 (2011), pp. 487–489; Abdullah Uçman, 'Recâizâde Mahmud Ekrem', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 34 (2007), pp. 503–504; Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  2. Nağme-i Seher and the young Ekrem l. 2

    Uçman's article describes Nağme-i Seher as Ekrem's first verse collection, made up of poems in the old style; he worked in the Hariciye office among older poets of the divan tradition such as Leskofçalı Galib and Hersekli Ârif Hikmet and beside young reformers, and his new-style verse belongs to the later Zemzeme collections. The theme here, the misery and impermanence of the world, is the stock of hikemî (sententious) poetry, and the vocabulary is heavily Persian and Arabic: 'dâr-ı pür-mihen', 'this abode full of tribulations' (mihen is the Arabic plural of mihnet). Compare Ziya Paşa's gazel on the world as tavern and his Tercî-i Bend, written in the same decades.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Recâizâde Mahmud Ekrem', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 34 (2007), pp. 503–504; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mihnet'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. V, ed. E. G. Browne (London: Luzac, 1907)

  3. Metre: müctess (mefâilün feilâtün mefâilün feilün) l. 5

    Line 5 scans cleanly: Bu ol-dı dey / de-ne-i rûz / gâ-rı bed-gir / dâr (. − . − / . . − − / . − . − / − −), that is mefâilün feilâtün mefâilün and a final fa'lün (the last foot may be feilün or fa'lün). This is the müctess-i müsemmen-i mahbûn, one of the commonest metres of the gazel. Some lines need the usual licences, for instance a short syllable held long ('pür-mi-he-ni' in l. 2). The scansion offered here is from the text; check it against a prosody handbook.

    Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs

  4. ayş u nûş: feasting and drinking ll. 3–4

    'Ayş' (Arabic ʿaysh, life, pleasant living) and 'nûş' (Persian nūsh, drinking; also sweetness, honey) form a fixed pair for revelry. The next line answers with its opposite: 'nîş-i belâ' (the sting of calamity; Persian nīsh, sting) and 'zahm-ı elem' (the wound of pain). The near-rhyme nûş / nîş, honey and sting, is a classical play (cinâs), since the bee gives both. The couplet states the theme of the whole first bend: every pleasure ends in mourning (mâtem, l. 6).

    Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  5. deydene: the habit of an ill-natured age ll. 5–6

    'Deydene' (Arabic daydan) is habit or custom; 'rûzgâr' (Persian rōzgār) means time, the age, fortune (in modern Turkish it means 'wind'); 'bed-girdâr' (Persian bad-kirdār) is 'evil in conduct'. Time is personified as a wicked actor whose fixed habit is to turn every pleasure (meserret, Arabic masarra) into mourning (mâtem). The personification of time and the heavens as hostile agents is the base of the classical complaint of fortune (şikâyet-i felek).

    Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'felek'

  6. çerh-i felek: the turning wheel of heaven ll. 9–10

    'Çerh' (Persian charkh, wheel) and 'felek' (Arabic falak, celestial sphere) together name the revolving heavens of Ptolemaic cosmology, believed to govern fortune. The image is close to the European Wheel of Fortune. 'İktizâ-yı devr' is 'what the rotation requires'. The archive's 'devr-i dijem' at the end of l. 10 is obscure and may be a transcription error for a word rhyming in -em; the sense of the line, that the wheel's turning eventually reverses every wish, is clear.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'felek'; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  7. Beggar and king ll. 11–12

    'Gedâ' (Persian gadā) is a beggar, 'muhakkar' (Arabic muḥaqqar) despised; 'şeh' is the shah, and 'efham' (Arabic afkham) the most magnificent. The pairing of beggar and king is the great levelling commonplace of moral verse: fortune and death make no distinction. Ziya Paşa's tesdis in this archive uses the same contrast ('Be he Khusrev ... or be he beggar'), and the thought goes back to the Persian moralists, for whom the grave equalises shah and dervish alike.

    Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  8. kabz u bast: contraction and expansion l. 13

    'Kabz' (Arabic qabḍ, contraction, seizing) and 'bast' (Arabic basṭ, expansion) are technical terms of Sufism for the alternating states of the heart: depression and joy, God withholding and God granting. The Qur'an says that God 'withholds and gives abundance' (Qur'an 2:245, yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu). The 'ârif', the gnostic who knows God, gives them no 'existence' (vücûd), that is, he does not let them rule him. The poet applies the mystic's vocabulary to the ups and downs of worldly fortune (dehr).

    Qur'an 2:245; Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994)

  9. Being turns out to be non-being ll. 15–16

    'Vücûd' and 'adem' (Arabic wujūd and ʿadam) are being and non-being, terms of Islamic philosophy and Sufi metaphysics. For the Sufi, only God truly exists and the world is a shadow; for the moralist, the world's goods are illusory. The next bend opens with the same idea: in this world 'its non-being and its being are all one' (yogu vârı cümle yeksândır, l. 19), and the one who knows this secret laughs at the state of the world.

    Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994); Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  10. The first linking couplet (vâsıta) ll. 17–18

    The vâsıta closes the first bend on a new rhyme (huzûr / gurûr). 'Bezm' (Persian bazm) is a drinking party, the classic setting of the gazel; 'fenâ' (Arabic fanāʾ) is mortality, passing away. The world is a banquet where nobody may stay, and 'câm-ı gurûr', the cup of pride (or of delusion: gurûr means both), is the dangerous drink served there. In a terkib-i bend the linking couplets carry the moral; here each states the lesson of its stanza.

    Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Terciibend', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 40 (2011), pp. 487–489; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  11. The rose that is a thorn ll. 23–24

    'Mugaylân' (Arabic umm ghaylān, the acacia of the Arabian desert, with long thorns) is the thorn bush of the wilderness where the lover Mecnun wanders. The rose-garden (gülşen) and rose (gül), the most cherished images of Ottoman lyric, are equated with it: in this world the opened rose (gül-i şüküfte) is no better than a thorn. The figure reverses the usual hope of the garden image (the beloved, spring, union) to say that every beauty is already pain.

    Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985); Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpaklı (eds and trans.), Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997), introduction and glossary

  12. kevn ü fesâd: generation and corruption ll. 31–32

    'Kevn ü fesâd' (Arabic kawn wa-fasād) translates the title of Aristotle's treatise On Generation and Corruption, and in Islamic philosophy names the sublunary world where things come into being and decay. The poet uses the philosopher's term to say that disorder (perîşânlık) is built into the world from the start. Philosophical vocabulary in a moral poem is typical of the learned divan manner Ekrem inherited.

    Haluk İpekten, Eski Türk Edebiyatı: Nazım Şekilleri ve Aruz (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapters on the bahirs; Victoria Rowe Holbrook, The Unreadable Shores of Love: Turkish Modernity and Mystic Romance (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1994)

  13. Second linking couplet: the fool who laughs ll. 35–36

    'Sakîm' (Arabic saqīm) is sick, 'dûn' base or low; 'hande-bahş' (Persian khanda-bakhsh) is 'laughter-giving'. The madman (merd-i mecnûn) is ordinarily the butt of laughter; here the man who trusts the world is so foolish that the madman laughs at him. The image picks up the earlier line in which the one who knows the world's secret is 'handân', laughing (l. 20). The vâsıta again distils the stanza's argument.

    Mustafa İsmet Uzun, 'Terciibend', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 40 (2011), pp. 487–489; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mecnun'

  14. The mirage taken for water ll. 39–40

    The archive's 'sınırsın' is evidently 'sanırsın', you suppose. The mirage (serâb, Persian sarāb) taken for water recalls the Qur'anic simile for the deeds of unbelievers, 'like a mirage in a desert, which the thirsty man thinks is water' (Qur'an 24:39). 'Fütâde-i çâh' (fallen into a well, Persian uftāda) may also evoke Joseph thrown into the well, though the main sense is simply ruin. The desert (bâdiye) that leads its travellers astray (l. 38) is the world itself.

    Qur'an 24:39; Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'serap'

  15. Refuge at God's threshold ll. 43–50

    'Dergâh' (Persian dargāh) is a royal court or threshold, and also a dervish lodge; 'penâh' (Persian panāh) is refuge. The poem now moves from the vanity of the world to trust in God (tevekkül): the cry 'yâ Allâh' opens the lock (kufl) of the treasury of graces; without God's help our only work would be a wail of 'alas' (tazallüm-i vâh). 'Cebîn-i hamdi ... sûde-i dergâh' means 'rub the forehead of praise on the threshold', the gesture of prostration. The final linking couplet tells the reader to contemplate God's art (sun') and not to impose meaning on the world with a short reason (akl-ı kasîr).

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'dergâh'; Abdullah Uçman, 'Recâizâde Mahmud Ekrem', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi (2007)
