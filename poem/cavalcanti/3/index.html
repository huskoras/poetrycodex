Love

Sonnet: A Rapture concerning his Lady

by Guido Cavalcanti c. 1255–1300

Translated by Dante Gabriel Rossetti (1861) · From The Early Italian Poets (1861)

Love

Who is she coming, whom all gaze upon,
Who makes the air all tremulous with light,
And at whose side is Love himself? that none
Dare speak, but each man's sighs are infinite.
Ah me! how she looks round from left to right,
Let Love discourse: I may not speak thereon.
Lady she seems of such high benison
As makes all others graceless in men's sight.
The honour which is hers cannot be said;
To whom are subject all things virtuous,
While all things beauteous own her deity.
Ne'er was the mind of man so nobly led
Nor yet was such redemption granted us
That we should ever know her perfectly.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Who is she coming l. 1

    Cavalcanti's 'Chi è questa che vèn' recalls the Vulgate's 'Quae est ista quae progreditur quasi aurora consurgens' (Song of Songs 6:9; KJV 6:10, 'Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?'), and also 3:6 ('Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness'). The Rossetti Archive and other commentators point to the Song of Songs and to Isaiah 63:1 ('Who is this that cometh from Edom'). The Song's verse was read in the liturgy of the Virgin, so the lady arrives with Marian and scriptural associations.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Song of Solomon 6:10; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes; Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation); Sololibri, commentary on Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn' (links the opening line to Song of Songs 6:9 and Isaiah 63:1)

  2. the sonnet and its translator l. 1

    Guido Cavalcanti (c. 1255-1300), Dante's 'first friend', was the most philosophical poet of the dolce stil novo. This sonnet, perhaps of the late 1270s (the Rossetti Archive's tentative date), is his best-known poem of praise; it is clearly indebted to Guinizzelli's sonnet 'Io voglio del ver la mia donna laudare'. The lady is seen in public, 'ch'ogn'om la mira' (whom every man gazes at), a scene Dante develops in 'Tanto gentile'. Rossetti's title 'A Rapture concerning his Lady' is his own; it appears in The Early Italian Poets (1861). His translation follows the Italian line by line.

    Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation); D. G. Rossetti, The Early Italian Poets (London: Smith, Elder, 1861), Rossetti Archive transcription; Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary

  3. the air tremulous with light l. 2

    'Chiaritate' is the scholastic claritas, radiance, one of the attributes of beauty and of glorified bodies in medieval theology. The lady's light acts on the very medium through which she is seen, in line with medieval optics, in which light passes through the air to the eye. Rossetti's 'tremulous with light' is among his most successful lines and preserves the sense that the atmosphere itself responds to her. Ezra Pound, who translated Cavalcanti repeatedly, prized this luminous, shimmering quality in Cavalcanti.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes; Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation)

  4. Love himself l. 3

    Amor, personified as a lord or companion, is a stock figure of the Italian love lyric and of the Vita Nuova, where Love appears to Dante in dreams and speaks. Here he walks at the lady's side, as if her attendant. Rossetti's rhetorical question ('And at whose side is Love himself?') breaks the Italian sentence differently: in Cavalcanti the whole quatrain is a single question ending at 'sospira'.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes

  5. speechlessness and sighs ll. 3-4

    The beholders are reduced to sighs, the involuntary sign of love's effect on the spirits. Rossetti adds 'infinite', heightening the effect. The motif of speechlessness returns at once in line 6 ('I may not speak thereon') and governs the poem's end, where the mind cannot know her. Dante's 'Tanto gentile' (in the archive as 'My lady looks so gentle and so pure') reworks the same motif of the mute tongue and the sigh.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011

  6. when she turns her eyes l. 5

    Rossetti turns the exclamation 'O Deo' into 'Ah me!' and spells out the turning of the eyes as looking 'from left to right'. The Song of Songs offers a loose parallel in 6:5 (Vulgate 6:4): 'Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me.' The lady's glance, which in medieval love theory sends spirits or rays into the beholder, is the moment of greatest power. The verb 'sembra' (seems, appears) is the same kind of word as Dante's 'pare'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Song of Solomon 6:5; Sololibri, commentary on Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn' (links the opening line to Song of Songs 6:9 and Isaiah 63:1); Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary

  7. Let Love discourse l. 6

    An inexpressibility topos: only Love, the personified power who accompanies her, could describe her effect. 'Discourse' in Rossetti's sense is to speak at length or relate. The poet's incapacity anticipates the sestet, where neither praise nor knowledge suffices. The same deferral of speech to Love appears in the Vita Nuova, where Dante repeatedly says that his tongue cannot express what Beatrice's presence does, and leaves the telling to the sigh or to Love. The topos is old, but in Cavalcanti it carries his recurrent sense that the lover's own faculties are overwhelmed and disabled by love.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes

  8. benison... graceless ll. 7-8

    The Italian says the lady seems so full of 'umiltà' (benign graciousness, not humility in the modern sense) that beside her every other woman 'I call wrath' ('i' la chiam'ira'): others seem harsh or hostile by comparison. Editors have debated the construction and the sense of 'ira'; some modern paraphrases render it as 'wicked' or 'disdainful'. Rossetti replaces umiltà with 'benison' (blessing; OED, benison, n.) and 'ira' with 'graceless', choosing the language of grace and turning the comparison theological.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes; Sololibri, commentary on Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn' (links the opening line to Song of Songs 6:9 and Isaiah 63:1); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'benison, n.'

  9. the sestet and rhyme l. 9

    Cavalcanti's sonnet rhymes ABBAABBA CDEEDC. Rossetti's octave rhymes ABABBAAB (upon, light, none, infinite; right, thereon, benison, sight) and his sestet CDECDE (said, virtuous, deity; led, us, perfectly), so the English moves differently while keeping fourteen lines and the turn at line 9. 'Piagenza' means pleasingness, charm; Rossetti's 'honour' is a free rendering that stresses dignity rather than delight. The sestet then moves from what cannot be said to what cannot be known.

    Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation); Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; D. G. Rossetti, The Early Italian Poets (London: Smith, Elder, 1861), Rossetti Archive transcription

  10. all things virtuous l. 10

    'Vertute' in medieval Italian means power or faculty (Latin virtus, strength) as much as moral virtue: the noble powers of the soul and of nature bow down to her. Rossetti's 'To whom are subject all things virtuous' keeps the hierarchy but leans toward the moral sense. 'Gentil' again denotes stilnovo nobility of heart. The bowing of the powers to the lady is a courtly gesture of homage transferred to abstractions.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes

  11. own her deity l. 11

    Beauty, personified, displays the lady as its own goddess. 'Own' in Rossetti's line means 'acknowledge'. The bold language of divinity applied to a woman is typical of the stilnovo's quasi-religious praise, and the Rossetti Archive's commentary singles out the poem for treating a mortal woman as a near-transcendent source of grace. It is also the kind of language that later made the tradition suspect to moralists.

    Rossetti Archive, commentary on 'Chi è questa, che ven, ch'ogni uom la mira' (Cavalcanti; Rossetti's 1861 translation); Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary

  12. the limits of the mind l. 12

    The sestet's last tercet turns from praise to epistemology. 'Mente' is the intellect. In Cavalcanti's philosophical canzone 'Donna me prega' love is a passion of the sensitive soul that clouds rational judgement, and the beloved's true form can be grasped only as an abstract idea in the possible intellect; the sonnet's ending reflects that Aristotelian, perhaps Averroist, psychology. Rossetti's 'so nobly led' adds the idea of guidance.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes; Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011

  13. redemption (salute) l. 13

    'Salute' means both health and salvation (Latin salus). Rossetti chooses the theological sense and heightens it to 'redemption', linking the poem to the scriptural opening. Dante plays on the same word in the Vita Nuova, where Beatrice's greeting (saluto) brings salute; compare 'Tanto gentile', where her greeting is the centre of the poem. The word thus links praise of the lady with the language of grace and salvation.

    Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes; Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011

  14. knowing her l. 14

    The poem ends not on rapture but on a limit: human minds lack the capacity to know her as she truly is. Harrison and others contrast this note with Dante's 'Tanto gentile', which describes the same public scene but ends with the lady's spirit actually reaching the beholder's soul. Cavalcanti's darker view of love, as an accident that the intellect cannot master, is the background. Rossetti's 'perfectly' renders 'propiamente', in its own proper nature.

    Robert Pogue Harrison, 'Saved by a Vision of Beatrice', New York Review of Books, 26 May 2011; Guido Cavalcanti, Rime, ed. Domenico De Robertis (Turin: Einaudi, 1986), no. IV, with commentary; Gianfranco Contini, ed., Poeti del Duecento, 2 vols (Milan and Naples: Ricciardi, 1960), II, Cavalcanti, 'Chi è questa che vèn', notes
