Love

To Althea from Prison

by Richard Lovelace 1617–1657

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Love

When Love with unconfinéd wings
Hovers within my gates,
And my divine Althea brings
To whisper at the grates;
When I lie tangled in her hair
And fetter'd to her eye,
The birds that wanton in the air
Know no such liberty.

When flowing cups run swiftly round
With no allaying Thames,
Our careless heads with roses crown'd,
Our hearts with loyal flames;
When thirsty grief in wine we steep,
When healths and draughts go free--
Fishes that tipple in the deep
Know no such liberty.

When, linnet-like confinéd, I
With shriller throat shall sing
The sweetness, mercy, majesty,
And glories of my King;
When I shall voice aloud how good
He is, how great should be,
Enlargéd winds, that curl the flood,
Know no such liberty.

Stone walls do not a prison make,
Nor iron bars a cage;
Minds innocent and quiet take
That for an hermitage:
If I have freedom in my love
And in my soul am free,
Angels alone, that soar above,
Enjoy such liberty.

Study notes by the editors

  1. To Althea, from Prison (occasion) title; l. 1

    According to Anthony Wood (quoted by Hazlitt), Lovelace was chosen to deliver the Kentish Petition, which asked Parliament to respect the king's rights, and was committed to the Gatehouse prison at Westminster on 30 April 1642, where he 'made that celebrated song'; he was released on bail some weeks later. The RPO editors give 1642 and the Gatehouse as probable but note that the prison origin has recently been questioned. The poem was published in Lucasta (1649), and it circulated widely in manuscript: CELM records twenty-three manuscript texts. John Wilson set at least its first stanza to music (Cheerful Ayres, 1660), as Hazlitt notes. The archive prints the text of Palgrave's Golden Treasury (1861), which differs from 1649 in several readings.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts (CELM), Folger Shakespeare Library, introduction to Richard Lovelace; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)

  2. birds / Gods; textual variants l. 7; also ll. 11, 17

    Hazlitt reports that the 1649 text reads 'Gods', and that 'birds' derives from a manuscript copy printed by Philip Bliss; Hazlitt preferred 'birds', and the Golden Treasury (the archive's text) follows it. Modern texts based on 1649, such as RPO, keep 'gods', which sets up a sequence of gods, fishes, winds and angels. Two other Golden Treasury readings also differ from 1649: 'roses crown'd' (1649 'bound', l. 11) and 'linnet-like confinéd' (1649 'like committed linnets', l. 17), the latter introduced by Bishop Percy in his Reliques, a change Hazlitt condemns. The poem was much copied and sung, so variants multiplied.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)

  3. Love with unconfinéd wings l. 1

    Love is personified as Cupid, whose wings let him pass any wall; he 'hovers within my gates' while the poet is shut in. The accented 'unconfinéd' (four syllables, as printed in the Golden Treasury) marks the key word of the poem: what is confined and what is not. The paradox develops through the stanza, since the lover is 'tangled' and 'fetter'd' by Althea, a willing captivity that he calls liberty.

    F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive); W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  4. Althea l. 3

    Like 'Lucasta', 'Althea' is a classicising pseudonym. The Greek althaia is the marsh mallow, a medicinal plant, and is related to the verb meaning 'to heal', so the name suggests a healer or comforter, apt for a visitor to a prisoner. In Ovid's Metamorphoses Althaea is the mother of Meleager who burns the brand on which his life depends, a darker association that Lovelace does not seem to invoke. Hazlitt notes that her identity is unknown and that a portrait called 'Althea' at Dulwich gives no clue; he speculated she later became the poet's wife, but Lovelace is generally said to have died unmarried, so the guess needs caution.

    Liddell-Scott-Jones, Greek-English Lexicon, s.v. ἀλθαία (marsh mallow) and ἀλθαίνω (to heal); Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII.445-525 (Althaea and the brand of Meleager); W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  5. grates l. 4

    A grate is a framework of bars across a window or opening, characteristic of a prison. Althea, or Love bringing her, whispers through them. Early modern prisons such as the Gatehouse allowed visitors, and prisoners of rank could receive friends and even entertain, a fact that helps explain the drinking scene of stanza 2. The image returns in stanza 4 as 'iron bars'.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes

  6. tangled in her hair / fetter'd to her eye ll. 5-6

    Being bound by the beloved's hair and eyes is a Petrarchan commonplace. Hazlitt compares George Peele's David and Bethsabe ('brings my longings tangled in her hair') and Middleton's 'prisoner to a woman's eye'. Lovelace's wit lies in setting this metaphorical captivity inside a literal prison: the only bonds he feels are those of love, and they are freedom compared with the birds' (or gods') liberty.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)

  7. refrain: no such liberty ll. 8, 16, 24, 32

    Each eight-line stanza (rhyming ababcdcd, alternating tetrameter and trimeter) builds through 'When' clauses to a comparison with a free creature: birds or gods of the air, fishes of the sea, the winds, and finally angels. The first three refrains deny that these creatures know 'such liberty' as the prisoner's; the last affirms that only angels share it. The ascending order, from air and water to wind and heaven, spiritualises the poem's freedom as it goes. The form suits a song, which is how the poem was set by John Wilson.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  8. allaying Thames l. 10

    To 'allay' wine was to mix it with water, to temper its strength. 'Thames' stands for water by metonymy, as RPO glosses it, the river of London where the prison stood. The phrase recalls Shakespeare's Menenius in Coriolanus, who loves 'hot wine with not a drop of allaying Tiber in't': Lovelace substitutes the English river for the Roman one, perhaps deliberately. The cups 'run swiftly round' as toasts are passed in company.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; Shakespeare, Coriolanus 2.1 (Menenius: hot wine 'with not a drop of allaying Tiber in't'), Folger Shakespeare

  9. heads with roses crown'd l. 11

    Crowning the head with roses at a banquet was a classical custom celebrated by Anacreon and Horace (e.g. Odes II.3, II.11), and Cavalier poets adopted it as an emblem of carefree conviviality. 'Careless' means free from care, not negligent. The 1649 text reads 'bound' instead of the archive's 'crown'd', which makes a closer link to the poem's vocabulary of binding.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)

  10. loyal flames l. 12

    The parallel with 'heads with roses' contrasts bodily pleasure with inner allegiance. In 1642, as conflict between King Charles I and Parliament came to a head, loyalty to the king was the cause for which Lovelace had been imprisoned. The drinking of 'healths' (l. 14) to the king was a royalist ritual. The stanza thus turns Cavalier hedonism into an act of political defiance.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes

  11. healths and draughts l. 14

    Drinking healths was a ceremonial practice of royalist sociability, condemned by Puritans. 'Go free' means pass round without restriction, but in a prison poem it carries an ironic pun: the drinks go free though the drinkers do not. 'Thirsty grief' steeped in wine (l. 13) is a witty inversion, as if sorrow itself were thirsty and drowned. The 'Fishes that tipple in the deep' (l. 15) are drinkers too, though only of water.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  12. linnet-like confinéd l. 17

    Linnets were popular cage-birds prized for their song. The 1649 text reads 'like committed linnets', where 'committed' means imprisoned (RPO glosses it so); Percy changed it to 'linnet-like confinéd', the reading Palgrave and this archive keep. Hazlitt thought the change needless, since 'committed' simply means confined. The idea that captivity sharpens song ('shriller throat') turns imprisonment into an occasion for poetry.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)

  13. my King l. 20

    The king is Charles I, for whom Lovelace had presented the Kentish Petition. The list 'sweetness, mercy, majesty, / And glories' borrows the language of praise used of God, a common feature of royalist panegyric. 'How great should be' (l. 22) is pointed: the king ought to be great, a hint that his power was under challenge. By 1649, when Lucasta was published, Charles had been executed, which gives the printed poem a retrospective poignancy.

    W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes

  14. Enlargéd winds l. 23

    'To enlarge' a prisoner was to release him, and the word was standard in that sense. The free winds that 'curl the flood' (raise ripples or waves on the sea) are the third of the poem's emblems of natural liberty. In classical myth the winds were kept imprisoned by Aeolus and released at will (Virgil, Aeneid I), so 'enlarged' winds are prisoners set free, a witty pairing with the speaker's own state.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  15. Stone walls do not a prison make ll. 25-26

    The final stanza states the poem's moral in Stoic terms: physical confinement cannot imprison a free mind. Epictetus makes the classic statement, telling a tyrant that he may fetter the leg but not the will. The idea was a commonplace of Christian and Stoic consolation literature. Hazlitt suggests Lovelace may have recalled George Wither's prison poems in The Shepherds Hunting (1615). The lines quickly became proverbial and are among the most quoted of the century.

    Epictetus, Discourses I.1 ('You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower'), trans. G. Long; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood)

  16. hermitage l. 28

    A hermitage is the cell of a religious recluse who withdraws from the world voluntarily. The innocent mind reinterprets enforced confinement as chosen retirement, turning punishment into devotion. The idea anticipates the later seventeenth-century cult of retirement and recalls the Stoic and Christian view that solitude can be a freedom. The qualification matters: only minds that are 'innocent and quiet' can make the conversion, so the claim is moral as well as consoling, and implicitly asserts the prisoner's innocence of any real crime against the state.

    Epictetus, Discourses I.1 ('You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower'), trans. G. Long; N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes

  17. Angels alone ... Enjoy such liberty ll. 31-32

    The poem's ascent ends in heaven: the prisoner's freedom equals that of the angels. The refrain shifts from 'Know no such liberty' to 'Enjoy such liberty', from negative comparison to positive kinship. 1649 spells 'sore', and the image of soaring returns to the 'unconfinéd wings' of l. 1, so that the poem is framed by winged beings, Love and the angels.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., 'To Althea, from Prison', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), text of Lucasta (1649) with notes; W. Carew Hazlitt, ed., Lucasta: The Poems of Richard Lovelace (London, 1864), 'To Althea, from Prison' and notes 50.1-50.5; Life (quoting Anthony Wood); F. T. Palgrave, ed., The Golden Treasury (1861), no. 99, 'To Althea from Prison' (the text used in this archive)
