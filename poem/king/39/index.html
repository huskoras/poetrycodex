Death & Dying

The Exequy.

by Henry King 1592–1669

Death & Dying

ACcept thou Shrine of my dead Saint,
Insteed of Dirges this complaint;
And for sweet flowres to crown thy hearse,
Receive a strew of weeping verse
From thy griev'd friend, whom thou might'st see
Quite melted into tears for thee.

Dear loss! since thy untimely fate
My task hath been to meditate
On thee, on thee: thou art the book,
The library whereon I look
Though almost blind. For thee (lov'd clay)
I languish out not live the day,
Using no other exercise
But what I practise with mine eyes:
By which wet glasses I find out
How lazily time creeps about
To one that mourns: this, onely this
My exercise and bus'ness is:
So I compute the weary houres
With sighs dissolved into showres.

Nor wonder if my time go thus
Backward and most preposterous;
Thou hast benighted me, thy set
This Eve of blackness did bege…,
Who was't my day, (though overcast
Before thou had'st thy Noon-tide past)
And I remember must in tears,
Thou scarce had'st seen so many years
As Day tells houres. By thy cleer Sun
My love and fortune first did run;
But thou wilt never more appear
Folded within my Hemisphear,
Since both thy light and motion
Like a fled Star is fall'n and gon,
And twixt me and my soules dear wish
The earth now interposed is,
Which such a strange eclipse doth make
As ne're was read in Almanake.

I could allow thee for a time
To darken me and my fad Clime,
Were it a month, a year, or ten,
I would thy exile live till then;
And all that space my mirth adjourn,
So thou wouldst promise to return;
And putting off thy ashy shrowd
At length disperse this sorrows cloud.

But woe is me! the longest da…e
Too narrow is to calculate
These empty hopes: never shall I
Be so much blest as to descry
A glimpse of thee, till that day come
Which shall the …arth to cinders doome,
And a fierce Feaver must calcine
The body of this world like thine,
(My Little World!) that fit of fire
Once off, our bodies shall aspire
To our soules bliss: then we shall rise,
And view our selves with cleerer eyes
In that calm Region, where no night
Can hide us from each others sight.

Mean time, thou hast her earth: much good
May my harm do thee. Since it stood
With Heavens will I might not call
Her longer mine, I give thee all
My short-liv'd right and interest
In her, whom living I lov'd best▪
With a most free and bounteous grief,
I give thee what I could not keep.
Be kind to her, and prethee look
Thou write into thy Dooms-day book
Each parcell of this Rarity
Which in thy Casket shrin'd doth ly:
See that thou make thy reck'ning streight,
And yield her back again by weight;
For thou must audit on thy trust
Each graine and atome of this du…t,
As thou wilt answer Him that lent,
Not gave thee my dear Monument.

So close the ground, and 'bout her shade
Black curtains draw, my Bride is laid.

Sleep on my Love in thy cold bed
Never to be disquieted!
My last good night! Thou wilt not wake
Till I thy fate shall overtake:
Till age, or grief, or sickness must
Marry my body to that dust
It so much loves; and fill the room
My heart keeps empty in thy Tomb.
Stay for me there; I will not faile
To meet thee in that hallow Vale.
And think not much of my delay;
I am already on the way,
And follow thee with all the speed:
Desire can make, or sorrows breed.
Each minute is a short degree,
And ev'ry houre a step towards thee.
A… night when I betake to rest,
Next morn I rise neerer my We…t
Of life, almost by eight houres saile,
Then when sleep breath'd his drowsie gale.

Thus from the Sun my Bottom stears,
And my dayes Compass downward bears:
Nor labour I to stemme the tide
Through which to Thee I swiftly glide.

'Tis true, with shame and grief I yield,
Thou like the Vann first took'st the field,
And gotten hast the victory
In thus adventuring to dy
Before me, whose more years might crave
A just precedence in the grave.
But heark! My Pulse like a soft Drum
Beats my approch, tells Thee I come;
And slow howere my marches be,
I shall at last sit down by Thee.

The thought of this bids me go on,
And wait my dissolution
With hope and comfort. Dear (forgive
The crime) I am content to live
Divided, with but half a heart,
Till we shall meet and never part.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Exequy: occasion and publication l. 1

    An 'exequy' is a funeral rite (Latin exsequiae, the procession that 'follows out' the dead). Henry King (1592-1669), later Bishop of Chichester, married Anne Berkeley around 1617; she died around 1624, leaving young children, and was buried in St Paul's. The poem circulated widely in manuscript (some copies are headed 'To his matchless never to be forgotten friend') before its printing in 1657. King was a close friend and executor of John Donne, and the poem's learned conceits (astronomy, alchemy, accounting, navigation) are in Donne's manner, though its tetrameter couplets are plainer and steadier. The archive's text is from an early print and has a few damaged letters (marked with ellipses).

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; "Henry King (poet)", Wikipedia (biographical summary from the DNB); Folger Shakespeare Library, Union First Line Index of English Verse, "Accept thou shrine of my dead saint" (manuscript copies; subject Anne King, d. 1624)

  2. Shrine ... Saint l. 1

    A shrine was a reliquary or tomb holding a saint's remains. King addresses the tomb itself, an unusual move that he sustains later (ll. 61-78), where he speaks to the earth that holds his wife. Calling her a 'saint' means she is among the blessed in heaven, the same sense as in Milton's 'late espoused saint'. The idiom was safe for a Protestant cleric because it is metaphorical, but it carries the warmth of Catholic devotion.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "shrine, n."

  3. strew l. 4

    Mourners strewed flowers on the coffin or hearse; King offers 'weeping verse' instead. The noun 'strew' (a number of things scattered about) is rare. 'Dirges' (l. 2) are funeral songs, from the first word of the antiphon Dirige, Domine in the Office of the Dead. 'Complaint' is the technical name for a poem of lament. 'Hearse' in the period could mean the framework over a coffin on which verses and tributes were pinned.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "dirge, n."; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "hearse, n."

  4. The book, the library l. 9

    Meditation on the dead was a recognised devotional exercise, and King makes his wife the text of that study: 'the book, / The library whereon I look'. The image matters for a scholar-cleric whose life was books. The paradox that he reads 'though almost blind' leads to the 'wet glasses' of line 15: his eyes, wet with tears, are like spectacles or hour-glasses through which he measures time.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657)

  5. wet glasses l. 15

    The phrase works on two levels. Eyes as 'glasses' (lenses) continue the reading image; but the following lines ('How lazily time creeps about') suggest the hour-glass, with tears falling like sand. A further association with the water-clock (clepsydra) is possible. 'Compute the weary houres / With sighs dissolved into showres' (ll. 19-20) completes the conceit: sighs are wind, tears are rain, and time is measured in grief. 'Languish out' (l. 12) means to pass the days in languor.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "glass, n.1"

  6. preposterous l. 22

    The Latin praeposterus means 'the hinder part first', and seventeenth-century writers often used the word in that literal sense of inverted order. King's time is preposterous because darkness has come while it should still be day, and because the younger has died before the older. The theme returns at lines 105-110, where she has 'taken the field' before him.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "preposterous, adj."

  7. benighted l. 23

    'Benighted' means overtaken by night. The wife is his day and his sun ('By thy cleer Sun / My love and fortune first did run'). The damaged word at the end of line 24 should read 'beget' (her setting begot an eve of blackness). The parenthesis 'though overcast / Before thou had'st thy Noon-tide past' notes that her life was clouded (by illness) before it reached its midday.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"

  8. As day tells hours l. 29

    'Tells' means counts (as a teller counts). The conceit equates her lifetime with a single day, so that her death at 'noon-tide' and the eve of blackness fit together. Biographical summaries give her age at death as about twenty-four; 'scarce' leaves the exact figure open. The line is a good example of how the poem's cosmic conceits keep returning to plain domestic fact.

    "Henry King (poet)", Wikipedia (biographical summary from the DNB); Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "tell, v."

  9. my Hemisphere l. 32

    In the old astronomy each observer sees one hemisphere of the heavens. She will never again rise into his. 'Light and motion' (l. 33) are the two properties of a star: she has lost both, 'like a fled Star', perhaps a shooting star or one of the new stars observed (and seen to vanish) in 1572 and 1604, which troubled ideas of unchanging heavens. The latter reference is suggestive rather than certain.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "hemisphere, n."

  10. A strange eclipse ... Almanake ll. 35-38

    In a lunar eclipse the earth comes between sun and moon. Here 'the earth' between the speaker and his 'soul's dear wish' is the earth of the grave, so the astronomical figure becomes literal. Almanacs, very popular in the period, forecast eclipses; this one appears in none. The wit is in Donne's manner, but the effect is of controlled grief rather than display.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "almanac, n."

  11. fad (sad) Clime l. 40

    Early printing used the long s (ſ), which differs from f only by a missing crossbar; transcriptions often confuse them. Read 'my sad Clime'. 'Clime' means region or climate, continuing the astronomical-geographical language: she darkens his whole region of the world. Lines 39-48 imagine her absence as a temporary exile from which she might return, 'putting off thy ashy shrowd'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "clime, n."; N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657)

  12. calcine l. 53

    To calcine is to reduce a substance to powder (calx) by intense heat, a standard process in alchemy, where it was thought to purify by burning away grosser parts. At Doomsday the earth will be burnt 'to cinders' (compare 2 Peter 3:10, 'the elements shall melt with fervent heat'). The 'fierce Feaver' of the world's end is paralleled with the fever that killed his wife, so that her death becomes a small version of the apocalypse, and her resurrection a promise of the general one.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:10; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "calcine, v."

  13. My Little World l. 55

    The idea that man is a microcosm (Greek mikros kosmos) corresponding part for part to the macrocosm was a Renaissance commonplace; Donne begins a Holy Sonnet 'I am a little world made cunningly'. King applies it tenderly to his wife: as the world will be calcined and renewed, so her feverish body will rise purified. 'Our bodies shall aspire / To our soules bliss' refers to the doctrine that the resurrected body rejoins the soul in heaven.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; John Donne, Holy Sonnet "I am a little world made cunningly", in Poems, ed. Grierson (1912)

  14. Doomsday book l. 70

    Lines 61-78 turn from astronomy to law and accountancy. The speaker addresses the earth as a trustee: he 'gives' it his short-lived 'right and interest' in her, but it must write each 'parcel' into its Doomsday book, make an exact 'reckoning', 'audit' every grain, and 'yield her back again by weight' at the resurrection. The Domesday Book was the great survey of England, nicknamed for its finality; King fuses it with the Day of Doom, when the dead are restored (Revelation 20:12-13). The legal tone, partly witty, also expresses an anxious love that wants none of her lost.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Bible (KJV), Revelation 20:12-13; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "Domesday, n."

  15. Casket l. 72

    'Casket' means a small box for jewels or valuables (the sense of 'coffin' is later and American). The earth holds 'this Rarity' as treasure in trust, which continues the language of shrine and relic from the opening. 'Streight' (l. 73) means strict, exact. The two images, casket and Doomsday book, combine the language of the jewel-house and of the counting-house: she is both treasure and asset held in trust, to be returned intact.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "casket, n."; Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"

  16. Lent, not gave ll. 77-78

    The distinction 'lent, / Not gave' expresses a common consolation: what God gives in this life is held on loan and may be recalled. 'Monument' (l. 78) here means something that keeps memory alive, a remembrance (RPO's gloss), not merely a tomb. The theological point is that her body belongs to God, and the earth is a steward, as in the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30), who must render an account.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:14-30; Bible (KJV), Job 1:21

  17. Bride ... cold bed ... Marry my body ll. 79-88

    The closing movement turns funeral into marriage. Black curtains are drawn round her as round a bridal bed; 'Sleep on my Love in thy cold bed' and 'My last good night!' use the words of a husband at bedtime; and his own death will 'Marry my body to that dust / It so much loves'. Sleep as a figure for death is biblical (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14, 'them which sleep in Jesus'), and it implies waking at the resurrection.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14; Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"

  18. that hallow (hollow) Vale l. 90

    The RPO editor notes that manuscripts read 'hallow' where the printed text has 'hollow'; the archive's text has 'hallow'. 'Hollow vale' suggests the grave and the 'valley of the shadow of death' (Psalm 23:4); 'hallow vale' suggests a hallowed, consecrated place. Some readers also connect the vale with the valley of Jehoshaphat, where Joel imagines the nations gathered for judgement (Joel 3:2, 12), a link that is possible but not certain. 'Stay for me there' is among the most quoted lines in English elegy, praised for its quiet directness.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Bible (KJV), Psalm 23:4; Bible (KJV), Joel 3:12

  19. The voyage westward ll. 95-104

    'Bottom' means a ship's hull, so a ship (RPO glosses 'boat'). Each night's sleep carries him westward, toward sunset and death, 'almost by eight houres saile'. 'Each minute is a short degree' uses the navigator's degrees of longitude. 'Compass' means both the navigational instrument and the course or circuit of his days, which now 'downward bears'. He does not 'stem the tide' (steer against it) but glides with it toward her.

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "bottom, n."; Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"

  20. the Van ... my Pulse like a soft Drum ll. 105-114

    'Vann' is the van or vanguard, the front of an army, which engages first; she has won 'the victory' by dying first, although his greater age 'might crave / A just precedence'. The marching image continues: his pulse is a 'soft Drum' (muffled, as at military funerals) beating his approach, and however slow his 'marches', he 'shall at last sit down by Thee', 'sit down' being the military term for encamping before a town. The heartbeat as a funeral drum is one of the poem's most admired figures.

    Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "van, n.3"; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "sit, v. (sit down)"

  21. The ending ll. 115-120

    The poem closes in hope rather than despair. 'Dissolution' is death (the soul's separation from the body; compare Philippians 1:23, 'having a desire to depart'). His 'crime' is consenting to live without her; the other half of his heart lies in her tomb (compare 'fill the room / My heart keeps empty in thy Tomb'). The last words, 'never part', answer the Christian hope of reunion and give the elegy its quiet resolution.

    Bible (KJV), Philippians 1:23; Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"

  22. Metre and form whole poem

    The poem is written in octosyllabic (four-stress) rhyming couplets, grouped into paragraphs of varying length. The short line keeps the poem moving and gives it the tone of speech, while the conceits drawn from astronomy, alchemy, law and navigation recall Donne. Many critics have praised the poem for combining metaphysical wit with deep, unforced feeling. Note the old spellings: 'Insteed', 'onely', 'cleer', 'ne're' (ne'er), 'heark' (hark).

    N. J. Endicott, ed., "The Exequy," Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), notes, from King's Poems, Elegies, Paradoxes, and Sonnets (1657); Margaret Crum, ed., The Poems of Henry King (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965), "An Exequy"
