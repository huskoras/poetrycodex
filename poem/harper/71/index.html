The Slave Mother

by Frances Ellen Watkins Harper 1825–1911

From Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects (1857)

Social Commentaries

Heard you that shriek? It rose
So wildly on the air,
It seemed as if a burden'd heart
Was breaking in despair.

Saw you those hands so sadly clasped—
The bowed and feeble head—
The shuddering of that fragile form—
That look of grief and dread?

Saw you the sad, imploring eye?
Its every glance was pain,
As if a storm of agony
Were sweeping through the brain.

She is a mother, pale with fear,
Her boy clings to her side,
And in her kirtle vainly tries
His trembling form to hide.

He is not hers, although she bore
For him a mother's pains;
He is not hers, although her blood
Is coursing through his veins!

He is not hers, for cruel hands
May rudely tear apart
The only wreath of household love
That binds her breaking heart.

His love has been a joyous light
That o'er her pathway smiled,
A fountain gushing ever new,
Amid life's desert wild.

His lightest word has been a tone
Of music round her heart,
Their lives a streamlet blent in one—
Oh, Father! must they part?

They tear him from her circling arms,
Her last and fond embrace:
Oh! never more may her sad eyes
Gaze on his mournful face.

No marvel, then, these bitter shrieks
Disturb the listening air:
She is a mother, and her heart
Is breaking in despair.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and setting l. 1

    Frances Ellen Watkins (later Harper, 1825-1911), born free in Baltimore, published Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects in Boston in 1854 with a preface by William Lloyd Garrison; it sold widely and was enlarged in 1857. She had begun lecturing for the Maine Anti-Slavery Society in 1854, and she read from the book at her lectures. The poem belongs to the abolitionist literature of sentiment that followed Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), which aimed to move readers by showing slavery's destruction of the family. Its scene is an auction or sale at which a mother loses her son.

    Wikipedia, 'Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects' (publication history, with references); Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Slave Mother', text from Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects (Boston, 1854)

  2. Questions to the reader ll. 1-12

    Rather than narrating, Harper begins with the inverted questions of the oral ballad and the platform speech. Each stanza moves to a closer view: the shriek, the clasped hands and bowed head, then the 'imploring eye'. Critics such as Carla Peterson and Frances Smith Foster stress the connection with Harper's lecturing: the poem was meant to be heard, and it positions listeners as spectators who must respond. The stanza with 'She is a mother' (l. 13) then answers the questions.

    Carla L. Peterson, 'Doers of the Word': African-American Women Speakers and Writers in the North (1830-1880) (Oxford University Press, 1995); Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994)

  3. Ballad metre st. 1

    The poem uses a variant of the hymn and ballad stanza, familiar to Harper's audiences from church singing: four lines rhymed on the second and fourth ('air/despair'). The opening stanza runs 6.6.8.6 syllables ('Heard you that shriek? It rose / So wildly on the air, / It seemed as if a burden'd heart / Was breaking in despair'), a little looser than strict common metre, and the pattern repeats through the poem. The simplicity was deliberate: Harper wrote for a broad public, including people who heard rather than read poetry.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Paul Fussell, Poetic Meter and Poetic Form, rev. ed. (McGraw-Hill, 1979), on common metre

  4. A storm of agony ll. 11-12

    Harper's imagery here is that of sentimental and gothic fiction: the shriek, the 'shuddering' frame, the storm 'sweeping through the brain'. Some later critics found her verse over-emphatic; scholars from the 1980s on, notably Foster and Boyd, have argued that its directness was a political choice suited to an antislavery public that had to be moved to act. The mother is described entirely from outside, by what a spectator can see and hear.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Carla L. Peterson, 'Doers of the Word': African-American Women Speakers and Writers in the North (1830-1880) (Oxford University Press, 1995)

  5. kirtle l. 15

    Old English cyrtel, a tunic or short garment, later a woman's gown or outer petticoat. By the 1850s the word was archaic and literary, associated with old ballads and Scott's romances. Its use lends the plain scene a timeless, folk-ballad tone. The child hiding in his mother's skirts is an image of utter helplessness: the only protection he has is cloth.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kirtle, n.'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Slave Mother', text from Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects (Boston, 1854); African American Poetry: A Digital Anthology (Lehigh University), 'The Slave Mother' (1854)

  6. Anaphora: 'He is not hers' ll. 17-22

    The repetition (anaphora) works like a speaker's refrain. Each clause sets a natural fact (she bore him; her blood is in his veins) against the legal fact that he is not hers. The second stanza (ll. 21-22) explains why: 'cruel hands / May rudely tear apart' the family. The repetition gives an argumentative spine to a poem that otherwise proceeds by images.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Carla L. Peterson, 'Doers of the Word': African-American Women Speakers and Writers in the North (1830-1880) (Oxford University Press, 1995)

  7. The law of slave birth ll. 19-20

    From a Virginia statute of 1662 onward, colonial and state laws applied the principle partus sequitur ventrem ('the offspring follows the womb'): children of enslaved women were enslaved and belonged to the mother's owner. The mother's blood in his veins thus makes the boy a slave rather than securing him to her, a bitter irony the stanza exposes. Enslaved families had no legal standing and could be separated by sale, the event the poem dramatises.

    Act XII, Virginia General Assembly, December 1662 ('Negro womens children to serve according to the condition of the mother'), Encyclopedia Virginia; Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); The Norton Anthology of African American Literature, ed. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Nellie Y. McKay, 2nd ed. (Norton, 2004), headnotes and footnotes

  8. Wreath of household love l. 23

    A wreath is a circle of woven flowers, so the image fits a bond that encircles and 'binds her breaking heart'. Harper uses a similar phrase in the companion poem 'The Slave Mother: A Tale of the Ohio', where the mother fears 'some cruel hand / Should tear my household wreaths apart'. The image anticipates the 'circling arms' of l. 33, which are torn open in the climax.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994)

  9. Fountain in the desert ll. 27-28

    Isaiah promises that 'in the wilderness shall waters break out, and streams in the desert' (35:6) and that God will make 'the wilderness a pool of water' (41:18). Harper's audiences would have heard the echo: the boy's love is a providential blessing in the 'desert' of slavery. The following stanza continues the water imagery with 'a streamlet blent in one', so that the separation becomes the dividing of a single stream.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 35:6; 41:18; Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990)

  10. blent l. 31

    'Blent' is a poetical form of 'blended', common in nineteenth-century verse. The image of two lives as one stream makes the coming separation unnatural: a stream cannot be torn apart. The diction, like 'kirtle' and 'No marvel', keeps the poem in the register of ballad and hymn. The word also echoes 'blood' and 'veins' earlier: mother and son are one life in body as well as in feeling.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blend, v.'; Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990)

  11. 'Oh, Father!' l. 32

    The apostrophe turns the poem from description to prayer and protest. 'Father' here is God, to whom the speaker (or the mother) appeals against the cruelty of men. Readers have noted that no human father appears in the poem: enslaved fathers had no legal rights over their children, and in many cases the father was the owner himself. The question is left unanswered by heaven; the next stanza simply reports the separation.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Carla L. Peterson, 'Doers of the Word': African-American Women Speakers and Writers in the North (1830-1880) (Oxford University Press, 1995)

  12. The separation ll. 33-36

    The climax comes in a single stanza with no description of buyers, only 'They' and their violence. 'Circling arms' recalls the 'wreath' of l. 23, now broken. The wish 'never more may her sad eyes / Gaze on his mournful face' states what such separations usually meant in practice: family members sold apart were rarely reunited, though formerly enslaved people searched for each other for decades after emancipation.

    Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994); Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); The Norton Anthology of African American Literature, ed. Henry Louis Gates Jr. and Nellie Y. McKay, 2nd ed. (Norton, 2004), headnotes and footnotes

  13. Circular frame ll. 37-40

    The poem ends almost where it began, repeating the rhyme 'air/despair' and the phrase about the breaking heart. What has changed is the reader's knowledge: the shriek of the first line is now understood. 'No marvel' means 'it is no wonder'. The repetition of 'She is a mother' from l. 13 makes motherhood the poem's appeal across racial lines, a strategy aimed especially at white women readers in the antislavery movement.

    Frances Smith Foster, ed., A Brighter Coming Day: A Frances Ellen Watkins Harper Reader (Feminist Press, 1990); Carla L. Peterson, 'Doers of the Word': African-American Women Speakers and Writers in the North (1830-1880) (Oxford University Press, 1995); Melba Joyce Boyd, Discarded Legacy: Politics and Poetics in the Life of Frances E. W. Harper, 1825-1911 (Wayne State University Press, 1994)
