Sürücü

by Mehmet Emin Yurdakul 1869–1944

From Türk Sazı (1914). Original Turkish text.

Social Commentaries

Gidiyorduk: Kar üstünde bir incecik çığırdan;
Gidiyorduk: İri buzlar sarkıtmıştı her saçak;
Gidiyorduk: Ben hayvanın üzerinde, o yayan;
Gidiyorduk: Ben giyimli, o zavallı yalnayak.

Sefilciğin yırtık pırtık bir uruba sırtında;
Görünüyor gibi idi vücudunun her yeri.
O çatlamış, mosmor olmuş dudakları altında
Birbirine vuruyordu beyaz, güzel dişleri.

— Oğlum, her bir seferinde sen kaç para alırsın?
— Bir metelik.
— Evin nerde? Kimin kimsen var mıdır?...
— Ne evim var, ne kimsem var...
— Gece nerde kalırsın?
— Ahırdaki gübrelikte... Sıcak olur, ısıtır.

Ey kimsesiz, sefil çocuk! ben seksen yıl yaşasam
Bu acıklı sözlerini hiç bir vakit unutmam...
Kim bilir ki bu akşam da böyle bir kış gününde,
Sana karşı duvar olan hangi ahır önünde
Aç ve çıplak bir dilenci gibi titrer durursun?
Ellerini: «Açın!» diye hangi taşa vurursun?...

Ah, bu acı hallerini düşündükçe senin ben,
Soğuk bir şey duyar gibi oluyorum içimden.
Ateşleri beyaz küllü mangalımın başında
Vücuduma kar yağıyor gibi bir şey olmada;
İşte ben de senin gibi titremeye başladım.
Donuyorum, çekiliyor her yerimden hayatım!...

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The title means 'The Drover'. On a snowy road the poet rides while the barefoot boy who leads his animal walks beside him; the boy has no home and sleeps in a stable's dung heap, and the poet says he will never forget his words.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sürücü: 'The Drover'; Türk Sazı (1914) title; l. 1

    A 'sürücü' is a drover, someone who drives or leads pack animals for hire; here a homeless boy hired to lead the poet's mount. Uçman's encyclopedia article calls Yurdakul (1869–1944) the first great national poet of the Turks: his 'Cenge Giderken' (1897), written during the Greco-Ottoman war in syllabic metre and plain Turkish, is regarded as a starting point for nationalist poetry in plain Turkish and hece, and his aim was to voice the sufferings of ordinary people. 'Sürücü' belongs to that populist (halkçı) side of his work. Uçman also notes the common judgement that his poems are technically weak but were widely popular and historically decisive.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Anadolu'dan Bir Ses yahut Cenge Giderken / Mehmet Emin Yurdakul' (from p. 176 in the current Dergâh printing)

  2. Metre: syllabic (hece), 15 syllables ll. 1–4

    Count: Gi-di-yor-duk / İ-ri buz-lar / sar-kıt-mış-tı / her sa-çak = 4 + 4 + 4 + 3 = 15. Every verse of the poem keeps this count (in the dialogue one verse is spread over two printed lines), with the pauses (durak) after the fourth, eighth and twelfth syllables. Hece metre, based on the equal number of syllables per line, was the metre of Turkish folk poetry; Yurdakul's choice of it, together with everyday Turkish, was a manifesto against the Persian-Arabic aruz of the divan and of the Servet-i Fünûn. Compare the same scene of snow in Cenab Şahabettin's aruz poem 'Elhân-ı Şitâ' (1897): the two poems show the two roads of Turkish verse around 1900.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Anadolu'dan Bir Ses yahut Cenge Giderken / Mehmet Emin Yurdakul' (from p. 176 in the current Dergâh printing)

  3. Anaphora and contrast: 'Gidiyorduk' ll. 1–4

    The repeated verb with a colon is like a refrain in a folk song or a ballad and gives the stanza its slow walking pace. The first two lines set the scene (a narrow path on the snow, great icicles hanging from every eave, 'saçak'), the last two divide the 'we' into two: 'I on the animal, he on foot; I clothed, he poor and barefoot (yalnayak).' The contrast of the comfortable speaker and the suffering child is the structural principle of the whole poem, and it ends by being overcome when the speaker himself begins to shiver.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  4. yalnayak, uruba: barefoot, in rags ll. 4–5

    Yurdakul draws on spoken, regional Turkish for his effects. 'Yalnayak' (yalın 'bare' + ayak 'foot') is shortened as in speech. 'Uruba', with a helping vowel, is the popular pronunciation of 'urba', clothing, which the Turkish Language Association derives from Italian roba. 'Yırtık pırtık' (ragged), a reduplicated pair, is another colloquial touch. The choice of such words in a printed poem in 1914 was itself a statement, after decades in which poetic Turkish had been mainly Persian and Arabic.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'urba'; Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  5. sefilcik: 'the poor little wretch' l. 5

    'Sefil' (Arabic safīl, low, wretched) is one of the few Arabic words in the poem, and Yurdakul turns it Turkish by adding the affectionate diminutive suffix -cik, a device of colloquial and folk speech. The child's body shows through the torn garment, his cracked purple lips, and beneath them, a striking detail, his 'white, beautiful teeth' chattering. The beauty of the teeth among the misery is the one touch that makes the child individual rather than a type.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'sefil'; Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  6. The dialogue and its broken lines ll. 9–14

    The poet asks: 'My son, how much do you get each trip?' 'One metelik.' 'Where is your home? Have you anyone?' 'I have no home, no one.' 'Where do you sleep at night?' 'In the dung heap in the stable; it is warm, it warms me.' The answers break the fifteen-syllable lines into pieces, each continued further along the page, but the syllable count across each full line is kept. Direct, plain dialogue of this kind was new in Turkish verse and shows the influence of narrative realism; the boy's matter-of-fact last words are the most painful lines of the poem.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614; Mehmet Kaplan, Şiir Tahlilleri 1: Tanzimat'tan Cumhuriyet'e Kadar (Istanbul: Dergâh), chapter 'Anadolu'dan Bir Ses yahut Cenge Giderken / Mehmet Emin Yurdakul' (from p. 176 in the current Dergâh printing)

  7. metelik: a quarter-kuruş coin l. 10

    The Turkish Language Association defines metelik as an iron coin worth a quarter kuruş, ten paras, and, figuratively, very little money; it derives the word from French métallique. The expression 'metelik vermemek', not to give a farthing, preserves the sense. Yurdakul's companion poem in Türk Sazı, 'On Para Ver' (Give Ten Paras), turns on the same sum, the beggar's plea for a single metelik.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'metelik'

  8. The dung heap that warms l. 14

    Rotting manure gives off heat, which is why the boy can sleep in it. The child says it without self-pity: 'it is warm, it warms me'. The realism of the detail, very far from the roses and nightingales of divan poetry, is the point. The poem's later imagined scene, the child trembling 'before which stable' with the wall as his only protection (ll. 17–20), grows out of this answer.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  9. From quatrains to couplets ll. 15–26

    The first three stanzas rhyme abab (saçak / yalnayak; sırtında / altında, yeri / dişleri; alırsın / kalırsın). The last two sections are in rhymed couplets (gününde / önünde; durursun / vurursun; ben / içimden), the commoner pattern of narrative folk verse (mesnevi-like). The opening exclamation 'Ey kimsesiz, sefil çocuk! ben seksen yıl yaşasam / Bu acıklı sözlerini hiç bir vakit unutmam' (even if I lived eighty years I would never forget your words) turns the anecdote into a vow of memory.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  10. 'On what stone do you beat, crying "Open!"?' ll. 17–20

    'Sana karşı duvar olan hangi ahır önünde' means 'in front of what stable that is a wall against you', with 'duvar' carrying both its literal and figurative senses: a wall that shelters and a wall that excludes. The child trembles 'like a hungry, naked beggar' and strikes his hands on stone crying 'Açın!' (Open!). The stone that does not open is the old image of the hard heart, here the society that leaves children outside. The rhetorical questions ('Kim bilir ki...', who knows...) move from report to imagination.

    Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614

  11. Sympathy as physical feeling ll. 21–26

    The 'mangal' (Arabic manqal, a brazier of burning charcoal) with its white-ashed coals is the bourgeois comfort that the poem sets against the stable. Yet thinking of the child makes the speaker feel 'something cold' inside, as if snow were falling on him, until he trembles like the boy ('İşte ben de senin gibi titremeye başladım'). The ending turns the opening contrast of rider and walker into identification. Critics may find it sentimental, but it states plainly the populist programme Uçman describes: to make the reader feel the people's pain.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük, s.v. 'mangal'; Abdullah Uçman, 'Yurdakul, Mehmet Emin', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, vol. 43 (2013), pp. 613–614
