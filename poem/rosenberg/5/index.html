Break of Day in the Trenches

by Isaac Rosenberg 1890–1918

From Poetry, Chicago (December 1916)

History & PoliticsNature

The darkness crumbles away—
It is the same old druid Time as ever.
Only a live thing leaps my hand—
A queer sardonic rat—
As I pull the parapet’s poppy
To stick behind my ear.
Droll rat, they would shoot you if they knew
Your cosmopolitan sympathies
(And God knows what antipathies).
Now you have touched this English hand
You will do the same to a German—
Soon, no doubt, if it be your pleasure
To cross the sleeping green between.
It seems you inwardly grin as you pass
Strong eyes, fine limbs, haughty athletes
Less chanced than you for life,
Bonds to the whims of murder,
Sprawled in the bowels of the earth,
The torn fields of France.
What do you see in our eyes
At the shrieking iron and flame
Hurled through still heavens?
What quaver—what heart aghast?
Poppies whose roots are in man’s veins
Drop, and are ever dropping;
But mine in my ear is safe,
Just a little white with the dust.

Codex Note

A rat leaps over the poet's hand as he picks a poppy at dawn. He thinks of the rat's 'cosmopolitan sympathies' passing between English and German lines and of the 'poppies whose roots are in man's veins'. Widely taken as Rosenberg's best poem.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and publication title, l. 1

    Rosenberg, a Jewish painter-poet from London's East End, enlisted in 1915 and served as a private. The Oxford archive dates the first typescript to June 1916. He sent the poem to Edward Marsh in August 1916, describing it as simple as ordinary talk, and to Gordon Bottomley (whose transcript preserves a variant); Harriet Monroe received it in October 1916 and printed it in Poetry in December 1916. The title names the dawn 'stand-to', when soldiers manned the parapet against attack. Fussell ranked it the greatest poem of the war, and its free verse and casual tone set it apart from much patriotic verse of 1914-16.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; First World War Poetry Digital Archive, typescripts of 'Break of Day in the Trenches'; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; Vivien Noakes, ed., The Poems and Plays of Isaac Rosenberg (Oxford UP, 2004)

  2. The crumbling darkness l. 1

    Rosenberg's verb gives darkness texture, as if it were the soil of the trench falling away. Dawn was the most dangerous hour on the Western Front, the time of stand-to and of attacks. Fussell placed the poem within the tradition of the dawn song, the aubade, which the war made ironic: daybreak brings not the parting of lovers but the threat of death.

    Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations

  3. 'druid Time' l. 2

    Classical writers describe the druids of Gaul and Britain as priests who performed human sacrifice (Caesar, Gallic War 6.16), so 'druid Time' suggests an ancient power that demands victims, now as always. The phrase also recalls Stonehenge, which popular belief linked with druids, and so the sense of dawn over old stones. In his letter to Marsh, Rosenberg said the second line might seem vague and that he meant it to give the sense of dawn. Critics differ over how heavily to load the image with sacrifice.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Julius Caesar, De Bello Gallico 6.16; Annotated text of 'Break of Day in the Trenches' (George Mason University course page)

  4. 'leaps my hand' l. 3

    'Leap' with a direct object (leap a fence, leap a ditch) is long-established English usage recorded in the OED. 'Only a live thing' stresses that the one obviously living creature in the trench at dawn is the rat; the soldiers appear later as 'strong eyes, fine limbs' whose hold on life is less secure than the animal's. The leap is the first action of the poem, abrupt and unexpected after the slow dawn of the opening lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'leap'; First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations

  5. 'sardonic' l. 4

    Greek sardanios, later sardonios, describes a bitter or scornful smile; Odysseus smiles 'sardonically' at Odyssey 20.302, and ancient writers derived the word from a Sardinian plant whose poison drew the face into a grin. Rats swarmed in the trenches, feeding on the dead, and were loathed by soldiers. Fussell observes that Rosenberg's rat, the natural emblem of the demonic, turns out charming and worldly, and seems to wonder at the terror of men. The tone of 'queer' and 'droll' is affectionate.

    Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sardonic'; Homer, Odyssey 20.302

  6. The parapet's poppy l. 5

    The parapet is the bank of earth on the enemy side of a trench. Fussell reads the poppy as a flower fed by the dead, and the picked flower as already doomed. By 1916 the poppy was linked with the war dead through John McCrae's 'In Flanders Fields' (Punch, December 1915), but Rosenberg's poppy is first a casual decoration, before the poem draws its blood meaning out.

    Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; John McCrae, 'In Flanders Fields', Punch, 8 December 1915

  7. 'cosmopolitan sympathies' l. 8

    Greek kosmopolites, 'citizen of the world', was the Cynic Diogenes' answer when asked where he came from (Diogenes Laertius 6.63). The rat crosses between English and German lines without national feeling, which is why 'they would shoot you if they knew'. Some readers point out that 'cosmopolitan' was used to accuse Jews of rootless, divided loyalty, and that Rosenberg, a Jewish private who met prejudice in the army, may be wryly identifying with the rat. Critics differ on how far to press this; the main sense is the absurdity of national hatred.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Annotated text of 'Break of Day in the Trenches' (George Mason University course page); Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 6.63; Vivien Noakes, ed., The Poems and Plays of Isaac Rosenberg (Oxford UP, 2004)

  8. The parenthesis l. 9

    The parenthesis matches 'sympathies' with its opposite and keeps the tone of half-joking talk. The Oxford hypermedia edition records that the line stands in the version Rosenberg wrote before June 1916 but was dropped from the printed text, so students should check which version an edition follows. The archive text includes the line. The aside also widens the joke: if the rat's sympathies are cosmopolitan, its dislikes are probably just as indiscriminate, and so, implicitly, are the hatreds of the human armies on either side.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Vivien Noakes, ed., The Poems and Plays of Isaac Rosenberg (Oxford UP, 2004)

  9. English hand, German hand l. 11

    The rat makes a bodily connection between the speaker and an unknown German soldier, and the poem's sympathy flows through the animal to the enemy. The Oxford annotation compares Donne's 'The Flea', in which an insect joins two bodies, and notes the Metaphysical poets' influence on Rosenberg. The idea of shared humanity across the lines returns in Owen's 'Strange Meeting'.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; John Donne, 'The Flea', in Poems, ed. H. J. C. Grierson (1912)

  10. 'the sleeping green between' l. 13

    No man's land was often mud and wire, but in a quiet sector at dawn it could look green and still. 'Green' suggests the English village green and pastoral; the Oxford edition compares Blake's 'The Ecchoing Green'. Fussell made the poem a central instance of soldiers' pastoral, in which images of the English countryside serve as an ironic counterpoint to the front.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'

  11. 'inwardly grin' l. 14

    The Oxford edition notes that 'inwardly' is absent from the earliest version, which shows Rosenberg refining the tone of the rat's mockery. The grin connects back to 'sardonic', the grimace of bitter laughter. The rat's mocking survival sets up the reversal of the next lines, in which the splendid human bodies are 'less chanced than you for life', that is, with less chance of surviving than the rat.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations

  12. 'haughty athletes' l. 15

    The list praises the soldiers as physical specimens, in the sporting language that recruitment and early war poetry applied to young men (Rupert Brooke's 1914 sonnets idealise the young dead). Rosenberg, a working-class Jew from the East End and a reluctant soldier, stands at an angle to that ideal: 'haughty' is admiring and ironic at once. Silkin reads Rosenberg's war poems as refusing both patriotic idealism and simple protest.

    Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972); Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'

  13. 'Bonds' l. 17

    'Bond' in the old sense of a serf or slave (bondman) is recorded by the OED, and the word also suggests a legal pledge. Either way the soldiers' lives belong to chance ('whims') and to 'murder', a strong word for battle. The next lines show the result: bodies 'sprawled in the bowels of the earth'. The abstract, Latinate phrasing contrasts with the colloquial address to the rat.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bond'; Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972)

  14. 'the bowels of the earth' l. 18

    The phrase is proverbial (Shakespeare's Hotspur reports a fop's complaint about saltpetre dug 'out of the bowels of the harmless earth', 1 Henry IV 1.3), but here it is literal: men lie in dug earth, dead or alive. 'The torn fields of France' makes the landscape a wounded body, preparing for the poppies 'whose roots are in man's veins'. The grotesque bodily metaphor is typical of Rosenberg.

    Shakespeare, 1 Henry IV 1.3 (Folger Shakespeare); Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972)

  15. 'shrieking iron and flame' l. 21

    The Oxford edition reports that an earlier draft had a different passage at this point, including a line about 'a shell's haphazard fury'; the revision replaces it with the shrieking iron and flame. The speaker asks what the rat sees in the soldiers' eyes: what 'quaver', what heart 'aghast'. The 'still heavens' set the calm dawn sky against the violence that passes through it. The question is unanswered; the poem turns at once to the poppies.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972)

  16. Poppies rooted in veins l. 24

    Poppies flourished in the churned ground of Flanders and France, and the popular notion that they drew their colour from the dead was widespread. Silkin argues that Rosenberg takes up this common belief and renews it. 'Drop, and are ever dropping' fuses flowers and men: poppies shed their petals, soldiers fall continuously. The poppy is also the classical flower of sleep and oblivion.

    Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972); Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; Annotated text of 'Break of Day in the Trenches' (George Mason University course page)

  17. 'a little white with the dust' l. 27

    Fussell calls 'safe' the poem's most ironic word, since the flower's apparent safety is illusory. The white dust of the hot summer of 1916 drains the red flower, a small foretaste of death. The Oxford edition reports Silkin's hearing of 'a little while' behind 'a little white', a little while, so that the poppy and the man survive only briefly. The understatement of the last line shows Rosenberg's control of tone.

    Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975), chapter 'Arcadian Recourses'; First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), 'Break of Day in the Trenches': hypermedia edition, versions and annotations; Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (Oxford UP, 1972)
