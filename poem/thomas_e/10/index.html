Nature

Adlestrop

by Edward Thomas 1878–1917

From Poems (1917, as 'Edward Eastaway')

NatureTime & Brevity

Yes. I remember Adlestrop--
The name, because one afternoon
Of heat the express-train drew up there
Unwontedly. It was late June.

The steam hissed. Someone cleared his throat.
No one left and no one came
On the bare platform. What I saw
Was Adlestrop--only the name

And willows, willow-herb, and grass,
And meadowsweet, and haycocks dry,
No whit less still and lonely fair
Than the high cloudlets in the sky.

And for that minute a blackbird sang
Close by, and round him, mistier,
Farther and farther, all the birds
Of Oxfordshire and Gloucestershire.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

An express train stops unexpectedly at a country station on an afternoon in late June. The speaker sees only willows, meadowsweet and haycocks, and hears a blackbird and 'all the birds of Oxfordshire and Gloucestershire'. Perhaps Thomas's most famous poem.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening and occasion l. 1

    On 24 June 1914 Thomas took the express from Paddington towards Worcestershire (Malvern); the train halted briefly at Adlestrop, Gloucestershire. Thomas noted the stop in his field notebook (now in the Berg Collection, New York). He began the poem in January 1915, after Frost had encouraged him to write verse, and drafts survive. The bare 'Yes.' makes the reader the unheard questioner and establishes the conversational, speech-based rhythm that Frost admired. It was published in the New Statesman shortly after Thomas was killed at Arras on 9 April 1917, and in Poems (1917) under the pseudonym 'Edward Eastaway'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011); William Cooke, Edward Thomas: A Critical Biography 1878-1917 (London: Faber, 1970), on the drafts of 'Adlestrop', as cited by RPO

  2. Adlestrop l. 1

    Adlestrop lies in Gloucestershire close to the Oxfordshire border, which explains the birds of 'Oxfordshire and Gloucestershire' in the last stanza. Its station was on the Great Western Railway's line from Oxford to Worcester and has long since closed. The poem gives the passenger only the platform and the station nameboard, not the village. The place-name has a quaint, old sound that helps explain why the name, rather than the place, is what the speaker remembers, and Thomas, who wrote widely on English place-names, often made names carry the weight of memory.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011)

  3. It was late June l. 4

    The stop happened on 24 June 1914, four days before the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo and six weeks before Britain entered the war on 4 August. Thomas wrote the poem in early 1915, when the war was already under way and he was deciding whether to enlist, which he did in July 1915. The poem never mentions war, but its stillness has been read as an image of the last peaceful English summer; Newey describes its mood as a 'time out of time'. Some readers resist this hindsight reading as a myth of 1914 imposed by later events.

    Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011); Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75

  4. Unwontedly l. 4

    'Wont' means custom or habit; 'unwontedly' means in an unaccustomed way (OED). The stop is felt as a break in routine, an unplanned pause in which perception sharpens. Newey notes that the GWR timetable for that day shows a scheduled stop at 12.48, which complicates the poem's claim, but the point is how it felt to the passenger. The long, formal adverb placed at the start of the line, before the abrupt 'It was late June', also slows the verse to a halt like the train.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014

  5. The steam hissed. Someone cleared his throat. l. 5

    Thomas's notebook records that at 12.45 the train stopped at Adlestrop, that blackbirds were singing in the willows, and that no one was to be seen, only the hiss of the engine letting off steam. The poem keeps the hiss and adds the human detail of a passenger clearing his throat. The two clipped sentences, each ending at a full stop, mimic the stillness of the halted train. 'No one left and no one came' stresses the emptiness of the 'bare platform', a moment outside ordinary social life.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014

  6. Adlestrop--only the name l. 8

    The speaker remembers 'The name' (l. 2) and saw 'Adlestrop--only the name', that is, the station nameboard, since the village itself was out of sight. The repetition makes the place-name a talisman of memory, a recurring interest of Thomas, who wrote much prose on English place-names and on how words carry the past. The dash and the run-on across the stanza break ('only the name / And willows') then open the name out into the surrounding natural world.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014

  7. willows, willow-herb . . . meadowsweet ll. 9-10

    Willows grow along streams; willow-herb (rosebay willowherb, Epilobium angustifolium) is a tall pink-flowered plant that colonises railway embankments; meadowsweet (Filipendula ulmaria) has frothy cream flowers and a sweet scent in damp meadows in June and July. Thomas, a skilled naturalist, names them precisely. His notebook records the willowherb and meadowsweet at a different, signal stop outside Campden, so the poem fuses two moments into one. The lilting list, joined by 'and', mirrors the leisurely gaze of the passenger.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011)

  8. haycocks l. 10

    A haycock is a small conical heap of hay left in the field to dry before being carted and stacked (OED). Their presence dates the scene to midsummer haymaking and adds a trace of human labour to an otherwise empty landscape. The inverted adjective ('haycocks dry') is a slightly old-fashioned poetic order that Thomas allows himself in this stanza, together with 'No whit' and 'lonely fair'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75

  9. No whit less still and lonely fair ll. 11-12

    'Whit' (from Old English wiht, a thing or creature) means the least amount, so 'no whit less' means 'not in the least less'. The comparison with the 'high cloudlets in the sky' lifts the eye from the trackside to the heavens and suggests a timeless calm. 'Lonely fair' is an unusual compound: beautiful in its solitude. 'Cloudlets' is a diminutive that adds delicacy.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014

  10. a blackbird sang / Close by ll. 13-14

    The notebook records blackbirds singing and a single thrush; the poem concentrates on one blackbird 'close by'. 'For that minute' marks a moment suspended in time. Newey notes how the final stanza's rhythm shifts towards a falling, dactylic movement, which matches the spreading sound. Critics often compare this to a Romantic 'spot of time' or epiphany, though Thomas's tone is understated rather than visionary.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014

  11. all the birds / Of Oxfordshire and Gloucestershire ll. 15-16

    The movement outward ('round him, mistier, / Farther and farther') turns sound into distance, and the counties become a kind of imagined map. The last line consists almost entirely of two long, musical place-names, an ending that rests on names as the poem began. Read with hindsight, the expanding chorus has been seen as an image of an English countryside about to be lost, or at least changed, by the war; others read it simply as a moment of perfect attention. 'Mistier' suggests both distance and the softening of memory.

    Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011)

  12. Form and metre st. 1-4

    The poem is written in four quatrains rhyming abcb, only lines 2 and 4 rhyming (afternoon/June, came/name, dry/sky, mistier/Gloucestershire), in a loose four-beat line. The rhymes are unobtrusive; lines run on within and across stanzas (ll. 3-4, 7-8, 8-9); and full stops fall mid-line ('Unwontedly. It was late June'). These effects create the sound of a speaking voice recalling a memory, the 'sound of sense' that Robert Frost was urging Thomas to capture. 'Gloucestershire' rhymes with 'mistier' only if both are lightly stressed, a deliberately soft final rhyme.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), 'Adlestrop', ed. Ian Lancashire, with notebook extracts from Anne Harvey, ed., Adlestrop Revisited (Sutton, 1999), citing Berg Collection FNB 75; Glen Newey, 'Goneness is everywhere', London Review of Books blog, 24 June 2014; 'Adlestrop (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Matthew Hollis, Now All Roads Lead to France, 2011)
