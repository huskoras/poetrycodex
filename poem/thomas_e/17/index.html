Death & Dying

Rain

by Edward Thomas 1878–1917

From Poems (1917, as 'Edward Eastaway')

Death & DyingThe Mind

Rain, midnight rain, nothing but the wild rain
On this bleak hut, and solitude, and me
Remembering again that I shall die
And neither hear the rain nor give it thanks
For washing me cleaner than I have been
Since I was born into this solitude.
Blessed are the dead that the rain rains upon:
But here I pray that none whom once I loved
Is dying to-night or lying still awake
Solitary, listening to the rain,
Either in pain or thus in sympathy
Helpless among the living and the dead,
Like a cold water among broken reeds,
Myriads of broken reeds all still and stiff,
Like me who have no love which this wild rain
Has not dissolved except the love of death,
If love it be towards what is perfect and
Cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Midnight rain falls on a bleak hut, and the speaker prays that no one he loves is lying awake, since the rain has dissolved every love he had except 'the love of death'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date l. 1

    Thomas enlisted in the Artists Rifles in July 1915 and was based at Hare Hall Camp, Gidea Park, near Romford, as a map-reading instructor. The Oxford manuscript of 'Rain' is dated 7 January 1916 and noted as written while travelling home from Hare Hall. The poem returns to a passage in Thomas's travel book The Icknield Way, where the narrator lies awake listening to the 'midnight and solitude of the rain' and hears a ghostly double speak of death; that passage ends with the saying 'Blessed are the dead that the rain rains on'. The opening line, with three 'rain's, sets a heavy incantatory rhythm.

    University of Oxford, First World War Poetry Digital Archive, manuscript of 'Rain', dated 7 January 1916, 'written travelling home from Hare Hall'; Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)

  2. this bleak hut l. 2

    Soldiers in training in 1915-16 lived in wooden huts in camps like Hare Hall. The word 'bleak' (exposed, cheerless) and the bare list 'and solitude, and me' make the speaker feel stripped to essentials. Readers sometimes assume the poem was written at the front, but Thomas did not go to France until January 1917; the manuscript evidence places it in England. The hut, however, already belongs to the world of war, and the thought of death in the next line is shaped by it.

    University of Oxford, First World War Poetry Digital Archive, manuscript of 'Rain', dated 7 January 1916, 'written travelling home from Hare Hall'; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University); Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text

  3. Remembering again that I shall die l. 3

    The thought is 'again' because it recurs, and because Thomas had written it before: in The Icknield Way the rain will 'fall darkly upon the grass over the grave when my ears can hear it no more'. In 1916, with the prospect of service abroad, the reflection has a new and literal urgency. The poem's syntax runs on without a break through the next three lines, imitating persistent rain and persistent thought. Thomas was killed at Arras on 9 April 1917.

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; University of Oxford, First World War Poetry Digital Archive, manuscript of 'Rain', dated 7 January 1916, 'written travelling home from Hare Hall'

  4. For washing me cleaner than I have been l. 5

    The speaker imagines a death in which he could not 'give it thanks / For washing me cleaner than I have been / Since I was born into this solitude'. The rain is a kind of baptism, a purification of the soul as well as the body, with an echo of Psalm 51:7 ('Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow'). But 'born into this solitude' makes isolation the human condition from birth, a recurrent theme in Thomas, who suffered from depression. The Icknield Way passage similarly imagines the rain 'accusing and trying me and passing judgment'.

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; Bible (KJV), Psalm 51:7

  5. Blessed are the dead that the rain rains upon l. 7

    Thomas had quoted the saying in The Icknield Way (1913) as words he 'used to love, I knew not why'. It belongs to a traditional pairing in which the bride whom the sun shines on and the dead whom the rain falls on are both counted fortunate. Its form echoes the Beatitudes of the Sermon on the Mount ('Blessed are they that mourn', Matthew 5:4) and especially Revelation 14:13, 'Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord . . . that they may rest from their labours'. Rain on a grave was thought a good omen for the soul.

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; Bible (KJV), Revelation 14:13; Bible (KJV), Matthew 5:3-11; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)

  6. here I pray that none whom once I loved ll. 8-12

    The colon after l. 7 marks a turn: the speaker's thoughts move to 'none whom once I loved'. 'Once' is chilling, implying that he no longer loves them, which the end of the poem confirms ('no love which this wild rain / Has not dissolved'). The prayer covers the dying, the sleepless and the 'Helpless among the living and the dead', which in early 1916 included the soldiers at the front and those who waited at home.

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)

  7. broken reeds ll. 13-14

    The reed is a biblical emblem of weakness: 'A bruised reed shall he not break' (Isaiah 42:3, quoted of Christ in Matthew 12:20), and Egypt is a 'broken reed' that pierces the hand of whoever leans on it (Isaiah 36:6). Thomas's 'Myriads of broken reeds all still and stiff', with cold water among them, is a desolate natural image that many readers also see as a battlefield of corpses. 'Cold water' contrasts with the cleansing rain earlier. The simile is notoriously hard to construe: it may describe the helpless sympathisers, or the speaker himself ('Like me').

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 42:3; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 36:6; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)

  8. Myriads l. 14

    'Myriad' comes from Greek myrias, 'ten thousand', and in English means an indefinitely great number (OED). In 1916 the word inevitably evoked the vast casualty lists. 'All still and stiff' extends the reeds into an image of rigid bodies, though Thomas never makes the comparison explicit. The heavy stresses and alliteration (still/stiff) slow the line, and the plural noun placed first in the line, before its verbless description, gives it the weight of a casualty statistic rather than an image of nature.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)

  9. no love . . . except the love of death ll. 15-16

    The final sentence admits that all his loves have been 'dissolved' except 'the love of death', a startling statement from a man who had suffered severe depression and once contemplated suicide. The qualification that follows ('If love it be towards what is perfect') turns the feeling into something like a longing for perfection and rest. Some readers find here a death-wish; others see a stoic or quasi-religious acceptance of death as the one thing that 'cannot disappoint'. The Icknield Way passage, where the narrator imagines melting into the rain, supports a reading of surrender rather than despair alone.

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University); University of Oxford, First World War Poetry Digital Archive, manuscript of 'Rain', dated 7 January 1916, 'written travelling home from Hare Hall'

  10. what is perfect and / Cannot . . . disappoint ll. 17-18

    The poem ends on 'disappoint', a quiet, almost domestic word for an enormous claim: unlike love and life, death cannot fail to fulfil what it promises. The line break after 'and' leaves the reader hanging before the conclusion, and the parenthetical 'the tempest tells me' gives the rain the voice of an oracle, as the 'ghostly double' does in The Icknield Way. The rain that begins as 'midnight rain' has risen to a 'tempest'. Thomas's preoccupation with death and perfection here can be compared with Keats's 'half in love with easeful Death' (Ode to a Nightingale).

    Edward Thomas, The Icknield Way (London: Constable, 1913), ch. XI, 'Streatley to East Hendred', Internet Archive text; John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale', l. 52 (Poetry Foundation text)

  11. Form: blank verse ll. 1-18

    The poem is in unrhymed iambic pentameter, often loosened, with long sentences that run over line endings so that the thought, like the rain, seems unbroken. 'Rain' appears seven times, with 'rains' once more, and 'solitude', 'love' and 'broken reeds' recur, giving an incantatory effect. The last line but one ends on the weak word 'and', an unusual enjambment that enacts suspense. Thomas came to verse late, at Frost's urging, and his blank verse imitates the rhythms of meditative speech rather than formal oratory.

    University of Oxford, First World War Poetry Digital Archive, manuscript of 'Rain', dated 7 January 1916, 'written travelling home from Hare Hall'; Robert Nanian, annotated text of 'Rain' (George Mason University)
