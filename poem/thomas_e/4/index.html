As the Team's Head-Brass

by Edward Thomas 1878–1917

From Poems (1917, as 'Edward Eastaway')

History & PoliticsDeath & Dying

As the team's head-brass flashed out on the turn
The lovers disappeared into the wood.
I sat among the boughs of the fallen elm
That strewed an angle of the fallow, and
Watched the plough narrowing a yellow square
Of charlock. Every time the horses turned
Instead of treading me down, the ploughman leaned
Upon the handles to say or ask a word,
About the weather, next about the war.
Scraping the share he faced towards the wood,
And screwed along the furrow till the brass flashed
Once more.

The blizzard felled the elm whose crest
I sat in, by a woodpecker's round hole,
The ploughman said. "When will they take it away?"
"When the war's over." So the talk began--
One minute and an interval of ten,
A minute more and the same interval.
"Have you been out?" "No." "And don't want to, perhaps?"
"If I could only come back again, I should.
I could spare an arm. I shouldn't want to lose
A leg. If I should lose my head, why, so,
I should want nothing more. . . . Have many gone
From here?" "Yes." "Many lost?" "Yes: good few.
Only two teams work on the farm this year.
One of my mates is dead. The second day
In France they killed him. It was back in March,
The very night of the blizzard, too. Now if
He had stayed here we should have moved the tree."
"And I should not have sat here. Everything
Would have been different. For it would have been
Another world." "Ay, and a better, though
If we could see all all might seem good." Then
The lovers came out of the wood again:
The horses started and for the last time
I watched the clods crumble and topple over
After the ploughshare and the stumbling team.

Codex Note

A conversation with a ploughman while lovers go in and out of a wood. The war enters it only slowly, with a mate killed and an elm that was not moved because of him. One of Thomas's best-known poems.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and situation l. 1

    Edward Thomas (1878-1917) enlisted in the Artists Rifles in July 1915. In 1916 he was a map-reading instructor at Hare Hall camp in Essex, and the poem, dated 27 May 1916 on the manuscript, was written as he moved toward applying for a commission that would take him to France. He was killed at Arras on 9 April 1917. The poem first appeared in Poems (1917) under his pseudonym 'Edward Eastaway'. Its dialogue with a ploughman about whether to go to the war is therefore close to Thomas's own decision.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), manuscript of 'As the team's head-brass', dated 27 May 1916; Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Edward Thomas, The Collected Poems, ed. R. George Thomas (Oxford UP, 1978)

  2. head-brass l. 1

    A 'team' is a pair or more of horses yoked to the plough. Horse brasses were polished brass ornaments fixed to the harness and bridle, and the 'head-brass' is the brass on the horses' heads. Its flash at each turn of the plough marks the poem's rhythm: the conversation happens in snatches each time the team comes round. The flashing light also hints, faintly, at military brass and the glint of war.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brass, n.'

  3. The lovers l. 2

    The lovers enter the wood in line 2 and come out in line 33, so they frame the whole conversation. They stand for love and the continuity of ordinary life, of generation, while the men talk of death. The image recalls Thomas Hardy's 'In Time of "The Breaking of Nations"' (1915), where a man harrowing clods and a pair of lovers outlast the 'dynasties' and wars of history. Critics have often compared the two poems.

    Thomas Hardy, 'In Time of "The Breaking of Nations"' (1915; Moments of Vision, 1917); Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008)

  4. the fallen elm l. 3

    The speaker sits in the branches of an elm brought down by a blizzard. The tree becomes the poem's quiet image of the war's effects on the home front: it lies where it fell because a farmworker who would have helped move it has been killed in France. The elm, a traditional feature of the English hedgerow landscape, has fallen like the soldiers.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Edward Thomas, The Collected Poems, ed. R. George Thomas (Oxford UP, 1978)

  5. fallow ... charlock l. 6

    'Fallow' is ploughed land left unsown for a season, here overgrown. Charlock (Sinapis arvensis, wild mustard) is a common yellow-flowered weed of cornfields, so the plough is turning a 'yellow square' of weed under. The square narrows with each turn, an image of time running out, and the precise botanical name is typical of Thomas, a prolific writer on the countryside before he turned to poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'charlock'; Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008)

  6. the share ... screwed along the furrow l. 10

    The ploughshare (share) is the iron blade at the front of the plough that cuts the soil; ploughmen scraped it clean of clinging earth at the end of each furrow. 'Screwed along the furrow' gives the effortful, twisting movement of man and team, and perhaps the ploughman screwing up his eyes. These technical details ground the poem in the real labour of the farm.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'share, n.'; Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008)

  7. Blank verse and broken lines l. 12

    The poem is in unrhymed iambic pentameter, flexible enough to carry everyday speech. 'Once more.' is a half-line, completed after the gap by 'The blizzard felled the elm whose crest', and the visual break marks the pause before the talk begins. Thomas's close friend Robert Frost, whose North of Boston (1914) Thomas had reviewed, encouraged him to write verse that caught the 'sound of sense', the rhythm of the speaking voice, and the dialogues of this poem show that influence.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Matthew Spencer, ed., Elected Friends: Robert Frost and Edward Thomas to One Another (Handsel Books, 2003)

  8. The blizzard l. 12

    Late March 1916 saw an exceptionally severe blizzard across southern England, which felled great numbers of trees, and the poem's date and details fit it. The ploughman ties the blizzard to the death of his mate in France 'the very night of the blizzard', so a home storm and a foreign death coincide. Natural and human catastrophe are both simply facts in his plain report.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008)

  9. One minute and an interval of ten l. 16

    The rhythm of the dialogue follows the work: each time the team reaches the speaker's end of the field the ploughman stops for a word, then disappears for ten minutes along the furrow. The fragmented exchange that follows is therefore realistic. It also gives the poem a reticence typical of Thomas, since so much is left unsaid in the intervals.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Edward Thomas, The Collected Poems, ed. R. George Thomas (Oxford UP, 1978)

  10. Have you been out? l. 18

    'Been out' was the common wartime idiom for having served at the front. The ploughman's follow-up ('And don't want to, perhaps?') touches the question of willingness that Thomas himself was weighing in May 1916. The speaker's reply weighs injuries with grim levity: an arm he could spare, not a leg; and if he lost his head, he would want nothing more. The joke on 'lose my head' (be killed, but also panic) masks real fear.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), manuscript of 'As the team's head-brass', dated 27 May 1916

  11. Many lost? ... Only two teams l. 23

    'Good few' is a dialect understatement meaning 'quite a lot'. With so many men gone, through volunteering and, from 1916, conscription under the Military Service Act, the farm works fewer teams. The ploughman's flat answers carry the scale of rural loss without any rhetoric. The exchange of short questions and one-word answers is spare, like the depleted village itself. Nobody names the dead.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Military Service Act 1916 (UK), introducing conscription

  12. One of my mates is dead l. 25

    The war enters the poem most directly in this plain sentence, and then at once returns to the fallen tree: if his mate had stayed, 'we should have moved the tree'. The logic links a death in France to a small change in an English field. Thomas's poetry often registers the war this way, obliquely, through its effects on the landscape at home.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Edward Thomas, The Collected Poems, ed. R. George Thomas (Oxford UP, 1978)

  13. Another world ... If we could see all l. 31

    The speaker follows the ploughman's 'if' to its end: had the man stayed, he would not be sitting on the elm, and the world would be another world. The ploughman agrees it would be 'a better' world, then qualifies: 'If we could see all all might seem good.' The doubled 'all all' is deliberate. This is a countryman's version of providential consolation, that from a complete view everything would seem right, and its tentative 'might seem' leaves it uncertain. Critics differ on whether the line offers real consolation or a fatalism the poem does not share.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); Edward Thomas, The Collected Poems, ed. R. George Thomas (Oxford UP, 1978)

  14. for the last time l. 34

    Literally, this is the last circuit the speaker watches before leaving. Readers who know Thomas's fate (killed at Arras less than a year later) hear an ominous second sense: a farewell to the English landscape. The lovers re-emerge at the same moment, so continuity (love) and ending (war, death) are juxtaposed in the poem's last movement. The poem gives no comment, leaving the reader to feel the doubleness.

    Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008); First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), manuscript of 'As the team's head-brass', dated 27 May 1916

  15. clods crumble ... the stumbling team l. 35

    The final lines show the earth crumbling and toppling behind the ploughshare, an image of renewal (ploughing prepares a crop) that also suggests destruction, even burial. The team is 'stumbling', no longer smooth. The scene recalls Hardy's 'In Time of "The Breaking of Nations"' ('Only a man harrowing clods'), where rural labour outlasts history. Thomas's version is less confident that the old rural order will survive the war.

    Thomas Hardy, 'In Time of "The Breaking of Nations"' (1915; Moments of Vision, 1917); Edward Thomas, The Annotated Collected Poems, ed. Edna Longley (Bloodaxe Books, 2008)
