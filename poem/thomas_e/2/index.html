Death & Dying

The Owl

by Edward Thomas 1878–1917

From Poems (1917, as 'Edward Eastaway')

Death & DyingHistory & Politics

Downhill I came, hungry, and yet not starved;
Cold, yet had heat within me that was proof
Against the North wind; tired, yet so that rest
Had seemed the sweetest thing under a roof.

Then at the inn I had food, fire, and rest,
Knowing how hungry, cold, and tired was I.
All of the night was quite barred out except
An owl's cry, a most melancholy cry

Shaken out long and clear upon the hill,
No merry note, nor cause of merriment,
But one telling me plain what I escaped
And others could not, that night, as in I went.

And salted was my food, and my repose,
Salted and sobered, too, by the bird's voice
Speaking for all who lay under the stars,
Soldiers and poor, unable to rejoice.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A tired walker eats and rests at an inn, and the owl's cry from the hill reminds him of all who lay out under the stars that night, 'soldiers and poor, unable to rejoice'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and wartime context l. 1

    Edward Thomas (1878-1917) began writing poetry only in late 1914, encouraged by Robert Frost. 'The Owl' dates from February 1915, months before he joined the Artists' Rifles in July 1915. Most of his poems were published after his death, in Poems (1917) under the pseudonym 'Edward Eastaway'. He was killed at Arras on 9 April 1917. The poem is not set at the front, but the war is present in its last line. It begins with a walker coming down a hill to an inn, a scene typical of Thomas's poetry of walking and rural England.

    Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'; First World War Poetry Digital Archive (University of Oxford), Edward Thomas, 'The Owl'

  2. hungry ... Cold ... tired ll. 1-3

    The first stanza lists three conditions, each qualified: 'hungry, and yet not starved; / Cold, yet had heat within me ...; tired, yet so that rest / Had seemed the sweetest thing'. The qualifications mark the walker's privileged state: his hardships are moderate and chosen. The triad returns in l. 6, 'how hungry, cold, and tired was I', and is implicitly contrasted with the unqualified hunger, cold and tiredness of those outside. The careful qualifying syntax is characteristic of Thomas.

    Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'; Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  3. proof / Against the North wind ll. 2-3

    'Proof' here is an adjective meaning able to withstand, impervious, as in 'proof against' attack, or the compound 'waterproof'. The OED records this sense from the sixteenth century, often of armour. The inner heat protects the walker from the north wind. The word has a slightly archaic, martial ring, faintly anticipating the soldiers of the last stanza. The sense is of a body that, for now, is well defended; by the end of the poem that self-sufficiency has been called into question.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'

  4. food, fire, and rest l. 5

    The second stanza answers the first: 'food, fire, and rest' meets the hunger, cold and tiredness of the walk. The alliteration of 'food, fire' adds warmth. 'Knowing how hungry, cold, and tired was I' shows that the satisfaction makes him aware, for the first time, of the extent of his need. The shelter of the inn is set against the open night outside.

    Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'; Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  5. All of the night was quite barred out l. 7

    The night is 'quite barred out', excluded by the inn's walls and shutters. 'Quite' means completely. The one exception, the owl's cry, breaks the protective enclosure. The enjambment 'except / An owl's cry' runs across the stanza's line end, imitating the cry's intrusion. Thomas often sets an interior against the weather and the dark outside, and here the walls that should guarantee comfort prove unable to exclude what the owl brings in: knowledge of others' exposure. The phrase prepares the moral turn of the last two stanzas.

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  6. a most melancholy cry l. 8

    The owl has long been the bird of night, death and melancholy in English poetry; Gray's 'Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard' has the 'moping owl' that 'does to the moon complain'. Thomas's phrase 'a most melancholy cry' is deliberately conventional and slightly archaic in its use of 'most'. The repetition 'An owl's cry, a most melancholy cry' extends the sound, as the next line's 'Shaken out long and clear' does. The Cambridge Companion chapter reads the cry as quietly undermining the poem's final rhyme.

    Thomas Gray, 'Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard' (1751), st. 3, Wikisource; Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  7. No merry note l. 10

    The winter song that ends Love's Labour's Lost has the lines 'Then nightly sings the staring owl, / Tu-whit, to-who, a merry note'. Thomas's owl gives 'No merry note, nor cause of merriment'. The Cambridge Companion chapter notes the contrast. Shakespeare's song depicts a rural winter of hardship ('When icicles hang by the wall') in a comic key; Thomas's owl brings the hardship of others into the warm inn without any merriment.

    Shakespeare, Love's Labour's Lost, 5.2, closing song of Winter ('Then nightly sings the staring owl'), Folger Shakespeare; Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  8. telling me plain what I escaped l. 11

    The cry's message is 'plain', clear: it tells the speaker 'what I escaped / And others could not, that night'. The stress on escape and on others' inability shows the speaker's awareness of his privilege. 'That night, as in I went' places the moment exactly. The owl becomes a messenger of conscience. The plainness matters: the cry is not a supernatural omen or a Romantic symbol but a clear statement, and the speaker accepts its meaning without argument. The verb 'escaped' carries a faint sense of guilt, as if comfort were a kind of evasion.

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org); Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'

  9. salted ll. 13-14

    'Salted' is the poem's key word, repeated: 'salted was my food, and my repose, / Salted and sobered'. Salt seasons food and gives it savour; the Cambridge Companion chapter glosses the sense as seasoned, but also made poignant or piquant. Salt also suggests tears and the sea. There may be a biblical undertone: in Mark 9:49, 'every one shall be salted with fire', and in Matthew 5:13 the faithful are 'the salt of the earth'. 'Sobered' adds seriousness: the comfort is not cancelled but chastened by awareness of others.

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Mark 9:49 (KJV)

  10. Speaking for all who lay under the stars l. 15

    The owl's voice is 'Speaking for all who lay under the stars', those without shelter that night. The Cambridge Companion chapter observes that the owl speaks for others, sparing the poet the presumption of doing so directly: the owl is insistent, the poet is not. 'Under the stars' is gentle in phrasing but stark in implication: sleeping in the open, in winter.

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org)

  11. Soldiers and poor l. 16

    The last line names those for whom the owl speaks: 'Soldiers and poor, unable to rejoice'. The absence of 'the' before 'poor' makes the two groups run together, and the Cambridge Companion chapter suggests the phrasing may imply that soldiers are themselves poor, a politically pointed suggestion. In February 1915 soldiers were sleeping out in the trenches and in camps; Thomas would join them within months. The poem's voice contrasts with the patriotic note of Rupert Brooke's 'The Soldier' (1914).

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org); Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'

  12. Form: rhyme and ending st. 1-4

    The poem's four quatrains of loose iambic pentameter rhyme only on the second and fourth lines: proof/roof, I/cry, merriment/went, voice/rejoice. The final rhyme pairs the owl's 'voice' with 'rejoice', but the line denies rejoicing. The Cambridge Companion chapter reads the owl's cry as undermining the closing rhyme. The plain, conversational syntax and the careful qualifications are typical of Thomas's style, which Frost helped shape.

    Neil Corcoran, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Twentieth-Century English Poetry (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), chapter on Owen, Sassoon and the poetry of war (reproduced at Literariness.org); Edna Longley, ed., Edward Thomas: The Annotated Collected Poems (Tarset: Bloodaxe, 2008), notes to 'The Owl'
