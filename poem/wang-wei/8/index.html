Nature

Deer-Park Hermitage

by Wang Wei AD 699–759

Translated by Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu (1929) · From The Jade Mountain (1929)

Nature

There seems to be no one on the empty mountain . . .
And yet I think I hear a voice,
Where sunlight, entering a grove,
Shines back to me from the green moss.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Wang Wei's 'Deer Enclosure' (Lu zhai), Wangchuan collection title

    The title 鹿柴 Lu zhai names a spot on Wang Wei's (c. 699-761) country estate at Wangchuan, in the Zhongnan mountains south of Chang'an. With his friend Pei Di he wrote the Wangchuan ji, twenty paired quatrains, one for each scenic site. 柴 (read zhai, the same character as chai, 'firewood') here means a palisade or fenced enclosure, so 'Deer Fence' or 'Deer Enclosure' is closer than Bynner and Kiang's 'Deer-Park Hermitage', which invents a hermitage. The poem has four lines of five characters. Weinberger and Paz's Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei uses this one poem to compare translations, including Bynner's, and is the best introduction to its difficulties.

    Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980)

  2. empty mountain (kong shan 空山) l. 1

    The poem opens 空山不見人, 'empty mountain, no one seen'. 空 kong means 'empty, deserted', but it is also the standard Chinese translation of the Buddhist sunyata, the emptiness of all phenomena of fixed self-nature. Wang Wei, whose courtesy name Mojie came from the Buddhist layman Vimalakirti, used the word often, and many readers see the landscape as a figure of enlightened perception: the world is empty, yet appearances (voices, light) arise. Others warn against reading every Wang Wei landscape as doctrine. Bynner's 'There seems to be no one' adds 'seems', a hedge not in the Chinese, which simply states that no one is seen.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980); Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  3. 'but hear the echo of voices' l. 2

    但聞人語響, 'only hear people's talk echo'. Classical Chinese often omits the subject, and the absence of 'I' in this poem is much discussed: the scene is perceived without a perceiver. Bynner supplies 'I think I hear', which makes the experience personal and doubtful. 響 xiang is 'sound, echo', suggesting voices heard from far off across the slopes, perhaps of woodcutters, without the speakers being seen. Weinberger's study compares how the translators he collects handle this line.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription

  4. returning light (fan jing 返景) l. 3

    返景入深林, 'returning light enters the deep forest'. 返景 fan jing (景 here read 'light, sunlight', = 影) is the light of the declining sun, which reaches low under the canopy only at day's end; some commentators understand it as light reflected back from the eastern hills. Bynner's 'sunlight, entering a grove' loses both the time of day and the depth (深 shen) of the forest; 'grove' suggests a small cluster rather than deep woods. The poem thus moves from sound to light, and from day to dusk.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980)

  5. light on green moss l. 4

    復照青苔上, 'again shines on the green moss'. 復 fu, 'again', implies that the light has touched the moss before, at an earlier hour, or that each evening it returns; the moment is one of a recurring cycle. Moss grows in shade where people seldom tread, so the light reveals an untouched place. The poem ends on a small bright patch in the dark wood, often read as a Buddhist image of momentary illumination. Bynner's 'Shines back to me' makes the light a message to the viewer, a characteristically Western, personal turn; Weinberger's study lets one compare many such choices.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980); Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription

  6. the poem in English translation l. 1

    Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz's Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (1987) prints the Chinese characters, a transliteration, a word-by-word gloss and a series of translations in chronological order, from W. J. B. Fletcher (1919) and Bynner and Kiang (1929) to Kenneth Rexroth, Burton Watson and Gary Snyder (1978). Bynner's 'seems', 'I think', and 'back to me' are examples of the added explanation and personal 'I' that set early versions apart from the Chinese. Their version is nonetheless typical of the 1920s attempt to render Tang poetry in natural, unrhymed English free verse. Comparing it with the Chinese gloss shows how much of a Chinese quatrain lies in what is left unsaid.

    Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980)

  7. the matching poem by Pei Di l. 2

    Every Wangchuan site has a poem by Wang Wei and one by Pei Di, written as an exchange. Pei Di's 'Deer Enclosure' mentions the cold mountain and the traces of deer, giving a more ordinary picture of the place. Comparing the two shows that Wang Wei leaves out the deer entirely: the title is the only reference to them. Pauline Yu's study discusses the collection as a sequence and the friendship behind it. Students may find the Chinese texts of both on Wikisource.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  8. the five-character quatrain (jueju) ll. 1-4

    The poem is a jueju ('cut-off line'), a quatrain of five-character lines, the shortest standard Tang form, valued for suggestion over statement. Its rhymes fall on 響 xiang (l. 2) and 上 shang (l. 4), both in an oblique (non-level) tone in Middle Chinese, a less common choice that some readers hear as giving the poem its hushed, inward close. Its structure pairs the two senses: lines 1-2 give hearing without sight (no one seen, voices heard), lines 3-4 give sight without people (light, moss). Bynner and Kiang's four unrhymed lines keep the number of lines but use thirty-one English words for twenty characters, and the turn from hearing to seeing is blurred by the run-on syntax of their 'Where sunlight ... Shines back'.

    Wang Wei, 'Lu zhai' (鹿柴), Wangchuan ji, Quan Tang shi juan 128, Chinese Wikisource; Pauline Yu, The Poetry of Wang Wei: New Translations and Commentary (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1980); Eliot Weinberger and Octavio Paz, Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei (Mount Kisco, NY: Moyer Bell, 1987); Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription
