Ode on a Grecian Urn

by John Keats 1795–1821

Arts & SciencesTime & BrevityLove

Thou still unravish’d bride of quietness,
Thou foster-child of silence and slow time,
Sylvan historian, who canst thus express
A flowery tale more sweetly than our rhyme:
What leaf-fring’d legend haunts about thy shape
Of deities or mortals, or of both,
In Tempe or the dales of Arcady?
What men or gods are these? What maidens loth?
What mad pursuit? What struggle to escape?
What pipes and timbrels? What wild ecstasy?

Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard
Are sweeter; therefore, ye soft pipes, play on;
Not to the sensual ear, but, more endear’d,
Pipe to the spirit ditties of no tone:
Fair youth, beneath the trees, thou canst not leave
Thy song, nor ever can those trees be bare;
Bold Lover, never, never canst thou kiss,
Though winning near the goal yet, do not grieve;
She cannot fade, though thou hast not thy bliss,
For ever wilt thou love, and she be fair!

Ah, happy, happy boughs! that cannot shed
Your leaves, nor ever bid the Spring adieu;
And, happy melodist, unwearied,
For ever piping songs for ever new;
More happy love! more happy, happy love!
For ever warm and still to be enjoy’d,
For ever panting, and for ever young;
All breathing human passion far above,
That leaves a heart high-sorrowful and cloy’d,
A burning forehead, and a parching tongue.

Who are these coming to the sacrifice?
To what green altar, O mysterious priest,
Lead’st thou that heifer lowing at the skies,
And all her silken flanks with garlands drest?
What little town by river or sea shore,
Or mountain-built with peaceful citadel,
Is emptied of this folk, this pious morn?
And, little town, thy streets for evermore
Will silent be; and not a soul to tell
Why thou art desolate, can e’er return.

O Attic shape! Fair attitude! with brede
Of marble men and maidens overwrought,
With forest branches and the trodden weed;
Thou, silent form, dost tease us out of thought
As doth eternity: Cold Pastoral!
When old age shall this generation waste,
Thou shalt remain, in midst of other woe
Than ours, a friend to man, to whom thou say’st,
“Beauty is truth, truth beauty,”—that is all
Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Keats questions a silent urn until it answers with the poem’s most debated line: ‘Beauty is truth, truth beauty.’ Its frozen figures never age and never quite live — art’s cold, consoling permanence.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition, the urn, and printing l. 1

    Like the other spring odes, this was written in May 1819. No single vase matches Keats's description; editors point to the Townley Vase and the Elgin Marbles (which Keats saw with Haydon and celebrated in a sonnet of 1817) in the British Museum, the Borghese Vase and the Sosibios Vase, of which Keats traced an engraving from Henry Moses's collection of antique vases (the tracing is at Keats House). Ian Jack also proposes paintings by Claude for the sacrificial procession. It was first printed anonymously in the Annals of the Fine Arts for 1819 (the issue is dated variously to late 1819 or January 1820), then in the 1820 volume.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Ian Jack, Keats and the Mirror of Art (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' (survey of the critical debate, with references)

  2. still unravish'd bride l. 1

    The pun on 'still' governs the poem. The urn has not yet been violated (by time, breakage or interpretation) and it is also quiet and unmoving. A bride 'unravish'd' is perpetually on the threshold of consummation, like the lovers on its side. Cleanth Brooks made the double meaning central to his New Critical reading: the urn is both a figure of arrested life and of enduring permanence. 'Ravish' can also mean to enrapture, so the urn is also not yet carried away.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still, adv.'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  3. foster-child of silence and slow time l. 2

    The urn's original potter and painter are long dead; it has been adopted and brought up by silence and the slow passing of centuries. The genealogical metaphors (bride, foster-child, then historian) give the object three successive identities in the first three lines. 'Slow time' suggests both duration and the way time seems to move slowly or not at all for the figures on the urn.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  4. Sylvan historian l. 3

    'Sylvan' means of the woods; the urn is a historian because it records a 'flowery tale', but its history is rustic and pictorial. Cleanth Brooks took the phrase for the title of his essay 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes' (1947), arguing that the urn's kind of truth is that of an imaginative, not factual, record. The claim that it tells its tale 'more sweetly than our rhyme' sets up the competition between visual art and poetry that the poem stages.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  5. leaf-fring'd legend l. 5

    Greek vases often bear bands of foliage ornament around their scenes. 'Legend' carries both its senses: a story (often of saints or heroes), and, from its Latin root, an inscription or caption to be read. The legend 'haunts about' the shape, as if the story were a ghost lingering round the vessel; the urn is figured as something both present and spectral.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'legend, n.'

  6. Tempe; Arcady l. 7

    Tempe is a gorge in Thessaly between Mounts Olympus and Ossa, through which the river Peneus runs; it was famous in classical poetry for its beauty and associated with Apollo. Arcady (Arcadia), a mountainous region of the central Peloponnese, became through Virgil's Eclogues and Renaissance pastoral the ideal country of shepherds and love. Keats's question cannot be answered: the urn's place is the idealised Greece of literary tradition rather than a real location.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Virgil, Eclogues (esp. 7 and 10), The Latin Library; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  7. the questions ll. 8-10

    Stanza 1 ends in seven questions, which the urn, being silent, cannot answer. They describe a Bacchic or Dionysiac scene: pursuit, 'maidens loth' (unwilling), struggle, music and 'wild ecstasy'. The frenzy contrasts with the 'quietness' of the first line. Vendler reads the stanza as the first of the speaker's several attempts to interpret the urn, each partial; the questions return in stanza 4 about the sacrifice.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  8. timbrels l. 10

    A timbrel is a small hand-drum or tambourine. The word is strongly biblical: Miriam takes 'a timbrel in her hand' and the women follow her 'with timbrels and with dances' (Exodus 15:20). On Greek vases the tympanon and the double pipe (aulos) accompany Dionysian revels. The pipes reappear as 'soft pipes' in stanza 2, now silenced by art. The list of pipes and timbrels prepares the paradox of silent music that opens stanza 2.

    The Bible (KJV), Exodus 15:20; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'timbrel, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  9. Heard melodies ... those unheard ll. 11-14

    The paradox has a Platonic flavour: the ideal is superior to its sensory embodiment. 'Ditties of no tone' are songs without sound. The idea also recalls Keats's letter to Bailey (22 November 1817) on the imagination's power to anticipate a greater happiness, 'repeated in a finer tone' hereafter. Critics debate how far the poem endorses this claim; by stanza 3 the advantage of the unheard and unchanging is shadowed by its coldness.

    Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  10. Bold Lover ll. 17-20

    The consolation is double-edged: arrested at the moment of desire, he will 'for ever' love and she 'be fair', but he will never have his 'bliss'. The repeated 'never, never' and 'for ever' set up the tension between frustration and permanence that the poem never fully resolves. Brooks saw here the urn's paradoxical truth; others, such as Earl Wasserman, read the poem as a dramatized movement towards and away from an ideal 'region' of permanence.

    Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Earl R. Wasserman, The Finer Tone: Keats' Major Poems (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1953); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  11. happy, happy ll. 21-7

    'Happy' occurs six times in the stanza and 'for ever' four times. Many critics (beginning with the earlier twentieth-century commentators surveyed by Bate) have thought the stanza the weakest in the ode; others argue that the overinsistence is dramatic, showing the speaker trying too hard to believe in the urn's happiness until the last three lines collapse into human 'breathing passion' and its aftermath. 'Still to be enjoy'd' again puns on 'still': yet to be, and for ever.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Wikipedia, 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' (survey of the critical debate, with references)

  12. high-sorrowful and cloy'd ll. 29-30

    'Cloy'd' means surfeited, sickened by excess of sweetness. Real love, unlike the urn's, is consummated and so passes into satiety and sorrow; its physical symptoms ('burning forehead', 'parching tongue') resemble those of fever, perhaps of consumption, which Keats had watched in Tom. The stanza thus turns the urn's advantage into a criticism of human life, but it also reminds us that the urn's figures cannot breathe.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cloy, v.'; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  13. the sacrifice ll. 31-4

    The scene of a heifer led to sacrifice is very close to the south frieze of the Parthenon among the Elgin Marbles, where a heifer raises its head as it is led; Keats had seen the marbles repeatedly. Ian Jack also points to Claude's painting of the sacrifice to Apollo. The heifer 'lowing at the skies' is the one sound-image of the stanza; the 'silken flanks' with garlands evoke the sacrificial ritual. The 'mysterious priest' and 'green altar' are left unexplained, as befits the urn's silence.

    Ian Jack, Keats and the Mirror of Art (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; B. R. Haydon, articles on Raphael's cartoons and the Elgin Marbles, The Examiner, 2 and 9 May 1819 (as discussed in Allott and in Jack)

  14. little town ll. 35-40

    The town is the speaker's invention: it is not depicted on the urn but inferred from the procession. Because the figures can never return, the town must remain 'desolate' for ever, with 'not a soul to tell' why. Many readers find this the poem's most haunting moment, where the logic of artistic permanence produces a permanent absence. Vendler sees in it the speaker's growing awareness of the cost of the urn's eternity.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  15. Attic shape! Fair attitude! l. 41

    'Attic' means Athenian, hence classically pure and restrained. 'Attitude' meant the posture of a figure in art (OED), a term of painting and sculpture. The jingle between the two words has been judged a lapse by some readers and a deliberate sound-play by others. The stanza turns from inside the urn's scenes back to the object as a whole. The exclamations mark the speaker's withdrawal from involvement with the figures to contemplation of the whole object.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'attitude, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  16. brede; overwrought ll. 41-2

    Keats's 'brede' is a variant spelling of 'braid', used of embroidery or interlacing; it describes the frieze of figures running round the vase. 'Overwrought' means 'worked over' (the surface is wrought with marble men and maidens), but its other sense, 'excessively agitated', recalls the 'mad pursuit' and 'wild ecstasy' of stanza 1. Note too 'marble': the urn of the poem seems to be carved rather than painted, like the Townley or Borghese vases.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brede, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'overwrought, adj.'

  17. tease us out of thought l. 44

    Keats had used the phrase in his verse Epistle to J. H. Reynolds (March 1818): 'Things cannot to the will / Be settled, but they tease us out of thought'. 'Tease' combines teasing out (as with wool) and provoking. The urn, like eternity, frustrates reasoning: it cannot be grasped by discursive thought. RPO glosses it as drawing us beyond the limits of thought.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tease, v.'

  18. Cold Pastoral! l. 45

    A pastoral is a representation of idealised rural life; 'cold' is both the literal coldness of marble and the absence of human warmth. The phrase gathers the poem's ambivalence: the urn is a 'friend to man' but cannot share 'breathing human passion'. Critics who read the ode as finally sceptical about art (e.g. those who stress the 'desolate' town) put weight on this epithet; others see it as part of a balanced, both/and attitude.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947)

  19. Beauty is truth, truth beauty ll. 49-50

    In the Annals of the Fine Arts the last two lines had no quotation marks; in the 1820 volume the five words are quoted, so the urn speaks them and the rest is ambiguous (urn or poet; addressed to readers or to the urn's figures). The transcripts by Keats's friends differ again. Keats had written to Bailey (22 November 1817) that 'What the imagination seizes as Beauty must be truth'. Bridges admired the lines; Quiller-Couch thought them vague; I. A. Richards treated them as a 'pseudo-statement'; T. S. Eliot called them 'a serious blemish on a beautiful poem' (1929); Cleanth Brooks defended them as a speech 'in character' for the urn. Some later critics read the final clause as the poet's qualification.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1978), text and textual notes for the poem; Cleanth Brooks, 'Keats's Sylvan Historian: History Without Footnotes', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York: Reynal & Hitchcock, 1947); Wikipedia, 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' (survey of the critical debate, with references)
