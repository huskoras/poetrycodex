Nature

To Autumn

by John Keats 1795–1821

NatureTime & Brevity

Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,
Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun;
Conspiring with him how to load and bless
With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run;
To bend with apples the moss’d cottage-trees,
And fill all fruit with ripeness to the core;
To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells
With a sweet kernel; to set budding more,
And still more, later flowers for the bees,
Until they think warm days will never cease,
For summer has o’er-brimm’d their clammy cells.

Who hath not seen thee oft amid thy store?
Sometimes whoever seeks abroad may find
Thee sitting careless on a granary floor,
Thy hair soft-lifted by the winnowing wind;
Or on a half-reap’d furrow sound asleep,
Drows’d with the fume of poppies, while thy hook
Spares the next swath and all its twinèd flowers:
And sometimes like a gleaner thou dost keep
Steady thy laden head across a brook;
Or by a cyder-press, with patient look,
Thou watchest the last oozings hours by hours.

Where are the songs of Spring? Ay, Where are they?
Think not of them, thou hast thy music too,—
While barrèd clouds bloom the soft-dying day,
And touch the stubble-plains with rosy hue;
Then in a wailful choir the small gnats mourn
Among the river sallows, borne aloft
Or sinking as the light wind lives or dies;
And full-grown lambs loud bleat from hilly bourn;
Hedge-crickets sing; and now with treble soft
The red-breast whistles from a garden-croft;
And gathering swallows twitter in the skies.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

No lament, only fullness: mists, ripeness, the last oozings of the cider-press. Keats hears autumn’s own music and lets the season stand, unhurried, as its own kind of perfection.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: Winchester, September 1819 l. 1

    Keats was staying in Winchester. On 21 September (Colvin dates the letter 22 September) he wrote to J. H. Reynolds: 'How beautiful the season is now ... I never lik'd stubble fields so much as now ... Somehow a stubble plain looks warm ... this struck me so much in my sunday's walk that I composed upon it.' The walk was probably along the water-meadows of the Itchen, though the exact field is not known. The ode was published in the 1820 Lamia volume. It is often called the most perfect of the odes.

    Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Hyder E. Rollins, ed., The Letters of John Keats 1814-1821, 2 vols (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1958); Wikipedia, 'To Autumn' (survey of composition and criticism, with references); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  2. Stanza form st. 1

    Keats adds a line to the ten-line stanza of the spring odes: a quatrain (abab) followed by a seven-line unit (in stanza 1, cdedcce) in which the couplet falls on lines 9-10, delaying the close. The extra line gives each stanza a feeling of overflow appropriate to its subject ('o'er-brimm'd'). The stanzas move from morning to evening, early to late autumn, and from touch and taste to sight to sound. Unlike the other odes there is no 'I'.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Wikipedia, 'To Autumn' (survey of composition and criticism, with references)

  3. mists and mellow fruitfulness l. 1

    The poem begins with an address, as in a hymn: autumn is called 'Season' and given attributes. 'Mellow' describes fruit that is soft, sweet and fully ripe (OED), and by extension a mature softness of light and sound. The alliteration of m and f, and the long vowels, slow the line. The poem's whole first stanza is one sentence without a main verb, a series of infinitives ('to load and bless', 'to bend', 'to swell') dependent on 'Conspiring'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mellow, adj.'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  4. bosom-friend ... Conspiring ll. 2-3

    'Bosom-friend' is an intimate companion. 'Conspiring' keeps its etymological sense of breathing together, as well as the sense of secret plotting, here benign. The 'maturing sun' is both the sun that matures the fruit and the sun that is itself maturing towards the lower light of autumn. Some political readings have noted the word 'conspiring' in the year of radical agitation and government suspicion of conspiracy, though this is a minority interpretation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'conspire, v.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Nicholas Roe, John Keats and the Culture of Dissent (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997)

  5. thatch-eves l. 4

    'Eves' is an old spelling of 'eaves', the overhanging lower edge of a roof. The picture is of a cottage with vines trained round its thatched eaves, an English rather than Mediterranean vine. The detail joins the 'moss'd cottage-trees' (old apple trees near the cottage) to place the ode in a domestic, cultivated English landscape rather than in classical myth.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eaves, n.'; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  6. o'er-brimm'd their clammy cells l. 11

    The bees are deceived by late flowers into thinking 'warm days will never cease', a gentle irony since the poem knows they will. 'Clammy' (moist and sticky) is precise for honeycomb, and 'o'er-brimm'd' suggests overflow, the stanza's keynote and an effect enacted by the eleventh line. Vendler notes that the stanza's abundance is all process and promise ('to set budding more, / And still more'), with ripeness pressing on excess.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clammy, adj.'

  7. Autumn personified ll. 12-22

    Autumn is seen in four guises: sitting 'careless' (carefree) on a granary floor, asleep on a half-reaped furrow, a gleaner crossing a brook, and a watcher at the cider-press. The figure is never gendered explicitly and is drowsy rather than busy; harvest is suspended at each moment. Critics compare classical images of Ceres and the seasons, and painterly analogues have been proposed, though none is certain.

    Ian Jack, Keats and the Mirror of Art (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  8. winnowing wind l. 15

    To winnow is to separate the light chaff from the heavier grain by tossing it in a current of air, traditionally on a threshing or granary floor. Autumn sits where winnowing is done, and her hair is 'soft-lifted' by the same wind. The image combines labour and repose: the work is done for her by the air. Its casual ease contrasts with the hard labour harvest actually required, a contrast historicist readers have stressed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'winnow, v.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  9. hook; swath ll. 17-18

    The 'hook' is a reaping-hook or sickle; a 'swath' is the strip of corn cut in one sweep, or the line of standing crop to be cut. The reaper, 'Drows'd with the fume of poppies' (poppies grew among the corn, and their juice is opium), has fallen asleep mid-task, so that the next swath and its 'twined flowers' (bindweed, poppies, cornflowers) are spared. The suspension of the sickle has been read as a reprieve from death.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swath, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  10. like a gleaner ll. 19-20

    Gleaning, the right of the poor to collect what the reapers left, was a customary right still contested in Keats's England (a famous 1788 judgment had limited it). The figure recalls Ruth, gleaner in the fields of Boaz, whom Keats had used in the Nightingale ode. The perfectly balanced line-break ('keep / Steady') mimics the gleaner's steadiness. Some historicist critics see in the image a glance at the rural poor.

    The Bible (KJV), Ruth 1:16-22 and 2:1-23; Nicholas Roe, John Keats and the Culture of Dissent (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  11. cyder-press ... last oozings ll. 21-2

    'Cyder' is an old spelling of cider. The patience of the watcher and the slowness of the 'last oozings' stretch out time at the end of the harvest stanza. The word 'last' introduces the note of ending which the third stanza takes up; the line's long vowels and repeated 'hours by hours' enact the dragging time. Cider-making was a central part of the autumn economy in southern English counties such as Hampshire.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  12. Where are the songs of Spring? l. 23

    The question echoes the medieval and classical topos of 'ubi sunt' ('where are they [who were before us]?'), as in Villon's 'Mais ou sont les neiges d'antan?'. Keats raises the lament only to dismiss it: 'Think not of them, thou hast thy music too'. The music of autumn that follows (gnats, lambs, crickets, robin, swallows) is a deliberately humble choir, replacing the nightingale of spring.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Francois Villon, 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis' (c. 1461)

  13. barr'd clouds bloom the soft-dying day l. 25

    'Barr'd' clouds are those lying in horizontal bars or streaks across the sunset. 'Bloom' is used transitively: the clouds make the day bloom, transferring the language of flowers to light. The day is 'soft-dying', a gentle death. The rosy light on the 'stubble-plains' joins the poem to Keats's letter: the stubble plain looked 'warm', like some pictures. The stanza's sound-world is framed by this visual image of evening.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg

  14. stubble-plains l. 26

    Stubble is the short stalks left in the ground after reaping. In his letter to Reynolds of 21 September 1819 Keats praised the season and said that he had never liked stubble fields so much, 'Aye better than the chilly green of the spring', adding that a stubble plain looks warm 'in the same way that some pictures look warm'. In the same letter he associated autumn with Chatterton, whom he called the purest writer in English. The ode thus grows from an observed landscape.

    Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Hyder E. Rollins, ed., The Letters of John Keats 1814-1821, 2 vols (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1958); Wikipedia, 'To Autumn' (survey of composition and criticism, with references)

  15. wailful choir ... river sallows ll. 27-9

    'Wailful' means lamenting; the choir of gnats is the first of the autumn musicians and the most elegiac. 'Sallows' are willows, especially the low-growing kinds by rivers (OED). The swarm is 'borne aloft / Or sinking as the light wind lives or dies', a line that enacts in its rhythm the rise and fall and, in its verbs, the poem's movement between life and death.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sallow, n.1'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  16. hilly bourn l. 30

    'Bourn' (bourne) has two distinct words behind it: a stream or brook (northern 'burn'), and a boundary or limit (French borne), as in Hamlet's 'undiscover'd country from whose bourn / No traveller returns'. Keats elsewhere uses it of a domain or region. Most editors gloss it here as the hilly boundary or region from which the full-grown lambs bleat; the Hamlet echo would bring a faint suggestion of death's frontier.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bourn, n.1 and n.2'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; William Shakespeare, Hamlet III.i.79-80

  17. red-breast ... garden-croft l. 32

    A croft is a small piece of enclosed ground, often attached to a cottage. The robin ('red-breast') sings through autumn and winter, so its 'treble soft' looks ahead to the coming season. The list of singers in the last lines moves from the low (crickets in the hedge) to the high (swallows in the sky). The homely compound 'garden-croft' keeps the poem in a small, cultivated English landscape to the end.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'croft, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  18. gathering swallows l. 33

    Swallows gather in flocks in September before flying south. The final image is thus one of imminent leaving, but it is presented without lament. Critics have read the ending as an image of finality (Bloom), as an image of renewal in the natural cycle (Bate), or, retrospectively, in the light of Keats's own death seventeen months later. The poem never mentions winter.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Wikipedia, 'To Autumn' (survey of composition and criticism, with references)

  19. Politics and history st. 2

    In 1979 Jerome McGann argued that the ode turns away from the political turmoil of 1819, including the Peterloo Massacre (16 August), and offers an idealised, ahistorical England. Nicholas Roe and others have replied that Keats followed the news closely (Henry Hunt's entry into London in September) and that words like 'conspiring', and the images of gleaning and the granary, carry contemporary charge. Others, such as Paul Fry, reject McGann's position and read the poem as an encounter with death. The debate remains open.

    Jerome J. McGann, 'Keats and the Historical Method in Literary Criticism', MLN 94 (1979), 988-1032; Nicholas Roe, John Keats and the Culture of Dissent (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997); Wikipedia, 'To Autumn' (survey of composition and criticism, with references)
