Ode to a Nightingale

by John Keats 1795–1821

From Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems (1820)

Arts & Sciences

My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains
My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk,
Or emptied some dull opiate to the drains
One minute past, and Lethe-wards had sunk:
'Tis not through envy of thy happy lot,
But being too happy in thine happiness,--
That thou, light-winged Dryad of the trees,
In some melodious plot
Of beechen green, and shadows numberless,
Singest of summer in full-throated ease.

O, for a draught of vintage! that hath been
Cool'd a long age in the deep-delved earth,
Tasting of Flora and the country green,
Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth!
O for a beaker full of the warm South,
Full of the true, the blushful Hippocrene,
With beaded bubbles winking at the brim,
And purple-stained mouth;
That I might drink, and leave the world unseen,
And with thee fade away into the forest dim:

Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget
What thou among the leaves hast never known,
The weariness, the fever, and the fret
Here, where men sit and hear each other groan;
Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs,
Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies;
Where but to think is to be full of sorrow
And leaden-eyed despairs,
Where Beauty cannot keep her lustrous eyes,
Or new Love pine at them beyond to-morrow.

Away! away! for I will fly to thee,
Not charioted by Bacchus and his pards,
But on the viewless wings of Poesy,
Though the dull brain perplexes and retards:
Already with thee! tender is the night,
And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne,
Cluster'd around by all her starry Fays;
But here there is no light,
Save what from heaven is with the breezes blown
Through verdurous glooms and winding mossy ways.

I cannot see what flowers are at my feet,
Nor what soft incense hangs upon the boughs,
But, in embalmed darkness, guess each sweet
Wherewith the seasonable month endows
The grass, the thicket, and the fruit-tree wild;
White hawthorn, and the pastoral eglantine;
Fast fading violets cover'd up in leaves;
And mid-May's eldest child,
The coming musk-rose, full of dewy wine,
The murmurous haunt of flies on summer eves.

Darkling I listen; and, for many a time
I have been half in love with easeful Death,
Call'd him soft names in many a mused rhyme,
To take into the air my quiet breath;
Now more than ever seems it rich to die,
To cease upon the midnight with no pain,
While thou art pouring forth thy soul abroad
In such an ecstasy!
Still wouldst thou sing, and I have ears in vain--
To thy high requiem become a sod.

Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!
No hungry generations tread thee down;
The voice I hear this passing night was heard
In ancient days by emperor and clown:
Perhaps the self-same song that found a path
Through the sad heart of Ruth, when, sick for home,
She stood in tears amid the alien corn;
The same that oft-times hath
Charm'd magic casements, opening on the foam
Of perilous seas, in faery lands forlorn.

Forlorn! the very word is like a bell
To toll me back from thee to my sole self!
Adieu! the fancy cannot cheat so well
As she is fam'd to do, deceiving elf.
Adieu! adieu! thy plaintive anthem fades
Past the near meadows, over the still stream,
Up the hill-side; and now 'tis buried deep
In the next valley-glades:
Was it a vision, or a waking dream?
Fled is that music:--Do I wake or sleep?

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and first printing l. 1

    Keats dated the ode May 1819; it was composed at Wentworth Place, Hampstead, the house he shared with Charles Brown. Brown later recalled that a nightingale nested near the house and that Keats sat under a plum tree for two or three hours and came in with scraps of paper which Brown helped to order. Brown wrote this some years afterwards, so it is best treated as tradition; a draft on two sheets survives in the Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge. Haydon passed the poem to James Elmes, who printed it in the Annals of the Fine Arts (July 1819); it was reprinted in Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems (1820). The opening sentence is unusually abrupt for an ode: it begins not with invocation but with a symptom, a pain that is also a pleasure.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1978), text and textual notes for the poem; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Wikipedia, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (composition and reception, with references)

  2. Stanza form st. 1

    Each stanza rhymes ababcdecde: a quatrain like the opening of a Shakespearean sonnet followed by a sestet like the end of a Petrarchan one. Keats developed this ode stanza in spring 1819 from his experiments with the sonnet, and used it, with variations, in the Grecian Urn, Melancholy and Indolence odes. In the Nightingale ode the eighth line is cut to three stresses ('In some melodious plot', 'And purple-stained mouth'), producing a catch or dip before the long final lines. Vendler reads the sequence of stanzas as a carefully ordered argument rather than a free reverie, while Bate stresses the speed of composition. The pentameter is heavy with spondees and long vowels, slowing the movement to suit the speaker's drowsiness.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  3. hemlock l. 2

    Hemlock (Conium maculatum) kills by creeping paralysis, which is why Keats pairs it with 'drowsy numbness'. The classical association is with the death of Socrates in Plato's Phaedo, where the poison numbs the body from the feet upwards. Keats does not say he has drunk it but that he feels 'as though' he had: the simile sets up the poem's balance between ecstasy and the wish for oblivion, which returns openly in stanza 6. The medical exactness may owe something to Keats's training at Guy's Hospital (he qualified as an apothecary in 1816).

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Plato, Phaedo 117e-118a (trans. Benjamin Jowett, PD), Project Gutenberg; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  4. Lethe-wards l. 4

    In Virgil's underworld the souls about to be reborn drink of Lethe to lose memory of their former lives (Aeneid VI.703-15); the river is also in Milton's hell (Paradise Lost II.582-6). Keats's compound '-wards' makes it a direction of movement, a sinking towards forgetfulness that the poem will pursue in 'Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget' (l. 21). Forgetting is the poem's double wish: to escape the world's sorrow, but at the cost of consciousness itself.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Virgil, Aeneid VI.703-15, The Latin Library; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  5. too happy in thine happiness ll. 5-6

    Keats disclaims envy and offers a paradox: the pain is the effect of an overwhelming, self-forgetting share in another creature's happiness. This fits his remarks in the letters on the poet who has 'no identity' and takes part in the existence of a sparrow pecking about the gravel (letter to Bailey, 22 November 1817; to Woodhouse, 27 October 1818). Critics note the strain in the repeated 'happy ... happiness', the same insistence that becomes almost hectic in stanza 3 of the Grecian Urn.

    Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Hyder E. Rollins, ed., The Letters of John Keats 1814-1821, 2 vols (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1958); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  6. Dryad l. 7

    Dryads were the nymphs of trees in Greek myth. Calling the nightingale a 'light-winged Dryad' makes it a presence of the place rather than an individual bird, preparing for the claim in stanza 7 that it is 'not born for death'. Keats avoids the usual classical myth of the nightingale (Philomela, raped and transformed) and its conventional sorrow; his bird sings 'of summer in full-throated ease', closer to Coleridge's joyful 'merry' nightingale (1798) than to Milton's 'most melancholy' one.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; S. T. Coleridge, 'The Nightingale: A Conversation Poem' (1798)

  7. a draught of vintage l. 11

    Having compared his state to poison and narcotic, the speaker now calls for wine. 'Vintage' is wine of a good year; 'deep-delved' means dug deep. The stanza turns the drink into a synaesthetic landscape: it tastes 'of Flora and the country green, / Dance, and Provencal song, and sunburnt mirth', so that sight, sound and movement are all swallowed. Keats told his brother and sister-in-law on 19 February 1819 that claret was the only palate affair in which he was at all sensual, and the letters often relish wine. Wine is one of three ways out the poem tries (drugs, drink, poetry), each rejected in turn.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  8. Provencal song l. 14

    Provence was the home of the troubadours, the twelfth- and thirteenth-century poets of courtly love who sang in Occitan. The phrase links the wine of the south with a tradition of song and romance; Keats was drawn to Provence as an imagined land of minstrelsy (compare the 'ancient ditty ... In Provence call'd, "La belle dame sans mercy"' in The Eve of St. Agnes, st. 33). 'Sunburnt mirth' extends the scene to the harvest and vintage of the warm south.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  9. Hippocrene l. 16

    The Hippocrene was the fountain on Mount Helicon sacred to the Muses (Hesiod, Theogony 6), said to have been opened by a blow of Pegasus' hoof (Ovid, Metamorphoses V.256-63). Keats's joke is to make the spring of inspiration 'blushful' and to fill it with red wine: wine and poetic rapture are fused, and the 'true' Hippocrene is the drink that makes poets sing. The wish to 'leave the world unseen' through wine is then dropped in stanza 4 in favour of 'the viewless wings of Poesy'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Hesiod, Theogony 1-8, Perseus Digital Library; Ovid, Metamorphoses V.256-63, The Latin Library

  10. Fade far away l. 21

    Stanza 3 picks up the last word-sense of stanza 2 ('fade away'), a linking device Keats repeats at the turn into the final stanza ('Forlorn! ... forlorn'). The three verbs, 'fade', 'dissolve', 'forget', move from loss of visibility to loss of substance to loss of memory. What is to be forgotten is precisely what the bird 'hast never known': human suffering, listed in the rest of the stanza.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  11. The weariness, the fever, and the fret l. 23

    The line has a triadic, almost proverbial weight; 'fret' means worry or vexation. The stanza that follows moves through old age ('palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs'), consumption in the young, and the brevity of beauty and love. Wordsworth's phrase 'the fretful stir / Unprofitable, and the fever of the world' (Tintern Abbey, ll. 53-4) is often heard behind it. 'Where men sit and hear each other groan' recalls the hospital wards Keats knew as a medical student.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem; William Wordsworth, 'Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey' (1798), ll. 53-4

  12. youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies l. 26

    Tom Keats, whom John nursed through the autumn of 1818, died of consumption at nineteen; the wasting, pallor and fever of the disease are exactly described here. Most editors make the connection, though the line is general in form. The poem was written only five months later. Keats himself would show signs of the same illness early in 1820 and die of it in Rome in February 1821, which has given the line a retrospective poignancy it did not have in 1819.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  13. Bacchus and his pards l. 32

    Bacchus (Dionysus) is often shown riding a chariot drawn by big cats; Ovid describes tigers, lynxes and painted panthers lying round him (Metamorphoses III.668-9), and painters from Titian onwards showed his car drawn by leopards or cheetahs. 'Pards' is the old poetic word for leopards. The stanza turns from the escape-by-wine of stanza 2 to an escape by imagination: the speaker will fly not on the god's car but on 'the viewless wings of Poesy'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Ovid, Metamorphoses III.668-9, The Latin Library; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pard, n.'

  14. viewless wings of Poesy l. 33

    'Viewless' meant invisible, as in Claudio's 'imprison'd in the viewless winds' (Measure for Measure III.i). RPO's note traces the word through several late-eighteenth-century poems. 'Poesy' is the slightly archaic, elevated term for poetry or the poetic faculty. The next line concedes that 'the dull brain perplexes and retards': the reasoning intellect slows the imaginative flight, a tension between fancy and thought that returns in the final stanza's 'the fancy cannot cheat so well'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; William Shakespeare, Measure for Measure III.i, Folger Shakespeare; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'viewless, adj.'

  15. embalmed darkness l. 43

    On the surface 'embalmed' means perfumed with balm: the speaker cannot see the flowers and must 'guess each sweet' by scent. But the word's usual sense, the preparation of a dead body, darkens the stanza and anticipates the turn to death in stanza 6. RPO notes that the flower-catalogue of ll. 43-9 echoes the flower-passages of A Midsummer Night's Dream (compare Oberon's 'bank where the wild thyme blows', II.i). The 'seasonable month' is May.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'embalm, v.'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  16. eglantine; musk-rose ll. 46-9

    Eglantine strictly means the sweet-briar (Rosa rubiginosa), though poets often use it loosely for wild roses; 'pastoral' suggests both its country habitat and its frequency in pastoral verse. Shakespeare places 'musk-roses' and 'eglantine' together over Titania's bower (A Midsummer Night's Dream II.i). The catalogue moves through time as well as space: violets 'fast fading', the 'coming musk-rose' as 'mid-May's eldest child', so that a single dark May night holds the passing of the seasons. The final line's murmuring m-sounds imitate the flies.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eglantine, n.'; William Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream II.i.249-52, Folger Shakespeare

  17. Darkling l. 51

    'Darkling' is an old adverb meaning in the dark. In the invocation to light in Paradise Lost III, Milton likens his own blind composition to 'the wakeful Bird' that 'Sings darkling' in the shade (III.38-40); the bird is the nightingale. Keats reverses the roles: the bird sings and the poet, in darkness, only listens. The word also appears in King Lear ('so out went the candle, and we were left darkling', I.iv). Matthew Arnold's 'Dover Beach' (1867) would borrow it again ('a darkling plain').

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (1674), III.38-40, Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'darkling, adv. and adj.'

  18. half in love with easeful Death l. 52

    'Easeful' means bringing ease. The qualification 'half in love' is important: the attraction is real but held at a distance, and personified Death is courted with 'soft names' in poems. 'Rich to die' uses 'rich' in a sense of sumptuous, abundant fulfilment that Keats favours. The logic turns on itself in the stanza's close: if he died, the bird would still sing and he would hear nothing, 'become a sod' to its 'high requiem'. Bate and Vendler both stress that the stanza discovers the cost of the wish even as it states it.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  19. requiem; sod l. 60

    A requiem is the Mass for the dead, named from its opening words 'Requiem aeternam dona eis' (grant them eternal rest). The bird's ecstatic song is reinterpreted as a solemn funeral chant, 'high' in pitch and dignity, sung over a listener who has become 'a sod', a lump of turf, insensible. The brutal monosyllable ends the stanza's romantic death-wish: dying would not mean merging with the music but losing it.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'requiem, n.'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem

  20. immortal Bird l. 61

    Critics since Robert Bridges and H. W. Garrod have objected that the bird is no more immortal than the poet. Common defences: the 'bird' is the song itself, heard unchanged across the centuries; or the species, which is not ground down by competing generations as humans are; or the claim is consciously the speaker's imaginative idealisation, soon to be undone by 'Forlorn!'. 'No hungry generations tread thee down' contrasts human history, where each generation presses on the last, with the timeless repetition of natural song.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Wikipedia, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (composition and reception, with references)

  21. Ruth ... amid the alien corn ll. 66-7

    In the Book of Ruth, Ruth, a Moabitess, refuses to leave her widowed mother-in-law Naomi ('whither thou goest, I will go', 1:16) and goes with her to Judah, where she gleans in the fields of Boaz (2:1-23). The Bible does not say she wept or was homesick: Keats adds the tears and 'sick for home', making her an image of exile. 'Corn' in British usage means grain generally; the crop in Ruth is barley and then wheat. 'Alien' is double: the corn is foreign to her, and she is a stranger in it.

    The Bible (KJV), Ruth 1:16-22 and 2:1-23; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Ode to a Nightingale', ed. J. R. MacGillivray, line notes; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  22. magic casements ... faery lands forlorn ll. 69-70

    A casement is a window that opens on hinges. The lines summon the world of medieval and Spenserian romance: a tower window, 'charm'd' (enchanted) by the song, looking out over 'perilous seas'. Editors since Ian Jack have compared Claude Lorrain's painting The Enchanted Castle (1664), which Keats described in his verse Epistle to J. H. Reynolds (March 1818). The final word, 'forlorn', meaning lost, desolate and also long ago, becomes the hinge of the last stanza.

    Ian Jack, Keats and the Mirror of Art (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1978), text and textual notes for the poem

  23. Forlorn! the very word is like a bell l. 71

    Keats repeats the last word of stanza 7 at the start of stanza 8, as he linked 'fade' between stanzas 2 and 3. The word that described the faery lands now describes the speaker: he is the one forlorn. 'Toll' suggests a funeral bell, recalling the death-thoughts of stanza 6. 'My sole self' means my solitary, isolated self. Robert Bridges disliked the self-conscious turn on 'the very word'; others, including Vendler, see it as the moment the poem recognises its own medium, language, as both bewitching and limiting.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Wikipedia, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (composition and reception, with references); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'forlorn, adj.'

  24. the fancy ... deceiving elf ll. 73-4

    'Fancy' was in Keats's time a near-synonym for imagination, though Coleridge had distinguished it as the lesser, associative faculty. Keats personifies it as an 'elf', a deceiving sprite, female ('she is fam'd to do'). The lines concede the failure of the flight on the 'viewless wings of Poesy'. Compare Keats's own earlier poem 'Fancy' (1818), which praises fancy's power to bring pleasures from elsewhere; here that power is shown to be brief.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. XIII

  25. Was it a vision, or a waking dream? ll. 79-80

    The ode closes not with a resolution but with two questions. A 'vision' would be a true revelation; a 'waking dream' an illusion experienced while conscious. The final 'Do I wake or sleep?' returns to the drowsiness of the opening. Critics divide on whether the ending is a balanced suspension (Bate), a recognition of art's limits, or a return to reality that is itself a kind of sleep. Compare the ending of the Grecian Urn, which ends in a pronouncement rather than a question.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1983), chapter on the poem; Wikipedia, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (composition and reception, with references)
