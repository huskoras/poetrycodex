Bright Star

by John Keats 1795–1821

LoveTime & BrevityNature

Bright star, would I were stedfast as thou art—
Not in lone splendour hung aloft the night
And watching, with eternal lids apart,
Like nature’s patient, sleepless Eremite,
The moving waters at their priestlike task
Of pure ablution round earth’s human shores,
Or gazing on the new soft-fallen mask
Of snow upon the mountains and the moors—
No—yet still stedfast, still unchangeable,
Pillow’d upon my fair love’s ripening breast,
To feel for ever its soft fall and swell,
Awake for ever in a sweet unrest,
Still, still to hear her tender-taken breath,
And so live ever—or else swoon to death.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The sonnet wants the star’s steadfastness but not its loneliness — Keats would be constant only while ‘pillow’d upon my fair love’s ripening breast,’ poised forever between eternity and a single human night.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Bright star: date and manuscripts l. 1

    The sonnet has no title of Keats's own; it is known by its first words. Its date is one of the most debated in Keats studies. Colvin placed it in the last week of February 1819, soon after Keats's understanding with Fanny Brawne; Gittings argued for a first draft in April 1818, before he met her, later revised for her; Motion proposed October 1819. Charles Brown's transcript preserves what is usually taken as an earlier draft (dated to early 1819) whose wording differs from the final text; in summaries of the dating debate, the earlier version has the poet loving on to death, where the final version offers death as the alternative. Keats copied the final text into his volume of Shakespeare's Poems, opposite 'A Lover's Complaint'; Joseph Severn later associated the copy with the voyage to Italy in autumn 1820, which is why the poem was long called 'Keats's last sonnet'. It was first printed in 1838 in the Plymouth and Devonport Weekly Journal, and again in Milnes's Life, Letters, and Literary Remains (1848). Stillinger's edition is the standard guide to the texts.

    Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; 'Bright star, would I were stedfast as thou art', Wikipedia (summary of the dating debate: Colvin, Gittings, Motion); 'Keats's Last Poem', Princeton University Poetry site (2009); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  2. stedfast as thou art l. 1

    'Stedfast' is an older spelling of 'steadfast', firm and unmoving (OED). The 'bright star' is usually taken as the Pole Star, which appears fixed while the other stars wheel round it and was the mariner's guide. Editors hear Julius Caesar 3.1 (about lines 58-62), where Caesar, just before his murder, claims to be as constant as the northern star, unequalled in the firmament; Keats's speaker wishes for that fixity, but the octave immediately rejects the star's solitary, superhuman mode of constancy. The word 'stedfast' returns at l. 9, where the wish is redefined: the speaker wants the star's permanence without its isolation. Compare also Shakespeare's Sonnet 116, where love is 'the star to every wand'ring bark'.

    Shakespeare, Julius Caesar 3.1.58-62 (line numbers vary slightly between editions), Folger Shakespeare Library text; 'Keats's Last Poem', Princeton University Poetry site (2009); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'steadfast, adj.'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  3. lone splendour hung aloft l. 2

    The octave is built as a long negation: 'Not in lone splendour...'. Everything from l. 2 to l. 8 describes the kind of steadfastness the speaker does NOT want, yet the description is so lovely that the rejected alternative fills most of the poem. 'Hung aloft' makes the star a lamp or ornament suspended in the sky, and 'lone' introduces the key objection, its isolation from human warmth. Readers often remark that the poem's grammar lets Keats enjoy the sublime vision while refusing it, a characteristic doubleness in his late lyrics.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  4. with eternal lids apart l. 3

    The personified star watches with eyelids permanently open, a sleepless observer. Editors compare Keats's journal-letter to his brother Tom from the Lake District (begun 25 June 1818), where the scenery around Windermere is said to refine one's 'sensual vision into a sort of north star which can never cease to be open lidded and stedfast over the wonders of the great Power'. The letter shows the image originating in a Wordsworthian experience of landscape; in the sonnet it is turned into something the lover finally finds too cold. The parallel is sometimes used in arguments about dating the sonnet (see the note on the poem's date).

    Keats, letter to Tom Keats, 25-27 June 1818 (Keats Letters Project); '26 June 1818', John Keats daily chronology, University of Victoria; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  5. Eremite l. 4

    'Eremite' is an archaic and poetic form of 'hermit', from Late Latin eremita and Greek eremites, 'dweller in the desert' (eremos, desert, solitude). The word carries religious associations, a monk or anchorite keeping a night vigil, and so begins the run of sacred vocabulary that continues in 'priestlike task' and 'ablution'. Milton uses the form in Paradise Regained ('the glorious Eremite', of Christ in the wilderness, 1.8). 'Patient' and 'sleepless' stress endurance without rest: admirable, but inhuman.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eremite, n.'; Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'eremite'; Milton, Paradise Regained 1.8; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  6. priestlike task / pure ablution ll. 5-6

    'Ablution' (Latin abluere, to wash away) is ceremonial washing, especially a priest's ritual cleansing before or during worship. The ebb and flow of the sea, watched by the star, becomes a sacred office endlessly performed round the shores inhabited by human beings. The image dignifies nature but also keeps it at a distance: the star only gazes on the ritual, it does not take part. The religious diction prepares by contrast for the sestet, where the closeness the speaker wants is intimate and bodily. Critics note the purifying, almost baptismal suggestion, set against the human (and so fallen, mortal) shores.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ablution, n.'; Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'ablution'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem

  7. earth’s human shores l. 6

    The transferred epithet 'human' gives the shores the quality of their inhabitants. From the star's height the whole peopled world becomes a coastline being washed. The phrase anticipates the turn of the sestet, in which the speaker abandons cosmic distance for a single human body. Some readers also feel an undertone of mortality: human shores are where life ends, as in the close of 'When I have fears', where the poet stands 'on the shore / Of the wide world'.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963)

  8. the new soft-fallen mask / Of snow ll. 7-8

    The second object of the star's gaze is newly fallen snow. A 'mask' both beautifies and conceals; the landscape's features are softened into a blank covering. The epithet 'soft-fallen' anticipates the 'soft fall and swell' of the beloved's breathing in l. 11, so that the cold, white world of the octave and the warm body of the sestet are linked by sound and image. Readers have also heard a hint of a death-mask, appropriate to a sonnet that ends with 'death'. The mountains and moors may recall the Lake District and Scottish scenery of Keats's 1818 walking tour.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Keats, letter to Tom Keats, 25-27 June 1818 (Keats Letters Project)

  9. No—yet still stedfast (volta) l. 9

    Line 9 marks the volta, where the sonnet changes direction. 'No' completes the long negation begun at l. 2 ('Not in lone splendour'); 'yet' introduces the alternative: steadfast and unchangeable, but in the bed of the beloved. The poem is formally Shakespearean (three quatrains and a couplet, rhymed abab cdcd efef gg), but its argument divides like a Petrarchan sonnet into octave and sestet. The repetitions 'still... still', and later 'for ever... for ever' and 'Still, still', enact the wish to arrest time. 'Still' means both 'always' and 'motionless', a pun that keeps the star's stasis alive inside the human scene.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still, adv.'

  10. Pillow'd upon my fair love's ripening breast l. 10

    'Ripening' is a characteristically Keatsian word of fruition (compare 'To Autumn'); it implies a process still under way, so the speaker longs to be unchangeable on a body that is itself changing. The paradox is central to the poem: eternity is desired in a moment defined by time. Stillinger's apparatus records the textual variants between the early and final versions. Readers who link the poem to Fanny Brawne take 'fair love' as her; Gittings argued the poem began before Keats knew her and was later adapted.

    Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; 'Bright star, would I were stedfast as thou art', Wikipedia (summary of the dating debate: Colvin, Gittings, Motion); Robert Gittings, John Keats (London: Heinemann, 1968)

  11. its soft fall and swell l. 11

    The breast's movement recalls the 'moving waters' of ll. 5-6: the sea's tidal ebb and flow, seen from a cosmic distance, is replaced by the rhythm of breathing felt at the closest range. The line's own sounds, soft labials and the long vowel of 'swell', mimic what they describe. The image also connects with 'soft-fallen' snow in l. 7. The sestet as a whole recasts the octave's natural images in human, sensuous terms.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem

  12. sweet unrest l. 12

    The speaker wishes to be 'Awake for ever', like the sleepless star, but in a state of 'sweet unrest', erotic agitation rather than calm contemplation. The oxymoron captures the poem's central contradiction: a desire for permanence in a feeling that is by nature unstable. Keats often joins pleasure and pain in this way (compare 'aching Pleasure' in the 'Ode on Melancholy'). Christopher Ricks's study of Keats's readiness to dwell in intensely physical, potentially embarrassing states of feeling is useful context for this line.

    Christopher Ricks, Keats and Embarrassment (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1974); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  13. tender-taken breath l. 13

    Keats's compound epithet is typical of his style (compare 'soft-fallen', 'droop-headed', 'high-piléd'). It means breath taken in tenderly, softly, but its passive form also allows the sense of a breath that is tenderly 'taken' or received by the lover. The doubled 'Still, still' places the poem's stress on continuing attentiveness: the star's watching has become listening, sight has become hearing and touch. The line slows the verse before the final turn of the couplet.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem

  14. And so live ever—or else swoon to death l. 14

    The final line sets 'live ever' against 'swoon to death', and readers disagree about how far these are true alternatives. 'Swoon' (faint, lose consciousness) carries an erotic charge, and the Renaissance pun on 'die' as sexual climax is often invoked, so that death becomes the consummation of the same feeling. Others read the line more darkly, as Keats's recognition, in illness, that only death can make a moment permanent. In the earlier draft preserved in Brown's transcript the poet loves on to death, rather than weighing life against death; the revision sharpens this into an either/or. Compare the 'Ode to a Nightingale', where it seems 'rich to die' at the height of feeling.

    Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; 'Bright star, would I were stedfast as thou art', Wikipedia (summary of the dating debate: Colvin, Gittings, Motion); Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  15. The sonnet form ll. 1-14

    Keats had experimented with both Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnets and by 1818-19 preferred the Shakespearean rhyme scheme, though in a letter to Reynolds of 3 May 1819 he complained that the couplet at the end of the Shakespearean sonnet seldom has a pleasing effect. Here the rhyme scheme is Shakespearean, but the syntax overrides it: the poem is one long optative sentence ('would I were...'), the octave is a single negative clause, and the couplet is prepared by 'Still, still' rather than set apart. The final couplet (breath / death) rhymes life's sign with its end. Bate discusses Keats's handling of the sonnet in 1818-19.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem
