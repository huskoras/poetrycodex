La Belle Dame Sans Merci

by John Keats 1795–1821

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Mythology & Folklore

"O what can ail thee, knight-at-arms,
Alone and palely loitering?
The sedge has wither'd from the lake,
And no birds sing.

"O what can ail thee, knight-at-arms,
So haggard and so woe-begone?
The squirrel's granary is full,
And the harvest's done.

"I see a lily on thy brow
With anguish moist and fever-dew,
And on thy cheeks a fading rose
Fast withereth too."

"I met a lady in the meads,
Full beautiful--a faery's child,
Her hair was long, her foot was light,
And her eyes were wild.

"I made a garland for her head,
And bracelets too, and fragrant zone;
She look'd at me as she did love,
And made sweet moan.

"I set her on my pacing steed
And nothing else saw all day long,
For sidelong would she bend, and sing
A faery's song.

"She found me roots of relish sweet,
And honey wild and manna-dew,
And sure in language strange she said,
'I love thee true.'

"She took me to her elfin grot,
And there she wept, and sigh'd full sore,
And there I shut her wild wild eyes
With kisses four.

"And there she lulléd me asleep,
And there I dream'd--Ah! woe betide!
The latest dream I ever dream'd
On the cold hill's side.

"I saw pale kings and princes too,
Pale warriors, death-pale were they all;
They cried--'La belle Dame sans Merci
Hath thee in thrall!'

"I saw their starved lips in the gloam
With horrid warning gapéd wide,
And I awoke and found me here,
On the cold hill's side.

"And this is why I sojourn here
Alone and palely loitering,
Though the sedge is wither'd from the lake,
And no birds sing."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and versions title, l. 1

    Keats copied the ballad into his long journal-letter to his brother George (February-May 1819), under 21 April. A revised version was printed, signed 'Caviare', in Leigh Hunt's Indicator on 10 May 1820, beginning 'Ah, what can ail thee, wretched wight'. The archive text follows the letter version ('knight-at-arms', 'kisses four'), which most modern editors prefer. The title comes from Alain Chartier's courtly poem (1424), translated into English and long printed among Chaucer's works; Keats had already used it in The Eve of St. Agnes (st. 33).

    Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared); Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1978), text and textual notes for the poem; Richard Roos (attrib.), 'La Belle Dame sans Mercy', trans. of Alain Chartier (1424), ed. Dana M. Symons, TEAMS Middle English Texts; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  2. Ballad stanza with shortened last line l. 4

    The poem imitates the traditional ballad in its quatrains, simple diction, repetition and dialogue, but Keats shortens the fourth line drastically (here four syllables), so that each stanza stops short. The effect is of breath giving out or of a song left unfinished. The opening three stanzas are spoken by a questioner; the knight answers in the remaining nine, and his last stanza echoes the first, closing the poem in a circle.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'La Belle Dame sans Merci', ed. J. R. MacGillivray; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  3. knight-at-arms l. 1

    The phrase belongs to the vocabulary of chivalric romance. In the 1820 Indicator text Keats replaced it with 'wretched wight' ('wight' is an archaic word for a person), which Hunt's headnote presented as in the old manner; many readers have thought it weaker. The knight's state, 'Alone and palely loitering', is that of the abandoned lover of romance, enchanted and wasted.

    Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wight, n.'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1978), text and textual notes for the poem

  4. palely loitering l. 2

    'Loiter' means to linger idly. Pallor was a standard sign of the lovesick, and in medical terms of fever or consumption. The knight cannot leave the place where he was abandoned, and the phrase is repeated in the final stanza. The adverb 'palely' is unusual, making pallor a manner of action as well as a colour. The sound pattern of the line, with its liquid l's, slows the verse and suggests listless drifting rather than purposeful movement.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'loiter, v.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  5. sedge ... no birds sing ll. 3-4

    Sedge is a coarse grass-like plant growing in wet ground (OED). The withered sedge and birdless air set the season (late autumn: the squirrel's store is full and harvest done) and reflect the knight's desolation. The barren landscape also recalls the waste lands of romance, where the land suffers with an enchanted or wounded knight. The poem's season thus contrasts with the spring in which it was written.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sedge, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  6. lily ... fading rose ll. 9-12

    Lily and rose are conventional emblems of a beautiful complexion (white and red), but here they become symptoms: the white brow is moist with 'anguish' and 'fever-dew', and the rose on the cheeks is 'fading'. Keats, trained in medicine and having nursed Tom through consumption, gives the courtly emblems a clinical edge. The questioner's diagnosis completes the frame of three stanzas before the knight begins his own story.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes

  7. a faery's child l. 14

    Her long hair, light foot and 'wild' eyes mark her as not human. The figure has many antecedents: the fairy queen who carries off the hero in the ballad of Thomas the Rhymer (in Scott's Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, 1802-3), Spenser's false Duessa and other enchantresses in The Faerie Queene, and the courtly cruel mistress of Chartier's poem. The 1820 text has 'faery's'; the letter has 'fairy's'.

    Walter Scott, ed., Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802-3), 'Thomas the Rhymer'; Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene (1590), I.vii (Redcrosse and Duessa) and III.i; Renascence Editions; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  8. zone l. 19

    'Zone' in its original Greek and poetic sense means a girdle worn round the waist (OED). With the garland and bracelets, the knight decks the lady with flowers, a courtship rite. The flower girdle had erotic associations in classical poetry (Aphrodite's embroidered girdle in Iliad XIV). The rhyme of 'zone' with 'moan' links the gift to the lady's response. The detail, like the garland and bracelets, belongs to a world of romance courtship in which the lover adorns his lady, reversing later in the poem when she feeds and lulls him.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'zone, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  9. as she did love ... sweet moan ll. 19-20

    'She look'd at me as she did love' can mean 'as one who loved' or 'as if she loved'. The ambiguity runs through the poem: the lady's love, like her 'language strange' in which 'sure' she said 'I love thee true', is reported entirely through the knight's interpretation. 'Moan' here is a sound of pleasure or longing, not grief. Critics divide on whether she is a deliberate destroyer or as much a victim of the meeting as he.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  10. pacing steed l. 21

    A 'pacing' horse moves at a gentle walk. The knight's total absorption ('nothing else saw all day long') shows enchantment. In the 1820 text Keats exchanged the order of the garland and steed stanzas. Her singing 'sidelong' combines flirtation and the oblique look of an enchantress. The lady's song is the second of the poem's 'faery' references, and the knight's surrender of his sight to her anticipates his later sleep and dream. The tableau of the lady carried on a knight's horse is common in romance and in Spenser.

    Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  11. honey wild and manna-dew l. 26

    Manna is the food from heaven that fell with the dew in the wilderness (Exodus 16:13-15); 'honey wild' recalls John the Baptist, who ate 'locusts and wild honey' (Matthew 3:4). Coleridge's 'Kubla Khan' ends with 'honey-dew' and 'the milk of Paradise'. The lady's gifts are otherworldly, sweet and possibly dangerous food, like the fairy fruit that keeps mortals in fairyland.

    The Bible (KJV), Exodus 16:13-15; The Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:4; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  12. language strange ll. 27-8

    'Sure' signals certainty and, at once, its absence: he cannot have understood her literally. The reported speech is the only thing she 'says' in the poem. The motif of a love declared across a gulf of language has been read as a figure for the mysteries of desire, and also for the reader's relation to the poem's own enigmatic meanings.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1973; 3rd ed. 1988), notes to the poem

  13. elfin grot l. 29

    'Grot' is the poetic form of grotto, a cave. The fairy cave or underground hall recalls ballads in which mortals are taken under the hill into fairyland. There the lady weeps and sighs 'full sore', an unexplained grief that complicates any simple reading of her as a heartless destroyer. The setting also recalls the cave of the enchantress in Spenser and the Venus-mountain of the Tannhauser legend, where knights are lost to love.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grot, n.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  14. kisses four l. 32

    Immediately after copying the poem, Keats added a playful comment explaining why the knight gives four kisses: he wished to restrain the headlong impetuosity of his Muse, who would gladly have said 'score' without hurting the rhyme; two kisses each for her 'wild wild' eyes. The jest shows Keats's ironic distance from the poem's mood. The 1820 text replaced the line with 'So kiss'd to sleep'.

    Sidney Colvin, ed., Letters of John Keats to His Family and Friends (1891; PD), Project Gutenberg; Hyder E. Rollins, ed., The Letters of John Keats 1814-1821, 2 vols (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1958); Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared)

  15. pale kings and princes ll. 37-40

    The dream reveals the knight as one in a series of men destroyed by the same enchantress; kings, princes and warriors suggest that rank and strength are no protection. The pallor of the victims matches his own 'palely loitering'. A 2019 proposal by Richard Marggraf Turley and Jennifer Squire links the ballad's knight and lady to the alabaster effigies of Richard FitzAlan and Eleanor of Lancaster in Chichester Cathedral, which Keats probably saw in January 1819; it concerns the knight and lady rather than the kings, and is suggestive rather than established.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared)

  16. thrall l. 40

    A thrall is a slave or bondman, and 'in thrall' means held in servitude (OED). The phrase belongs to the language of courtly love, where the lover is the lady's servant. The title phrase appears only here, spoken by the dead, in French: 'the beautiful lady without mercy', mercy (merci) being in courtly terms the lady's granting of favour to the lover.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'thrall, n.'; Richard Roos (attrib.), 'La Belle Dame sans Mercy', trans. of Alain Chartier (1424), ed. Dana M. Symons, TEAMS Middle English Texts; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  17. gloam l. 41

    'Gloam' is a rare word for twilight formed from 'gloaming' (OED); Keats's use is among the earliest literary instances, and it may be his own back-formation. The starved, gaping lips of the dead warriors seen in half-light are the poem's most horrific image: 'starved' suggests both hunger and, in an older sense, dying of cold. The phrase 'horrid warning' uses 'horrid' in its older, stronger sense of bristling or terrifying. After this vision the knight wakes alone on the cold hillside, the place where the questioner finds him at the start.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gloam, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'starve, v.'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  18. circular ending ll. 45-8

    'Sojourn' means to stay temporarily, ironically, since he seems unable to leave. The knight's answer repeats the questioner's words almost exactly, so the poem closes where it began, an endless loop without explanation of why the lady acted as she did. Readings range widely: biographical (Keats's love for Fanny Brawne and fear of losing independence), the femme fatale of Romantic tradition, a parable of the poet seduced by imagination, or a reflection on illness and death.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats, Longman Annotated English Poets (London: Longman, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Wikipedia, 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (1819 and 1820 texts compared); Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard UP, 1963), chapters on the 1819 odes
