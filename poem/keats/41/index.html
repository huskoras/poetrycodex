Nature

On the Grasshopper and Cricket

by John Keats 1795–1821

From Poems (1817)

Nature

The poetry of earth is never dead:
When all the birds are faint with the hot sun,
And hide in cooling trees, a voice will run
From hedge to hedge about the new-mown mead;
That is the Grasshopper's--he takes the lead
In summer luxury,--he has never done
With his delights; for when tired out with fun
He rests at ease beneath some pleasant weed.
The poetry of earth is ceasing never:
On a lone winter evening, when the frost
Has wrought a silence, from the stove there shrills
The Cricket's song, in warmth increasing ever,
And seems to one in drowsiness half lost,
The Grasshopper's among some grassy hills.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: a sonnet contest with Leigh Hunt l. 1; title

    Hunt liked to set friends to write sonnets on a given subject against the clock. On 30 December 1816, at his cottage in the Vale of Health, Hampstead, he proposed 'the grasshopper and the cricket', and he and Keats each wrote a sonnet. Charles Cowden Clarke, who was present, later recalled that he could not say exactly how long the trial took, and that Keats finished first; the often repeated 'fifteen minutes' rests on later retellings. Keats included the sonnet in Poems (March 1817), and Hunt printed both poems in The Examiner (21 September 1817). The opening line is one of Keats's most quoted, an assertion that nature's music is continuous through the year.

    Charles and Mary Cowden Clarke, Recollections of Writers (1878), chapter on Keats; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard, 1978), notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard, 1963), ch. 5 (Hunt circle and the sonnet contests)

  2. Grasshopper and cricket: the tradition l. 5

    Keats's 'Grasshopper' descends from the Greek tettix, the cicada of Greek poetry, celebrated in the Anacreontic ode 'To the Cicada' as a tiny singer drunk on dew, beloved of the Muses. English versions rendered the insect as 'grasshopper', notably Abraham Cowley's Anacreontiques (1656); Richard Lovelace's 'The Grasshopper' (1649) also uses the creature as an emblem of summer pleasure. Hunt's competing sonnet ('Green little vaulter in the sunny grass') pairs grasshopper and cricket as summer and winter singers, much as Keats does. Aesop's fable of the grasshopper and the ant lies further in the background, though Keats ignores its moral of thrift.

    Anacreontea 34 ('To the Cicada'); Abraham Cowley, 'The Grasshopper', in Anacreontiques (1656); Leigh Hunt, 'To the Grasshopper and the Cricket' (written 30 Dec. 1816; printed in The Examiner, 21 Sept. 1817, and in Foliage, 1818); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  3. Midsummer silence ll. 2-3

    The first quatrain sets up the logic of the whole sonnet: whenever one voice of nature fails, another takes over. 'Faint' means weak or languid with heat. 'Cooling trees' is a transferred epithet, the trees cooling the birds that hide in them. The image also reflects Keats's settled preference for rich midsummer scenes, which he develops in the Odes of 1819.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard, 1978), notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  4. Mead l. 4

    'Mead' (Old English mæd) is the old and poetic form of 'meadow', common in pastoral verse. A 'new-mown' meadow places the scene at haymaking time in early summer, and the voice that 'will run / From hedge to hedge' suggests the grasshopper's chirr moving along the field's borders. The enjambment across lines 3-4 lets the sound 'run' over the line ending.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mead'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  5. Luxury l. 6

    In the Hunt circle and in Keats's early poetry 'luxury' means delighted sensuous indulgence, often in nature or poetry; the word recurs in the 1817 volume (e.g. 'Sleep and Poetry', 'I stood tip-toe'). Older senses of the word included lust or vicious self-indulgence, and hostile reviewers of the 'Cockney School' (notably Blackwood's from 1817) mocked Keats and Hunt for exactly this kind of soft, pleasurable diction. Here the grasshopper is a little master of summer pleasure who 'has never done / With his delights', and even rests 'at ease beneath some pleasant weed'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'luxury'; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard, 1963), ch. 5 (Hunt circle and the sonnet contests); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  6. Weed l. 8

    'Weed' could mean any herb or small plant, not only an unwanted one. The phrase 'pleasant weed' is deliberately casual and comfortable, closing the octave on a note of ease. Some readers have found the octave's diction ('fun', 'pleasant') too slack, an example of Huntian informality; others value it as playful and fitting to its subject. The casual tone also suits the occasion, a light-hearted contest among friends.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weed'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard, 1978), notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  7. The volta (line 9) l. 9

    The sonnet is Petrarchan: an octave rhymed abbaabba and a sestet rhymed cdecde. Keats marks the turn by restating line 1 in inverted form ('is never dead' becomes 'is ceasing never'), so the sestet repeats the octave's argument in a new season. The inversion supplies the rhyme with 'ever' (l. 12) and gives the line a falling, feminine ending. The repetition binds the two halves together, matching the poem's claim that nature's song simply passes from one singer to another.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard, 1978), notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  8. Frost's silence ll. 10-11

    'Wrought' is the old past participle of 'work', 'made, fashioned'. Frost is imagined as an artisan who has created silence, an image that Coleridge's 'Frost at Midnight' (1798), with its 'secret ministry of frost', may anticipate. The 'lone winter evening' sets a domestic scene indoors, 'from the stove' (the hearth or fireplace). The sestet thus mirrors the octave: in summer heat silenced the birds, in winter frost silences the fields, and in each case an insect keeps singing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wrought'; S. T. Coleridge, 'Frost at Midnight' (1798), l. 1; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  9. The cricket on the hearth l. 12

    The house cricket (Acheta domesticus) favours the warmth of fires and bakehouses and sings through the winter; it was proverbially a sign of good luck on the hearth (Milton's 'Il Penseroso' mentions 'the cricket on the hearth', l. 82, later Dickens's title). Keats's cricket 'shrills' from the stove, and its song is 'in warmth increasing ever', growing louder as the room grows warmer. The phrase ties the song to the heat that the grasshopper enjoyed in summer.

    John Milton, 'Il Penseroso' (1645), l. 82; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'

  10. Drowsy listener and imagination ll. 13-14

    The closing couplet of the sestet introduces a human listener for the first time. In a state between waking and sleep, a favourite Keatsian threshold (compare the 'Ode to a Nightingale'), the hearer mistakes the cricket for the grasshopper on 'grassy hills'. The insects' songs become one continuous 'poetry of earth', and the mind's drowsy confusion is what makes the continuity felt. Critics often see here an early sign of Keats's interest in the imagination's power to recover absent things, though the poem treats the idea lightly.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to 'On the Grasshopper and Cricket'; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard, 1963), ch. 5 (Hunt circle and the sonnet contests)
