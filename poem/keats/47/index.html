Death & Dying

The Terror of Death

by John Keats 1795–1821

From The Golden Treasury, ed. F. T. Palgrave (1861)

Death & Dying

When I have fears that I may cease to be
Before my pen has glean'd my teeming brain,
Before high-piléd books, in charact'ry,
Hold like rich garners the full-ripen'd grain;

When I behold, upon the night's starr'd face,
Huge cloudy symbols of a high romance,
And think that I may never live to trace
Their shadows, with the magic hand of chance;

And when I feel, fair Creature of an hour!
That I shall never look upon thee more,
Never have relish in the fairy power
Of unreflecting love--then on the shore

Of the wide world I stand alone, and think,
Till Love and Fame to nothingness do sink.

Study notes by the editors

  1. When I have fears: date, title, form l. 1

    Keats copied this sonnet into a letter to John Hamilton Reynolds dated 31 January 1818 (the letter survives in Richard Woodhouse's transcript). It was not printed in Keats's lifetime; Milnes published it in Life, Letters, and Literary Remains (1848). The title in this archive, 'The Terror of Death', was supplied by F. T. Palgrave in The Golden Treasury (1861); Keats left it untitled and it is usually known by its first line. It is one of his earliest sonnets in the Shakespearean form, written during an intense period of reading Shakespeare's sonnets and plays. Keats was twenty-two; his fear of early death predates his own illness but follows the deaths of his parents and his brother Tom's decline.

    Keats Letters Project, January 1818 (letter to J. H. Reynolds, 31 January 1818, from Woodhouse's transcript); 'When I Have Fears', Wikipedia (composition and publication); Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  2. cease to be l. 1

    'Cease to be' is starker than 'die': it suggests annihilation rather than passage to an afterlife, and the poem offers no consolation of immortality. The phrase is set against the ambitions listed in the quatrains (books written, romances traced, love enjoyed), each of which death would cut short. The final word of the poem, 'nothingness', answers this opening. The same words recur a few months later in Keats's letter to Tom of June 1818, where the 'north star' is one that can 'never cease to be open lidded'.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); Keats, letter to Tom Keats, 25-27 June 1818 (Keats Letters Project)

  3. glean'd my teeming brain l. 2

    To 'glean' is to gather the ears of grain left after the reapers, and more generally to collect carefully (the gleaning of Ruth in Ruth 2 is the best-known biblical instance). The 'teeming' brain is overfull, swarming with ideas, like a field heavy with crop. The conceit runs through the quatrain: the pen gleans, books are 'garners' (granaries), and the thoughts are 'full-ripen'd grain'. Keats inverts the usual order, since gleaning comes after the harvest; the image suggests a poet who fears he will not have time even to gather the leavings of his own abundance.

    Bible (KJV), Ruth 2:2-3; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'glean, v.'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  4. high-piléd l. 3

    The acute accent tells the reader to pronounce the -ed ending as a separate syllable, keeping the line a regular iambic pentameter ('Before high-pi-led books, in char-act'ry'). Compound epithets like this are frequent in Keats and recall Elizabethan diction. The image of piled-up volumes is both an ideal of copious achievement and, implicitly, a measure of the time such production would need.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem

  5. charact'ry l. 3

    'Charactery' means expression of thought by characters or symbols, i.e. writing; the elision shows it is pronounced in three syllables. The word is rare and Shakespearean: Brutus speaks of 'All the charactery of my sad brows' (Julius Caesar 2.1) and the fairies 'use flowers for their charactery' (Merry Wives of Windsor 5.5). Keats was reading Shakespeare intensively in early 1818, and the word is one sign of that reading in the sonnet. Here it refers to the printed letters in which the brain's harvest would be stored.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'charactery, n.'; Shakespeare, Julius Caesar 2.1; The Merry Wives of Windsor 5.5, Folger Shakespeare Library text; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  6. rich garners l. 4

    A 'garner' is a storehouse for grain (from Latin granarium via Old French). The simile completes the harvest conceit of the quatrain: the books are full barns, the poems 'full-ripen'd grain'. The figure has biblical resonance (the wheat gathered into the garner in Matthew 3:12) and suggests both abundance and preservation against time. The quatrain's single, unbroken sentence imitates the fullness it describes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'garner, n.'; Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:12; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  7. Huge cloudy symbols of a high romance ll. 5-6

    In the second quatrain the source of poetry is not the poet's brain but the world: the starry night ('the night's starr'd face') presents shapes that look like symbols of some grand romance. 'Cloudy' means both literally made of clouds and obscure, not yet clearly formed. The poet's task would be to 'trace / Their shadows', to copy or outline them. Critics connect the lines with Keats's ambition, after Endymion, to write a larger romance or epic (he would begin Hyperion later in 1818).

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  8. high romance l. 6

    'Romance' here names the genre of chivalric and marvellous narrative, the tradition of Spenser's Faerie Queene which first moved Keats to write, and which he was then reassessing; in the same weeks he wrote the sonnet 'On Sitting Down to Read King Lear Once Again', bidding farewell to 'golden-tongued Romance' in favour of tragedy. 'High' suggests elevated subject and style. The phrase thus records Keats's sense of large works still unwritten.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem

  9. with the magic hand of chance l. 8

    Editors read 'the magic hand of chance' as the unplanned, inspired spontaneity Keats prized in composition (compare his remark in a letter of February 1818 that poetry should come 'as naturally as the Leaves to a tree'). On this reading the poet's hand, guided by chance, works magic. Others take 'chance' as fortune or fate: only luck would allow him time and occasion to complete such work. The ambiguity suits the poem's double fear, of losing both the inspiration and the time it needs.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); Keats, letter to John Taylor, 27 February 1818, in The Letters of John Keats, ed. H. E. Rollins (Harvard, 1958)

  10. fair Creature of an hour l. 9

    The apostrophe addresses a beloved who is both beautiful and transient, 'of an hour' meaning briefly known as well as short-lived. Richard Woodhouse reported that Keats was thinking of a woman he had seen at Vauxhall Gardens (accounts of the poem give 1814 as the date). She is therefore not Fanny Brawne, whom Keats met in autumn 1818. The identification rests only on Woodhouse's report and the woman is unknown; the line's force lies in its generality, love as fleeting encounter.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; 'When I Have Fears', Wikipedia (composition and publication)

  11. the fairy power l. 11

    'Fairy' here means magical, enchanting, as in Spenser and in Keats's own romances (compare the 'faery' women of 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' and 'Lamia'). It suggests that love's power works like a spell; it may also imply that the spell is unreal or impermanent, as fairy gold vanishes. 'Relish' (taste, enjoyment) gives the experience a sensuous, gustatory quality typical of Keats.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fairy, adj.' and 'relish, n.'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  12. unreflecting love l. 12

    'Unreflecting' means without thought or self-examination. Most readers take it as positive: the poet longs for a love that is immediate and instinctive, free of the brooding that characterizes this sonnet itself. Others note the irony that the poem is entirely reflection, and that the speaker's thoughtfulness is exactly what separates him from such love. The line belongs to Keats's ambivalence, in 1817-18, between sensation and thought (compare 'O for a Life of Sensations rather than of Thoughts', letter to Bailey, 22 November 1817).

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Keats, letter to Benjamin Bailey, 22 November 1817, in Letters, ed. Rollins

  13. then on the shore / Of the wide world ll. 12-13

    Three 'when' clauses (ll. 1, 5, 9) build suspense; the main clause arrives only with 'then' near the end of l. 12, so the volta does not wait for the couplet. The enjambment 'on the shore / Of the wide world' carries the sense across the quatrain-couplet boundary, which Keats later judged an awkward feature of the Shakespearean sonnet. The image of standing alone on a shore suggests the edge of life, facing the sea of eternity, and echoes the solitary meditation of Shakespeare's Sonnet 29 ('I all alone beweep my outcast state').

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); Shakespeare, Sonnets 29, 64, Folger Shakespeare Library text

  14. When... When... And when (structure) ll. 1, 5, 9

    Each quatrain opens with a temporal clause ('When I have fears', 'When I behold', 'And when I feel'), building one sentence that resolves only at the turn. Shakespeare uses the same construction in Sonnets 12 ('When I do count the clock'), 15 ('When I consider every thing that grows') and 64 ('When I have seen by Time's fell hand defaced'), all meditations on time and loss; Keats's opening recalls Sonnet 64 (and, in its images of time and the sea, Sonnet 60). The three clauses move from fame (writing), through imagination (romance), to love, the order that is reversed in the closing 'Love and Fame'.

    Shakespeare, Sonnets 12, 15, 64, Folger Shakespeare Library text; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem

  15. Love and Fame l. 14

    The couplet names the desires the quatrains have described: love and poetic fame. Editors compare Milton's 'Lycidas' (1637), whose speaker, mourning a young poet, calls fame 'the spur' that makes the clear spirit scorn delights, only to have it cut short by 'the blind Fury with th'abhorred shears'. Keats returned to the subject in two sonnets 'On Fame' (1819). Here both ambitions are dissolved by contemplation of mortality, not achieved or defended.

    Milton, Lycidas (1637), ll. 70-76; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963)

  16. to nothingness do sink l. 14

    The last line describes a change in perspective: by thinking alone at the edge of the world, the speaker sees love and fame shrink until they seem as nothing. Readers disagree whether this is consolation (a stoic release from anxious desire) or desolation (the loss of all motives). The verb 'sink' completes the shore image, as if the ambitions drown. The ending answers 'cease to be' in l. 1, turning the fear of personal annihilation into a perception of the nothingness of worldly goals.

    Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem
