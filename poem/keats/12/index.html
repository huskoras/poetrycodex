The Mind

Ode on Melancholy

by John Keats 1795–1821

From Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems (1820)

The Mind

No, no, go not to Lethe, neither twist
Wolf's-bane, tight-rooted, for its poisonous wine;
Nor suffer thy pale forehead to be kiss'd
By nightshade, ruby grape of Proserpine;
Make not your rosary of yew-berries,
Nor let the beetle, nor the death-moth be
Your mournful Psyche, nor the downy owl
A partner in your sorrow's mysteries;
For shade to shade will come too drowsily,
And drown the wakeful anguish of the soul.

But when the melancholy fit shall fall
Sudden from heaven like a weeping cloud,
That fosters the droop-headed flowers all,
And hides the green hill in an April shroud;
Then glut thy sorrow on a morning rose,
Or on the rainbow of the salt sand-wave,
Or on the wealth of globed peonies;
Or if thy mistress some rich anger shows,
Emprison her soft hand, and let her rave,
And feed deep, deep upon her peerless eyes.

She dwells with Beauty--Beauty that must die;
And Joy, whose hand is ever at his lips
Bidding adieu; and aching Pleasure nigh,
Turning to poison while the bee-mouth sips:
Ay, in the very temple of Delight
Veil'd Melancholy has her sovran shrine,
Though seen of none save him whose strenuous tongue
Can burst Joy's grape against his palate fine;
His soul shall taste the sadness of her might,
And be among her cloudy trophies hung.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The ode: date, cancelled opening, Burton st. 1

    The ode belongs to the group of odes Keats wrote in spring 1819 and was printed in Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems (1820). The manuscript shows a cancelled opening stanza, beginning 'Though you should build a bark of dead men's bones', which parodied gothic quests for melancholy; its removal leaves the poem starting abruptly with a doubled 'No'. Keats owned and annotated Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy (1621), which supplied much traditional lore about melancholy. The poem's argument is that true melancholy is found not in images of death but in the intense experience of beauty and joy, which carry their own transience.

    Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes on this poem; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; 'Ode on Melancholy', Wikipedia (composition, cancelled stanza, critical survey); Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621; Project Gutenberg text); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  2. Lethe l. 1

    In Greek and Roman myth Lethe (Greek 'forgetfulness') is a river of Hades; the souls of the dead drink from it to forget their former lives, as in Virgil, Aeneid 6, where souls waiting to be reborn drink the waters of 'long oblivion'. The speaker forbids the melancholic to seek oblivion, because forgetfulness would deaden the feeling melancholy depends on. Keats had used Lethe in the opening of the 'Ode to a Nightingale' ('Lethe-wards had sunk'), written in the same spring.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.703-15 (Perseus Digital Library); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Lemprière, Classical Dictionary (1788), s.v. 'Lethe'

  3. Wolf's-bane l. 2

    Wolf's-bane is aconite or monkshood (Aconitum), whose roots are highly toxic; the name comes from its use in poisoning wolves. In Ovid (Metamorphoses 7) aconite springs from the foam of Cerberus' jaws, and Medea uses it to poison Theseus, so it carries classical associations with the underworld. 'Tight-rooted' stresses how firmly the plant grips the ground, picturing a deliberate and laborious gathering of poison. The poisonous 'wine' anticipates the grape imagery of ll. 4 and 28.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.406-19 (trans. Brookes More, Perseus); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wolf's-bane, n.'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  4. nightshade, ruby grape of Proserpine l. 4

    Deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna) bears poisonous berries; Keats calls it the 'ruby grape' of Proserpine (Greek Persephone), who was carried off by Pluto to be queen of the underworld and was bound there because she ate pomegranate seeds (Ovid, Metamorphoses 5). The metaphor makes the poison a seductive fruit offered by death's queen; to be 'kiss'd' on the forehead by it is to be drugged towards death. Belladonna's name ('beautiful lady') and its botanical genus named after the Fate Atropos may also lie behind the image of a deadly beauty. The grape returns transformed as 'Joy's grape' in l. 28.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.385-571 (Proserpina; trans. Brookes More, Perseus); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  5. rosary of yew-berries l. 5

    A rosary is a string of beads used to count prayers, especially in Catholic devotion. The yew, long planted in churchyards, is an emblem of death and mourning, and its seeds and foliage are poisonous. A rosary of yew-berries would turn melancholy into a morbid religious ritual, praying on death. Keats's catalogue rejects this conventional 'graveyard' apparatus of eighteenth-century melancholy poetry (Young, Blair, Gray's 'Elegy').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rosary, n.' and 'yew, n.'; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  6. the beetle, nor the death-moth l. 6

    The 'death-moth' is the death's-head hawk-moth, whose thorax bears a mark like a human skull and which was regarded as an omen of death. The beetle has been read as the Egyptian scarab, placed in tombs as a symbol of the soul's rebirth, or as the death-watch beetle, whose ticking in old timbers was supposed to foretell a death. Either way, these insects are offered as false emblems of the soul; see the note on 'Psyche'.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'death's-head moth' and 'death-watch'

  7. mournful Psyche l. 7

    In Greek, psyche means breath, soul and also butterfly, and ancient art represented the soul as a butterfly leaving the body. Psyche is also the heroine of the myth in Apuleius' Golden Ass, loved by Cupid and finally made immortal; Keats had just written his 'Ode to Psyche' (April 1819). The line warns against letting a beetle or a death's-head moth stand in for one's soul: to do so would be to make death the image of the self.

    Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'psyche'; Apuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass), books 4-6; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  8. the downy owl l. 7

    The owl's nocturnal cry was a conventional omen of death (compare Macbeth 2.2) and it had become a stock prop of melancholy verse, as in Gray's 'Elegy' ('the moping owl'). 'Downy' (soft-feathered) adds a sleepy softness that matches the warning that follows: these comforts lull rather than sharpen sorrow. 'A partner in your sorrow's mysteries' turns melancholy into a secret religious cult, continuing the rosary image.

    Thomas Gray, Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), l. 10; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Gray, Collins and Goldsmith (Longman, 1969)

  9. For shade to shade will come too drowsily ll. 9-10

    This is the reason for all the prohibitions. 'Shade to shade' may mean the gloom of these death-images joining the gloom of the melancholic mind, or the soul (a 'shade' in classical idiom) coming to the realm of shades, death. Either way the result is drowsiness, which would 'drown' the 'wakeful anguish' that the speaker values. The paradox, that anguish should be preserved because it is a form of intense consciousness, governs the whole ode. Vendler stresses the poem's preference for heightened, not numbed, sensation.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963)

  10. the melancholy fit l. 11

    In humoral medicine melancholy was caused by an excess of black bile (Greek melaina chole), and a 'fit' was a sudden onset or paroxysm of the condition. Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy, which Keats read closely, treats melancholy both as a disease and as a disposition. Keats imagines the fit descending 'Sudden from heaven', like weather, not something one seeks out with poisons and charms.

    Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621; Project Gutenberg text); Online Etymology Dictionary, s.v. 'melancholy'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'melancholy, n.' and 'fit, n.2'

  11. weeping cloud / April shroud ll. 12-14

    The simile is double-edged. The 'weeping cloud' fosters the drooping flowers, so sorrow is fertile, part of the cycle of growth; yet it also hides the green hill in a 'shroud', the cloth in which the dead are wrapped. April showers are brief and life-giving, so the image suggests that melancholy is natural and seasonal rather than morbid. The 'droop-headed' flowers are both refreshed and bowed by the rain.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  12. glut thy sorrow on a morning rose l. 15

    'Glut' means to feed to excess, to gorge, and introduces the ode's language of eating and tasting. The objects on which sorrow should feast are beautiful and short-lived: a morning rose, a rainbow in the spray of a breaking wave, a full peony. The advice is to intensify melancholy by attending fully to beauty in the knowledge that it will vanish. Keats's sensuous excess here risks, and embraces, a kind of indulgence.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'glut, v.'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  13. the rainbow of the salt sand-wave l. 16

    A compound image of extreme transience: the colours appear in the spray only as the wave breaks, and vanish at once. 'Sand-wave' suggests a wave churned with sand at the shoreline. The rainbow, a traditional sign of promise and beauty (Genesis 9:13), is here valued precisely for its brevity. 'Salt' adds a sharp, tasting sensation, continuing the ode's appeal to the palate, and the sea setting recalls the shores and waters of Keats's other 1819 poems. Each object in the series is a beauty that exists only in the moment of perception.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; John Barnard, ed., John Keats: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 3rd ed. 1988), notes on this poem; Bible (KJV), Genesis 9:13

  14. the wealth of globed peonies l. 17

    Peonies form heavy spherical flower-heads ('globed') that drop their petals soon after full bloom. 'Wealth' stresses their lavish fullness. The roundness anticipates the bursting grape of l. 28, and some critics have read sensual, even sexual suggestion into the ode's imagery of full, ripe forms. The run of three objects (rose, rainbow, peonies) moves from a single flower to a light-effect to massed abundance.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; 'Ode on Melancholy', Wikipedia (composition, cancelled stanza, critical survey); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  15. if thy mistress some rich anger shows l. 18

    The ode turns from nature to a woman. Her anger is 'rich' because it heightens her beauty and intensity; the lover is told to 'Emprison her soft hand' (hold it captive) and let her 'rave'. Modern readers often find the lines troubling, since the woman becomes an object of aesthetic consumption whose feelings matter only as spectacle. Others read the stanza as dramatizing the lover's absorption rather than endorsing it.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  16. feed deep, deep upon her peerless eyes l. 20

    The verb 'feed' continues 'glut' and anticipates the tasting of Joy's grape. The doubled 'deep, deep' slows the line and imitates prolonged absorption. 'Peerless' (without equal) echoes Renaissance love poetry. The stanza ends in a moment of total, almost devouring attention, which the third stanza will reveal as itself the dwelling place of Melancholy. The eyes are the traditional seat of the beloved's power in Petrarchan verse, but Keats makes the lover's gaze a kind of nourishment, consistent with his habit of rendering every sense through taste.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  17. She dwells with Beauty l. 21

    Grammatically 'She' follows the mistress of stanza 2, who lives with a beauty that must die. But the stanza goes on to describe Melancholy's shrine, and many editors and critics read 'She' as Melancholy, personified as a goddess who lives alongside Beauty, Joy and Pleasure. Many readers take the slippage to be deliberate: the beloved and melancholy become indistinguishable, since to love mortal beauty is already to dwell with sadness. The personified Joy, 'Bidding adieu' with hand at lips, and 'aching Pleasure' are allegorical figures in a classical or Spenserian manner.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Harold Bloom, The Visionary Company (1961; rev. ed. Cornell University Press, 1971), chapter on Keats

  18. Joy, whose hand is ever at his lips / Bidding adieu ll. 22-23

    The gesture is a kiss of farewell. The allegory compresses a philosophical point, that joy is inherently fleeting, into a single frozen pose, much like the figures on the Grecian urn. 'Aching Pleasure' in the next line combines pain and delight; Pleasure turns 'to poison' even as the bee sips the nectar, recalling the poisons of stanza 1, now found inside delight itself.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  19. while the bee-mouth sips l. 24

    The compound 'bee-mouth' is a striking Keatsian coinage. The bee was a traditional image of gathering sweetness, but here the sweetness becomes poison as it is consumed. The ancient belief in poisonous honey (the 'mad honey' of the Black Sea region) may lie behind the idea; the main point is the simultaneity of pleasure and its loss. The line prepares for the climactic tasting of 'Joy's grape'.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy

  20. Veil'd Melancholy has her sovran shrine ll. 25-26

    'Sovran' is an archaic, Miltonic spelling of 'sovereign', meaning supreme. The architectural allegory places the goddess Melancholy within the temple of Delight: sorrow is not opposed to joy but enshrined at its centre. She is 'Veil'd', hidden like a mystery-cult deity, visible only to the initiated (continuing 'sorrow's mysteries' from stanza 1). Burton describes melancholy as close neighbour to joy, and Keats's image gives this commonplace a religious, ritual form.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sovereign / sovran, adj.'; Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621; Project Gutenberg text); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  21. burst Joy's grape against his palate fine l. 28

    The initiate is one 'whose strenuous tongue / Can burst Joy's grape against his palate fine'. The grape recalls Proserpine's poisonous berry in stanza 1, now become the fruit of joy. 'Strenuous' implies effort and vigour; 'palate fine' a discriminating taste, both literal and aesthetic. The image says that only maximum intensity of pleasure reveals its inseparable sadness. The phrase 'burst Joy's grape' has attracted detailed critical analysis (Daniel Brass is cited in the Wikipedia survey of the poem).

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; 'Ode on Melancholy', Wikipedia (composition, cancelled stanza, critical survey); The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem

  22. among her cloudy trophies hung l. 30

    In Greek and Roman practice, victors hung captured arms and spoils (trophies) in temples as offerings to a deity. The soul that has tasted joy fully becomes one of Melancholy's spoils, defeated and displayed. 'Cloudy' recalls the 'weeping cloud' of stanza 2 and suggests the trophies are shadowy, insubstantial. The ending is ambiguous: to be hung among her trophies is to be conquered, yet also to be consecrated in her shrine, the privilege of those capable of the deepest feeling.

    The Poems of John Keats, ed. Miriam Allott (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), note on this poem; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; Lemprière, Classical Dictionary (1788), s.v. 'Tropaeum'

  23. The ode stanza st. 1-3

    Keats built the stanza of the 1819 odes out of his experience with the sonnet: an opening Shakespearean quatrain (abab) followed by a Petrarchan-style sestet. In this ode stanzas 1 and 2 rhyme ababcdecde; stanza 3 varies the sestet to cdedce. Most lines are iambic pentameter, though the lines are indented to mark the rhyme pattern, as the archive text preserves. The compact form suits the poem's argument, which moves from prohibition (st. 1) to prescription (st. 2) to allegorical revelation (st. 3).

    'Ode on Melancholy', Wikipedia (composition, cancelled stanza, critical survey); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1983), chapter on the Ode on Melancholy; Walter Jackson Bate, John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1963)
