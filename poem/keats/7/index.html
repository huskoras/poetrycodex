Ode to Psyche

by John Keats 1795–1821

From Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems (1820)

Mythology & Folklore

O Goddess! hear these tuneless numbers, wrung
By sweet enforcement and remembrance dear,
And pardon that thy secrets should be sung
Even into thine own soft-conched ear:
Surely I dreamt to-day, or did I see
The winged Psyche with awaken'd eyes?
I wander'd in a forest thoughtlessly,
And, on the sudden, fainting with surprise,
Saw two fair creatures, couched side by side
In deepest grass, beneath the whisp'ring roof
Of leaves and trembled blossoms, where there ran
A brooklet, scarce espied:
'Mid hush'd, cool-rooted flowers, fragrant-eyed,
Blue, silver-white, and budded Tyrian,
They lay calm-breathing on the bedded grass;
Their arms embraced, and their pinions too;
Their lips touch'd not, but had not bade adieu,
As if disjoined by soft-handed slumber,
And ready still past kisses to outnumber
At tender eye-dawn of aurorean love:
The winged boy I knew;
But who wast thou, O happy, happy dove?
His Psyche true!

O latest born and loveliest vision far
Of all Olympus' faded hierarchy!
Fairer than Phoebe's sapphire-region'd star,
Or Vesper, amorous glow-worm of the sky;
Fairer than these, though temple thou hast none,
Nor altar heap'd with flowers;
Nor virgin-choir to make delicious moan
Upon the midnight hours;
No voice, no lute, no pipe, no incense sweet
From chain-swung censer teeming;
No shrine, no grove, no oracle, no heat
Of pale-mouth'd prophet dreaming.

O brightest! though too late for antique vows,
Too, too late for the fond believing lyre,
When holy were the haunted forest boughs,
Holy the air, the water, and the fire;
Yet even in these days so far retir'd
From happy pieties, thy lucent fans,
Fluttering among the faint Olympians,
I see, and sing, by my own eyes inspired.
So let me be thy choir, and make a moan
Upon the midnight hours;
Thy voice, thy lute, thy pipe, thy incense sweet
From swinged censer teeming;
Thy shrine, thy grove, thy oracle, thy heat
Of pale-mouth'd prophet dreaming.

Yes, I will be thy priest, and build a fane
In some untrodden region of my mind,
Where branched thoughts, new grown with pleasant pain,
Instead of pines shall murmur in the wind:
Far, far around shall those dark-cluster'd trees
Fledge the wild-ridged mountains steep by steep;
And there by zephyrs, streams, and birds, and bees,
The moss-lain Dryads shall be lull'd to sleep;
And in the midst of this wide quietness
A rosy sanctuary will I dress
With the wreath'd trellis of a working brain,
With buds, and bells, and stars without a name,
With all the gardener Fancy e'er could feign,
Who breeding flowers, will never breed the same:
And there shall be for thee all soft delight
That shadowy thought can win,
A bright torch, and a casement ope at night,
To let the warm Love in!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Psyche and the occasion l. 1

    In Apuleius's second-century tale (Metamorphoses IV-VI), Cupid visits Psyche by night forbidding her to look at him; when she lights a lamp to see him, he flees, and after trials imposed by Venus she is reunited with him and made immortal. Keats knew the story from Adlington's translation and Lemprière's Classical Dictionary, and from Mary Tighe's poem Psyche (1805). In his journal-letter of 30 April 1819 he copied the ode, calling it the first poem over which he had taken even moderate pains, and remarked that Psyche was not embodied as a goddess before Apuleius, so was never worshipped with ancient fervour. It is the first of the great odes of spring 1819.

    John Keats, journal-letter to George and Georgiana Keats, 14 February-3 May 1819, entry of 30 April 1819 (text of 'Ode to Psyche' and comment); Apuleius, The Golden Ass (Metamorphoses), Books IV-VI, tr. William Adlington (1566); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Keats Foundation, 'Ode to Psyche' (study guide)

  2. tuneless numbers, wrung / By sweet enforcement ll. 1-2

    The formula of the reluctant poet, compelled by an occasion that is both sweet and dear, recalls the opening of 'Lycidas' (1637), with its 'Bitter constraint, and sad occasion dear'; editors note the echo. Keats turns Milton's grief into pleasure: 'sweet enforcement and remembrance dear'. 'Tuneless' is conventional modesty, but it also anticipates the poem's theme, since Psyche has had no hymns, and the poet offers to supply them. The poet also apologises for singing her 'secrets' into her own ear, a playful indiscretion.

    John Milton, 'Lycidas' (1638), l. 6; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1978), 'Ode to Psyche'

  3. soft-conched ear l. 4

    The compound adjective, like 'cool-rooted', 'fragrant-eyed', 'calm-breathing' and 'soft-handed' later in the stanza, condenses an image into a word. 'Conched' suggests both the spiral form of the ear and the sea-shell's power to hold sound. Keats's hyphenated epithets owe something to Spenser and to Chapman's Homer, both formative for him, and hostile reviewers of his earlier volumes mocked such coinages as affected. Here the word also has a point: the poet is singing directly into the goddess's ear, which is shaped to receive sound.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'conched'

  4. Surely I dreamt to-day, or did I see ll. 5-6

    The speaker wandered 'thoughtlessly' in a forest and came upon two figures, which he identifies as Cupid and Psyche, sleeping in each other's arms; Psyche has 'awaken'd eyes', perhaps meaning she is now immortal and conscious. The uncertainty of 'dreamt ... or did I see' anticipates the famous ending of 'Ode to a Nightingale' ('Was it a vision, or a waking dream?'). Vendler reads the opening as the poet's discovery of the subject that his own mind will later house, since the seen world becomes the inner sanctuary of the last stanza.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  5. budded Tyrian l. 14

    Tyre in Phoenicia produced the famous purple dye from the murex shellfish, reserved for royalty in Rome. Keats lists the flowers' colours, 'Blue, silver-white, and budded Tyrian', using the adjective as a noun. The scene, with its 'cool-rooted' flowers and 'whisp'ring roof / Of leaves', is a classical bower in the tradition of Spenser's Bower of Bliss and the bowers of Keats's own Endymion.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Tyrian'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  6. The winged boy ... His Psyche true ll. 21-23

    Cupid, the son of Venus, is familiar from countless poems and paintings, but Psyche was not traditionally depicted, so the speaker's question 'But who wast thou, O happy, happy dove?' is apt. The dove, Venus's bird, is a term of endearment. 'His Psyche true' stresses her fidelity, the theme of her trials in Apuleius. The lovers' lips 'touch'd not, but had not bade adieu', a poised moment between kisses, like the arrested lovers on the Grecian Urn.

    Apuleius, The Golden Ass (Metamorphoses), Books IV-VI, tr. William Adlington (1566); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'

  7. At tender eye-dawn of aurorean love l. 20

    The rare adjective is formed from Aurora, the Roman goddess of the dawn (Greek Eos). Together with 'eye-dawn', it presents waking and new love as one event: the lovers are ready 'past kisses to outnumber' when their eyes open. The phrase links to the myth, in which Psyche's union with Cupid is consummated in darkness, and to the ode's last stanza, which opens a window 'at night' for Love.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aurorean'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  8. latest born ... Of all Olympus' faded hierarchy ll. 24-25

    The idea comes from Keats's letter: because Psyche was deified only by Apuleius, a writer after the Augustan age, she was never worshipped with the antique fervour of the older gods. The 'faded hierarchy' of Olympus, faded because no longer believed in, contrasts with her loveliness. The poem's argument is that a modern poet, in an age without such faith, can still offer her worship of another kind. The theme of the gods' decline links this ode to Keats's Hyperion, on which he had been working.

    John Keats, journal-letter to George and Georgiana Keats, 14 February-3 May 1819, entry of 30 April 1819 (text of 'Ode to Psyche' and comment); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'

  9. Phoebe's sapphire-region'd star ... Vesper ll. 26-27

    Phoebe is a name of Diana (Artemis), goddess of the moon; her 'star' is the moon itself in its sapphire region of sky. Vesper (Hesperus) is Venus as the evening star, whimsically called an 'amorous glow-worm', perhaps because the female glow-worm shines to attract a mate. Both are older deities with their own cults, against whom Psyche, 'temple thou hast none', is set.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; J. Lemprière, A Classical Dictionary (1788; Keats owned an edition), s.v. 'Phoebe', 'Hesperus'

  10. No voice, no lute ... Thy voice, thy lute ll. 32-35, 46-49

    The negative catalogue (no temple, altar, virgin-choir, voice, lute, pipe, incense, shrine, grove, oracle, prophet) is answered word for word after the poet's resolution 'So let me be thy choir'. The device turns rhetorical absence into presence and dramatises the poem's claim that poetry can make up for lost religion. The 'pale-mouth'd prophet dreaming' is the oracle-priest in trance, like the Pythia at Delphi; 'chain-swung censer' (later 'swinged censer') is the swung incense-burner of temple worship. Vendler analyses the ode's structure around this repetition.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1978), 'Ode to Psyche'

  11. fond believing lyre l. 37

    The stanza recalls a time 'When holy were the haunted forest boughs, / Holy the air, the water, and the fire', a world in which every element had its divinity, before 'these days so far retir'd / From happy pieties'. The older sense of 'fond' (OED: foolish, credulous) sits alongside the modern 'affectionate'. Keats does not lament simply; he sees, and sings, 'by my own eyes inspired', an assertion of a poetry grounded in the individual imagination rather than inherited belief. The passage is often compared with Wordsworth's sonnet 'The world is too much with us'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fond'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'

  12. thy lucent fans, / Fluttering among the faint Olympians ll. 41-42

    'Lucent' (Latin lucens) means shining or translucent. In ancient art Psyche was often shown with butterfly wings, the butterfly being an emblem of the soul emerging from the body as from a chrysalis; Keats's 'winged Psyche' (l. 6) and 'fans' recall that iconography. The 'faint Olympians' are the faded gods of stanza 2, through whom she flutters as the newest and brightest.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lucent'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  13. build a fane / In some untrodden region of my mind ll. 50-51

    A 'fane' is a temple. The final stanza internalises the cult: the temple is in 'some untrodden region of my mind', the pines of its grove are 'branched thoughts, new grown with pleasant pain', and its landscape of mountains, Dryads and zephyrs is mental. Keats had written in a letter of May 1818 of the mind as a 'Mansion of Many Apartments', and the stanza works out that conceit. Critics debate whether the inner temple is a triumph of imagination over lost faith, as Vendler argues, or a retreat into solitude that the later odes question.

    Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'; Kenneth Allott, 'The "Ode to Psyche"', in John Keats: A Reassessment, ed. Kenneth Muir (Liverpool, 1958); Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; John Keats to J. H. Reynolds, 3 May 1818 ('Mansion of Many Apartments')

  14. Fledge l. 55

    The verb usually describes a young bird growing its feathers. Applied to trees on mountains it suggests both the feathery look of distant pines and the new growth of thought ('new grown with pleasant pain'). Keats's landscape here is sublime but inward, with 'wild-ridged mountains steep by steep', where the 'moss-lain Dryads', tree nymphs, are lulled to sleep.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fledge'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  15. the gardener Fancy ... will never breed the same ll. 62-63

    'Fancy' for Keats is the playful, image-making imagination (compare his poem 'Fancy', 1818), not yet sharply distinguished from imagination as Coleridge distinguished them. The 'wreath'd trellis of a working brain' supports flowers 'without a name', unlike any in nature, and the gardener 'will never breed the same', a phrase that suggests both endless variety and the impossibility of exact repetition. Some critics find a note of unease here: the sanctuary is made of 'shadowy thought', and what the mind breeds is unreal.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1978), 'Ode to Psyche'

  16. A bright torch, and a casement ope at night, / To let the warm Love in ll. 66-67

    In the myth Cupid comes to Psyche only in darkness; when she lights a lamp to look at him, a drop of hot oil wakes him and he flees. Keats's final image offers both light and an open casement, so that Love can come and be seen. 'Warm Love' is Cupid, but the lines also suggest the warmth of human love in a poem written during Keats's love for Fanny Brawne. The open window, letting the outside in, slightly qualifies the self-enclosed mental sanctuary: the shrine of thought waits for something it cannot produce itself.

    Apuleius, The Golden Ass (Metamorphoses), Books IV-VI, tr. William Adlington (1566); Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), 'Ode to Psyche', headnote and notes

  17. Form whole poem

    Keats's letter says the ode was written with care, in a period when he was experimenting with stanza forms; the same letter contains his sonnet 'If by dull rhymes our English must be chain'd', which expresses dissatisfaction with the sonnet's fixed schemes. 'Psyche' combines sonnet-like quatrains and couplets in irregular stanzas with short lines interspersed. Critics such as Stillinger and Vendler see it as the experimental first of the odes; the following odes ('Nightingale', 'Grecian Urn', 'Melancholy') use a regular ten-line stanza derived from the sonnet.

    John Keats, journal-letter to George and Georgiana Keats, 14 February-3 May 1819, entry of 30 April 1819 (text of 'Ode to Psyche' and comment); Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1978), 'Ode to Psyche'; Helen Vendler, The Odes of John Keats (Cambridge, Mass., 1983), chapter on 'Ode to Psyche'
