Love

Lines Supposed to Have Been Addressed to Fanny Brawne

by John Keats 1795–1821

From Posthumous and Fugitive Poems

Love

This living hand, now warm and capable
Of earnest grasping, would, if it were cold
And in the icy silence of the tomb,
So haunt thy days and chill thy dreaming nights
That thou wouldst wish thine own heart dry of blood
So in my reins red life might stream again,
And thou be conscience-calm'd — see here it is —
I hold it towards you.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, manuscript and addressee l. 1

    H. Buxton Forman found the lines in the margin of a page of the manuscript of The Cap and Bells, the comic fairy poem Keats left unfinished in late 1819, and published them in 1898. The archive title, 'Lines supposed to have been addressed to Fanny Brawne', records the traditional view that they were addressed to her. Later editors are more cautious: the lines may be a speech drafted for a future play or poem (Keats was writing Otho the Great and King Stephen in 1819), and the 'thou' need not be a real person. The poem has no title in manuscript; titles in anthologies are editorial.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes

  2. capable / Of earnest grasping l. 2

    'Earnest' means serious and heartfelt, but also, as a noun, a pledge or token given to secure a bargain (OED), so the 'earnest grasping' hand is one that makes promises. The enjambment 'capable / Of' turns on the gap between being able and doing; the next clause, 'would, if it were cold', moves at once to death. The poem is a single sentence in blank verse, its syntax of conditionals ('would, if', 'So ... That ... So') delaying the main point until the final short line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'earnest'; Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  3. the icy silence of the tomb l. 3

    Written months before Keats's first lung haemorrhage (February 1820), but after he had nursed his brother Tom through tuberculosis to his death in December 1818, the lines imagine the speaker's hand 'cold' and the body in the 'icy silence' of the grave. The dead hand would 'haunt thy days and chill thy dreaming nights': the addressee would be pursued by guilt. Readers who take the lines as addressed to Fanny Brawne hear in them the anguish of the late letters to her; readers who take them as dramatic hear a revenge speech. The Gothic motif of the haunting corpse is turned into a direct threat.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  4. wish thine own heart dry of blood l. 5

    The hypothetical is extreme: haunted by the dead hand, 'thou' would wish to drain her own heart so that the speaker's body might 'stream again' with 'red life'. It is a fantasy of transfusion, love as an exchange of blood, with overtones of the vampire tales then fashionable (Polidori's 'The Vampyre' appeared in April 1819) and of sacrificial religion. The cruelty is unusual in Keats, and critics have read it either as the bitterness of a jealous, dying lover or as the rhetoric of a dramatic villain. The note of reproach continues in 'conscience-calm'd'.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes

  5. reins l. 6

    In the archive's text the line reads 'So in my reins red life might stream again'. 'Reins' (Latin renes, kidneys) appears in the King James Bible as the seat of the emotions and conscience: 'my reins also instruct me in the night seasons' (Psalm 16:7); God 'trieth the hearts and reins' (Psalm 7:9). That sense chimes with 'conscience' in the next line. Standard modern texts, however, print 'veins', which suits 'stream' and 'red life'. Readers should be aware of the difference.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 16:7; Psalm 7:9; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reins'

  6. conscience-calm'd l. 7

    The compound means 'calmed in conscience', relieved of guilt. Its implication is accusatory: the addressee bears some responsibility for the speaker's death and could only be at peace if he were alive again. The dash that follows breaks the long conditional sentence and the poem turns from hypothesis to the present: 'see here it is'. Readers who take the poem biographically connect this with Keats's jealous, reproachful letters to Fanny Brawne of 1820.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem

  7. see here it is -- / I hold it towards you l. 8

    After seven blank-verse lines, the poem breaks off in a line of only six syllables, and shifts from 'thou' to 'you'. The deictic 'This' of the opening and 'see here it is' make the poem a performance: the hand held out is at once the speaker's, the writing hand that made the manuscript, and, for later readers, the poem itself. Many critics have read the fragment as a meditation on poetic survival: the hand is now cold, yet still reaches the reader. Whether the gesture is an offer of love, a threat or a plea is left open, and the incomplete line enacts the incompleteness of the fragment.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes

  8. Blank verse l. 1

    The fragment is in blank verse, with strong enjambment ('capable / Of', 'cold / And', 'blood / So') and one short final line. That is the metre of Otho the Great and the fragment King Stephen, which Keats wrote in 1819 in hope of stage success, and of Shakespearean drama; editors who think the lines dramatic point to it. The verse is unusually bare for Keats: almost no adjectives beyond 'warm', 'cold', 'icy', 'red', and a plain monosyllabic close.

    Miriam Allott, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1970), headnote and notes to the poem; Jack Stillinger, ed., The Poems of John Keats (Harvard University Press, 1978), textual notes
