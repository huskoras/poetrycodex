Death & Dying

Casabianca

by Felicia Hemans 1793–1835

Death & DyingHistory & Politics

The boy stood on the burning deck
Whence all but he had fled;
The flame that lit the battle’s wreck
Shone round him o’er the dead.

Yet beautiful and bright he stood,
As born to rule the storm--
A creature of heroic blood,
A proud, though child-like form.

The flames roll’d on--he would not go
Without his father’s word;
That father, faint in death below,
His voice no longer heard.

He call’d aloud:--“Say, father! say
If yet my task is done!”
He knew not that the chieftain lay
Unconscious of his son.

“Speak, father!” once again he cried,
“If I may yet be gone!”
And but the booming shots replied,
And fast the flames roll’d on.

Upon his brow he felt their breath,
And in his waving hair,
And look’d from that lone post of death
In still yet brave despair;

And shouted but once more aloud,
“My father! must I stay?”
While o’er him fast, through sail and shroud,
The wreathing fires made way.

They wrapt the ship in splendour wild,
They caught the flag on high,
And stream’d above the gallant child
Like banners in the sky.

There came a burst of thunder-sound--
The boy--oh! where was he?
Ask of the winds that far around
With fragments strew’d the sea!--

With mast, and helm, and pennon fair,
That well had borne their part;
But the noblest thing which perish’d there
Was that young faithful heart!

Codex Note

A ballad of the boy who stays at his post on a burning ship after the battle is lost, because his father has not released him. It is Hemans's best-known poem, and its first line has outlived the poem in parody.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The event: Battle of the Nile, 1 August 1798 l. 1

    On the night of 1 August 1798, Nelson's fleet attacked the French fleet anchored in Aboukir Bay, near Alexandria. The flagship L'Orient, carrying Admiral Brueys, caught fire and exploded at about ten o'clock. Its captain, Luc-Julien-Joseph Casabianca, died with his young son, whose age is given variously as ten, twelve or thirteen. Hemans's note calls him 'about thirteen' and says he stayed at his post after the ship took fire and the guns were abandoned, until the flames reached the powder. The poem first appeared in the Monthly Magazine in August 1826 and was added to later editions of The Forest Sanctuary. How much of the story is fact is uncertain.

    Felicia Hemans, 'Casabianca', The Monthly Magazine, August 1826, with the author's note (Wikisource); Wikipedia, 'Battle of the Nile'; Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'

  2. 'The boy stood on the burning deck': reception l. 1

    From the mid-nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth, 'Casabianca' was a staple of school readers and recitation. Its opening line was endlessly parodied in playground rhymes and by comedians such as Spike Milligan and Eric Morecambe. Elizabeth Bishop's poem 'Casabianca' (collected in North & South, 1946) reworks the first line to make the boy an emblem of obstinate love. Modern critics, including Susan Wolfson in her Princeton edition, have taken the poem more seriously as a study of duty, obedience and the cost of patriarchal and military codes to the young.

    Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'; Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000); The Victorian Web, 'The boy stood on the burning deck' (text with Hemans's note)

  3. Whence l. 2

    'Whence' (OED) is the formal adverb of motion from a place. The line establishes the boy's isolation and is matched by the 'lone post of death' of stanza 6. The stanza form is the ballad or common-metre quatrain, alternating lines of four and three stresses rhyming abab, the metre of hymns and popular ballads, which made the poem easy to memorize and recite.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'whence'; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Casabianca'

  4. the battle's wreck l. 3

    The battle had been fought at night, so the fire of L'Orient lit the scene of devastation, 'Shone round him o'er the dead'. 'Wreck' here means destruction and its debris rather than a single shipwreck (OED). The combination of horror and beauty ('beautiful and bright he stood') is characteristic of Hemans's heroic scenes, which aestheticize suffering and invite the reader's tears.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'wreck'; Wikipedia, 'Battle of the Nile'

  5. As born to rule the storm l. 6

    The phrase gives him the bearing of a hero or a young god; 'heroic blood' and 'proud, though child-like form' in the next lines keep the double focus on heroism and childhood. The irony, which some readers see as deliberate, is that this apparent ruler of the storm is only waiting for permission to leave. The poem's pathos depends on the gap between his heroic appearance and his childlike dependence.

    Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000); Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'

  6. his father's word l. 10

    The whole action depends on a word that never comes. Victorian readers took this as exemplary loyalty and discipline. Later critics have read it more darkly: the son's absolute obedience to a father who can no longer speak dramatizes how codes of military honour and paternal authority consume the young. Hemans herself was the mother of five sons and had been deserted by her soldier husband, and Wolfson and others have related her treatment of masculine heroism to her experience. The poem may hold admiration and critique together.

    Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000); Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'

  7. That father, faint in death below l. 11

    Accounts report that Captain Casabianca was gravely wounded during the battle before the ship exploded. The placing of the father 'below' and the son on the open deck creates the scene's tragic geometry: the voice the boy needs is out of reach. 'His voice no longer heard' can mean both that the father can no longer speak and that he can no longer hear.

    Wikipedia, 'Battle of the Nile'; Felicia Hemans, 'Casabianca', The Monthly Magazine, August 1826, with the author's note (Wikisource)

  8. the boy's three cries st. 4-7

    Incremental repetition, where a line or question returns with a variation each time, is a hallmark of traditional ballads (as in 'Lord Randal' or 'Edward'). Here the three appeals mark stages of the fire's advance. The answer is given only by the 'booming shots', and finally by the explosion. The device made the poem effective for recitation, and the cries are what parodists most often exploited.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Casabianca'; Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'

  9. the chieftain l. 15

    'Chieftain' is a romantic, Scott-like word for a leader that lifts the father out of the specifics of naval rank. Historically, Vice-Admiral François-Paul Brueys commanded the fleet and died on L'Orient; Luc-Julien-Joseph Casabianca was the flag captain. Hemans's note, following the English accounts, makes the father the 'Admiral'. 'Unconscious of his son' means unaware, and also literally unconscious from his wounds.

    Felicia Hemans, 'Casabianca', The Monthly Magazine, August 1826, with the author's note (Wikisource); Wikipedia, 'Battle of the Nile'; Oxford English Dictionary, 'chieftain'

  10. that lone post of death l. 23

    'Post' is the military term for a position one must not abandon without orders (OED). The oxymoron of the next line, 'In still yet brave despair', captures the boy's paralysis: he is motionless but not cowardly. The flames now touch his brow and hair; the phrase 'their breath' personifies the fire as a living pursuer. The phrase makes the deck both a military station and a deathbed, and the boy a sentinel who will not desert.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'post'; The Victorian Web, 'The boy stood on the burning deck' (text with Hemans's note)

  11. sail and shroud l. 27

    In nautical usage the shrouds are the set of ropes running from the masthead to the sides of the ship to support the mast (OED, 'shroud', n.). The fire climbs up through sail and rigging, 'wreathing' (twisting like a garland). The pun on the winding-sheet of the dead is apt: the burning rigging becomes the boy's shroud. The verb phrase 'made way' turns the fire into an advancing army, continuing the poem's military vocabulary.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'shroud'

  12. Like banners in the sky l. 32

    The fire 'caught the flag on high', so the ship's own colours burn, and the flames themselves become banners above 'the gallant child'. The image makes the burning ship a military spectacle and the boy a hero at a ceremony. 'Splendour wild' shows Hemans's characteristic fusion of beauty and catastrophe. The simile answers the battle itself: the French flag is lost, but the boy, in the poem's eyes, wins a victory of fidelity.

    Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Casabianca'

  13. the explosion l. 33

    The explosion of L'Orient around 10 p.m. was the most famous moment of the battle: the fighting stopped for some minutes afterwards, and debris fell over the surrounding ships. Hemans renders it with a single line and then the stunned question 'The boy--oh! where was he?' The dashes and exclamation imitate shock. Hemans's own note says simply that the boy perished in the explosion when the flames reached the powder.

    Wikipedia, 'Battle of the Nile'; Felicia Hemans, 'Casabianca', The Monthly Magazine, August 1826, with the author's note (Wikisource)

  14. Ask of the winds l. 35

    The answer to 'where was he?' is no answer: the boy has vanished with the fragments strewn over the sea. The device resembles the ancient 'ubi sunt' ('where are they?') formula of elegy. The following stanza lists what perished, 'mast, and helm, and pennon fair', before naming the noblest loss. The winds that strewed the sea with fragments become the only witnesses, as in elegies where nature is asked to speak for the dead.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Casabianca'; Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000)

  15. pennon l. 37

    A pennon (OED) is a narrow, often swallow-tailed flag, originally the knight's lance-flag; the medieval associations suit the chivalric tone of the poem. Mast, helm and pennon are personified as having 'well ... borne their part', like loyal soldiers. The list moves from the ship's working parts (mast, helm) to its emblem (the pennon) before the final contrast with the boy's heart, 'the noblest thing' of all. 'Fair' keeps the tone of chivalric romance.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'pennon'

  16. the noblest thing ll. 39-40

    The closing couplet weighs material and spiritual loss and gives the verdict to fidelity. Victorian readers took it as a lesson in duty; later readers note that the poem celebrates a death that obedience made needless, and some, like Elizabeth Bishop, turned the image towards obstinate love. Hemans's emphasis on the heart rather than the battle typifies her domestic, affective treatment of heroic subjects.

    Susan J. Wolfson, ed., Felicia Hemans: Selected Poems, Letters, Reception Materials (Princeton UP, 2000); Wikipedia, 'Casabianca (poem)'
