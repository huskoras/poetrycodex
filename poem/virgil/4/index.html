Nature

Eclogue IV

by Virgil 70–19 BC

Translated by James Rhoades (World's Classics, 1921) · From The Eclogues

Nature

Muses of Sicily, essay we now
A somewhat loftier task! Not all men love
Coppice or lowly tamarisk: sing we woods,
Woods worthy of a Consul let them be.

Now the last age by Cumae's Sibyl sung
Has come and gone, and the majestic roll
Of circling centuries begins anew:
Justice returns, returns old Saturn's reign,
With a new breed of men sent down from heaven.
Only do thou, at the boy's birth in whom
The iron shall cease, the golden race arise,
Befriend him, chaste Lucina; 'tis thine own
Apollo reigns. And in thy consulate,
This glorious age, O Pollio, shall begin,
And the months enter on their mighty march.
Under thy guidance, whatso tracks remain
Of our old wickedness, once done away,
Shall free the earth from never-ceasing fear.
He shall receive the life of gods, and see
Heroes with gods commingling, and himself
Be seen of them, and with his father's worth
Reign o'er a world at peace. For thee, O boy,
First shall the earth, untilled, pour freely forth
Her childish gifts, the gadding ivy-spray
With foxglove and Egyptian bean-flower mixed,
And laughing-eyed acanthus. Of themselves,
Untended, will the she-goats then bring home
Their udders swollen with milk, while flocks afield
Shall of the monstrous lion have no fear.
Thy very cradle shall pour forth for thee
Caressing flowers. The serpent too shall die,
Die shall the treacherous poison-plant, and far
And wide Assyrian spices spring. But soon
As thou hast skill to read of heroes' fame,
And of thy father's deeds, and inly learn
What virtue is, the plain by slow degrees
With waving corn-crops shall to golden grow,
From the wild briar shall hang the blushing grape,
And stubborn oaks sweat honey-dew. Nathless
Yet shall there lurk within of ancient wrong
Some traces, bidding tempt the deep with ships,
Gird towns with walls, with furrows cleave the earth.
Therewith a second Tiphys shall there be,
Her hero-freight a second Argo bear;
New wars too shall arise, and once again
Some great Achilles to some Troy be sent.
Then, when the mellowing years have made thee man,
No more shall mariner sail, nor pine-tree bark
Ply traffic on the sea, but every land
Shall all things bear alike: the glebe no more
Shall feel the harrow's grip, nor vine the hook;
The sturdy ploughman shall loose yoke from steer,
Nor wool with varying colours learn to lie;
But in the meadows shall the ram himself,
Now with soft flush of purple, now with tint
Of yellow saffron, teach his fleece to shine.
While clothed in natural scarlet graze the lambs.
"Such still, such ages weave ye, as ye run,"
Sang to their spindles the consenting Fates
By Destiny's unalterable decree.
Assume thy greatness, for the time draws nigh,
Dear child of gods, great progeny of Jove!
See how it totters- the world's orbed might,
Earth, and wide ocean, and the vault profound,
All, see, enraptured of the coming time!
Ah! might such length of days to me be given,
And breath suffice me to rehearse thy deeds,
Nor Thracian Orpheus should out-sing me then,
Nor Linus, though his mother this, and that
His sire should aid- Orpheus Calliope,
And Linus fair Apollo. Nay, though Pan,
With Arcady for judge, my claim contest,
With Arcady for judge great Pan himself
Should own him foiled, and from the field retire.

Begin to greet thy mother with a smile,
O baby-boy! ten months of weariness
For thee she bore: O baby-boy, begin!
For him, on whom his parents have not smiled,
Gods deem not worthy of their board or bed.

Codex Note

POLLIO

Study notes by the editors

  1. Eclogue 4 ('Pollio'): occasion and translation l. 1

    The fourth Eclogue, headed 'Pollio' in the archive, foretells that the Golden Age is about to return with the birth of a child, in the year when Gaius Asinius Pollio is consul. Conington fixes the date to 40 BC, when Pollio helped negotiate the peace of Brundisium between Octavian and Antony. 'Muses of Sicily' addresses the Muses of Theocritus, the Sicilian founder of pastoral, whom Virgil follows in the other Eclogues. The archive's text is James Rhoades's blank-verse translation, reprinted in the World's Classics (1921). The identity of the child and the poem's relation to Hebrew prophecy have been argued over since antiquity. Line references in these notes follow Rhoades's lines; line numbers mentioned inside the notes are Virgil's Latin lines.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Wendell Clausen, A Commentary on Virgil, Eclogues (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1994), on Eclogue 4; 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others)

  2. 'essay we now / A somewhat loftier task' ll. 1-3

    The Latin is paulo maiora canamus, 'let us sing of things a little greater'. Virgil announces that this pastoral will rise above its usual level. Line 2 gives the reason: non omnis arbusta iuvant humilesque myricae, 'orchards and low tamarisks do not please everyone'. Conington notes that tamarisks belong to Theocritus's landscape and here stand for the humble strain of rural song. 'Essay' is the archaic verb 'to attempt' (OED, essay v.). Rhoades's 'Coppice or lowly tamarisk' renders arbusta (trees used to train vines) loosely. The Italian poet Giovanni Pascoli later took the title of his collection Myricae (1891) from this line.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'essay'; 'Myricae di Giovanni Pascoli: significato e poesie', Studenti.it (on the title and Virgilian epigraph)

  3. 'Woods worthy of a Consul' l. 4

    The Latin is si canimus silvas, silvae sint consule dignae, 'if we sing of woods, let the woods be worthy of a consul'. Conington paraphrases: if the theme is still the country, let it rise to a dignity a consul need not be ashamed of. Pollio was consul in 40 BC and a patron of poets: he is praised in Eclogue 3 and was himself a poet, historian and orator. Silvae, 'woods', is a standard metonymy for pastoral poetry, as Conington notes. Rhoades capitalises 'Consul' and repeats 'woods' as the Latin does.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robert Coleman, ed., Vergil: Eclogues (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1977), on Eclogue 4

  4. 'the last age by Cumae's Sibyl sung' l. 5

    The Latin is ultima Cumaei venit iam carminis aetas, 'now the last age of the Cumaean song has come'. The Sibyl of Cumae was the most famous of the prophetesses whose oracles circulated in Greek verse at Rome. Conington explains that the original Sibylline books burned with the Capitol in 83 BC and that a new collection was made. He adds that nothing is known of the specific oracle Virgil means, though it apparently foretold a return of the Golden Age at the end of a great cycle. Servius speaks of ten ages, the last ruled by the Sun. Rhoades's 'Has come and gone' makes the last age already over, which is one of two possible readings that Conington discusses.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Wendell Clausen, A Commentary on Virgil, Eclogues (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1994), on Eclogue 4

  5. 'the majestic roll / Of circling centuries begins anew' l. 7

    The Latin is magnus ab integro saeclorum nascitur ordo, 'the great order of the ages is born anew'. Conington explains the doctrine of the annus magnus or 'Platonic year': a vast period, variously reckoned from about 2,500 to 18,000 years, completed when all the heavenly bodies return to their original places, after which history repeats itself. He notes that its relation to the Etruscan doctrine of saecula is unclear. Nisbet argues that Virgil blends several such cosmological schemes. Rhoades's 'majestic roll / Of circling centuries' stresses the cycle, and 'begins anew' renders ab integro.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others); Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  6. 'Justice returns' l. 8

    The Latin is iam redit et Virgo, 'now the Maiden too returns'. Conington identifies her as Justitia (Justice), who left the earth in the iron age (compare Georgics 2.474). The source is Aratus's Phaenomena, where Dike, the Maiden, lived among men in the Golden Age, withdrew to the hills in the Silver Age and fled to the sky in the age of bronze. She became the constellation Virgo, also called Astraea ('the starry one'). Rhoades translates the meaning ('Justice') rather than the word Virgo. This loses the astral image and the later Christian link with the Virgin Mary.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Aratus, Phaenomena 96-136 (the Maiden, Justice), trans. G. R. Mair (Loeb, 1921), Theoi Classical Texts; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  7. 'returns old Saturn's reign' l. 8

    Redeunt Saturnia regna means 'the reign of Saturn returns'. In Hesiod the golden race of men lived when Kronos ruled, without toil or old age. Roman writers identified Kronos with Saturn and placed his reign in Italy (compare Aeneid 8.319-27, where Saturn brings law and a golden age to Latium). Conington notes Wagner's view that the repeated verb (redit ... redeunt) links Virgo and Saturnia regna as one event. The Saturnalia festival preserved memories of a time without masters and slaves. Rhoades's 'old Saturn's reign' keeps the name.

    Hesiod, Works and Days 109-201 (the Ages of Man), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Loeb, 1914), Project Gutenberg; John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  8. 'a new breed of men sent down from heaven' l. 9

    The Latin is iam nova progenies caelo demittitur alto, 'now a new offspring is sent down from high heaven'. Conington glosses nova progenies as 'a new and better race of men', the gens aurea of line 9. From Constantine onward, Christian readers took the line as a prophecy of Christ's incarnation. Constantine's Oration to the Saints discusses the Eclogue at length, and Augustine (City of God 10.27) ascribes the prophecy to the Sibyl. Dante has the poet Statius say that these verses made him a Christian (Purgatorio 22.70-72, translating lines 5-7). Rhoades's 'breed of men' keeps the collective sense.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Constantine, Oration to the Assembly of the Saints, chs 19-21 (in Eusebius's Life of Constantine), New Advent; Dante, Purgatorio 22.67-73, Digital Dante (Columbia University); Augustine, City of God 10.27, New Advent

  9. Who is the child? l. 10

    Conington found the identity of the puer 'an unsolved problem', partly because the hopes were never fulfilled. Candidates include Pollio's own sons, one of whom, Asinius Gallus, reportedly claimed to be the child. Others suggest the expected child of Antony and Octavia, whose marriage sealed the peace of Brundisium, or the child of Octavian and Scribonia, who turned out to be Julia. Cairns argues for Pollio's offspring, and some modern critics read the child as a symbol of the new age or of the poem itself. The question remains open.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Wendell Clausen, A Commentary on Virgil, Eclogues (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1994), on Eclogue 4; 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others)

  10. 'chaste Lucina; 'tis thine own / Apollo reigns' l. 13

    The Latin is casta fave Lucina: tuus iam regnat Apollo, 'chaste Lucina, favour him: your own Apollo now reigns'. Lucina ('she who brings to light') was the goddess of childbirth, identified with Diana, the twin sister of Apollo, which explains tuus ('your'). Conington connects Apollo's reign with Servius's report that the last of the Sibyl's ten ages belonged to the Sun. He also mentions a passage of Nigidius Figulus that names a reign of Apollo. He notes that any allusion to Octavian, who claimed Apollo as his patron, depends on who the child is thought to be. Rhoades's 'Befriend him' renders fave.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others)

  11. Pollio's consulship ll. 14-15

    The Latin is teque adeo decus hoc aevi, te consule, inibit, / Pollio, 'and in your consulship, yours, Pollio, this glory of the age will begin'. Pollio (75 BC-AD 4) was a partisan of Antony, a tragedian and historian of the civil wars, and later founder of Rome's first public library. Conington glosses decus hoc aevi as 'this glorious age' and the magni menses as the months of the Great Year. He explains te duce in line 13 as 'under your auspices as consul', since the consul gave his name to the year. Rhoades's 'months enter on their mighty march' renders incipient magni procedere menses.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robert Coleman, ed., Vergil: Eclogues (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1977), on Eclogue 4

  12. 'whatso tracks remain / Of our old wickedness' l. 17

    The Latin is si qua manent sceleris vestigia nostri, / inrita perpetua solvent formidine terras, 'whatever traces of our crime remain will be made void and free the lands from perpetual fear'. Conington insists that sceleris refers specifically to the guilt of civil bloodshed. He compares Horace's Odes 1.2 and Epode 7, where the civil wars are a crime needing expiation. Rome had been at war with itself almost continuously since 49 BC. Rhoades's 'old wickedness' is more general, and 'whatso' is an archaic form of 'whatsoever'.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Horace, Epode 7, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  13. 'see / Heroes with gods commingling' ll. 20-21

    The Latin is ille deum vitam accipiet divisque videbit / permixtos heroas et ipse videbitur illis, 'he will receive the life of the gods, and will see heroes mingled with gods, and will himself be seen by them'. Conington notes that 'the life of gods' is Hesiod's description of the golden race. He adds that familiar intercourse with the gods is another golden-age privilege, recalling the end of Catullus 64. There Catullus says the gods once visited human homes but now keep away because of men's crimes. Virgil reverses that pessimism. Rhoades's 'himself / Be seen of them' keeps the reciprocal passive.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Catullus 64 (Peleus and Thetis), Latin text (The Latin Library); Hesiod, Works and Days 109-201 (the Ages of Man), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Loeb, 1914), Project Gutenberg

  14. The earth's 'childish gifts' ll. 25-27

    The Latin is at tibi prima, puer, nullo munuscula cultu ... fundet, 'but for you, boy, the earth untilled will pour out its first little gifts'. Conington notes that munuscula are presents for children and that nullo cultu ('without cultivation') is a mark of the Golden Age. The plants are ivy, baccar (perhaps foxglove, perhaps asarabacca, he says), colocasia (the Egyptian bean, introduced into Italy) and acanthus. Rhoades follows the foxglove interpretation. 'Gadding' means 'straying, rambling' (OED, gad v.). 'Laughing-eyed' is a bold rendering of ridenti, 'smiling'. Even the cradle, Virgil adds, will sprout 'caressing' (blandos) flowers of its own accord.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gad'

  15. 'flocks afield / Shall of the monstrous lion have no fear' l. 29

    The Latin is nec magnos metuent armenta leones, 'and the herds will not fear great lions'. Conington calls Isaiah 11 a 'remarkable parallel' to the whole passage. There the wolf lies with the lamb, the lion eats straw, and the nursing child plays over the asp's hole. The Sibylline Oracles (3.791-3) contain a similar Jewish-Greek prophecy. Nisbet concluded that Virgil knew such eastern material indirectly, through oracles, while Conington doubted whether Virgil uses any image without a classical parallel. Rhoades's 'monstrous' adds menace to magnos ('great').

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Isaiah 11:6-8, King James Version; 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others)

  16. 'The serpent too shall die, / Die shall the treacherous poison-plant' ll. 31-32

    The Latin repeats occidet ('will perish') at the start of two lines: occidet et serpens, et fallax herba veneni / occidet; Assyrium vulgo nascetur amomum. Rhoades copies the repetition in 'shall die, / Die shall'. Conington explains that serpents and poisonous plants are removed for the child's sake. He calls fallax ('deceptive') apt, since poison plants can be mistaken for harmless ones, and glosses herba veneni as 'poisonous herb'. Amomum was an aromatic shrub imported from the east, here growing 'commonly' (vulgo). Rhoades's 'far / And wide Assyrian spices spring' renders vulgo.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Isaiah 11:6-8, King James Version

  17. 'stubborn oaks sweat honey-dew' l. 39

    The Latin is et durae quercus sudabunt roscida mella, 'and the hard oaks will sweat dewy honey'. Conington explains roscida: honey was believed to fall like dew and be gathered by bees from leaves. In the new age men will gather it for themselves. He compares Hesiod, Works and Days 232-3, where the oak bears acorns at the top and bees in the middle for the just, and Horace's Epode 16. Rhoades's 'stubborn' renders durae ('hard'), and 'honey-dew' combines the two Latin words. The stage of the child's youth is marked by these changes in plants.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Hesiod, Works and Days 109-201 (the Ages of Man), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Loeb, 1914), Project Gutenberg; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  18. 'Nathless / Yet shall there lurk within of ancient wrong / Some traces' l. 39

    The Latin is pauca tamen suberunt priscae vestigia fraudis, 'yet a few traces of the old wrongdoing will lurk beneath'. Conington explains fraus as the wickedness of artificial society, opposed to the innocence of nature. The traces lead men 'to tempt Thetis with ships', to gird towns with walls and to cut furrows in the earth. Sailing, fortification and agriculture were all marks of the decline from the Golden Age. Rhoades's 'Nathless' is an archaic form (OED, natheless adv.), and 'tempt the deep' keeps the idea of temptare, testing the sea.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'natheless'

  19. 'a second Tiphys ... a second Argo' ll. 43-44

    The Latin is alter erit tum Tiphys, et altera quae vehat Argo / delectos heroas, 'there will then be another Tiphys, and another Argo to carry the chosen heroes'. Tiphys steered the Argo, on which Jason and the Argonauts sailed for the Golden Fleece. Ancient tradition made it the first ship, and thus the first breach of the Golden Age. Conington explains that in the Sibylline cycle all history comes over again. Virgil mixes this with the idea of a return to gold so as to give some scope to Roman love of conquest. Rhoades's 'hero-freight' compresses delectos heroas into a compound word.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Catullus 64 (Peleus and Thetis), Latin text (The Latin Library)

  20. 'Some great Achilles to some Troy be sent' l. 46

    The Latin is atque iterum ad Troiam magnus mittetur Achilles, 'and again great Akhilleus will be sent to Troy'. Conington regards Akhilleus as the emblem of the youthful warrior. He observes that a second conquest of Troy would otherwise be a strange achievement to predict for a descendant of the Trojans, as the Romans claimed to be. In Hesiod the heroes who fought at Troy form a fourth age between bronze and iron. Rhoades's 'Some great Achilles to some Troy' turns the specific names into types, which brings out Conington's reading.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Hesiod, Works and Days 109-201 (the Ages of Man), trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (Loeb, 1914), Project Gutenberg; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  21. 'Nor wool with varying colours learn to lie' l. 53

    The Latin is nec varios discet mentiri lana colores, 'and wool will not learn to counterfeit various colours'. Conington notes that the idea of fraud in mentiri ('to lie') continues the theme of artificial society from line 31. Instead, the ram in the meadow will change his fleece to sweet-blushing purple (murex) or saffron yellow, and the lambs will be clothed in sandyx (a scarlet dye). Conington explains that the country will enjoy luxury without its artificial apparatus. Rhoades's 'natural scarlet' brings out sponte ('of its own accord'). The image has struck many readers as playful.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. sandyx

  22. 'Such still, such ages weave ye, as ye run' ll. 59-60

    The Latin is talia saecla, suis dixerunt, currite, fusis / concordes stabili fatorum numine Parcae, '"Run, such ages," said the Parcae to their spindles, in agreement with the fixed will of fate'. Conington notes that Virgil clearly had in mind the refrain of the Fates at the wedding of Peleus in Catullus 64, currite ducentes subtegmina, currite, fusi ('run, spindles, drawing the threads'). But Virgil varies it by making the Fates address the ages themselves. In Catullus they sing of the birth of Akhilleus, which links this passage to line 36. Rhoades's 'consenting' renders concordes.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Catullus 64 (Peleus and Thetis), Latin text (The Latin Library); Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  23. 'Dear child of gods, great progeny of Jove!' l. 62

    The Latin is cara deum suboles, magnum Iovis incrementum, 'dear offspring of the gods, great increase of Jupiter'. Conington calls Iovis incrementum a singular expression: the word is seldom applied to a person and usually means the beginning or seed of something. The next lines show the world 'nodding with its convex weight', which Conington, after Heyne, compares to a temple trembling at the approach of its god. Rhoades's 'progeny' renders incrementum, and 'See how it totters' gives nutantem, though the Latin implies a nod of assent rather than collapse.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Lewis and Short, A Latin Dictionary (1879), s.v. incrementum

  24. 'Nor Thracian Orpheus should out-sing me then, / Nor Linus' ll. 68-70

    The poet wishes to live long enough to sing the child's deeds. Then neither Thracian Orpheus nor Linus could beat him, though Orpheus's mother is the Muse Calliope and Linus's father is Apollo. Even Pan, judged by his own Arcadia, would admit defeat. Conington notes that the poet naturally chooses mythic singers to contrast with himself as bard of the new golden age. He adds that Arcadians would be competent and partial judges. Rhoades's 'With Arcady for judge' repeated over two lines copies the Latin repetition of Arcadia iudice.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Robert Coleman, ed., Vergil: Eclogues (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1977), on Eclogue 4

  25. The ending: whose smile? ll. 75-79

    Virgil ends: incipe, parve puer, risu cognoscere matrem ... cui non risere parentes, / nec deus hunc mensa, dea nec dignata cubili est. With the manuscripts' cui, the last lines mean 'one on whom his parents have not smiled no god has honoured with his table, no goddess with her bed'. Quintilian (9.3.8) quotes qui non risere parentes, 'those who have not smiled at their parents'. Most modern editors adopt qui, so that the baby's smile is required, and Nisbet argues that the baby must be the one who smiles. Conington kept cui, as does Rhoades ('on whom his parents have not smiled'). The table and bed recall Hercules, who feasted with the gods and married Hebe.

    John Conington, ed., P. Vergili Maronis Opera, vol. 1 (London: Whittaker and Bell, 1858), Eclogue 4, pp. 46-53; 'Eclogue 4', Wikipedia (summarising Nisbet 1978, Clausen, Cairns and others); Virgil, Eclogue 4, Latin text (The Latin Library); Wendell Clausen, A Commentary on Virgil, Eclogues (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1994), on Eclogue 4
