Love

To a Young Gentleman

by Anonymous (Classical Chinese) various

Translated by Herbert A. Giles (1898) · From Chinese Poetry in English Verse (1898)

Love

Don't come in, sir, please!
Don't break my willow-trees!
Not that that would very much grieve me;
But alack-a-day! what would my parents say?
And love you as I may,
I cannot bear to think what that would be.

Don't cross my wall, sir, please!
Don't spoil my mulberry-trees!
Not that that would very much grieve me;
But alack-a-day! what would my brothers say?
And love you as I may,
I cannot bear to think what that would be.

Keep outside, sir, please!
Don't spoil my sandal-trees!
Not that that would very much grieve me;
But alack-a-day! what the world would say!
And love you as I may,
I cannot bear to think what that would be.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Shijing, Odes of Zheng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (Mao 76) title, l. 1

    The Shijing (Classic of Poetry, Book of Songs) contains 305 songs dating roughly from 1000 to 600 BCE, traditionally said to have been selected by Confucius. This poem, 將仲子 Jiang Zhongzi ('Please, Zhongzi'), is no. 76 in the standard Mao numbering, in the 'Airs of the States' (Guofeng) section under the state of Zheng. A young woman begs her lover not to climb in to see her, because of what her family and neighbours will say. The original has three stanzas of eight short lines (mostly four characters). Giles (1845-1935), a Cambridge sinologist, turned it into rhymed English stanzas of six lines with a ballad-like refrain, in his Chinese Poetry in English Verse (1898). His title 'To a Young Gentleman' is his own.

    Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record; Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); 'Classic of Poetry', Wikipedia

  2. 'Zhongzi': 'second son' l. 1

    Each stanza begins 將仲子兮, 'I beg you, Zhongzi'. 將 qiang here means 'please, I pray you'; 仲 zhong denotes the second of brothers, and 子 zi is a polite suffix, so Zhongzi is a familiar name like 'Second Son'. Giles's 'sir, please' turns intimacy into Victorian politeness, which suits his comic, coy reading of the speaker. Legge translates 'I pray you, Mr. Chung'. In the Mao tradition, the name was identified with Zhai Zhong, a minister of Zheng (see note on the allegorical reading).

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record

  3. 'do not leap into my hamlet' (wu yu wo li 無踰我里) ll. 1, 7, 13

    The three stanzas say 無踰我里 'do not climb into my hamlet', 無踰我牆 'do not climb over my wall', 無踰我園 'do not climb into my garden'. 里 li was a walled village or ward, traditionally of twenty-five households. The movement is from the village boundary to the house wall to the garden. Giles keeps the wall in stanza 2 but renders the first and third as 'Don't come in' and 'Keep outside', so the spatial progression is lost. 踰 yu means 'to leap over, climb across', suggesting a secret night visit.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record

  4. willow (qi 杞) l. 2

    無折我樹杞, 'do not break my planted qi'. The identity of 杞 qi is uncertain: Legge and Giles give 'willow', following old commentary that it is a kind of willow or osier, while some modern scholars identify it with the boxthorn or wolfberry. Broken branches are evidence of an intruder, so her plea is practical as well as figurative. Each stanza names a different tree: qi, mulberry and tan, matching the different boundaries.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record; Arthur Waley, trans., The Book of Songs (London: Allen & Unwin, 1937), courtship section

  5. fear of parents' words l. 4

    豈敢愛之？畏我父母, 'how would I dare to begrudge them? I fear my father and mother'. 愛 ai here means 'to begrudge, be sparing of', not 'to love', so the girl says she does not really care about the trees. Giles's 'Not that that would very much grieve me' captures this. The fear of parents reflects marriage customs in which unions were arranged and secret liaisons dishonoured a family. Giles's interjection 'alack-a-day!' is an archaic English cry of regret (OED: 'alas the day'), which he adds for comic, mock-old-fashioned effect.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword

  6. refrain and incremental repetition ll. 5-6

    The Chinese refrain is 仲可懷也，父母之言，亦可畏也, 'Zhong may be cherished, but my parents' words are also to be feared'. Each stanza repeats it, changing only the persons feared. This incremental repetition (重章疊句) is characteristic of the 'Airs of the States', and suggests sung performance. Giles adapts it into a fixed two-line refrain, 'And love you as I may, / I cannot bear to think what that would be', which repeats unchanged in every stanza, while he varies the fourth line. His couplet loses the balance of the Chinese, in which love for Zhong and fear of others' words are set side by side.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record; 'Classic of Poetry', Wikipedia

  7. mulberry (sang 桑) l. 8

    無折我樹桑, 'do not break my planted mulberry'. Mulberry trees, planted by house walls, fed the silkworms that women raised for silk production, so they belong to the domestic world of the household. In other Shijing songs, notably 'Sang zhong' ('Among the Mulberries', Mao 48), mulberry groves are where lovers meet, and the tree had erotic associations in early Chinese poetry. Giles's 'spoil' replaces 'break' for variety.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Arthur Waley, trans., The Book of Songs (London: Allen & Unwin, 1937), courtship section

  8. the brothers l. 10

    畏我諸兄, 'I fear my elder brothers'. 諸兄 'the elder brothers' widens the circle of those she fears from parents to siblings, and in the third stanza to the whole community. The progression parallels the spatial movement of the stanzas. Elder brothers, with parents, had a say in a sister's marriage, and their anger at a secret affair could be serious. Giles keeps the persons feared exactly and in order, varying only his fourth line, so the English reader can still follow the widening circle of disapproval.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record

  9. tan (檀): 'sandal-tree' l. 14

    無折我樹檀, 'do not break my planted tan'. 檀 tan in early texts is a hard-timbered tree used for carts and tools; it is not the fragrant tropical sandalwood, though the same character later came to mean that. Legge's 'sandal trees' and Giles's 'sandal-trees' are therefore approximate. The garden (園) of this stanza is the most intimate space, inside the house compound.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record; Arthur Waley, trans., The Book of Songs (London: Allen & Unwin, 1937), courtship section

  10. 'people's many words': gossip l. 16

    畏人之多言, 'I fear people's many words'. 多言 'much talk' is gossip, which in the closely watched life of a village could ruin a reputation. Giles's 'what the world would say!' turns the plain phrase into an English idiom of social propriety, slightly Victorian in tone, and changes the question mark of the earlier stanzas to an exclamation mark. The progression parents, brothers, people ends with the anonymous voice of the community.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; James Legge, trans., The She King, The Chinese Classics vol. IV (Hong Kong/London, 1871), Odes of Ch'ing, 'Tseang Chung-tsze' (Chinese Text Project parallel text); Herbert A. Giles, trans., Chinese Poetry in English Verse (London: Quaritch; Shanghai: Kelly & Walsh, 1898), HathiTrust record

  11. allegory or love song? l. 6

    The Mao Preface (Han dynasty) says the poem 'criticises Duke Zhuang' of Zheng, who would not restrain his ambitious younger brother Duan and refused the advice of his minister Zhai Zhong (the story is told in the Zuo zhuan, Duke Yin year 1); in this reading 'Zhongzi' is Zhai Zhong and the speaker the duke. Zhu Xi (1130-1200), rejecting such allegories, called it the words of a woman in a forbidden liaison, consistent with Confucius's warning that the 'tunes of Zheng are licentious' (Analects 15.11). Modern readers, since the early twentieth century, take it as a love song of a girl torn between desire and family. Giles, writing in 1898, already treats it as a lighthearted love song.

    Shijing, Guofeng, Zheng feng, 'Jiang Zhongzi' (將仲子), Mao 76, with the Mao Preface, Chinese Wikisource; Zhu Xi, Shi jizhuan (詩集傳, 1177), on Zheng feng 'Jiang Zhongzi'; Confucius, Analects 15.11 (Wei Ling Gong), Chinese Text Project; Arthur Waley, trans., The Book of Songs (London: Allen & Unwin, 1937), courtship section; Stephen Owen, ed. and trans., An Anthology of Chinese Literature: Beginnings to 1911 (New York: Norton, 1996)
