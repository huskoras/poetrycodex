Idyll I: The Death of Daphnis

by Theocritus fl. c. 270 BC

Translated by C. S. Calverley (1869) · From Idylls

Nature

THYRSIS.
Sweet are the whispers of yon pine that makes
Low music o'er the spring, and, Goatherd, sweet
Thy piping; second thou to Pan alone.
Is his the horned ram? then thine the goat.
Is his the goat? to thee shall fall the kid;
And toothsome is the flesh of unmilked kids.

GOATHERD.
Shepherd, thy lay is as the noise of streams
Falling and falling aye from yon tall crag.
If for their meed the Muses claim the ewe,
Be thine the stall-fed lamb; or if they choose
The lamb, take thou the scarce less-valued ewe.

THYRSIS.
Pray, by the Nymphs, pray, Goatherd, seat thee here
Against this hill-slope in the tamarisk shade,
And pipe me somewhat, while I guard thy goats.

GOATHERD.
I durst not, Shepherd, O I durst not pipe
At noontide; fearing Pan, who at that hour
Rests from the toils of hunting. Harsh is he;
Wrath at his nostrils aye sits sentinel.
But, Thyrsis, thou canst sing of Daphnis' woes;
High is thy name for woodland minstrelsy:
Then rest we in the shadow of the elm
Fronting Priapus and the Fountain-nymphs.
There, where the oaks are and the Shepherd's seat,
Sing as thou sang'st erewhile, when matched with him
Of Libya, Chromis; and I'll give thee, first,
To milk, ay thrice, a goat--she suckles twins,
Yet ne'ertheless can fill two milkpails full;--
Next, a deep drinking-cup, with sweet wax scoured,
Two-handled, newly-carven, smacking yet
0' the chisel. Ivy reaches up and climbs
About its lip, gilt here and there with sprays
Of woodbine, that enwreathed about it flaunts
Her saffron fruitage. Framed therein appears
A damsel ('tis a miracle of art)
In robe and snood: and suitors at her side
With locks fair-flowing, on her right and left,
Battle with words, that fail to reach her heart.
She, laughing, glances now on this, flings now
Her chance regards on that: they, all for love
Wearied and eye-swoln, find their labour lost.
Carven elsewhere an ancient fisher stands
On the rough rocks: thereto the old man with pains
Drags his great casting-net, as one that toils
Full stoutly: every fibre of his frame
Seems fishing; so about the gray-beard's neck
(In might a youngster yet) the sinews swell.
Hard by that wave-beat sire a vineyard bends
Beneath its graceful load of burnished grapes;
A boy sits on the rude fence watching them.
Near him two foxes: down the rows of grapes
One ranging steals the ripest; one assails
With wiles the poor lad's scrip, to leave him soon
Stranded and supperless. He plaits meanwhile
With ears of corn a right fine cricket-trap,
And fits it on a rush: for vines, for scrip,
Little he cares, enamoured of his toy.
The cup is hung all round with lissom briar,
Triumph of Æolian art, a wondrous sight.
It was a ferryman's of Calydon:
A goat it cost me, and a great white cheese.
Ne'er yet my lips came near it, virgin still
It stands. And welcome to such boon art thou,
If for my sake thou'lt sing that lay of lays.
I jest not: up, lad, sing: no songs thou'lt own
In the dim land where all things are forgot.

THYRSIS.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
The voice of Thyrsis. Ætna's Thyrsis I.
Where were ye, Nymphs, oh where, while Daphnis pined?
In fair Penëus' or in Pindus' glens?
For great Anapus' stream was not your haunt,
Nor Ætna's cliff, nor Acis' sacred rill.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
O'er him the wolves, the jackals howled o'er him;
The lion in the oak-copse mourned his death.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
The kine and oxen stood around his feet,
The heifers and the calves wailed all for him.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
First from the mountain Hermes came, and said,
"Daphnis, who frets thee? Lad, whom lov'st thou so?"
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
Came herdsmen, shepherds came, and goatherds came;
All asked what ailed the lad. Priapus came
And said, "Why pine, poor Daphnis? while the maid
Foots it round every pool and every grove,
(Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song)
"O lack-love and perverse, in quest of thee;
Herdsman in name, but goatherd rightlier called.
With eyes that yearn the goatherd marks his kids
Run riot, for he fain would frisk as they:
(Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song):
"With eyes that yearn dost thou too mark the laugh
Of maidens, for thou may'st not share their glee."
Still naught the herdsman said: he drained alone
His bitter portion, till the fatal end.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
Came Aphroditè, smiles on her sweet face,
False smiles, for heavy was her heart, and spake:
"So, Daphnis, thou must try a fall with Love!
But stalwart Love hath won the fall of thee."
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
Then "Ruthless Aphroditè," Daphnis said,
"Accursed Aphroditè, foe to man!
Say'st thou mine hour is come, my sun hath set?
Dead as alive, shall Daphnis work Love woe."
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"Fly to Mount Ida, where the swain (men say)
And Aphroditè--to Anchises fly:
There are oak-forests; here but galingale,
And bees that make a music round the hives.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"Adonis owed his bloom to tending flocks
And smiting hares, and bringing wild beasts down.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"Face once more Diomed: tell him 'I have slain
The herdsman Daphnis; now I challenge thee.'
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"Farewell, wolf, jackal, mountain-prisoned bear!
Ye'll see no more by grove or glade or glen
Your herdsman Daphnis! Arethuse, farewell,
And the bright streams that pour down Thymbris' side.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"I am that Daphnis, who lead here my kine,
Bring here to drink my oxen and my calves.
Begin, sweet Maids, begin the woodland song.
"Pan, Pan, oh whether great Lyceum's crags
Thou haunt'st to-day, or mightier Mænalus,
Come to the Sicel isle! Abandon now
Rhium and Helicè, and the mountain-cairn
(That e'en gods cherish) of Lycaon's son!
Forget, sweet Maids, forget your woodland song.
"Come, king of song, o'er this my pipe, compact
With wax and honey-breathing, arch thy lip:
For surely I am torn from life by Love.
Forget, sweet Maids, forget your woodland song.
"From thicket now and thorn let violets spring,
Now let white lilies drape the juniper,
And pines grow figs, and nature all go wrong:
For Daphnis dies. Let deer pursue the hounds,
And mountain-owls outsing the nightingale.
Forget, sweet Maids, forget your woodland song."

So spake he, and he never spake again.
Fain Aphroditè would have raised his head;
But all his thread was spun. So down the stream
Went Daphnis: closed the waters o'er a head
Dear to the Nine, of nymphs not unbeloved.
Now give me goat and cup; that I may milk
The one, and pour the other to the Muse.
Fare ye well, Muses, o'er and o'er farewell!
I'll sing strains lovelier yet in days to be.

GOATHERD.
Thyrsis, let honey and the honeycomb
Fill thy sweet mouth, and figs of Ægilus:
For ne'er cicala trilled so sweet a song.
Here is the cup: mark, friend, how sweet it smells:
The Hours, thou'lt say, have washed it in their well.
Hither, Cissætha! Thou, go milk her! Kids,
Be steady, or your pranks will rouse the ram.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Idyll 1 and Calverley's translation opening

    Theocritus, a Greek poet from Syracuse working in the early third century BC (partly at Alexandria and on Kos), invented the literary form later called pastoral. Idyll 1 stood first in the ancient collections and became the model for later pastoral elegy: Virgil's Eclogues 5 and 10, Milton's 'Lycidas', Shelley's 'Adonais' and Arnold's 'Thyrsis' all descend from it. Its structure is a frame dialogue between Thyrsis and an unnamed goatherd, a long description of a carved cup offered as payment, and Thyrsis's song with a refrain. The Greek is in dactylic hexameters in a literary Doric dialect. Charles Stuart Calverley (1831-1884), a Cambridge scholar better known for comic verse, renders it in English blank verse with the refrain kept as a recurring line.

    C. S. Calverley, trans., Theocritus Translated into English Verse (1869), Idyll I; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  2. Matched compliments and prizes Greek ll. 1-11

    Thyrsis praises the goatherd's piping as second only to Pan's, the god of shepherds and inventor of the reed pipe (syrinx); the goatherd answers by comparing Thyrsis's song to falling water and ranking him just below the Muses. Each opens with 'sweet' (Greek hadu), and the sound of the first line (hadu ti to psithurisma, 'something sweet is the whispering') imitates the pine's whisper. The careful matching of speeches is the technique of 'amoebean' exchange used in singing contests (compare Idylls 5 and 8). Calverley keeps the balance but turns the Greek's opening sound-play into the plainer 'Sweet are the whispers'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.1-11, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  3. Pan at noon Greek ll. 15-18

    Noon was a dangerous hour in Greek folk belief, when gods and spirits were abroad and Pan, the goat-legged god of Arcadia, slept after the hunt; waking him might bring sudden fear (our word 'panic' derives from his name). Calverley's 'Wrath at his nostrils aye sits sentinel' expands the Greek image of sharp bile (chole) sitting at the nose, a physiological picture of anger (compare Akhilleus's 'bile' in Iliad 18.108-110 and the snorting of an angry animal). The refusal provides the dramatic reason why Thyrsis, not the goatherd, will perform, and fixes the scene's time at the still heat of midday.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.15-18, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  4. Daphnis Greek l. 19

    Ancient sources give several stories. Aelian (Varia Historia 10.18) says Stesichorus first made Daphnis a subject of song, and tells how he was blinded after breaking faith with a nymph who loved him; Diodorus (4.84) gives a similar account of the herdsman who invented bucolic song. Theocritus' Daphnis dies 'wasting away' (etaketo) under love, defying Aphrodite, without blindness. Scholars disagree whether Theocritus assumes the nymph story, invents a vow of chastity, or deliberately keeps the cause obscure (see the note on Aphrodite's taunt). Calverley's 'woes' renders algea, 'pains, sufferings'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.19, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Aelian, Varia Historia 10.18; Diodorus Siculus 4.84; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; D. M. Halperin, Before Pastoral: Theocritus and the Ancient Tradition of Bucolic Poetry (New Haven, 1983)

  5. Priapus and the Fountain-nymphs Greek ll. 21-23

    Priapus, a god of gardens and flocks whose cult came from Lampsacus on the Hellespont, was represented by roughly carved wooden figures set up in orchards and pastures. Theocritus later makes him one of Daphnis's mocking visitors. The 'Fountain-nymphs' (Greek Kranaiai, 'of the springs'; the reading varies in the manuscripts) are the local water-deities. The elm, oaks and 'Shepherd's seat' complete one of the earliest literary pictures of the pleasant place (locus amoenus) that pastoral would make standard.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.21-23, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  6. The carved cup Greek ll. 27-56

    Kissybion is a rustic drinking vessel, probably of ivy-wood, here 'washed with sweet wax' and so new it still smells of the carving-knife. The description (ekphrasis) of its decoration recalls Homer's account of the Shield of Akhilleus (Iliad 18.478-608), but replaces cities, war and law with small pastoral scenes; critics see this as a programmatic statement of Theocritus' art, epic technique applied to humble subjects. Ivy dusted with golden helichryse frames the rim, with a tendril (helix) bearing saffron berries; Calverley's 'woodbine' stands for that tendril. The cup has also been read as a figure for the poem itself: crafted, enclosed, and 'virgin', never yet touched.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.27-56, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Homer, Iliad 18.478-608 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25); R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  7. Three scenes: the woman, the fisherman, the boy Greek ll. 32-54

    The three panels have been read as a progression of human ages (youth in love, old age at work, childhood at play) or of activities (eros, toil, craft). The woman's indifference to her suitors anticipates Daphnis's resistance to love; the fisherman's straining sinews are vivid enough that 'every fibre' seems to fish; the boy, so engrossed in plaiting an akridothera (a trap or cage for crickets or grasshoppers) that he ignores the foxes, is often taken as an image of the poet absorbed in delicate craft. Calverley's 'a miracle of art' renders ti theon daidalma, 'a work of the gods' cunning'. 'Snood' (amphix) is a headband; 'scrip' (pera) is a shepherd's bag.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.32-54, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; D. M. Halperin, Before Pastoral: Theocritus and the Ancient Tradition of Bucolic Poetry (New Haven, 1983); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scrip'

  8. Æolian Greek l. 56

    The phrase is disputed. The adjective aiolikos can mean 'Aeolian', from Aeolis, the Greek region of north-west Asia Minor (home of Lesbian lyric), which would make the cup an import or a work in the Aeolian style. Many commentators prefer to connect it with aiolos, 'shifting, many-coloured', so that the acanthus border is a 'shimmering wonder'. Calverley's capital and his 'Triumph of Æolian art' choose the geographical sense; Lang's prose instead has 'a miracle of varied work', with a footnote giving 'Aeolian' as the alternative reading. Gow and Hunter both discuss the alternatives. The 'lissom briar' around the cup renders hugros akanthos, 'pliant acanthus', the plant familiar from Corinthian capitals.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.55-56, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; A. Lang, trans., Theocritus, Bion and Moschus Rendered into English Prose (1880), Idyll I

  9. Calydon Greek ll. 57-58

    Calydon, in Aetolia on the north side of the Gulf of Corinth, was famous in myth for the Calydonian boar hunt (Iliad 9.529-599). A ferryman from there suggests that the cup has travelled, and the price (a goat and a white cheese) places it within the barter economy of the herdsmen. The detail that the goatherd has never drunk from it ('virgin still') raises its value as a gift and echoes the theme of untouched virginity in Daphnis's story.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.57-60, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; Homer, Iliad 9.529-599 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25)

  10. Hades, 'which brings forgetting' Greek l. 63

    Greek Hades is the world of the dead, where the shades drink of Lethe ('forgetfulness') in later tradition; Theocritus calls it ton eklelathonta, 'that makes forget'. The line introduces the poem's concern with death and poetic survival, and it anticipates Daphnis' defiant claim that he will be a grief to Love even in Hades. Calverley's 'dim land' gives a shadowy colouring to the plain Greek.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.62-63, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  11. The refrain Greek l. 64 and passim

    The Greek refrain (archete boukolikas, Moisai philai, archet' aoidas) addresses the Muses ('sweet Maids' in Calverley). Its repetition gives the song a ritual quality, like a dirge with a choral response. It varies at significant points: from line 94 it becomes 'begin again', and after Daphnis' last appeal to Pan (from line 127) it becomes 'cease, Muses, come cease the bucolic song' (legete), as the song and Daphnis' life end together. Calverley renders this last form 'Forget, sweet Maids, forget your woodland song', which keeps the repetition but changes 'cease' to 'forget', perhaps to echo the 'land where all things are forgot'. Virgil imitated the device in Eclogue 8.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.64, 94, 127, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; Virgil, Eclogues 8.21 etc.

  12. Thyrsis of Etna Greek l. 65

    The self-naming acts as a 'seal' (sphragis), authenticating the song as a performer's own. It also locates the poem in Sicily, the land of Daphnis and Theocritus's homeland (he came from Syracuse), and the following stanza maps the island's landmarks against distant mainland Greece. 'Thyrsis' became a stock pastoral name: Virgil uses it in Eclogue 7, Milton in 'L'Allegro' and Matthew Arnold for his elegy on Clough.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.65, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; C. S. Calverley, trans., Theocritus Translated into English Verse (1869), Idyll I

  13. Where were ye, Nymphs? Greek ll. 66-69

    The rhetorical question of the absent protectors became a fixture of pastoral elegy. Peneus is the river of Tempe in Thessaly and Pindus the mountain range of northern Greece; Anapus is a river near Syracuse, and Acis a stream below Etna named after the lover of Galatea. Virgil adapted the question for Gallus (Eclogue 10.9-12) and Milton for Edward King ('Where were ye Nymphs when the remorseless deep / Clos'd o'er the head of your lov'd Lycidas?', 'Lycidas' l. 50). The beasts' lament that follows (jackals, wolves, a lion) shows nature mourning, another convention of the genre.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.66-75, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Virgil, Eclogues 10.9-12; John Milton, 'Lycidas' (1638), ll. 50-55; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  14. The visitors: Hermes, herdsmen, Priapus Greek ll. 77-93

    Hermes, god of herdsmen and born on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia, comes 'first from the mountain'; ancient tradition sometimes made him Daphnis's father. His question, 'whom do you love so much?', assumes the cause is love. The procession of visitors, ending with the goddess herself, became a convention: Virgil's Gallus is visited by Apollo, Silvanus and Pan (Eclogue 10.19-30), and Milton's Lycidas by Triton, Camus and Saint Peter. Daphnis's silence ('Still naught the herdsman said'), until Aphrodite speaks, is pointed: he will answer only the power he resists. 'He drained ... His bitter portion' renders a repeated verb (anue), 'he was accomplishing his bitter love, accomplishing it to the end of his fate'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.77-93, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Virgil, Eclogues 10.19-30; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  15. herdsman ... goatherd Greek ll. 85-91

    Pastoral society in Theocritus has a hierarchy: cowherds (boukoloi) rank above shepherds, who rank above goatherds, and goats are proverbially lustful. Priapus says that as the goatherd watches the she-goats being mounted and pines because he is not a goat himself, so Daphnis watches the girls laughing and pines because he does not dance with them. Calverley tones down the Greek: his kids 'Run riot' and the goatherd 'would frisk as they', where the Greek verb (bateuntai) is frankly sexual. The jibe implies Daphnis is refusing a love that is available, since 'the maid' is searching for him through every spring and grove.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.82-91, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  16. Why does Daphnis die? Greek ll. 95-98

    The Greek verb lugizein means to bend or throw in wrestling, and kateucheo can mean 'you boasted' or 'you vowed'. So Daphnis seems to have claimed he could defeat Eros. Scholars have argued for three main explanations: Daphnis had vowed chastity and now resists a passion that would break the vow; he loves a girl but refuses to yield to Aphrodite's power; or (following the tradition in Aelian) he is bound by fidelity to a nymph and resists a mortal girl. Gow and Halperin set out the alternatives; Hunter stresses that Theocritus seems to leave the motive deliberately unclear. Calverley's 'try a fall' is the English wrestling idiom, apt for the Greek.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.95-98, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; D. M. Halperin, Before Pastoral: Theocritus and the Ancient Tradition of Bucolic Poetry (New Haven, 1983); R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  17. Defiance in death Greek ll. 100-103

    Daphnis's reply is the poem's moment of heroic defiance. He addresses Aphrodite with three curses ('grievous', 'resentful', 'hateful to mortals'), recognises that his sun has set, and promises to go on harming Eros after death. Critics compare his stance to that of a tragic hero, or to Hippolytus in Euripides, who also refuses Aphrodite and is destroyed by her. Calverley's 'Dead as alive' adds a balance not in the Greek, which simply says 'even in Hades'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.100-103, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; Euripides, Hippolytus

  18. Anchises, Adonis, Diomedes Greek ll. 105-113

    Each example turns the goddess's power against her. On Mount Ida near Troy she seduced the cowherd Anchises (the story of the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite); Daphnis, himself a herdsman, tells her to go back to that kind of lover. Adonis, the beautiful youth she loved, was killed while hunting; the Greek says only that he too tends flocks and hunts, a sneer at her taste. Diomedes wounded her in battle (Iliad 5.330-351), so Daphnis invites her to boast to him of beating a mere cowherd. 'Galingale' (kupeiros) is a sedge, and Calverley's 'there are oak-forests; here but galingale' interprets a passage that is textually troubled (l. 105 is marked corrupt, and a line about bees humming at the hives is not printed in every edition).

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.105-113, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite (Hymn 5), on Anchises; Homer, Iliad 5.330-351 (ed. and trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1924-25); A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  19. Arethusa and Thybris Greek ll. 115-118

    Arethusa is the freshwater spring on the island of Ortygia at Syracuse; myth said the nymph Arethusa fled the river-god Alpheus under the sea from Greece to Sicily. Thybris (Calverley's 'Thymbris') was, according to the ancient commentators, a place near Syracuse; its exact identity is uncertain. The farewell to animals and landscape makes Daphnis's death a loss to the whole countryside, and Milton echoes the invocation of Arethusa in 'Lycidas' ('O fountain Arethuse', l. 85).

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.115-118, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.572-641 (Arethusa); Milton, 'Lycidas' l. 85

  20. Pan's Arcadian haunts Greek ll. 123-126

    Mount Lycaeus (Calverley's 'Lyceum') and Maenalus are Arcadian mountains sacred to Pan. Where the printed Greek has erion, 'barrow, burial mound', a variant reading is rhion, 'peak'; Calverley takes 'Rhium and Helice' as place-names (Helice was a town of Achaea on the Gulf of Corinth, Rhium a headland on that gulf). Gow, by contrast, takes Helice to be Callisto, whom myth made a bear on the Arcadian mountains, so that the Greek means the high places of Arcadia. The 'tomb of Lycaonides' is identified by Gow with the grave of Arcas, son of Callisto, grandson of Lycaon and eponymous hero of Arcadia (his tomb was shown on Maenalus; Pausanias 8.9.3); Calverley's 'Lycaon's son' translates the Greek patronymic literally. Summoning Pan to take the pipe marks the symbolic end of Daphnis's career as a singer, and after this stanza the refrain changes from 'begin' to 'cease'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.123-127, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1

  21. The pipe bequeathed to Pan Greek ll. 128-130

    The syrinx is the Panpipe, a row of reeds bound with wax. Returning it to Pan is both a dedication (worshippers dedicated tools at the end of a career) and a passing-on of the tradition. The line 'For surely I am torn from life by Love' renders 'for I am dragged now by love to Hades'. Calverley's 'arch thy lip' interprets the Greek description of the pipe as 'curved about the lip'.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.128-130, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  22. Adynata: the world turned upside down Greek ll. 132-136

    This is the classic example of the adynaton, a list of impossibilities used to show that an event overturns the natural order. Virgil imitated it in Eclogue 8.52-56. Calverley alters two items: the Greek has the narcissus flowering on junipers (he writes 'white lilies') and pines bearing pears (ochnas; he writes 'figs'); the pear line (l. 134) is bracketed as doubtful in some modern texts, so editions differ here. The final contrast, owls from the mountains singing against nightingales, inverts the hierarchy of song and leads into the singer's own silence.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.132-136, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; Virgil, Eclogues 8.52-56; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1

  23. Daphnis 'went to the stream' Greek ll. 138-141

    Aphrodite wished to raise him, but the threads the Fates had spun had run out, and 'Daphnis went to the stream; the eddy closed over the man dear to the Muses, not hated by the Nymphs'. Some ancient and modern readers think he literally drowned (perhaps taken by the nymphs who loved him, as in other myths); others take the stream as Acheron, the river of the underworld, so that 'went to the stream' simply means 'died'. Gow surveys both views. Calverley's 'down the stream' and 'closed the waters o'er a head' favour drowning. Milton's Lycidas, who is drowned, belongs to the same elegiac tradition, and the echo of 'Clos'd o'er the head' is often noticed.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.138-141, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; A. S. F. Gow, ed., Theocritus, 2 vols (Cambridge, 1950; 2nd ed. 1952), commentary on Idyll 1; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; John Milton, 'Lycidas' (1638), ll. 50-55

  24. The goatherd's blessing: Aegilus, cicada, Hours, Cissaetha Greek ll. 146-152

    Aegilus was a place in Attica noted for figs (so ancient commentators). The cicada (tettix, Calverley's 'cicala') was the Greek emblem of sweet, ceaseless song (Plato, Phaedrus 259b-d). The Hours (Horai), goddesses of the seasons, are imagined washing the cup at their spring, so fresh is its smell. The poem then returns abruptly to the farmyard: Cissaetha ('Ivy', a goat's name) is to be milked, and the frisky she-goats warned lest the he-goat rise against them. The bathos frames the lament within ordinary rural life, and the earthy last line echoes the sexual imagery of Priapus's jibe.

    Theocritus, Idyll 1.146-152, Greek text ed. R. J. Cholmeley (1901-19), Perseus Digital Library; R. Hunter, ed., Theocritus: A Selection (Cambridge Greek and Latin Classics, 1999), commentary on Idyll 1; Plato, Phaedrus 259b-d
