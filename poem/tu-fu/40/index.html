A Spring View

by Tu Fu 712–770

Translated by Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu (1929) · From The Jade Mountain (1929)

History & Politics

Though a country be sundered, hills and rivers endure;
And spring comes green again to trees and grasses
Where petals have been shed like tears
And lonely birds have sung their grief.
. . . After the war-fires of three months,
One message from home is worth a ton of gold.
. . . I stroke my white hair. It has grown too thin
To hold the hairpins any more.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Du Fu's 'Spring View' (Chun wang), 757 title

    春望 Chun wang means 'Spring Gaze' or 'Looking Out in Spring'. Du Fu (712-770) wrote it in the third month of 757, during the An Lushan rebellion (755-763), the catastrophe that ended the Tang golden age. Rebel forces had taken Chang'an in 756; the emperor Xuanzong had fled to Sichuan, and Du Fu, trying to join the new emperor Suzong, was caught and held in the city while his wife and children were at Fuzhou. The poem is a regulated verse (lüshi) of eight five-character lines, with strict tonal pattern and parallel middle couplets. Bynner and Kiang translated it from Three Hundred Tang Poems; their version keeps eight lines but breaks parallelism and adds ellipses.

    Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face; Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  2. 'The state is broken, mountains and rivers remain' l. 1

    國破山河在, 'the state (or capital) broken, mountains and rivers remain'. 國 guo can mean both 'the dynasty, the state' and 'the capital'; 破 po is 'broken, defeated, sacked'. The line juxtaposes two facts without a connective, letting the reader feel the irony: political order has collapsed, while landscape persists indifferently. Bynner's 'Though a country be sundered, hills and rivers endure' supplies the logic in a concessive clause and generalises the line into a maxim ('a country'), whereas the Chinese is concrete, about this state now. The line is one of the most quoted in Chinese and has been echoed by later poets, including the Japanese haiku poet Bashō, who quoted it at the battlefield of Hiraizumi in The Narrow Road to the Deep North (journey of 1689, published 1702).

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss)

  3. 'the city in spring, grass and trees grow deep' l. 2

    城春草木深, 'city, spring: grass and trees deep'. The point is double: spring has come as always, but the grass and trees grow 'deep' because the capital's streets and gardens are untended and its people gone. Bynner omits the city (城 cheng) altogether and makes the line a statement of seasonal return, 'spring comes green again', which loses the image of the wild growing over the city. The parallel between 'mountains and rivers' and 'grass and trees' in the opening couplet sets nature's persistence against human ruin.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss)

  4. 'Moved by the times, flowers splash tears' l. 3

    感時花濺淚, 'feeling the times, flowers splash tears'. Grammatically, the flowers can be the subject, so that nature itself seems to grieve (petals dropping, dew like tears); or the poet is the implied subject, who, moved by the times, sheds tears on or at the flowers, which normally give pleasure. Traditional Chinese commentary often took the second reading, while many modern readers and translators enjoy the ambiguity. Bynner chooses the first reading and turns it into a simile, 'petals have been shed like tears', which makes the flowers' tears figurative and removes 'the times' (時 shi) entirely. Hawkes's word-by-word gloss lets students see how open the line is.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss); Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription

  5. parallel couplet: flowers / birds l. 4

    恨別鳥驚心, 'resenting separation, birds startle the heart'. In a regulated verse the second and third couplets must be parallel; here 'feel' answers 'resent', 'times' answers 'separation', 'flowers' answers 'birds', 'splash tears' answers 'startle heart'. The same ambiguity of subject applies: birdsong startles the grieving poet, or the birds themselves seem to grieve at parting. The separation is Du Fu's from his family (see l. 6). Bynner's 'lonely' and 'sung their grief' are additions that make the birds mourners, and his two lines run on syntactically from l. 2 ('Where petals ... And lonely birds'), dissolving the strict parallel structure into an English sentence.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  6. Bynner's ellipses ll. 5, 7

    Classical Chinese texts were not punctuated as English is, and the original has no sign of hesitation here. Bynner uses ellipses to mark the move from the general landscape (ll. 1-4) to the personal situation (ll. 5-8), and again to the final self-portrait. The device gives the translation a halting, meditative voice, typical of Bynner's attempt to suggest the gaps between Chinese lines. It also imposes a modern, conversational tone on a highly formal poem.

    Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource

  7. beacon fires 'for three months' (lian san yue 連三月) l. 5

    烽火連三月, 'beacon fires continue three months'. 烽火 fenghuo were alarm beacons lit on frontier towers, and the term stands for war in general. 連三月 is read either as 'for three months on end' or 'continuing into the third month', i.e. the end of spring 757, since the rebellion had begun in the winter of 755 and the fighting round the capital had gone on since the previous year; commentators differ. Bynner's 'the war-fires of three months' keeps the ambiguity. This line opens the third couplet, also parallel: 'beacon fires' against 'family letter', 'three months' against 'ten thousand in gold'.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss)

  8. a letter from home 'worth ten thousand in gold' l. 6

    家書抵萬金, 'a letter from home is worth ten thousand gold'. 萬金 wanjin, 'ten thousand (units of) gold', is a conventional hyperbole for great wealth, and the line has become a proverb. Bynner's 'a ton of gold' turns the number into a weight familiar from English idiom; it keeps the hyperbole but loses the Chinese counting. 'One message' adds a stress on singularity: during the occupation communication with the family at Fuzhou was almost impossible, and Du Fu's poem 'Moonlit Night' (also in this archive, Bynner's 'On a Moonlight Night') imagines his wife there.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; Witter Bynner and Kiang Kang-hu, trans., The Jade Mountain: A Chinese Anthology, Being Three Hundred Poems of the T'ang Dynasty (New York: Knopf, 1929), Wikisource transcription; Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face

  9. white hair grown thin l. 7

    白頭搔更短, 'white head, I scratch it, ever shorter'. Scratching the head is a conventional gesture of anxiety or perplexity in Chinese poetry, and white hair the stock sign of age brought on by sorrow. 'Shorter' means thinner, as hair falls out with each scratch. Bynner's 'stroke' is gentler than 搔 sao, 'scratch'. Du Fu habitually presents himself as prematurely old and frail, and this self-portrait turns the poem from public disaster to private decline.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss); Stephen Owen, The Great Age of Chinese Poetry: The High T'ang (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1981), chapters on Li Po, Tu Fu and Wang Wei

  10. the official's hatpin (zan 簪) l. 8

    渾欲不勝簪, 'almost too thin to bear a hairpin'. Chinese men wore their hair long in a topknot, and officials fixed their caps to it with a pin (簪 zan). The image is literal (his hair is too sparse) and also social: the cap and pin were emblems of office, and Du Fu, cut off from the court in the occupied capital, cannot serve his ruler. Bynner's plural 'hairpins' slightly blurs the single official pin. The poem ends with the small, almost comic detail of the body, characteristic of Du Fu's way of bringing great history down to private experience.

    Du Fu, 'Chun wang' (春望), Quan Tang shi juan 224, Chinese Wikisource; David Hawkes, A Little Primer of Tu Fu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1967), 'Spring Scene' (character-by-character gloss); Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu, 6 vols. (Boston/Berlin: De Gruyter, 2016), open access, Chinese text en face
