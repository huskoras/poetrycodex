The Harlem Dancer

by Claude McKay 1889–1948

From Harlem Shadows

Social Commentaries

Applauding youths laughed with young prostitutes
And watched her perfect, half-clothed body sway;
Her voice was like the sound of blended flutes
Blown by black players upon a picnic day.
She sang and danced on gracefully and calm,
The light gauze hanging loose about her form;
To me she seemed a proudly-swaying palm
Grown lovelier for passing through a storm.
Upon her swarthy neck black shiny curls
Luxuriant fell; and tossing coins in praise,
The wine-flushed, bold-eyed boys, and even the girls,
Devoured her shape with eager, passionate gaze;
But looking at her falsely-smiling face,
I knew her self was not in that strange place.

Study notes by the editors

  1. First publication l. 1

    The Seven Arts, edited by James Oppenheim and Waldo Frank, was a leading little magazine of the American 'renaissance' of 1916-17. McKay, then working as a waiter, published 'The Harlem Dancer' and 'Invocation' there under the name Eli Edwards. Critics have called the appearance a milestone, since a Black poet's sonnets appeared in a white avant-garde journal before the Harlem Renaissance proper. The poem was reprinted in Spring in New Hampshire (1920) and Harlem Shadows (1922).

    Claude McKay's Early Poetry (1911-1922): A Digital Collection (Lehigh University), publication timeline; The Seven Arts, October 1917, Modernist Journals Project (Brown University and University of Tulsa); Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  2. The audience ll. 1-2

    The first line sets the dancer among a crowd that consumes her as spectacle; the second gives the object of their gaze. The audience's mixed laughter and applause is careless. The speaker distinguishes himself from them by a different kind of seeing, which the sestet and couplet develop. The juxtaposition of 'youths' and 'young prostitutes' in a single line stresses that everyone in the room is young, and the dancer's dignity stands out against their careless pleasure.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987)

  3. Flutes on a picnic day ll. 3-4

    The simile moves out of the smoky room into a sunlit, communal scene. Many critics connect such pastoral images with McKay's Jamaican childhood, a recurrent source of nostalgia in Harlem Shadows (for instance 'The Tropics in New York'). The 'black players' make the music collective and racial, and contrast with the white-owned commercial world of the cabaret. The phrase 'picnic day' strikes a deliberately homely, innocent note.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  4. The palm through the storm ll. 7-8

    The tropical palm, which bends without breaking in hurricanes, again recalls the Caribbean. 'Proudly-swaying' transforms the erotic swaying of l. 2 into an image of pride and resilience. 'Passing through a storm' implies a hard past without specifying it, perhaps poverty or migration. The 'storm' may also be read collectively, as the history of the race, which links the dancer's private dignity to a communal endurance; McKay often used tropical images to set a remembered home against the alien city.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992)

  5. swarthy; luxuriant ll. 9-10

    Both words are part of the sensuous description of the sestet. 'Swarthy' was a common literary word for dark skin; 'luxuriant' suggests vegetation, continuing the palm image. The description shows that the speaker too is attracted, which complicates his separation from the crowd. The lingering on the 'black shiny curls' prepares the sharp turn of the couplet, where the speaker looks from the body to the face and finds there something the others cannot see.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'swarthy, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'luxuriant, adj.'

  6. The devouring gaze ll. 11-12

    The verb 'devoured' makes the spectators predatory, and the tossed coins make the performance a transaction, linking the dancer to the prostitutes of l. 1. 'Even the girls' widens the gaze beyond male desire. Feminist critics have pointed out that the poem's speaker also gazes at her body in detail, so the poem both critiques and participates in the spectacle; others answer that the couplet separates his insight from their consumption.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992); George Hutchinson, The Harlem Renaissance in Black and White (Harvard University Press, 1995)

  7. 'her self was not in that strange place' ll. 13-14

    Writing 'her self' as two words stresses an inner identity separate from the performing body. The 'falsely-smiling face' recalls Dunbar's 'We Wear the Mask' (1895), and readers often connect the couplet with Du Bois's idea of double consciousness. 'That strange place' may be the cabaret, Harlem, or America itself, depending on how far one reads her as an emblem of displacement; McKay's own sense of exile suggests the wider reading.

    Paul Laurence Dunbar, 'We Wear the Mask', in Lyrics of Lowly Life (1896); Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  8. Shakespearean sonnet whole poem

    McKay's sonnet puts the spectacle in the quatrains and saves the insight for the couplet, opened by 'But'. The rhymes are full ('calm/palm', 'form/storm', 'face/place'). The use of a classic love-poem form for a cabaret dancer is pointed: the sonnet, traditionally a vehicle of praise of a beloved, becomes a vehicle of sympathy and alienation. The volta is delayed to the couplet.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987)
