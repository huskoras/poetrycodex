If We Must Die

by Claude McKay 1889–1948

From Harlem Shadows

Social Commentaries

If we must die, let it not be like hogs
Hunted and penned in an inglorious spot,
While round us bark the mad and hungry dogs,
Making their mock at our accursèd lot.
If we must die, O let us nobly die,
So that our precious blood may not be shed
In vain; then even the monsters we defy
Shall be constrained to honor us though dead!
O kinsmen! we must meet the common foe!
Though far outnumbered let us show us brave,
And for their thousand blows deal one death-blow!
What though before us lies the open grave?
Like men we’ll face the murderous, cowardly pack,
Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Red Summer, 1919 l. 1

    McKay, a Jamaican-born poet then working on the railways as a dining-car waiter, wrote the sonnet during the wave of anti-Black riots that James Weldon Johnson named the 'Red Summer'. It appeared in the July 1919 issue of The Liberator, the radical New York monthly edited by McKay's friend Max Eastman, and was soon reprinted in The Messenger and in Sylvia Pankhurst's Workers' Dreadnought in London; McKay collected it in Harlem Shadows (1922). Many critics take it as an opening note of the Harlem Renaissance.

    Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references); Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'If We Must Die'

  2. A Shakespearean sonnet l. 5

    McKay chose traditional forms deliberately. The sonnet, inherited from Shakespeare, Milton and Wordsworth (whose political sonnets, such as 'To Toussaint L'Ouverture', are a precedent), lends the poem dignity and authority. The repeated 'If we must die' (ll. 1, 5) frames the first two quatrains; the third quatrain turns to direct address ('O kinsmen!'); the couplet delivers the resolve. Critics have debated whether the inherited form restrains McKay's radicalism or, as most now argue, turns the dominant culture's own form into a weapon.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987); Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references)

  3. Hogs penned for slaughter l. 1

    The image draws on the reality of lynching and mob violence, in which victims were hunted down. Robert A. Lee (CLA Journal, 1974) traces the poem's movement from 'hogs' in line 1 to 'men' in line 13, a change of identity achieved through the choice to fight. 'Inglorious spot' denies the victims even an honourable place of death; the word may recall Gray's 'mute inglorious Milton' in the Elegy, a poem about the unremembered dead.

    Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references); Thomas Gray, 'Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard', l. 59; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987)

  4. The dogs l. 3

    The poem reverses the racist rhetoric that likened Black people to animals: here the attackers are the beasts. Dogs had literally been used to hunt fugitives from slavery. 'Mad' means rabid as well as furious. The image is resumed and judged in line 13, where the pack is called 'murderous, cowardly'. The noise of the pack (barking) is set against the silence and resolve of the speaker's 'we'.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes

  5. 'Making their mock' l. 4

    'To make a mock at' (to deride) is King James English (Proverbs 14:9). The archaic diction suits the sonnet's elevated register and places the mob's jeers within a scriptural world in which the righteous are mocked by the wicked. The mob's mockery is set against the honour that the 'monsters' will be 'constrained' to show in line 8, so the first two quatrains move from humiliation to respect. The phrase also gives the oppressors the role of the scoffers of the Psalms and Proverbs.

    Bible (KJV), Proverbs 14:9; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mock'

  6. 'accursèd lot' l. 4

    The grave accent tells the reader to sound the -ed, giving the line its ten syllables. 'Lot' is fate, as in the casting of lots. The phrase evokes the idea of an inherited curse, the old racist misreading of Genesis 9 as a 'curse of Ham', which the poem implicitly refuses: the lot is made accursed by the oppressors' violence, not by God.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lot'; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes; Bible (KJV), Genesis 9:25

  7. Noble death l. 5

    The ideal of choosing a noble death over a shameful life runs from Homer, where Hektor resolves not to die without a struggle and without glory (Iliad 22.304-305), to Horace's 'dulce et decorum est pro patria mori' (Odes 3.2.13). McKay claims that heroic tradition for people whom the mob treats as less than human. The vocative 'O' raises the speech to prayer and oratory.

    Homer, Iliad 22.304-305; Horace, Odes 3.2.13

  8. 'precious blood' l. 6

    1 Peter 1:19 speaks of redemption 'with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish'. The allusion makes the sacrifice sacred and contrasts with the hogs of line 1. The run-on into 'In vain' (l. 7), one of the poem's few enjambments, puts weight on the refusal of a meaningless death and recalls Lincoln's resolve 'that these dead shall not have died in vain' (Gettysburg Address, 1863).

    Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 1:19; Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address (1863), Library of Congress

  9. Honour from enemies l. 8

    'Constrained' means compelled. The thought is old: brave enemies earn respect, as when Akhilleus, at the end of the Iliad, receives Priam with honour and returns Hektor's body for burial. McKay's claim is political as well as moral: resistance changes how the oppressor must see the oppressed. The phrase 'though dead' admits that the victory may come only after death.

    Homer, Iliad 24; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987)

  10. The turn: 'O kinsmen!' l. 9

    The apostrophe marks the sonnet's turn, from conditional reflection ('If we must die') to command ('we must meet the common foe'). 'Kinsmen' implies Black solidarity but names no race; the poem nowhere mentions colour, which allowed it to be read universally. Tonya Foster calls it a call to arms for workers, in keeping with McKay's socialist politics of 1919. The word is also a rhetorical claim of kinship that creates the community it addresses.

    Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references); Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987)

  11. The few against the many l. 10

    The image of a small band fighting a vast enemy recalls Thermopylae (Herodotus, book 7) and the last stands of epic. 'Let us show us brave' is an archaic reflexive ('show ourselves brave'). 'For their thousand blows deal one death-blow' asks not for victory but for a single answering blow, the minimum of resistance. The tone is defiant rather than hopeful.

    Herodotus, Histories 7.202-228; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes

  12. 'Like men' l. 13

    Line 13 completes the structure begun in line 1. 'Like men' carries the period's language of manhood, central to Black political rhetoric of 1919: W. E. B. Du Bois's editorial 'Returning Soldiers' (The Crisis, May 1919) urged Black veterans to fight for democracy at home. Robert A. Lee (CLA Journal, 1974) notes the arc from animal to human across the poem. The phrase is the moral climax of the sonnet.

    Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references); W. E. B. Du Bois, 'Returning Soldiers', The Crisis, May 1919

  13. The pack l. 13

    Cowardice belongs to the many who attack the few. The comma between the adjectives makes each a separate charge. The rhyme pack/back closes the poem on the image of a cornered fighter turning to face his attackers. 'Pack' takes the dogs of line 3 to their logical end: the mob is not a crowd of men but a hunting pack, and the poem's final judgement is moral, charging the attackers with cowardice while the defenders, though dying, keep their courage and their humanity.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (U of Illinois P, 2004), notes

  14. The closing couplet l. 14

    'Pressed to the wall' (with one's back to the wall) means cornered. The participles 'dying, but fighting back' make the final position simultaneous and active. The heroic clinch explains why the poem travelled far beyond its occasion: prisoners in the 1971 Attica uprising are said to have passed it around, though this is unconfirmed. The story that Churchill quoted it in wartime speeches is a myth: Lee M. Jenkins (Notes and Queries, 2003) traced the story to Black writers after the war, and the Churchill Centre found no reference to it in Hansard or the Congressional Record.

    International Churchill Society, 'Myths: Churchill quoted radical poet Claude McKay'; Lee M. Jenkins, article on the Churchill-McKay legend, Notes and Queries, September 2003 (as reported by the International Churchill Society); Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references)

  15. Universality and race l. 9

    Nothing in the text says 'Black' or 'white', yet in 1919 its reference to the riots was plain to readers of The Liberator. Its openness let others claim it, as the Churchill legend shows. Some critics see the silence as strategic, allowing the poem to speak for all the oppressed and for workers generally; others regret that its anthology fame has detached it from the lynching violence that produced it.

    Wikipedia, 'If We Must Die' (publication and reception, with references); International Churchill Society, 'Myths: Churchill quoted radical poet Claude McKay'; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State UP, 1987)
