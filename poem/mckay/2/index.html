Social Commentaries

America

by Claude McKay 1889–1948

From Harlem Shadows

Social Commentaries

Although she feeds me bread of bitterness,
And sinks into my throat her tiger’s tooth,
Stealing my breath of life, I will confess
I love this cultured hell that tests my youth!
Her vigor flows like tides into my blood,
Giving me strength erect against her hate.
Her bigness sweeps my being like a flood.
Yet as a rebel fronts a king in state,
I stand within her walls with not a shred
Of terror, malice, not a word of jeer.
Darkly I gaze into the days ahead,
And see her might and granite wonders there,
Beneath the touch of Time’s unerring hand,
Like priceless treasures sinking in the sand.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and stance l. 1

    McKay (1889-1948), born in Jamaica, came to the United States in 1912 and worked as a labourer and railway waiter while publishing in radical magazines. By 1921 he was an editor of The Liberator, the socialist monthly of Max Eastman. The poem appeared there in December 1921 and in Harlem Shadows the next year. Its paradox, that the speaker loves the country that oppresses him, made it one of the most quoted poems of the early Harlem Renaissance and a precursor of later poems such as Langston Hughes's 'Let America Be America Again'.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Harlem Shadows, with an introduction by Max Eastman (New York: Harcourt, Brace, 1922); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), Claude McKay, 'America'

  2. Bread of bitterness l. 1

    Scripture speaks of 'the bread of tears' (Psalm 80:5), 'the bread of affliction' (Deuteronomy 16:3) and the 'bitter herbs' of Passover (Exodus 12:8); Lamentations 3:15 has 'He hath filled me with bitterness'. McKay's phrase compresses these: America nourishes him, but with bitterness. The alliteration on b sets the tone of hard music that runs through the sonnet. The image also suggests the speaker's dependence: he must eat what America gives him.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 80:5; Deuteronomy 16:3; Exodus 12:8; Lamentations 3:15; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  3. The tiger's tooth l. 2

    The image is violently physical: the country is a tiger killing its prey. Some readers hear Blake's 'The Tyger', whose terrible energy is also a kind of splendour, and the sonnet does go on to admire America's 'vigor' and 'bigness'. 'Breath of life' recalls Genesis 2:7, where God breathes life into Adam; America steals what God gave. The predatory figure contrasts with the passive, erect resistance of the speaker later in the octave.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:7; William Blake, 'The Tyger', Songs of Experience (1794); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  4. 'this cultured hell' l. 4

    'Cultured' can mean refined and sophisticated, so that the line acknowledges America's civilisation while condemning its racism; it can also suggest a hell deliberately cultivated, made by human (white) culture. 'Tests my youth' implies an ordeal or trial that strengthens. Critics such as Cooper and Tillery read the line as the heart of McKay's divided allegiance, a radical immigrant's attraction to American energy combined with outrage at its treatment of Black people.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  5. Strength from the enemy ll. 5-7

    The second quatrain reverses the first: America's 'vigor' and 'bigness' flood into him like tides, giving him 'strength erect against her hate'. The paradox is that resistance draws its power from what it resists. Images of tides and flood suggest something elemental and overwhelming, which the speaker absorbs rather than drowns in. Critics have linked this to McKay's admiration for the industrial power of the modern city, which he also celebrated in poems such as 'The White City'.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  6. Rebel before a king l. 8

    'Fronts' means confronts, faces; 'in state' means seated in royal pomp and ceremony. The simile, set at the traditional turn of the sonnet (here introduced by 'Yet' at l. 8), makes the speaker's stance political, recalling revolutionary language. The following lines insist on dignity: 'not a shred / Of terror, malice, not a word of jeer'. Readers have noted that a rebel 'within her walls' implies that the speaker is an insider, a citizen-rebel rather than an outsider.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'front, v.'; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987)

  7. Dark prophecy ll. 11-12

    'Darkly' suggests both foreboding and the speaker's own colour; it may also recall 1 Corinthians 13:12, 'we see through a glass, darkly'. The 'granite wonders' are New York's monumental buildings and skyscrapers. The prophetic stance gives the poem its final authority: the oppressed see further than the powerful. The turn to the future also changes the sonnet's time-frame, from present endurance to historical judgement.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 13:12; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992)

  8. Treasures in the sand ll. 13-14

    Shelley's sonnet ends with the 'lone and level sands' around a fallen royal statue, and McKay's sonnet echoes its theme of imperial ruin. The image is not triumphant: the treasures are 'priceless', so the speaker mourns as well as foresees their loss. 'Time's unerring hand' is a traditional personification. Critics differ on whether the ending is a curse, a warning or a lament; most see all three.

    Percy Bysshe Shelley, 'Ozymandias' (1818); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987)

  9. Shakespearean sonnet whole poem

    McKay favoured the English sonnet for his protest poems, a choice some modernists thought old-fashioned. The form's argumentative structure suits the poem: two quatrains of oppression and strength, a third of defiance and vision, and a couplet of prophecy. Some critics see McKay's mastery of a canonical English form as itself an assertion of cultural equality, while others note the tension between radical content and traditional form.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); George Hutchinson, The Harlem Renaissance in Black and White (Harvard University Press, 1995)
