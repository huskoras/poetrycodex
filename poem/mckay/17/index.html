Harlem Shadows

by Claude McKay 1889–1948

From Harlem Shadows

Social Commentaries

I hear the halting footsteps of a lass
In Negro Harlem when the night lets fall
Its veil. I see the shapes of girls who pass
To bend and barter at desire’s call.
Ah, little dark girls who in slippered feet
Go prowling through the night from street to street!

Through the long night until the silver break
Of day the little gray feet know no rest;
Through the lone night until the last snow-flake
Has dropped from heaven upon the earth’s white breast,
The dusky, half-clad girls of tired feet
Are trudging, thinly shod, from street to street.

Ah, stern harsh world, that in the wretched way
Of poverty, dishonor and disgrace,
Has pushed the timid little feet of clay,
The sacred brown feet of my fallen race!
Ah, heart of me, the weary, weary feet
In Harlem wandering from street to street.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and subject l. 1

    The poem appeared in Pearson's Magazine, edited by Frank Harris, who championed McKay's early work, and gave its name to his first American collection. It treats young Black women working as prostitutes on the night streets of Harlem with compassion rather than moral condemnation. In 1918 Harlem was becoming the largest Black community in the United States as migrants arrived from the South and the Caribbean, and McKay himself was living there.

    Claude McKay's Early Poetry (1911-1922): A Digital Collection (Lehigh University), publication timeline; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Harlem Shadows, with an introduction by Max Eastman (New York: Harcourt, Brace, 1922)

  2. Negro Harlem l. 2

    Harlem was developed as a white suburb in the late nineteenth century; after a housing collapse around 1904-05, Black tenants began to move into its new apartment blocks, and with the Great Migration it became the cultural capital of Black America. McKay's 'Negro Harlem' marks the neighbourhood's racial identity, already familiar enough to need no explanation. The poem's night scene is a counterpart to the glamorous 'Harlem' of cabaret writing.

    Gilbert Osofsky, Harlem: The Making of a Ghetto, 1890-1930 (Harper and Row, 1966); George Hutchinson, The Harlem Renaissance in Black and White (Harvard University Press, 1995)

  3. Night's veil ll. 2-3

    The veil suggests modesty, concealment and mourning at once. Some readers also connect it with Du Bois's 'veil' of the colour line in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), behind which Black life is hidden from white sight. Enjambment across the line-break makes the veil 'fall' visibly. The image also prepares the poem's title: these are 'shadows', figures half-seen in darkness.

    W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk (1903), ch. 1; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  4. bend and barter l. 4

    'Bend' means both to stoop and to yield; 'barter' means to trade, usually goods for goods, here sex for payment. The alliteration on b links the two. The phrase states the economic fact of prostitution plainly, without the language of sin. 'Desire's call' places the agency with the men who buy; the girls respond under economic compulsion, a point the final stanza makes explicit.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'barter, v.'; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  5. Feet l. 5

    Each stanza focuses on feet: 'slippered feet' (l. 5), 'little gray feet' (l. 8), 'tired feet' (l. 11), 'feet of clay' and 'sacred brown feet' (ll. 15-16), 'weary, weary feet' (l. 17). The synecdoche makes the women anonymous but also intimately seen, and the repetition mimics their pacing. Thin shoes in winter emphasise poverty: these are girls, 'little dark girls', not the glamorous 'vamps' of popular fiction.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987)

  6. Stanza and refrain ll. 6, 12, 18

    The stanza is that of Shakespeare's Venus and Adonis, a quatrain followed by a couplet. The couplets end each time on 'feet / street', and the repeated phrase captures the aimless, compulsive walking of the women. The variation in the last line ('In Harlem wandering') shifts from 'prowling' and 'trudging' to wandering, a word of lostness that gathers the speaker's sympathy.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Paul Fussell, Poetic Meter and Poetic Form, rev. ed. (McGraw-Hill, 1979)

  7. Snow on the earth's breast ll. 9-10

    The whiteness of the snow and of 'the earth's white breast' contrasts with the 'dusky' girls, and with the sexual commerce of the street; the purity of the natural world is untouched by what happens beneath it. Some readers see a racial irony in the white 'heaven' that falls on the dark girls; others read it primarily as an image of the long night's cold.

    Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes; Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992)

  8. Feet of clay l. 15

    The idiom 'feet of clay' normally means a hidden weakness in someone revered, from the statue in Daniel 2:31-35. McKay uses it more literally and tenderly: the girls are made of frail human clay (Genesis 2:7), pushed by a 'stern harsh world' of poverty. Placing 'feet of clay' next to 'sacred brown feet' in the next line refuses to let their frailty cancel their sacredness.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Daniel 2:31-35; Genesis 2:7; Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  9. 'my fallen race' l. 16

    'Fallen woman' was the Victorian euphemism for a woman who had lost her sexual honour, and McKay extends the adjective to the whole race, 'fallen' through slavery and poverty rather than through sin. The possessive 'my' marks his solidarity. Critics differ on the poem's sexual politics: some praise its sympathy and its placing of blame on the 'stern harsh world'; others note the male speaker's sentimental idealisation of women he does not let speak.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Tyrone Tillery, Claude McKay: A Black Poet's Struggle for Identity (University of Massachusetts Press, 1992); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes

  10. 'heart of me' l. 17

    The phrase has the ring of ballad and lament. The address turns the closing refrain inward: the speaker, like the girls, is a wanderer, a Black migrant in Harlem.

    Wayne F. Cooper, Claude McKay: Rebel Sojourner in the Harlem Renaissance (Louisiana State University Press, 1987); Claude McKay, Complete Poems, ed. William J. Maxwell (University of Illinois Press, 2004), notes
