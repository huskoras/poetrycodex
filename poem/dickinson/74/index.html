Death & Dying

There's a certain slant of light

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Death & Dying

There's a certain slant of light,
On winter afternoons,
That oppresses, like the weight
Of cathedral tunes.

Heavenly hurt it gives us;
We can find no scar,
But internal difference
Where the meanings are.

None may teach it anything,
'T is the seal, despair, --
An imperial affliction
Sent us of the air.

When it comes, the landscape listens,
Shadows hold their breath;
When it goes, 't is like the distance
On the look of death.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and this text l. 1

    Dickinson copied the poem into one of her hand-sewn booklets, Fascicle 13, about 1862, the year of her greatest productivity; it is F320 in Franklin's variorum and J258 in Johnson. It first appeared after her death in Poems (1890), edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and Thomas Wentworth Higginson, in the 'Nature' section and under the editors' title 'Winter'. The archive prints that 1890 text, which regularises her dashes and capitals and alters several words: the manuscript has 'Heft' for 'weight' (l. 3), 'Winter Afternoons' without 'On' (l. 2), and 'Any' for 'anything' (l. 9). Readers wanting Dickinson's own text should consult Johnson or Franklin. Charles R. Anderson called it her 'finest poem on despair'.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J258; Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire, 'There's a certain slant of light' (1890 text, with notes on manuscript readings); Charles R. Anderson, Emily Dickinson's Poetry: Stairway of Surprise (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1960)

  2. slant l. 1

    Literally, 'slant' describes the low angle of winter sunlight in late afternoon in New England, which lengthens shadows and thins colour. The word also carries a mental sense: something that strikes the mind obliquely rather than directly. Dickinson uses 'slant' elsewhere for indirection as a mode of truth-telling ('Tell all the truth but tell it slant'), and here too the experience is felt sideways, as a mood that cannot be named directly. 'A certain' is both precise and evasive: the speaker recognises this particular light but can describe it only through comparisons.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'slant, n.'; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  3. weight (MS 'Heft') l. 3

    Dickinson's manuscript reads 'Heft', a word the OED records in the sense of weight or heaviness, especially as judged by lifting; it was current in American and dialect speech and carries the physical effort of bearing a load. Todd and Higginson substituted the more literary 'weight', probably thinking 'Heft' rustic. The change matters: 'Heft' makes the oppression bodily and muscular, as if the light had to be lifted, while 'weight' is more abstract. RPO glosses 'weight' as solemnity or 'weightiness', a sense that suits the comparison with church music. The line is a synaesthetic simile: light oppresses like the heaviness of sound.

    Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire, 'There's a certain slant of light' (1890 text, with notes on manuscript readings); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'heft, n.'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  4. cathedral tunes l. 4

    The simile compares a visual experience (light) with an aural one (music), and both with a physical one (weight). Amherst had no cathedral, and Dickinson's own Congregational tradition used hymns rather than cathedral liturgy, so 'cathedral tunes' suggests grandeur and religious solemnity in general: massive, slow, ponderous music that presses on the listener rather than consoling him. John P. Wargacki examined this synaesthetic simile in detail. The religious vocabulary, once introduced, runs through the poem ('Heavenly', 'seal', 'imperial', 'affliction').

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320; John P. Wargacki, note on the poem, The Explicator (2011); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  5. Heavenly hurt l. 5

    'Heavenly' can mean 'coming from heaven' (the hurt is sent by God, or from the sky) and 'sublime, exquisite' in the colloquial sense. Both are active: the light descends from the sky, and the pain it brings is strangely beautiful. The alliteration of h-h binds the paradox. The pronoun 'us' makes the experience communal rather than private; Dickinson presents it as something everyone who has seen such light will recognise. Paula Bennett has stressed how hard the poem is to pin down because it gives no grounded speaker or setting.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320; Paula Bennett, Emily Dickinson: Woman Poet (Iowa UP, 1990); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'heavenly, adj.'

  6. internal difference ll. 6-8

    The second stanza distinguishes between outward and inward wounds. There is 'no scar', no mark on the body, but there is an 'internal difference' located 'Where the meanings are', that is, in the mind or soul where experience is interpreted. The phrase is abstract and almost philosophical; Dickinson's point is that the change is in how things mean rather than in what they are. Sharon Cameron argued that the poem holds two explanations in suspension: the landscape causes the despair, and the despair is projected on to the landscape. This line is where that interchange happens.

    Sharon Cameron, Lyric Time: Dickinson and the Limits of Genre (Johns Hopkins UP, 1979); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320

  7. None may teach it anything l. 9

    The line is ambiguous. 'It' may be the light or the despair it brings: no one can teach it anything, it is beyond correction or consolation. Alternatively 'teach it' may mean 'teach it to anyone', i.e. it cannot be learned from others, only suffered. The manuscript reads 'None may teach it - Any -', where 'Any' stands alone, either as an object ('anything') or as a delayed subject ('none ... any[one]'), making the syntax even more open. The 1890 'anything' resolves an ambiguity Dickinson seems to have kept.

    Representative Poetry Online, ed. Ian Lancashire, 'There's a certain slant of light' (1890 text, with notes on manuscript readings); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320

  8. the seal, despair l. 10

    A seal confirms authenticity and finality; it is pressed into wax on a royal or legal document. Scriptural seals deepen the sense: in 2 Corinthians 1:22 God 'hath also sealed us', marking believers as his; in Revelation 6-8 the opening of seven seals releases the catastrophes of the last days. Dickinson inverts the consoling seal of election into the seal of despair. Helen Vendler notes that despair was, in nineteenth-century Protestant teaching, one of the two sins against hope (with presumption) that could bar the soul from heaven, so the word is theologically heavy. The 1890 commas make 'despair' an appositive; the manuscript ('the Seal Despair') fuses the two nouns.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 2 Corinthians 1:22; Revelation 6-8; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  9. imperial affliction l. 11

    'Imperial' continues the language of seals and authority: the affliction comes from an emperor, perhaps God, and cannot be refused or appealed. 'Affliction' (Latin affligere, to strike down) is the word of Job and the Psalms for suffering sent from above, often as a test. The phrase is grand and Latinate, set against the plain monosyllables around it. Readers differ on whether the 'imperial' sender is God, nature, or simply the sky; Bennett notes that the poem keeps 'hurt' and 'affliction' unexplained.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'imperial, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'affliction, n.'; Paula Bennett, Emily Dickinson: Woman Poet (Iowa UP, 1990)

  10. Sent us of the air l. 12

    'Of' here has its older sense 'from' or 'by', as in the King James phrasing 'tempted of the devil'. The affliction is 'sent ... of the air', delivered by the atmosphere, by the light itself. The phrase brings the grand abstractions of the stanza back to the physical scene of l. 1, and it leaves the ultimate sender unnamed. Some readers hear 'air' also as 'heir' or as 'aria' (a tune, picking up 'cathedral tunes'), but these puns are speculative.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'of, prep.'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  11. the landscape listens ll. 13-14

    The final stanza turns from the inner to the outer world, and the landscape becomes a living, frightened creature. 'Listens' is a strange verb for light (again synaesthetic: we expect the landscape to look), and 'Shadows hold their breath' gives the long winter shadows a human tension. Jonathan Holden argued that this personification makes the landscape the poem's real protagonist. Cameron reads the stanza as the point where the boundary between the speaker's mind and the scene dissolves.

    Sharon Cameron, Lyric Time: Dickinson and the Limits of Genre (Johns Hopkins UP, 1979); Jonathan Holden, Style and Authenticity in Postmodern Poetry (Missouri UP, 1986); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320

  12. the distance / On the look of death ll. 15-16

    The closing simile is one of Dickinson's most discussed. The 'distance / On the look of death' is commonly taken as the remote, unfocused gaze of the dying or the newly dead, a look that seems already elsewhere. The light's departure thus does not relieve the despair: it leaves a vacancy as final as death. Ed Folsom read the ending as typical of Dickinson's winter poems, where winter strips away the hope of transcendence. The poem therefore moves from oppression (st. 1) through inner change (st. 2-3) to an emptiness worse than the hurt.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F320; Ed Folsom, '"The Souls That Snow": Winter in the Poetry of Emily Dickinson', American Literature 47 (1975); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F320

  13. Metre and rhyme st. 1-4

    Like most of Dickinson's verse, the poem adapts the quatrains of Protestant hymnody, but it compresses them: the lines are short (mostly five to seven syllables) and the stress pattern is loose, shifting between iambic and trochaic movement, so that only the last stanza settles fully into a ballad-like beat. Only the second and fourth lines of each stanza rhyme (afternoons/tunes, scar/are, despair/air, breath/death); the first and last pairs are slant rhymes, the middle two full rhymes. The 1890 text loses the manuscript's dashes, which create pauses and suspensions; compare the manuscript images on the Emily Dickinson Archive.

    Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J258; Emily Dickinson Archive (manuscript images, Houghton Library); Cristanne Miller, ed., Emily Dickinson's Poems: As She Preserved Them (Harvard UP, 2016), introduction on metre
