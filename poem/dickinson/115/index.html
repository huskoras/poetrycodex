I'm nobody! Who are you

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Living

I'm nobody! Who are you?
Are you nobody, too?
Then there 's a pair of us -- don't tell!
They 'd banish us, you know.

How dreary to be somebody!
How public, like a frog
To tell your name the livelong day
To an admiring bog!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, text and publication l. 1

    Dickinson left the poem in a fascicle of about 1861; it was published in Poems, Second Series (1891), edited by T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd, and the archive reproduces that text. The editors regularised her dashes and punctuation and altered two words: where the archive reads 'banish us', Franklin's manuscript-based text has 'advertise', and the final stanza's 'the livelong day' replaces 'the livelong June'. Students quoting the poem should know which version they are using. The opening exclamation, with its sudden direct question to the reader, is typical of Dickinson's conversational openings: the reader is drawn into complicity before the poem has properly begun.

    Poems by Emily Dickinson, Second Series, ed. T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd (Roberts Brothers, 1891), Project Gutenberg; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Academy of American Poets, 'I'm Nobody! Who are you? (260)', text from Franklin's edition

  2. nobody / somebody ll. 1-2

    'Somebody' and 'nobody' were everyday words for persons of importance and of none (OED). The speaker claims nobody-hood as a secret club of two. Classically minded readers have heard a faint echo of Odysseus, who tells the Cyclops his name is 'Nobody' (Outis) and escapes by that anonymity (Odyssey 9.366-67); the echo is unprovable but apt, since here too namelessness is a form of protection. Biographically, the poem is often linked to Dickinson's refusal to seek publication, though it dates from before her correspondence with Higginson began in April 1862, and it should not be read only as autobiography.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nobody'; Homer, Odyssey 9.364-67 (Odysseus' name 'Outis'); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288)

  3. 'a pair of us -- don't tell!' l. 3

    The joke is that being nobody, which ought to be nothing, becomes a secret worth keeping: if the two were discovered, they would lose their anonymity. The hushed 'don't tell!' gives the poem the tone of children whispering, and the conspiratorial pair is the opposite of the public 'somebody' with an audience of a whole bog. Dickinson's poems often address an unnamed 'you' whose identity remains open: here it is any reader willing to admit to obscurity.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  4. 'They 'd banish us' (manuscript 'advertise') l. 4

    In Franklin's text the somebodies would 'advertise' the pair: publicise them, and so destroy their precious nobody-hood by making them somebodies. That is the sharper paradox, since the threat is fame, and it fits the second stanza's satire on publicity. The editors' 'banish' makes the threat exclusion, a more conventional idea of the unconventional pair as outcasts. Most modern editors and critics follow the manuscript, and the change is a standard example of how the early editions normalised Dickinson's wit. 'Advertise' in Webster's 1844 sense also meant simply to inform or make known.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Academy of American Poets, 'I'm Nobody! Who are you? (260)', text from Franklin's edition; Poems by Emily Dickinson, Second Series, ed. T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd (Roberts Brothers, 1891), Project Gutenberg; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  5. 'How dreary to be somebody!' l. 5

    The adjective 'dreary' is usually attached to loneliness; here it describes fame. The stanza is a satire on public life: politicians, lecturers and, by implication, published poets who must constantly tell their name. Dickinson's America was a culture of the lyceum lecture, the stump speech and the newspaper, and Amherst itself was a political town (her father, Edward Dickinson, served in Congress in 1853-55). 'How public' makes publicity a kind of indecency.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Alfred Habegger, My Wars Are Laid Away in Books: The Life of Emily Dickinson (Random House, 2001)

  6. like a frog l. 6

    The comic simile reduces the 'somebody' to a frog whose only song is its own name, endlessly repeated. Frog-calls were proverbially monotonous, and the frog who puffs himself up is also a fable figure of vanity (Aesop's frog who swells to rival an ox). The joke depends on the sound: the frog's croak is a self-advertisement that only a bog could admire.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Aesop, 'The Frog and the Ox', in Aesop's Fables, trans. V. S. Vernon Jones (1912), Project Gutenberg

  7. the livelong day (manuscript 'June') l. 7

    'Livelong' (from 'lief' + 'long', dear and long) is an intensive used of time felt as tediously long (OED). 'The livelong day' is a stock phrase; Dickinson's 'the livelong June' is fresher and more exact, since June is the season when frogs call loudest, and it makes the self-advertisement last a whole month rather than a day. As with 'banish', the editors replaced a surprising word with an expected one.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'livelong'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Academy of American Poets, 'I'm Nobody! Who are you? (260)', text from Franklin's edition

  8. an admiring bog l. 8

    The poem's final rhyme 'frog / bog' clinches the satire: the public that admires celebrity is itself a mire, and the frog is exactly the kind of somebody such a bog would admire. 'Admiring' here keeps its older sense of 'wondering at' as well as approving. The form, two quatrains in loose common metre with an exclamatory, almost nursery-rhyme lightness, makes the attack seem playful; its target, the reputation economy of public life, is serious.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr260 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J288); Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary; Poems by Emily Dickinson, Second Series, ed. T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd (Roberts Brothers, 1891), Project Gutenberg
