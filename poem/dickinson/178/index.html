Nature

Wild nights! Wild nights

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Nature

Wild nights! Wild nights!
Were I with thee,
Wild nights should be
Our luxury!

Futile the winds
To a heart in port, --
Done with the compass,
Done with the chart.

Rowing in Eden!
Ah! the sea!
Might I but moor
To-night in thee!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its publication l. 1

    Dickinson wrote the poem about 1861. It appeared in Poems: Second Series (1891), edited by Thomas Wentworth Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd. Higginson told Todd that this was the one poem he hesitated to print, 'lest the malignant read into it more' than its author intended, a revealing comment on how openly erotic it seemed then. The exclamatory repetition of the opening announces passion rather than describes a storm. The text here follows the early printed version, with its exclamation marks and the dash in line 6, not the manuscript's dashes and capitals.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), no. 249; Wikipedia, 'Wild Nights - Wild Nights!' (publication history and Higginson's letter)

  2. Were I with thee l. 2

    'Were I with thee' is conditional: the wild nights are imagined, not enjoyed. The poem is therefore about longing and anticipation. The beloved, 'thee', is never named or given a gender. Critics have proposed various addressees, including the unknown 'Master' of Dickinson's letters, and, in Martha Nell Smith's influential readings, Susan Gilbert Dickinson, the poet's sister-in-law and close correspondent. Smith titled her study of Dickinson's manuscripts and her relationship with Susan Rowing in Eden, after line 9. The archaic 'thee' also gives the poem the solemnity of prayer.

    Martha Nell Smith, Rowing in Eden: Rereading Emily Dickinson (University of Texas Press, 1992); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269

  3. luxury l. 4

    From Latin luxuria (excess, extravagance), 'luxury' in earlier English often meant lechery: Shakespeare's fairies sing 'Fie on lust and luxury!' (The Merry Wives of Windsor 5.5), and Lear cries 'To't, luxury, pell-mell!' (King Lear 4.6). By Dickinson's time the main sense was indulgence or rare pleasure, but the older meaning was still available to a reader of Shakespeare, as she was. The word makes the wild nights a shared self-indulgence and gives the poem its erotic charge, which is what Higginson feared readers would notice.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'luxury'; William Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor 5.5; King Lear 4.6; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  4. Futile the winds l. 5

    The inverted syntax (adjective first, verb omitted) is compressed and emphatic. 'Futile' (from Latin futilis, 'that easily pours out', hence vain or useless) declares the winds powerless. The storm of the opening, the 'wild nights', no longer threatens. A heart that has found its harbour is safe from the weather, so the poem turns passion's storm into the calm of arrival. Some readers find a paradox here: the 'wild' nights are wild only in the joy of union, not in danger.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'futile'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  5. a heart in port l. 6

    The poem now develops its sea conceit. The heart is a vessel, and being with the beloved is being in port. The image draws on a long tradition of the soul or lover as a ship at sea. Dickinson, who rarely travelled and never went to sea, often used sea and voyage as figures for emotional and spiritual extremity. Here, unusually, the voyage ends in safety rather than shipwreck.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  6. Done with the compass, / Done with the chart l. 7

    Compass and chart are the instruments of navigation, needed only while one is still at sea and looking for a way. Once in port they are useless. Figuratively, the lines throw off rules, prudence and moral bearings: arriving in love means one no longer steers by convention. The anaphora ('Done with ... / Done with ...') has the ring of a vow of renunciation.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  7. Rowing in Eden l. 9

    Eden is the garden God planted for Adam and Eve before the Fall (Genesis 2:8-25); the Hebrew name was traditionally linked with 'delight'. Here it is strangely a place one rows in, so the paradise garden merges with the sea of the conceit. The phrase suggests innocent, unfallen pleasure, and Genesis 2 ends with the man and woman 'naked, and ... not ashamed' (2:25). It also suggests effort: rowing is labour, not drifting. Martha Nell Smith borrowed the phrase for her book on Dickinson and Susan Gilbert.

    The Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:8-25; Martha Nell Smith, Rowing in Eden: Rereading Emily Dickinson (1992)

  8. moor / To-night in thee l. 11

    'Moor' means to secure a vessel with ropes or anchors at a berth. The wish to 'moor ... in thee' makes the beloved the harbour and the speaker the boat, which reverses the conventional gendering of desire if the speaker is read as a woman. The optative 'Might I but' repeats the longing of 'Were I with thee': the poem ends on a wish, not on fulfilment. 'To-night' brings the timeless Eden back to a particular night. Critics differ on how sexual the image is meant to be, but few now accept Higginson's view of a 'virgin recluse' who meant nothing of the kind.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'moor, v.'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269; Martha Nell Smith, Rowing in Eden (1992)

  9. Form l. 8

    The poem is tiny: twelve lines, mostly of two stresses, many ending with exclamation marks. The short lines enact breathlessness. The rhymes and near-rhymes cluster on the long 'ee' vowel (thee, luxury, sea, thee), so that the beloved's pronoun echoes through the poem; the middle stanza pairs port with chart in a slant rhyme. The movement is from storm (stanza 1), to harbour (stanza 2), to the paradise sea and the wish to moor (stanza 3).

    Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), no. 249; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 269
