Death & Dying

Because I could not stop for Death

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Death & Dying

Because I could not stop for Death,
He kindly stopped for me;
The carriage held but just ourselves
And Immortality.

We slowly drove, he knew no haste,
And I had put away
My labor, and my leisure too,
For his civility.

We passed the school where children played,
Their lessons scarcely done;
We passed the fields of gazing grain,
We passed the setting sun.

We paused before a house that seemed
A swelling of the ground;
The roof was scarcely visible,
The cornice but a mound.

Since then 't is centuries; but each
Feels shorter than the day
I first surmised the horses' heads
Were toward eternity.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and this text l. 1

    Dickinson copied the poem into Fascicle 23 about 1862-63; Franklin numbers it F479, Johnson J712. It first appeared after her death in Poems (1890), edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and Thomas Wentworth Higginson, who titled it 'The Chariot'. The text here is that 1890 version: its punctuation is regularised (commas and semicolons for her dashes), the house stanza has been re-rhymed, and a whole stanza of the manuscript (the dews, the gossamer gown and tulle tippet) is missing between 'setting sun' and 'We paused'. Readers who want Dickinson's own poem should consult Johnson (1955) or Franklin (1998). Mary Loeffelholz notes that the version Allen Tate famously praised was this altered one. The opening sets the poem's tone: a busy speaker who 'could not stop' is stopped for, politely, by someone else.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J712; Dartmouth 'White Heat' seminar page on F479/J712 (manuscript text, Fascicle 23); Representative Poetry Online, 'The Chariot' (1890 text, with notes); Mary Loeffelholz, 'Faith and Doubt', in The Value of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge UP, 2016)

  2. Death as gentleman caller l. 2

    The personification is the poem's central conceit: Death is not a skeleton or reaper but a gentleman with a carriage who calls for the speaker, as a suitor might take a young woman out driving. 'Kindly' carries both its senses, 'courteously' and 'benevolently', and also a quiet irony, since his kindness is to end her life. Critics differ on how far to press the courtship: some read Death as a suitor and the drive as a kind of wedding journey (a reading strengthened by the bridal gown and veil-like tulle of the omitted manuscript stanza); others stress his menace behind the manners, a seducer or abductor whose 'civility' disarms. Many readers also stress the speaker's slowly dawning understanding of where the drive leads. Either way, the domestic social register makes the unthinkable sound ordinary.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479; Allen Tate, 'Emily Dickinson', in Reactionary Essays on Poetry and Ideas (1936); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479

  3. Immortality, the third passenger l. 4

    The carriage holds 'but just ourselves / And Immortality': the speaker, Death, and an abstraction who never speaks or acts again in the poem. Readers often see Immortality as a chaperone, completing the courtship decorum of a gentleman driving out with a lady. The pairing also states the Christian promise that death leads to eternal life, yet the poem never shows that promise fulfilled: the drive ends at a grave and the last word, 'eternity', is a direction rather than an arrival. Allen Tate praised the poem for holding such ideas in concrete images without explaining them; others find in the silent third passenger a hint that immortality is assumed rather than experienced.

    Allen Tate, 'Emily Dickinson', in Reactionary Essays on Poetry and Ideas (1936); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479

  4. Common metre st. 2

    Like most of Dickinson's poems, this one is in common metre (8.6.8.6 syllables, iambic tetrameter alternating with trimeter, rhyming on the second and fourth lines), the metre of the Protestant hymns she grew up with, especially those of Isaac Watts, and also of the ballad. The steady, unhurried rhythm suits the line's content: 'he knew no haste'. Dickinson often uses the hymn's form to question the hymn's certainties, and the regular pace here can sound either serene or eerily slow. Her rhymes are frequently slant ('me' / 'Immortality', 'away' / 'civility'), which keeps the familiar music slightly off true.

    Victoria N. Morgan, Emily Dickinson and Hymn Culture: Tradition and Experience (Ashgate, 2010); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010)

  5. civility l. 8

    Webster's dictionary, which Dickinson used, defines civility as politeness, 'good breeding' and courteous behaviour (from Latin civilitas, the conduct proper to a citizen). The speaker 'put away' her labour and her leisure, that is, all the activities of life, in return for his courtesy, as a well-mannered lady sets aside her work to receive a caller. 'Put away' may also hint at laying aside things one will not need again. The stanza's gentle social comedy carries a hard meaning: the whole of a life is surrendered as a matter of etiquette.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'civility'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479

  6. The three 'passed' scenes st. 3

    The anaphora 'We passed ... We passed ... We passed' moves the carriage through a sequence widely read as the stages of a life: children at school, grain ripe in the fields, the setting sun. The drive thus replays the speaker's life in reverse perspective, as she leaves each stage behind. The 1890 text reads 'Their lessons scarcely done'; Dickinson's manuscript has the children striving 'At Recess - in the Ring', a livelier picture of play and competition that the editors replaced. The repetitions also create the sense of a journey seen from a moving vehicle, each scene glimpsed and gone.

    Dartmouth 'White Heat' seminar page on F479/J712 (manuscript text, Fascicle 23); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479

  7. gazing grain l. 11

    The grain cannot gaze; the epithet transfers human attention to the crop, which seems to stand and watch the carriage go by. Ripe grain suggests maturity and, in biblical idiom, the harvest of death ('all flesh is grass', Isaiah 40:6). The alliteration of 'gazing grain' slows the line. Some readers sense in the staring fields the indifference of nature to the individual death; others an almost ceremonial line of witnesses.

    The Bible, King James Version, Isaiah 40:6; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479

  8. The setting sun and the missing stanza l. 12

    The sunset completes the life sequence, but in Dickinson's manuscript the next stanza immediately revises it: rather than the carriage passing the sun, the sun passed them, and the dews grew cold; the speaker notices she is dressed only in gossamer and a tippet (shoulder cape) of tulle, clothes too thin for the night and resembling bridal or burial wear. The 1890 editors omitted this stanza entirely, so in this text the drive moves straight from sunset to the grave. The omission removes the poem's turning point, where the speaker first feels the cold of death and time begins to move independently of her. Most modern editions restore it.

    Dartmouth 'White Heat' seminar page on F479/J712 (manuscript text, Fascicle 23); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J712; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Chariot' (1890 text, with notes)

  9. The grave as house st. 4

    The carriage stops before a house that is only 'a swelling of the ground', a burial mound. The image fits the courtship fiction (the gentleman brings the lady home) while revealing that the destination is the grave. Biblical language had long called the grave a house: Job speaks of death as 'the house appointed for all living' (Job 30:23). The word 'seemed' records the speaker's uncertainty, the first sign that what she sees is not what it first appeared.

    The Bible, King James Version, Job 30:23; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479

  10. cornice l. 16

    A cornice is the ornamental moulding that crowns a building's wall under the roof (Italian cornice, ultimately from Greek koronis, a curved stroke or finial). The architectural word dignifies the grave as a house while showing how little of it is visible. Dickinson's manuscript reads 'The Cornice - in the Ground -', which repeats 'Ground' from the stanza's first line (a rhyme of the word with itself) and pushes the house below the surface. The 1890 editors substituted 'but a mound', creating a more regular rhyme and losing the sense of a building sunk into the earth. The change is a standard example of how the first editors smoothed Dickinson's poems.

    Dartmouth 'White Heat' seminar page on F479/J712 (manuscript text, Fascicle 23); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J712; Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'cornice'; Representative Poetry Online, 'The Chariot' (1890 text, with notes)

  11. The speaker's time after death l. 17

    The last stanza reveals that the whole poem is spoken from beyond death, centuries after the drive. Time is now paradoxical: centuries 'feel shorter than the day' on which she understood her destination. That day, perhaps the moment of dying, has an intensity that dwarfs eternity. Dickinson frequently writes from the perspective of the dead (compare 'I heard a Fly buzz - when I died'), and the effect is to make the afterlife a matter of consciousness rather than of doctrine: we hear what the dead speaker felt, not where she is.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F479; Allen Tate, 'Emily Dickinson', in Reactionary Essays on Poetry and Ideas (1936)

  12. surmised l. 19

    To surmise is to suppose or infer on slight evidence (Webster). The word is carefully tentative: even now the speaker records a guess, not a revelation. She realised the direction only by noticing which way the horses' heads were pointed, a small concrete detail standing in for a vast knowledge. The line suggests that the courtesy of the drive concealed its purpose until late.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'surmise'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479

  13. 'toward eternity' l. 20

    The final word gives the destination as 'eternity' but places the speaker only 'toward' it, still travelling after centuries. Readers divide on the effect. Some take the ending as calm and consoling: Death has been kind, Immortality rode with them, and the journey continues into eternal life. Others note that the only place reached is a grave, that the speaker has merely 'surmised' the goal, and that eternity remains an endless approach rather than a fulfilment. Allen Tate praised the poem as one of the greatest in English for fusing such ideas with exact images; Loeffelholz reminds us that he was reading the 1890 text.

    Allen Tate, 'Emily Dickinson', in Reactionary Essays on Poetry and Ideas (1936); Mary Loeffelholz, 'Faith and Doubt', in The Value of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge UP, 2016); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F479
