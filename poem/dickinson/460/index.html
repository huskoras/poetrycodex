The Mind

After great pain a formal feeling comes—

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

From Further Poems of Emily Dickinson (1929)

The Mind

After great pain a formal feeling comes—
The nerves sit ceremonious like tombs;
The stiff Heart questions—was it He that bore?
And yesterday—or centuries before?

The feet mechanical go round
A wooden way
Of ground or air or Ought,
Regardless grown,
A quartz contentment like a stone.

This is the hour of lead
Remembered if outlived
As freezing persons recollect
The snow—
First chill, then stupor, then
The letting go.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Text and date whole poem

    Dickinson wrote the poem around 1862, the year of her greatest productivity, and it remained unpublished until Further Poems of Emily Dickinson (1929), edited by her niece Martha Dickinson Bianchi and Alfred Leete Hampson. The archive reproduces that edition. It differs from Johnson's text (1955) in several ways: Johnson prints Dickinson's dashes and capitals and prints the second stanza in the same order as the 1929 text, with its own punctuation ('The Feet, mechanical, go round - / A Wooden way / Of Ground, or Air, or Ought - / Regardless grown'). The last stanza in Johnson has four lines, where the 1929 text breaks it into six. The meaning is the same, but the lineation affects the poem's pacing.

    Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J341; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (1998), Fr372; Further Poems of Emily Dickinson, ed. M. D. Bianchi and A. L. Hampson (Boston: Little, Brown, 1929)

  2. a formal feeling l. 1

    The poem describes the aftermath of a great pain rather than the pain. 'Formal' means both 'conventional, ceremonial' and 'having form only, without substance': the sufferer goes through the outward motions while inward feeling is suspended. The opening is a general statement, almost a definition, and the rest of the poem tests it on the body: nerves, heart, feet.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844)

  3. The nerves sit ceremonious like tombs l. 2

    Dickinson personifies the nerves as mourners at a funeral, sitting in ceremonious stillness, then turns the simile further: they are like the tombs themselves. The sufferer is both the mourner and the dead. 'Ceremonious' continues the vocabulary of 'formal', and the image of stone monuments anticipates the 'quartz contentment' and 'lead' of later stanzas. The poem moves through a series of stiffening materials: tombs, wood, quartz, stone, lead, snow.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J341

  4. 'was it He that bore?' l. 3

    The 'stiff Heart' cannot be sure who endured the pain or when: 'was it He that bore? / And yesterday, or centuries before?' One reading takes 'He' as the heart itself (Dickinson's personified Heart), now so detached from its experience that it asks whether it bore the pain at all. Another, supported by the capital and by 'bore', hears Christ, of whom Isaiah 53:4 says 'Surely he hath borne our griefs' and the cross: the heart's pain is so great that it merges with the Passion of 'centuries before'. Both readings convey the confusion of time and identity in shock.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Isaiah 53:3-4 (KJV); Sharon Cameron, Lyric Time: Dickinson and the Limits of Genre (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins UP, 1979)

  5. The feet mechanical go round ll. 5-6

    After the seated nerves and the stiff heart, the feet keep moving, but 'mechanical' and 'round' make the movement purposeless, like a machine or a treadmill. 'A wooden way' suggests the stiffness of the walker (wooden as lifeless) and possibly a plank path or the boards of a floor. Pain has turned the living body into an object: tomb, wood, quartz, stone.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844)

  6. Ought l. 7

    Webster's 1844 dictionary gives 'ought' as a variant of 'aught', anything. The line means that the sufferer walks over ground, air, or anything else, unable to tell or to care which: 'Regardless grown' (become heedless). Some readers also hear 'ought' as duty, the moral obligation that keeps the feet going, and 'nought', nothing, in the background.

    Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (1955), J341; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010)

  7. A quartz contentment, like a stone l. 9

    Quartz is a hard, crystalline mineral, common in New England granite. 'Contentment' should be a warm emotion; 'quartz contentment' is an oxymoron in which calm has become petrified. The redundancy of 'like a stone' (quartz is already stone) underlines the deadening. The line closes the second stanza on the poem's strongest image of the body turned to mineral. It is the opposite of real peace.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J341

  8. This is the hour of lead l. 10

    Lead is the heaviest and dullest of common metals, and in alchemical tradition the base metal of Saturn, associated with melancholy. 'Hour of lead' gives time itself weight, as in the phrase 'leaden hours'. The present tense ('This is') shifts from description to the speaker's own moment. 'Remembered if outlived' adds a chilling condition: the hour will be remembered only by those who survive it, and not all do.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Oxford English Dictionary

  9. As freezing persons recollect the snow ll. 12-15

    Survivors of freezing describe a sequence of cold, then drowsy numbness, then a giving-up in which the freezing person ceases to struggle. Dickinson uses this as an exact model for the stages of grief: the pain, the stupor described in the poem, and a final 'letting go'. Critics divide on that phrase: it may mean death (for those who do not 'outlive' the hour), surrender to numbness, or the moment when the grip of pain finally relaxes and recovery begins. The run of short, dash-divided steps imitates the slowing of consciousness, and the poem itself ends by letting go.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Cleanth Brooks and Robert Penn Warren, Understanding Poetry, commentary on 'After great pain'; Sharon Cameron, Lyric Time (1979)

  10. Form: metre breaking down whole poem

    The first stanza is in rhymed couplets of roughly iambic pentameter ('comes / tombs', 'bore / before'), a measured, formal line that suits the 'formal feeling'. The second stanza breaks into lines of varying length (four, two, three stresses) and the rhymes become uncertain ('round / grown / stone'). The last stanza moves between short and long lines and ends in a broken sequence of stages. The form enacts the content: control gives way to mechanical motion and finally to letting go.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J341
