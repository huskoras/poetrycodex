Hope is the thing with feathers

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Religion

Hope is the thing with feathers
That perches in the soul,
And sings the tune without the words,
And never stops at all,

And sweetest in the gale is heard;
And sore must be the storm
That could abash the little bird
That kept so many warm.

I 've heard it in the chillest land,
And on the strangest sea;
Yet, never, in extremity,
It asked a crumb of me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its text l. 1 (st. 1)

    The poem survives in Fascicle 13, one of the hand-sewn booklets Dickinson made around 1861; Johnson dates it 1861, and Franklin's later editions put it about 1862. Franklin numbers it 314 (Johnson 254). It was first printed in Poems: Second Series (1891), edited by Higginson and Todd, whose text the archive follows. That edition regularised Dickinson's punctuation: the manuscript puts "Hope" in quotation marks, as if the word itself were being defined, and uses dashes throughout ('And sweetest - in the Gale - is heard -'). Students quoting the poem should know which text they use; Franklin's and Miller's editions give the manuscript form.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard, 1955), no. 254; Emily Dickinson, Poems: Second Series, ed. T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd (1891) [the archive's text]; "'Hope' is the thing with feathers", Wikipedia (fascicle 13, dating, publication); Cristanne Miller, ed., Emily Dickinson's Poems: As She Preserved Them (Harvard, 2016)

  2. the thing with feathers l. 1 (st. 1)

    Dickinson often writes definition poems ('Hope is ...', 'Faith is a fine invention'), and here she defines an abstraction with a riddle: not 'a bird' but 'the thing with feathers'. The vagueness of 'thing' keeps the metaphor strange and slightly comic. Feathered hope has biblical associations: God shelters the faithful under his 'feathers' (Psalm 91:4). The bird that 'perches in the soul' turns the soul into a tree or perch, a domestic, homely image of an inner state.

    Bible (KJV), Psalms 91:4; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844

  3. Hope as a winged figure l. 2 (st. 1)

    In Hesiod's Works and Days, when Pandora opens the jar of evils, Hope (Elpis) alone remains inside; and allegorical tradition often depicted Hope with wings, an anchor or a bird. Dickinson takes the familiar personification and makes it modest: not a goddess but a 'little bird'. Hesiod's story is itself ambiguous (is Hope a blessing kept for men, or one more evil?), and Dickinson elsewhere offers a darker view: in 'Hope is a subtle Glutton' (Franklin 1493) hope feeds on what it desires. The two poems are often read together.

    Hesiod, Works and Days 90-105 (Pandora; Hope remains in the jar); Emily Dickinson, "Hope is a subtle Glutton -", Franklin no. 1493; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254)

  4. the tune without the words l. 3 (st. 1)

    Hope's song has melody but no words: it carries feeling without propositional content, as hope persists without evidence. Some readers hear an echo of the vogue for Mendelssohn's piano 'Songs without Words' (Lieder ohne Worte) in nineteenth-century parlours, though there is no evidence Dickinson intended it. 'And never stops at all' is deliberately plain; the colloquial 'at all' gives the line the tone of a child's assertion, typical of Dickinson's mixing of hymn and speech.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; Felix Mendelssohn, Lieder ohne Worte (1829-45)

  5. sweetest in the gale l. 5 (st. 2)

    A gale is a strong wind. The paradox is that the bird's song is 'sweetest' when conditions are worst, as hope matters most in trouble. Dickinson's manuscript isolates the words with dashes ('And sweetest - in the Gale - is heard -'), slowing the line so that each phrase is felt; the 1891 text runs it together. The word order (verb last, 'is heard') is a hymn-like inversion.

    Poetry Foundation, text of "'Hope' is the thing with feathers -" (314), from Franklin's edition; Emily Dickinson, Poems: Second Series, ed. T. W. Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd (1891) [the archive's text]; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844

  6. sore l. 6 (st. 2)

    'Sore' as an adjective or adverb of intensity ('sore afraid', 'a sore famine') is common in the King James Bible and was old-fashioned even in Dickinson's day. Webster defines it in this sense as violent or severe. The line implies that no ordinary storm could defeat hope; it would take an exceptional one, and the poem never says that such a storm has come.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'sore' (the dictionary of Dickinson's household, in its later editions); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; Bible (KJV), Genesis 41:56

  7. abash l. 7 (st. 2)

    'Abash' (Old French esbahir, to astonish) means to destroy someone's self-possession, to confound or make ashamed. Applied to a bird in a storm it is unexpected: the storm is imagined not as killing the bird but as discountenancing it, making it lose its nerve. The personification keeps the poem's tone light. 'That kept so many warm' gives the bird a further role: hope, though small, warms many people, perhaps like a bird brooding over its young.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'abash' (the dictionary of Dickinson's household, in its later editions); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abash, v.'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844

  8. the chillest land ... the strangest sea l. 9 (st. 3)

    The superlatives 'chillest' and 'strangest' (Dickinson freely forms such superlatives) suggest the farthest regions of experience, perhaps of despair, isolation or the unknown. Dickinson rarely travelled, so the land and sea are generally read as inner states, in keeping with the soul as the bird's perch. 'I 've heard it' (the 1891 spacing) shifts from definition to personal testimony, as in a church testimony of faith.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; Cristanne Miller, ed., Emily Dickinson's Poems: As She Preserved Them (Harvard, 2016)

  9. extremity l. 11 (st. 3)

    Webster gives 'extremity' as the utmost point, and also the utmost distress or necessity. The line praises hope's generosity: it gives warmth and song and never takes. Dickinson's manuscript reads 'Yet - never - in Extremity, / It asked a crumb - of me.', capitalising the key words and ending on the self. Some critics hear a note of irony: hope asks nothing, but does it give anything real? The poem leaves that question open.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'extremity' (the dictionary of Dickinson's household, in its later editions); Poetry Foundation, text of "'Hope' is the thing with feathers -" (314), from Franklin's edition; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254)

  10. a crumb l. 12 (st. 3)

    Feeding birds with crumbs in winter is the literal picture. A biblical undertone may be heard in Matthew 15:27, where the Canaanite woman says 'yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table', a story of persistent, humble faith rewarded. Here the roles are reversed: hope sustains the speaker and asks nothing in return. The final line closes the poem with understatement, the bird's selflessness becoming the measure of its value.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 15:27; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844

  11. Hymn metre l. 4 (st. 1)

    Dickinson's favourite form is the hymn stanza she knew from Isaac Watts and the Protestant hymnal: lines of four and three iambic stresses alternating (the first line is a seven-syllable variation on the pattern). Stanza 1 rhymes only on lines 2 and 4, and slantly ('soul/all'); stanza 2 rhymes alternately ('heard/bird', 'storm/warm'); stanza 3 rhymes 'sea/me' fully, with 'extremity' echoing them in line 3. The hymn form lends a devotional frame to a poem that never mentions God, a characteristic Dickinson strategy of using the church's music for private meanings.

    Isaac Watts, Hymns and Spiritual Songs (1707) [common-metre hymns familiar to Dickinson]; Cristanne Miller, ed., Emily Dickinson's Poems: As She Preserved Them (Harvard, 2016); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 314 (Johnson no. 254)
