Religion

The soul selects her own society

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Religion

The soul selects her own society,
Then shuts the door;
On her divine majority
Obtrude no more.

Unmoved, she notes the chariot's pausing
At her low gate;
Unmoved, an emperor is kneeling
Upon her mat.

I've known her from an ample nation
Choose one;
Then close the valves of her attention
Like stone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, text and title l. 1

    The archive's text is the edited version of Poems (1890), where Todd and Higginson called the poem 'Exclusion' and adopted two of Dickinson's alternative readings in lines 3-4. Dickinson's fascicle text reads 'To her divine Majority - / Present no more -', with 'Obtrude' as an alternative, and offers 'Rush mat' and 'lids' as variants later. The poem is one of the touchstones of 1862, Dickinson's most productive year. Its subject, choosing one companion and closing the door on all others, has been read biographically, theologically and as an Emersonian poem of self-reliance.

    'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303); Poems by Emily Dickinson, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson (Roberts Brothers, 1890), Project Gutenberg

  2. the soul's choice l. 1

    'Society' means both companionship and the social world. The soul is feminine ('her'), sovereign and self-governing. Readers connect the poem with Emerson's self-reliance and inward retreat; Some readers hear Puritan theological language of election recast in Transcendentalist terms, so that the soul's choosing mimics God's choosing of the elect. Others read a personal choice of a single beloved, or the artist's choice of solitude.

    'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F409; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303)

  3. shuts the door l. 2

    The short second line enacts the shutting. Readers have heard biblical echoes: the parable of the wise virgins, after whom 'the door was shut' (Matthew 25:10), and the injunction to pray in the closet with the door shut (Matthew 6:6). The White Heat commentary notes that each stanza moves from long lines to short ones, a pattern of closing down that becomes sharper as the poem proceeds.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:10; Matthew 6:6; 'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College

  4. her divine majority l. 3

    The word has puzzled readers. A 'majority' can be the larger number: the soul together with her chosen one, or with God, makes a majority against the world. It can also mean legal adulthood, so the soul has 'come of age' and governs herself (OED). A third, darker sense is 'the great majority', the dead (OED records 'to join the majority' for dying), which would make the chosen society otherworldly. 'Divine' may mean sacred or simply 'excellent'. The archive's 'On her' follows 1890; Dickinson wrote 'To her'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'majority'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303); 'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College

  5. Obtrude (manuscript 'Present') l. 4

    'Obtrude' means to force oneself or something on another unasked (OED). It is the more aggressive alternative; 'Present' (offer, introduce, or present oneself at court) is cooler and more ceremonious. The 1890 editors chose 'Obtrude', the archive's reading. The command is addressed to the world at large, the would-be visitors of the next stanza. Its brusque imperative, after the stately first line, is the first sign of the poem's gradual hardening into the finality of 'stone'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'obtrude'; 'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303)

  6. the chariot and the kneeling emperor ll. 5-8

    The second stanza dramatises the refusal with royal images set against domestic humility: the chariot pauses at a 'low gate', the emperor kneels on her 'mat' (Dickinson's variant is 'Rush mat', a humble woven mat). The repetition of 'Unmoved' makes her indifference ceremonial. Critics note the inversion of hierarchy: power kneels to the private soul, not the soul to power.

    'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303); Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  7. an ample nation l. 9

    'Ample' (large, spacious) makes the choice drastic: a nation against a single person. The speaker steps back and speaks of the soul in the third person ('I've known her'), as if observing someone else; the White Heat commentary notes this distancing. The stanza moves from the plural world to 'one' and then to 'stone'. 'Nation' also gives the soul's choice a political scale, continuing the regal language of 'majority', chariot and emperor: the soul behaves like a sovereign state.

    'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  8. 'Choose one' l. 10

    In Calvinist theology God chooses or 'elects' the saved, a doctrine still preached in Dickinson's Amherst. The soul's choosing mirrors that sovereign, inexplicable act, and the reading of the poem as Puritan language applied to Transcendentalist self-reliance turns on it. Whether the 'one' is a lover, a friend, God or the soul itself is left open.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F409; 'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College

  9. the valves of her attention l. 11

    'Valve' had the older sense of one leaf of a double or folding door (OED, from Latin valva), which continues the door image of line 2, and also the anatomical sense of a valve of the heart or a shell's halves. The image is precise and chilling: attention is a mechanism that can be closed. Dickinson's alternative 'lids' would suggest eyelids or the lid of a coffin. The White Heat commentary reads the image as suggesting exclusion, blindness and suffocation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'valve'; 'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303)

  10. 'Like stone': closure l. 12

    The stanzas shrink: each opens with long lines and ends with short ones, and the last lines of the third stanza are the shortest in the poem. The final 'Like stone' is two stresses in a row (a spondee), and critics hear it banging shut. The rhyme 'one / stone', exact to the eye but slant to the ear, encloses 'one' inside 'stone'. The image may recall the stone rolled before a tomb, consistent with a reading of the soul's society as death; the poem leaves the tone suspended between admiration and fear of such absolute choice.

    'The Soul selects her own Society' (F409), White Heat: Emily Dickinson in 1862, Dartmouth College; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr409 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J303)
