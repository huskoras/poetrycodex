Death & Dying

I heard a fly buzz when I died

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Death & Dying

I heard a fly buzz when I died;
The stillness round my form
Was like the stillness in the air
Between the heaves of storm.

The eyes beside had wrung them dry,
And breaths were gathering sure
For that last onset, when the king
Be witnessed in his power.

I willed my keepsakes, signed away
What portion of me I
Could make assignable, -- and then
There interposed a fly,

With blue, uncertain, stumbling buzz,
Between the light and me;
And then the windows failed, and then
I could not see to see.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and this text l. 1

    Dickinson copied the poem into Fascicle 26 about 1862-63 (Franklin F591; Johnson J465). It was first printed in Poems: Third Series (1896), edited by Mabel Loomis Todd, under the title 'Dying'. The archive's text follows that printing, which regularises Dickinson's dashes and capitals and alters several words: the manuscript has 'the Stillness in the Room' (l. 2), 'The Eyes around' (l. 5), 'Breaths were gathering firm' (l. 6) and 'Be witnessed - in the Room' (l. 8). The opening line states the poem's paradox at once: the speaker narrates her own death in the past tense, and the first thing she reports is not God or heaven but a fly.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J465; Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); Poems by Emily Dickinson, Three Series, Complete (Todd and Higginson texts, 1890-96), Project Gutenberg

  2. The stillness ll. 2-4

    The room's silence is likened to air 'Between the heaves of storm': not peace but a lull in which the next violent surge is expected. 'Heaves' are the swells or gusts of a storm, and also suggest the heaving breath of the dying. The 1896 text reads 'round my form', which makes the speaker a body observed from outside; Dickinson's manuscript has 'in the Room', locating the stillness in the space where the mourners wait. The simile sets up the expectation of a climactic 'onset' that the fly will deflate.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591

  3. The mourners l. 5

    Dickinson presents the mourners by synecdoche, as 'eyes' and 'breaths', the organs of watching and waiting. Their eyes have been 'wrung' dry like wet cloth, an image of exhausted weeping. (The manuscript reads 'The Eyes around'.) The scene draws on the nineteenth-century custom of the deathbed watch, in which family gathered to witness the dying person's last moments, which were thought to reveal the state of the soul. Barton Levi St. Armand discusses this culture of 'dying well' as a context for Dickinson's many deathbed poems.

    Barton Levi St. Armand, Emily Dickinson and Her Culture: The Soul's Society (Cambridge UP, 1984); Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591

  4. breaths gathering l. 6

    The mourners are 'gathering' their breath, steadying themselves as one does before a shock. The 1896 'sure' replaces Dickinson's 'firm', which more clearly suggests bracing for an ordeal. The line keeps the language of preparation for battle that 'onset' completes. Their breath, held for the expected arrival, contrasts with the speaker's own breathing, which is about to stop. The editors' change to 'sure' also adds a faint suggestion of certainty, as if the watchers were confident of what they would see.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J465

  5. onset l. 7

    In Webster an onset is a violent attack or assault, especially the first rush of an army. The military word makes death a final assault on the body, and the mourners are bracing for it. It also carries the evangelical sense of a last spiritual struggle at the deathbed. The phrase is grand and climactic, and the poem then deliberately fails to deliver the climax: what arrives is a fly.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'onset'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Barton Levi St. Armand, Emily Dickinson and Her Culture: The Soul's Society (Cambridge UP, 1984)

  6. 'the king / Be witnessed' ll. 7-8

    The watchers expect 'the king' to 'Be witnessed in his power'. Two readings are common. In the evangelical deathbed tradition, the dying were watched for signs that Christ or God had come to receive the soul, so the King is divine. Alternatively the King is Death, as in Job 18:14, 'the king of terrors'; RPO's note glosses the word as possibly death. The subjunctive 'Be witnessed' (rather than 'is') makes the event something hoped for or feared, not yet real. The poem never resolves the question, because what is actually witnessed is a fly. The 1896 text alters the manuscript's 'Be witnessed - in the Room -' to 'in his power', which strengthens the royal image.

    The Bible, King James Version, Job 18:14; Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); Barton Levi St. Armand, Emily Dickinson and Her Culture: The Soul's Society (Cambridge UP, 1984); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591

  7. willing and assigning ll. 9-11

    The speaker settles her affairs: she 'willed' her keepsakes and 'signed away' what portion of herself could be 'made assignable'. 'Assignable' is a legal term for property that can be transferred to another. The clause implies a remainder that is not assignable, the self or soul, which no document disposes of. The enjambment across 'I / Could' isolates the pronoun at a line end, emphasising the self that survives the paperwork. The orderly legal register is then interrupted by the dash and 'and then'.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'assignable'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591

  8. The fly l. 12

    To 'interpose' is to place oneself between, often as an obstacle. The fly arrives just where 'the king' was awaited, and readings of it vary widely. For some it is simply the banal physical world intruding on a solemn scene, a comic deflation that makes death ordinary. For others it signifies decay, since flies feed on corpses, and so the body's corruption rather than the soul's ascent. Some readers recall Beelzebub, the 'lord of flies' (Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron, 2 Kings 1:2), making the fly a parody of the King. The poem lets each reading stand; its effect depends on the gap between expectation and what is witnessed.

    The Bible, King James Version, 2 Kings 1:2; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591; Barton Levi St. Armand, Emily Dickinson and Her Culture: The Soul's Society (Cambridge UP, 1984); Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'interpose'

  9. blue, uncertain, stumbling l. 13

    The buzz is 'blue', 'uncertain' and 'stumbling': a colour, a state of mind and a gait assigned to a sound. The blending of senses suits a dying perception in which sight and hearing are failing together. 'Blue' may also identify the insect as a bluebottle, the carrion fly attracted to dead bodies. 'Uncertain' and 'stumbling' describe the fly's erratic flight, but they also fit the speaker's own faltering consciousness, so that fly and dying woman seem to merge.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), J465

  10. the light l. 14

    The fly comes 'Between the light and me', literally between the speaker's eyes and the window. In Christian deathbed literature the dying were expected to see a heavenly light; here an insect obscures it. The line therefore repeats the crux of 'interposed': the trivial creature stands where revelation should be. Christ's words 'I am the light of the world' (John 8:12) show how charged the word was for a reader of Dickinson's upbringing; the speaker's last view of light is blocked by something small and physical, and the next line records the failure of sight itself.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591; Barton Levi St. Armand, Emily Dickinson and Her Culture: The Soul's Society (Cambridge UP, 1984); The Bible, King James Version, John 8:12

  11. the windows failed l. 15

    'Windows' can be read both literally (the light from the room's windows fades for the dying woman) and figuratively, as the eyes, proverbially the windows of the soul; compare 'The light of the body is the eye' (Matthew 6:22). The verb 'failed' is plain and final, like the failing of a light or of strength. The anaphora 'And then ... and then' gives the last moments a breathless, step-by-step quality.

    The Bible, King James Version, Matthew 6:22; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591

  12. 'I could not see to see' l. 16

    The famous last line repeats 'see' in two senses. Literally, she could not see well enough to see, as one says 'too dark to see to read'. The second 'see' also suggests understanding or spiritual vision: at the moment of death she cannot perceive whatever the King or the light might have revealed. The poem ends there, without telling us what followed, though the speaker is evidently still conscious enough to narrate. Readers divide over whether the ending implies extinction, a blank afterlife, or simply the limits of what the living can report.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Harvard UP, 2010), commentary on F591; Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591

  13. Hymn metre and slant rhyme st. 1

    The poem uses common metre, the alternating tetrameter and trimeter of English hymns, here printed with the shorter lines indented. Its rhymes are often slant: 'sure' / 'power', 'I' / 'fly', and in the manuscript 'Room' / 'Storm'. The hymn form, associated with assured faith in death and resurrection, frames a deathbed that delivers no assurance. Victoria Morgan's study of Dickinson and hymn culture explores this use of the hymn's form against its content.

    Victoria N. Morgan, Emily Dickinson and Hymn Culture: Tradition and Experience (Ashgate, 2010); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), F591; Representative Poetry Online, 'Dying' (1896 text, with notes on manuscript variants)
