Death & Dying

I felt a funeral in my brain

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Death & Dying

I felt a funeral in my brain,
And mourners, to and fro,
Kept treading, treading, till it seemed
That sense was breaking through.

And when they all were seated,
A service like a drum
Kept beating, beating, till I thought
My mind was going numb.

And then I heard them lift a box,
And creak across my soul
With those same boots of lead, again.
Then space began to toll

As all the heavens were a bell,
And Being but an ear,
And I and silence some strange race,
Wrecked, solitary, here.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem, its date and text l. 1 (st. 1)

    The manuscript is in Fascicle 16; Johnson dates it 1861 and Franklin about 1862. Mabel Loomis Todd printed it in Poems: Third Series (1896), regularising punctuation (the manuscript's dashes become commas), lowering capitals ('Funeral', 'Brain', 'Being') and omitting the fifth stanza, which describes 'a Plank in Reason' breaking and the speaker falling through 'World' after 'World'. That stanza was first printed in 1947 and is now restored in modern editions. The archive's text therefore ends with 'Wrecked, solitary, here', a different conclusion from Dickinson's: an image of isolation rather than of a fall into unconsciousness.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard, 1955), no. 280; Emily Dickinson, Poems: Third Series, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd (1896) [the archive's text]; Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), "I felt a funeral in my brain", note on the omitted stanza (fascicle 16); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Manuscript Books of Emily Dickinson (Harvard, 1981), fascicle 16

  2. The funeral as inner event l. 2 (st. 1)

    Nineteenth-century Amherst funerals were domestic: mourners gathered at the house, a service was held, the coffin was carried out and the church bell tolled. Dickinson, who lived in the centre of Amherst and attended many funerals, knew the sequence well. Transferring it 'in my Brain' makes the speaker both the corpse and the witness. Critics read the crisis variously: as madness or nervous breakdown, as severe depression, as a loss of faith, or as an account of losing consciousness; the poem's precision about sensations and vagueness about cause allow all these.

    "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Richard B. Sewall, The Life of Emily Dickinson (1974)

  3. treading, treading l. 3 (st. 1)

    Repetition ('treading, treading'; 'beating, beating') is the poem's main sound device. It imitates the heavy, rhythmic sounds of the funeral and the pounding of a headache, and it slows the hymn metre into something oppressive. Each stanza has a similar shape: a ritual action, a repeated verb, and a consequence for the mind ('till it seemed', 'till I thought'). The present participles also suspend time, so that the ritual seems endless, and the relentless pattern prepares for the moment when the repetition stops and space itself begins to toll.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  4. sense was breaking through l. 4 (st. 1)

    'Sense' can mean reason, the faculty of understanding, or the bodily senses and perception. 'Breaking through' can mean collapsing (like a floor giving way, anticipating the 'Plank in Reason' of the omitted stanza) or emerging (like light breaking through). Some readers take the line to say that the treading is about to break the mind; others that a painful clarity or awareness is forcing its way in. Dickinson's dictionary (Webster) lists both 'understanding' and 'perception by the senses' among the meanings of 'sense'. The ambiguity is generally considered deliberate.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'sense' (the dictionary of Dickinson's household, in its later editions); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  5. A service like a drum l. 6 (st. 2)

    The service, which should offer words of consolation, becomes a percussive noise. The drum also recalls military funerals, where muffled drums beat time for the procession, and the pulse of blood in the head. That its effect is numbness ('My mind was going numb') suggests that ritual, far from consoling, overwhelms the speaker. This is one ground for reading the poem as sceptical of religious consolation.

    "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280)

  6. numb l. 8 (st. 2)

    Dickinson writes often about numbness as the mind's response to great pain, most famously in 'After great pain, a formal feeling comes' (Franklin 372), with its 'Hour of Lead'. Here the mind goes numb under the drumbeat; in the next stanza the boots are 'of lead', another link between the two poems. The sequence (treading, numbness, loss of space and selfhood) charts an unusually precise phenomenology of collapse.

    Emily Dickinson, "After great pain, a formal feeling comes -", Franklin no. 372; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  7. a box l. 9 (st. 3)

    Calling the coffin 'a box' is typical of Dickinson's defamiliarising understatement. The speaker hears rather than sees: the poem is entirely auditory, as if experienced from inside the coffin with eyes closed. The pallbearers 'creak across my soul', the soul becoming a floor of creaking boards: the mind is now a house, with a floor that may give way, which is exactly what happens in the manuscript's final stanza, where 'a Plank in Reason' breaks.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  8. boots of lead l. 11 (st. 3)

    Lead is proverbially heavy and dull, and in Dickinson's poems it often figures paralysing grief ('the Hour of Lead'). 'Those same boots' refers back to the treading mourners of stanza 1; the funeral has come full circle. 'Again' marks the oppressive repetition. Early coffins were sometimes lead-lined, which adds a further, literal association. The heaviness of the boots presses on the 'soul' as the treading of stanza 1 pressed on the brain, keeping the poem's movement inward and downward.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844

  9. space began to toll l. 12 (st. 3)

    At a funeral the church bell tolls (rings slowly, with pauses). Dickinson makes space itself the bell: sound fills the universe. The sentence runs on across the stanza break ('toll / As all the heavens'), the only place where it does so, enacting the sudden enlargement. The following lines ('As all the heavens were a bell, / And Being but an ear') extend the conceit: the cosmos rings and existence is reduced to hearing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'toll, v.2'; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  10. Being but an ear l. 14 (st. 4)

    'As all the heavens were a bell' is a subjunctive ('as if all the heavens were'). 'Being' (capitalised even in the 1896 text) may mean existence in general or the speaker's own self. If the heavens are a bell and Being only an ear, there is no room left for thought or will: the self is pure, passive reception. Some readers see this as a mystical moment, others as the final stage of mental breakdown; the omitted last stanza, where the speaker falls and 'Finished knowing', supports the second.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; "I felt a Funeral, in my Brain", EBSCO Research Starters (survey of readings)

  11. I and silence some strange race l. 15 (st. 4)

    'Race' in Webster includes 'the descendants of a common ancestor; a family, tribe, people', and also a running contest. The first sense is generally preferred: 'I' and 'silence' are the last two members of some strange species, cast away together. The personification of silence as a companion is striking: after the noise of treading, drumming and tolling, silence is all that remains. The manuscript reads 'And I, and Silence, some strange Race'.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'race' (the dictionary of Dickinson's household, in its later editions); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster 1844; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280)

  12. Wrecked, solitary, here l. 16 (st. 4)

    The speaker and silence are like castaways after a shipwreck, alone 'here' (an unspecified place). In the 1896 printing these are the last words, leaving the poem in stunned stasis. In Dickinson's manuscript the poem goes on: a 'Plank in Reason' breaks, and the speaker drops down and down, hitting a world at every plunge, and 'Finished knowing - then -'. Critics note that the restored ending changes the poem's shape from burial to fall, and ends on an unfinished dash: whether this means unconsciousness, madness, death or a new kind of knowledge is left unresolved.

    Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), "I felt a funeral in my brain", note on the omitted stanza (fascicle 16); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Manuscript Books of Emily Dickinson (Harvard, 1981), fascicle 16; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Emily Dickinson, Poems: Third Series, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd (1896) [the archive's text]

  13. Hymn metre and slant rhyme l. 7 (st. 2)

    Like most of Dickinson's poems, this one uses the hymn stanza (alternating four- and three-stress lines). The rhymes on lines 2 and 4 are full ('soul/toll', 'drum/numb', 'ear/here') except the slant 'fro/through'. The familiar church metre is ironically apt for a funeral, but the poem offers none of the consolation the hymns promised. The 1896 editors replaced Dickinson's dashes with commas and periods, smoothing a rhythm that in the manuscript is broken and halting.

    Isaac Watts, Hymns and Spiritual Songs (1707) [common-metre hymns familiar to Dickinson]; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard, 1998), no. 340 (Johnson no. 280); Emily Dickinson, Poems: Third Series, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd (1896) [the archive's text]
