Death & Dying

Success is counted sweetest

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Death & Dying

Success is counted sweetest
By those who ne'er succeed.
To comprehend a nectar
Requires sorest need.

Not one of all the purple host
Who took the flag to-day
Can tell the definition,
So clear, of victory,

As he, defeated, dying,
On whose forbidden ear
The distant strains of triumph
Break, agonized and clear!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and publication l. 1

    The poem first appeared anonymously in the Brooklyn Daily Union on 27 April 1864, during the Civil War. Helen Hunt Jackson persuaded Dickinson to let it appear in A Masque of Poets (Roberts Brothers, 1878), an anonymous anthology in the 'No Name' series, where it was titled 'Success' and slightly altered; reviewers attributed it to Emerson. In 1890 Todd and Higginson placed it first in Poems by Emily Dickinson, the archive's text. Dickinson's manuscript differs in punctuation and in a few words (for example 'Burst' for 'Break' in the last line).

    'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom); 'A Masque of Poets' (Roberts Brothers, 1878), Wikipedia; Poems by Emily Dickinson, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson (Roberts Brothers, 1890), Project Gutenberg; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr112 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J67)

  2. The paradox of success ll. 1-2

    The opening states a general law in the manner of a proverb or a moral maxim, and the rest of the poem proves it by two examples, the nectar and the battlefield. 'Counted' means 'reckoned, valued', as in an accounting. The idea that desire is sharpened by deprivation is ancient (and Christian: 'blessed are they that mourn'), but Dickinson pushes it to an extreme: the full meaning of victory belongs only to the defeated. Harold Bloom notes that the poem's lesson applies to any contest between winners and losers.

    'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr112 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J67); Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  3. comprehend a nectar ll. 3-4

    Nectar is the drink of the Olympian gods in Greek myth, hence any delicious drink (OED). 'Comprehend' has its root sense of grasping or containing as well as understanding: only the thirsting can take the full measure of nectar. 'Sorest need' uses 'sore' in the older sense of painful, grievous. The analogy moves the abstract claim into the body: success is a taste, and only the starved can taste it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nectar'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'comprehend'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  4. the purple host l. 5

    'Host' is an old word for an army (from Latin hostis, 'enemy'); 'purple' was the colour of emperors and of Roman triumphal robes, so the victors are already dressed for their triumph. Some readers also hear the colour of blood and bruises, so that the winners are marked by the battle too. Line 5 is the poem's only tetrameter line (four beats against the trimeter elsewhere), so that the victorious host literally takes up more room.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'host'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary; 'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom)

  5. took the flag l. 6

    To take the flag or colours of an enemy regiment was the emblem of a battle won; the phrase may also suggest raising one's own flag over the field. Written in 1859, before the Civil War, the poem's battlefield is generic, but its 1864 printing during the war gave it a sharp topical sense, which Civil War readings of Dickinson have emphasised.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flag'; 'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr112 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J67)

  6. definition l. 7

    A 'definition' is a setting of limits (Latin finis, 'boundary'); the victor, inside the experience, cannot see its edges, while the defeated man, outside it, sees its shape exactly. 'So clear' anticipates the final 'clear', framing the last stanza in clarity. The stanza runs on into the next ('As he, defeated, dying'), so the syntax postpones the answer until the last possible moment.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'definition'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary

  7. forbidden ear l. 10

    The phrase is compressed. Most readers take 'forbidden' as transferred: the ear belongs to one who is forbidden to enjoy the victory, so the sounds that reach it are a torment. It can also suggest that the ear is about to be closed by death, forbidden further hearing. Either way, the ear is the only organ left to him, and it receives the clearest knowledge of what he has lost. Hearing, rather than sight, is often the sense of extreme states in Dickinson.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr112 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J67); Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's 1844 American Dictionary; 'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom)

  8. strains of triumph l. 11

    'Strains' are passages of music. A Roman triumph was the formal procession of a victorious general into the city, with spoils, captives and music, and Dickinson's 'purple host' supports the classical colouring. The distance is crucial: the triumph is heard, not seen, by one excluded from it. The plural 'strains' also hints at strain in the sense of stress and effort, which the next line's 'agonized' makes explicit, so the music of victory reaches the loser as pain.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'triumph'; 'Roman triumph', Wikipedia

  9. 'Break, agonized and clear!' l. 12

    Agony is etymologically the struggle of a contest (Greek agon), so the word folds the battle into the sound. The 1890 text, followed by the archive, reads 'Break'; Dickinson's manuscript has 'Burst'. The poem is in three quatrains of trimeter (except line 5) rhyming on the second and fourth lines (abcb), with exact rhymes at the end ('ear / clear'). The final exclamation gives the dying man's perception the force of revelation.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), Fr112 (Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson, 1955, J67); 'Success is counted sweetest', Wikipedia (publication history, with references to Franklin and to Bloom); Poems by Emily Dickinson, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson (Roberts Brothers, 1890), Project Gutenberg; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'agony'
