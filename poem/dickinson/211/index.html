Nature

A narrow fellow in the grass

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

Nature

A narrow fellow in the grass
Occasionally rides;
You may have met him, -- did you not,
His notice sudden is.

The grass divides as with a comb,
A spotted shaft is seen;
And then it closes at your feet
And opens further on.

He likes a boggy acre,
A floor too cool for corn.
Yet when a child, and barefoot,
I more than once, at morn,

Have passed, I thought, a whip-lash
Unbraiding in the sun, --
When, stooping to secure it,
It wrinkled, and was gone.

Several of nature's people
I know, and they know me;
I feel for them a transport
Of cordiality;

But never met this fellow,
Attended or alone,
Without a tighter breathing,
And zero at the bone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A riddle without the answer l. 1

    Dickinson often writes riddle-poems that withhold their subject (compare 'I like to see it lap the Miles', on a train). Here the snake is a 'narrow fellow', a social term that treats it like a slightly suspect acquaintance. When the poem was printed without her consent in the Springfield Daily Republican on 14 February 1866, the editors titled it 'The Snake', and the 1891 Poems (Second Series) kept the title, supplying the answer she had left out. The archive text is that of the 1891 edition, which differs from her manuscripts in several words (see 'when a child' and 'at morn').

    Emily Dickinson to T. W. Higginson, early 1866, Letters, ed. T. H. Johnson and T. Ward (1958), L316; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1998), Fr1096

  2. rides l. 2

    The verb suggests ease and speed, as if the snake were a rider passing through, and it continues the comic social register of 'fellow'. It may also hint at the gliding, wave-like motion of a snake's body, which seems carried rather than walking. 'Occasionally' makes the encounter a matter of chance, preparing for the suddenness stressed in l. 4. The verb is the first of several that make the snake human and sociable.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986

  3. 'did you not / His notice sudden is' ll. 3-4

    In early 1866 Dickinson wrote to T. W. Higginson that her snake had been 'robbed of me' and 'defeated too of the third line by the punctuation. The third and fourth were one'. Her manuscript reads 'You may have met Him - did you not / His notice sudden is': that is, 'did you not notice him? His notice is sudden', or 'if you did not, it is because his appearance is so sudden'. The newspaper's question mark after 'not' cut the syntax in two. The archive's 1891 text, with a comma after 'him', keeps something of her run-on. Johnson's note records that a copy sent to Susan Dickinson around 1872 reads 'instant' for 'sudden'.

    Emily Dickinson to T. W. Higginson, early 1866, Letters, ed. T. H. Johnson and T. Ward (1958), L316; Emily Dickinson Archive, 'Poems sent from Dickinson to Higginson': J986 (archive.emilydickinson.org); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1998), Fr1096

  4. The grass divides as with a comb l. 5

    The simile is domestic and exact: the grass parts in a line, briefly revealing 'a spotted shaft', and closes behind. We see the movement, not the animal. The 'shaft' (a spear-shaft or arrow) gives the snake the straightness of a weapon, and 'spotted' recalls the markings of an American garter or milk snake. The stanza's quiet present tense makes the encounter feel recurrent and close ('at your feet').

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844)

  5. a boggy acre, a floor too cool for corn ll. 9-10

    'Acre' is a farmer's measure, and 'floor' a domestic word, so the snake's habitat is described in the terms of a household and a farm. Corn (in American usage maize) needs warm soil; the boggy acre is a wild margin that human cultivation leaves alone. The lines quietly separate the snake's world from the human one before the speaker's memory brings them together.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986

  6. 'when a child' (manuscript: 'when a Boy') l. 11

    Both Dickinson's manuscript copies read 'when a Boy, and Barefoot', and 'at Noon' rather than 'at morn' in the next line; the 1891 editors, Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson, altered both words, presumably to make the speaker match the woman poet. Dickinson often used male or child personae (she told Higginson in 1862 that the speaker of her verse was 'a supposed person', not herself), and the boy's barefoot rambles suit the scene. Modern editions (Johnson 1955, Franklin 1998) restore 'Boy'. The archive text keeps the 1891 reading, so students should be aware of the variant.

    Emily Dickinson Archive, 'Poems sent from Dickinson to Higginson': J986 (archive.emilydickinson.org); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986; R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1998), Fr1096; Emily Dickinson to T. W. Higginson, July 1862, Letters, ed. Johnson and Ward (1958), L268

  7. a whip-lash unbraiding in the sun ll. 13-16

    A whip-lash is the braided leather thong of a whip. 'Unbraiding' is the poem's finest word: it suggests both the loosening coils of the snake and a braided thing coming undone, as if the object had come alive. The child's mistake turns the snake from a tool into a living creature in an instant, and 'wrinkled' catches the ripple of its sudden movement. The dangerous whip is also a hint of the snake's menace, preparing the fear of the last stanza.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844)

  8. Several of nature's people ll. 17-20

    'Nature's people' treats animals as neighbours, continuing the social language of 'fellow'. 'Transport', in Webster's 1844 dictionary (which Dickinson used), means rapture or ecstasy, and 'cordiality' comes from Latin cor, heart: a warm, heartfelt friendliness. The Latinate, slightly formal phrasing is playful, as if the speaker were describing social calls, and it sets up the sharp contrast with the visceral last stanza.

    Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010)

  9. Attended or alone l. 22

    'Attended' (accompanied, waited on) keeps the courtly, social idiom of the poem: the speaker could meet the snake with companions or alone, and it would make no difference. The line widens the experience from the single childhood memory to every meeting since. 'Attended' may also suggest a lady or gentleman with servants in attendance, so that the snake meets the speaker as one social being meets another; whatever the company, the body reacts the same way, as the final two lines record. The courtly word thus sets up the abrupt shift into physical fear.

    Emily Dickinson Lexicon (Brigham Young University), based on Webster's American Dictionary (1844); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986

  10. 'a tighter breathing / And zero at the bone' ll. 23-24

    The end is one of Dickinson's most quoted images. 'Tighter breathing' is the physical shock of fear; 'zero at the bone' gives it a temperature, as if the body's marrow fell to zero degrees, the lowest number and freezing point. The abstract mathematical word set against the bodily 'bone' is typical of her compression. Critics also hear the old association of the serpent with the Fall (Genesis 3), so that the speaker's instinctive horror carries a trace of an ancient enmity: 'I will put enmity between thee and the woman' (Genesis 3:15). The poem's light social comedy thus gives way to a fear no friendliness can tame.

    Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010); Genesis 3:1-15 (KJV); Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986

  11. Metre: common measure varied whole poem

    The poem uses the common metre of English hymnody (as in Isaac Watts), with lines of eight and six syllables alternating, rhymed on the second and fourth lines; in the third stanza the first line is shortened ('He likes a boggy acre'). Many rhymes are slant ('seen' / 'on', 'sun' / 'gone'), some are full ('alone' / 'bone'), and 'me' / 'cordiality' rhymes on a lightly stressed final syllable. The run-on from stanza three to four ('at morn, // Have passed') imitates the long, surprised memory.

    Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1955), J986; Helen Vendler, Dickinson: Selected Poems and Commentaries (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 2010)
