Death & Dying

My life had stood a loaded gun

by Emily Dickinson 1830–1886

From Further Poems of Emily Dickinson (1929)

Death & Dying

My life had stood a loaded gun
In corners, till a day
The owner passed—identified,
And carried me away.

And now we roam the sov'reign woods,
And now we hunt the doe—
And every time I speak for him
The mountains straight reply.

And do I smile, such cordial light
Upon the valley glow—
It is as a Vesuvian face
Had let its pleasure through.

And when at night, our good day done,
I guard my master's head,
'Tis better than the eider duck's
Deep pillow to have shared.

To foe of his I'm deadly foe,
None stir the second time
On whom I lay a yellow eye
Or an emphatic thumb.

Though I than he may longer live,
He longer must than I,
For I have but the art to kill—
Without the power to die.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its text l. 1

    Dickinson copied the poem into a fascicle around 1863; it remained unpublished until Further Poems of Emily Dickinson (1929), edited by Martha Dickinson Bianchi and Alfred Leete Hampson, which is the text printed here. Those editors normalised her capitals and most of her dashes and chose among her alternative readings, so the familiar modern text (Franklin's) begins 'My Life had stood - a Loaded Gun -' and differs in small ways. The opening establishes the governing conceit: the speaker's life is a gun, an instrument that is inert until someone takes it up. Few Dickinson poems have provoked more competing readings, because the poem never says who the owner is or what the gun stands for.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), no. 754; Emily Dickinson Archive (Harvard and partners), manuscript images and transcriptions

  2. In corners l. 2

    A loaded gun propped in a corner is ready but idle: the charge is already there, but nothing can release it. Many readers take this as the condition of an unrecognised life, a self full of force that domestic obscurity keeps standing in the corner. Adrienne Rich, in her influential essay, read the poem as being about the woman poet's own power, which must be 'identified' and carried off before it can act, and which is figured, tellingly, as a weapon. The phrase 'till a day' gives the life a before and after, as in a conversion narrative.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764

  3. The owner ... identified l. 3

    'Identified' means that the owner recognised the gun as his, and perhaps that the gun's identity began at that moment. The poem never names him, and every major reading turns on this gap. Readers have proposed a lover; God or Christ, who claims the soul; a 'master' figure like the addressee of Dickinson's so-called Master letters; and the poet's own creative genius, the daemon that takes possession of her. Rich argued that the poem dramatises a woman's power experienced as dangerous and as belonging to a masculine other. Susan Howe read it against American frontier history, the hunt and the colonial wilderness, as well as against Browning and Brontë. The poem supports several of these readings at once, which is part of its force.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); Susan Howe, My Emily Dickinson (North Atlantic Books, 1985); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764

  4. sov'reign woods l. 5

    'Sovereign' (contracted for the metre) means supreme or royal, and also independent and subject to no one. Hunting forests were historically royal preserves, so the word gives the woods the dignity of a king's domain. Read together with 'roam', it suggests the freedom the gun gains once it is carried away from the corner: owner and gun now move through a world that seems to be theirs. Dickinson's lexicon (Webster's American Dictionary, which she used) gives 'supreme in power' as the primary sense.

    Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sovereign'

  5. hunt the doe l. 6

    The doe is the female deer, traditionally a figure of gentleness and vulnerability. Critics in the feminist tradition, following Rich, have noticed the irony: a speaker often read as a woman, once she is identified with her owner, turns her power against a female creature. On this reading, taking on the master's role means aggression against the 'feminine' self. Other readers take the line more literally, as part of the poem's frontier hunting world, which Susan Howe connected to American wilderness narratives. Note the rhyme doe/reply, a typical Dickinson slant rhyme.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); Susan Howe, My Emily Dickinson (North Atlantic Books, 1985)

  6. speak ... reply l. 8

    The gun's speech is its report, the sound of firing, and the answering mountains are its echo. 'Straight' here has its older adverbial sense of 'immediately'. The conceit also works as an image of poetic utterance: the speaker 'speaks for' the owner, as a spokesperson or a poet voicing another's meaning, and the whole landscape answers. This double sense, violence and voice, runs through the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'straight, adv.'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  7. cordial light l. 9

    The gun's 'smile' is the flash of firing, which lights up the valley. 'Cordial' (from Latin cor, 'heart') means warm and sincere, and in older usage a cordial was a medicine that strengthened the heart. The polite social word is disturbingly applied to a lethal discharge. The tension between hospitality and violence continues into the next lines, where the smile becomes a volcano.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cordial'; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  8. Vesuvian face l. 11

    Mount Vesuvius, near Naples, erupted in 79 CE and buried Pompeii and Herculaneum; it was famous again in the nineteenth century as the excavations proceeded. Volcanoes are one of Dickinson's recurrent images for passion or power hidden under a calm surface, and Adrienne Rich took the title of her essay, 'Vesuvius at Home', from this imagery, seeing in it the volcanic force contained within a quiet domestic life. Here the gun's 'pleasure' bursts through like an eruption: delight and destruction are the same event.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764

  9. the eider duck's / Deep pillow l. 15

    The eider is a northern sea duck whose breast-down, plucked from its nests, was the most prized filling for pillows and quilts (hence 'eiderdown'). The gun says that standing guard by its master's head at night is better than having shared his soft pillow. Readers have taken the lines as a choice of devoted service over sexual intimacy: the speaker is guardian, not bedfellow. This chaste watchfulness has supported both religious readings (the soul as watchful servant) and erotic or psychological ones, in which intimacy is displaced into a weapon's loyalty.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eider'; Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979)

  10. yellow eye ... emphatic thumb l. 19

    'Yellow eye' suggests the flash at the muzzle when the gun fires, and perhaps the eye of the marksman sighting along it. The 'emphatic thumb' is the thumb that pulls back the hammer, or presses the trigger, of a nineteenth-century firearm; 'emphatic' makes the action a kind of decisive speech, continuing the idea of the gun 'speaking'. The anatomy is ambiguous, since eye and thumb belong more to the owner than to the gun, and the ambiguity fits the poem's merging of owner and instrument.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764; Emily Dickinson Lexicon, ed. Cynthia Hallen (Brigham Young University)

  11. None stir the second time l. 18

    A deliberately dry understatement for the gun's deadly efficiency: no victim moves after the first shot. The stanza turns the gun's loyalty into ferocity: to its master's enemy it is 'deadly foe'. Readers who take the gun as the poet's art see here the destructive precision of language, and readers who take it as the speaker's passion see a love that defends its object absolutely.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764

  12. The closing riddle l. 21

    The last stanza is the most debated passage in the poem. One way to read it: an object can survive its owner, but without him it is inert, so he 'must' live longer for it to have any life at all. A gun can bring death but cannot experience it, because it is not alive in the way he is. To have only 'the art to kill' without 'the power to die' is to lack full mortal life. Some readers hear a religious paradox (the mortal owner may gain immortality while the soulless instrument cannot). Others hear an account of art, which outlasts the poet and wounds but never dies. Rich saw the poet's ambivalence about her own destructive power. The poem leaves the riddle open.

    Adrienne Rich, 'Vesuvius at Home: The Power of Emily Dickinson', Parnassus 5.1 (1976); repr. in On Lies, Secrets, and Silence (1979); Susan Howe, My Emily Dickinson (North Atlantic Books, 1985); R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764

  13. art to kill (textual) l. 23

    The 1929 text printed here reads 'the art to kill'. The standard modern text (Franklin's reading edition) reads 'the power to kill', which makes the line pair neatly with 'the power to die'. Dickinson often listed alternative words on her fascicle sheets, and her early editors chose among such readings freely; Franklin's apparatus records which words she offered. The choice matters: 'art' makes killing a skill or craft, which strengthens readings of the gun as poetic art. 'Power' stresses capacity, and makes the parallel with dying stark. Students quoting the poem should say which text they use. The manuscript can be inspected in the Emily Dickinson Archive.

    R. W. Franklin, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson: Variorum Edition (Harvard UP, 1998), no. 764; Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), no. 754; Emily Dickinson Archive (Harvard and partners), manuscript images and transcriptions

  14. Hymn metre l. 4

    Like most of Dickinson's poems, this one uses common metre: quatrains of alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter, rhyming on the second and fourth lines. This is the stanza of the hymns of Isaac Watts, which she knew from childhood. Against that familiar devotional frame she sets violent content and rhymes that are often only approximate (doe/reply, time/thumb). Some readers hear in the hymn form a hint that the master is divine. The steady ballad-like movement also suits the poem's narrative of a hunt.

    Thomas H. Johnson, ed., The Poems of Emily Dickinson (Harvard UP, 1955), no. 754; Cristanne Miller, Emily Dickinson: A Poet's Grammar (Harvard UP, 1987)
