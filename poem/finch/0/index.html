Nature

A Nocturnal Reverie

by Anne Finch, Countess of Winchilsea 1661–1720

From Miscellany Poems, on Several Occasions (1713)

NatureTime & Brevity

In such a Night, when every louder Wind
Is to its distant Cavern safe confin'd;
And only gentle Zephyr fans his Wings,
And lonely Philomel, still waking, sings ;
Or from some Tree, fam'd for the Owl's delight,
She, hollowing clear, directs the Wand'rer right:
In such a Night, when passing Clouds give place,
Or thinly vail the Heav'ns mysterious Face;
When in some River, overhung with Green,
The waving Moon and trembling Leaves are seen;
When freshen'd Grass now bears it self upright,
And makes cool Banks to pleasing Rest invite,
Whence springs the Woodbind, and the Bramble-Rose,
And where the sleepy Cowslip shelter'd grows ;
Whilst now a paler Hue the Foxglove takes,
Yet checquers still with Red the dusky brakes
When scatter'd Glow-worms, but in Twilight fine,
Shew trivial Beauties watch their Hour to shine;
Whilst Salisbury stands the Test of every Light,
In perfect Charms, and perfect Virtue bright:
When Odours, which declin'd repelling Day,
Thro' temp'rate Air uninterrupted stray ;
When darken'd Groves their softest Shadows wear,
And falling Waters we distinctly hear;
When thro' the Gloom more venerable shows
Some ancient Fabrick, awful in Repose,
While Sunburnt Hills their swarthy Looks conceal,
And swelling Haycocks thicken up the Vale:
When the loos'd Horse now, as his Pasture leads,
Comes slowly grazing thro' th' adjoining Meads,
Whose stealing Pace, and lengthen'd Shade we fear,
Till torn up Forage in his Teeth we hear:
When nibbling Sheep at large pursue their Food,
And unmolested Kine rechew the Cud ;
When Curlews cry beneath the Village-walls,
And to her straggling Brood the Partridge calls ;
Their shortliv'd Jubilee the Creatures keep,
Which but endures, whilst Tyrant -Man do's sleep;
When a sedate Content the Spirit feels,
And no fierce Light disturb, whilst it reveals;
But silent Musings urge the Mind to seek
Something, too high for Syllables to speak ;
Till the free Soul to a compos'dness charm'd,
Finding the Elements of Rage disarm'd,
O'er all below a solemn Quiet grown,
Joys in th' inferiour World, and thinks it like her Own :
In such a Night let Me abroad remain,
Till Morning breaks, and All's confus'd again ;
Our Cares, our Toils, our Clamours are renew'd,
Or Pleasures, seldom reach'd, again pursu'd.

Codex Note

A fifty-line poem built as one long sentence on the repeated phrase 'In such a night,' which reaches its main clause only near the end. Wordsworth singled it out as an exception in a century that gave little fresh description of nature.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and Wordsworth's praise title; l. 1

    Anne Finch, Countess of Winchilsea (1661-1720), published the poem in Miscellany Poems, on Several Occasions, Written by a Lady (1713). Myra Reynolds notes that it appears in neither of the two surviving manuscript collections, which suggests a late date, close to publication. Its modern fame begins with Wordsworth's Essay, Supplementary to the Preface (1815), which claims that, apart from this poem and a passage or two of Pope's Windsor Forest, the poetry between Paradise Lost and Thomson's Seasons offers hardly a single new image of external nature drawn from steady observation. Wordsworth later sent a manuscript of extracts from her poems to Lady Mary Lowther (Christmas 1819). His judgement made the Reverie the poem by which she was known for a century and more; recent critics read it also for its treatment of solitude, gender and the freedom of the night.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction, pp. xxxvi-xxxvii and the section on Wordsworth (quoting the Essay, Supplementary to the Preface, 1815); William Wordsworth, 'Essay, Supplementary to the Preface', Poems (1815)

  2. 'In such a Night': the anaphora and the one long sentence ll. 1, 7, 47

    Lorenzo and Jessica open the last act of The Merchant of Venice with a duet of lines each beginning 'In such a night', recalling lovers of classical legend under the moon. Jane Williams, quoted by Reynolds, had already linked the poem to Shakespeare's 'In such a night' scene. Finch turns the lovers' game into a structure: 'In such a Night' (ll. 1, 7, 47) and a long chain of 'When' and 'Whilst' clauses defer the main clause until l. 47, 'let Me abroad remain'. The syntax enacts the poem's experience, a suspended, accumulating attention in which the speaker waits on each sight and sound before she can state what she wants. The heroic couplets are kept quiet: many lines run on, and the stresses are light.

    Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice V.i.1-24 (Folger Shakespeare); Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction, pp. lxxxi-lxxxii (quoting Jane Williams, The Literary Women of England, 1861)

  3. the winds in their cavern l. 2

    The image comes from classical mythology: in Aeneid I.52-63 the wind-king Aeolus holds the struggling winds in a vast cave, curbing them with chains and prison, and Jupiter has piled mountains over them lest they sweep away sea and sky. In Virgil Juno persuades Aeolus to let them out to wreck Aeneas's fleet; Finch's night begins where that storm is prevented. The phrase 'louder Wind' implies that only the gentle wind is left abroad, so the line prepares the contrast at l. 44, where the soul finds 'the Elements of Rage disarm'd'.

    Virgil, Aeneid I.52-63 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), text of 'A Nocturnal Reverie', pp. 268-70

  4. Zephyr l. 3

    Greek Zephyros, the west wind, was the gentlest of the winds and the herald of spring in Greek and Latin poetry; he is often shown winged. By Finch's time 'Zephyr' was a stock poetic word for any soft breeze (the OED records the sense of a mild wind from the seventeenth century). Here it marks the first of the poem's many reductions of force: the stormy winds are confined, and only a breeze 'fans his Wings'. The personification is light and conventional, and it gives way at once to the more closely observed sounds of birds.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'zephyr, n.'; Virgil, Aeneid I.52-63, for the classical winds and their keeper (The Latin Library)

  5. Philomel l. 4

    In Ovid's Metamorphoses VI Tereus rapes his sister-in-law Philomela and cuts out her tongue; after the sisters' revenge the characters become birds, and later tradition made Philomela the nightingale. English poets used 'Philomel' as the bird's poetic name, and Milton fixed its associations: the 'wakeful Nightingale' sings all night in Eden (Paradise Lost IV.602-3), and in Il Penseroso she is 'most musical, most melancholy'. 'Still' here has its older sense 'always, continually'. Finch's adjective 'lonely' may also recall the violated, silenced woman behind the myth, though the line itself stays within the poetic convention.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses VI.424-674 (Latin text, The Latin Library); John Milton, Paradise Lost IV.602-3 and Il Penseroso 56-62 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still, adv.' (sense 'always, continually')

  6. the owl hollowing ll. 5-6

    'Hollow' was a common spelling of 'hollo' or 'halloo', to shout or call out, and Johnson's Dictionary lists the verb to hollow in that sense. The owl was traditionally a bird of ill omen in poetry (Shakespeare's 'fatal bellman' in Macbeth II.ii), but Finch makes its clear call a friendly signal that 'directs the Wand'rer right', as though the bird were a guide. The tree is 'fam'd for the Owl's delight': the owl has a known haunt, so the sound is local and familiar. This domestication of a night creature anticipates the poem's later sense that the night world belongs to the animals.

    Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'to hollow'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hollo, v.' and 'hollow, v.2'; Shakespeare, Macbeth II.ii (Folger Shakespeare)

  7. vail l. 8

    The spelling 'vail' for 'veil' was common in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries; Finch's 1713 text has it, and the archive keeps the original spelling. (There is also a separate verb 'to vail', to lower, but the sense here is plainly to cover.) The passing clouds either give way or only thinly veil the sky. Calling the heavens' face 'mysterious' gives the sky a hidden, half-revealed quality that the poem returns to at ll. 39-42, where the light 'reveals' without disturbing and the mind seeks something beyond words.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'veil, v.' (spelling vail) and 'vail, v.1'; Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), text of the poem from the 1713 edition

  8. the waving moon ll. 9-10

    The couplet observes reflection, not the moon itself: in a river overhung with green, the moon's image 'waves' with the current, and the reflected leaves 'tremble'. Such attention to a seen effect, rather than to an emblem, is what Wordsworth admired in the poem. The scene is entirely visual and quiet; Finch notes surfaces, the freshened grass standing upright again after the heat (l. 11), and the 'cool Banks' that invite rest. The verb choice transfers the motion of water to the moon, a small instance of the poem's habit of making the sky and the earth mirror each other.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction (Wordsworth's comment on the poem's images); William Wordsworth, 'Essay, Supplementary to the Preface' (1815), as quoted by Reynolds

  9. Woodbind, Bramble-Rose, Cowslip, Foxglove ll. 13-16

    'Woodbind' (woodbine) usually means the wild honeysuckle, a climber of hedges; the 'Bramble-Rose' is the wild briar or dog rose. The 'sleepy Cowslip' (a primrose of meadows) hangs its head, as if asleep, and the foxglove's purple-red bells look paler in the dusk though they still 'checquer' the dark thickets with red. These are the plants of an English lane at night, named exactly and seen as colour fades. The passage is often cited as evidence of the poem's close observation; it also shows how sight is failing, preparing for the shift to scent and sound in ll. 21-4.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'woodbine, n.' and 'bramble, n.' (bramble-rose); Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'woodbine' and 'cowslip'

  10. brakes l. 16

    'Brake' in this sense means a thicket or brushwood, and also bracken or fern; both senses suit a hedgebank. 'Dusky' marks the half-dark. The archive text has no punctuation at the end of l. 16 (the 1713 print as transcribed by Reynolds has none either), so the line runs straight into the glow-worm clause; readers should take a pause there. 'Checquers' means variegates, marks with patches, as on a chessboard.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brake, n.3' (thicket) and 'brake, n.1' (bracken); Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), text of the poem

  11. the glow-worms and 'trivial Beauties' ll. 17-18

    The couplet turns natural observation into a moral comparison. Glow-worms, visible only in dusk, 'shew' (show, demonstrate) that 'trivial Beauties' (slight, ordinary ones) must wait for the right moment to be seen. The next couplet sets against them the Countess of Salisbury, who 'stands the Test of every Light', beautiful and virtuous by day as by night. Some readers have found the compliment an intrusion: Reynolds records that Wordsworth thought the Salisbury lines marred the poem and should be cut. Others see the courtly turn as part of the poem's social world, a reminder of the daylight society the speaker has briefly left.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction, pp. xxxvi-xxxvii (Wordsworth's suggestion that the lines be omitted); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trivial, adj.' (ordinary, commonplace)

  12. Salisbury ll. 19-20

    The poem does not say which countess is meant, and early commentators disagreed. Leigh Hunt (Men, Women, and Books, 1847) named Frances Bennet, wife of the fourth Earl; Jane Williams (1861) named Lady Anne Tufton (1693-1757), second daughter of Thomas Tufton, sixth Earl of Thanet, and of Catherine Cavendish, Finch's close friend; she married James Cecil, fifth Earl of Salisbury, in 1709. Neither gave evidence. Reynolds weighed the case and judged the probabilities to favour Anne Tufton, which would date the poem between 1709 and 1713, when she was between about fifteen and twenty. Later scholarship (Barbara McGovern, as cited in the article below) accepts the Tufton identification and links her with Finch's pseudonym 'Lamira'.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction, pp. xxxvi-xxxvii; 'Anne Cecil, Countess of Salisbury', Wikipedia (citing Barbara McGovern, Anne Finch and Her Poetry: A Critical Biography, 1992)

  13. odours that shunned the day ll. 21-4

    'Declin'd' here means avoided or withdrew from (a Latinate sense, from declinare, to turn aside), and 'repelling' describes the day, which drove the scents away. In the night's 'temp'rate Air' they 'stray' uninterrupted. As sight dims, the poem's senses widen: scent here, and sound in the next couplet, where 'falling Waters we distinctly hear'. The shift from eye to ear and nose is part of the poem's realism and also its argument: night offers a different, gentler kind of perception.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'decline, v.' (to turn away from, avoid); Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'to decline'

  14. Fabrick, awful ll. 25-6

    'Fabrick' was the usual eighteenth-century word for an edifice or structure (Latin fabrica, a workshop or construction), and Johnson defines it first as 'a building; an edifice'. 'Awful' keeps its older sense of 'inspiring awe or reverence', and 'venerable' means deserving reverence for its age. The building is not identified; Finch lived at Eastwell Park in Kent from about 1690, but the poem keeps the scene general. The couplet foreshadows the mid-century taste for twilight ruins found in Collins and Gray.

    Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'fabrick' and 'awful'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fabric, n.' (a building) and 'awful, adj.'

  15. the loosed horse ll. 29-32

    The couplets on the horse are among the most praised in the poem. Jane Williams (1861), quoted by Reynolds, singled out the grazing horse wandering at will as 'wonderfully true to nature'. The passage records a real experience in the right order: first an unidentified 'stealing Pace' and 'lengthen'd Shade' (moonlight lengthens shadows) that 'we fear', then the comic relief of hearing 'torn up Forage in his Teeth'. Sound again corrects sight. The plural 'we' briefly admits a companion or a shared human response, before the poem returns to the solitary 'Me' of l. 47.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction, pp. lxxxi-lxxxii (quoting Jane Williams, The Literary Women of England, 1861); William Wordsworth, 'Essay, Supplementary to the Preface' (1815), as quoted by Reynolds

  16. Kine ll. 33-6

    'Kine' (from Old English cyna, genitive plural of cu) was already archaic and poetic in prose by 1700, though still current in rural speech and in the Bible (Pharaoh's 'seven well favoured kine', Genesis 41). The word fits the pastoral register. 'Unmolested' and 'at large' (ranging freely) prepare the point made explicit in ll. 37-8: the animals' freedom depends on human absence. The curlews and the partridge calling her 'straggling Brood' add sounds of birds that are normally wary of people.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kine, n.'; Genesis 41:2 (King James Version)

  17. Jubilee and 'Tyrant-Man' ll. 37-8

    In Leviticus 25 every fiftieth year is a 'jubile' in which liberty is proclaimed through the land, slaves are freed and land returns to its owners; the word comes from Hebrew yobel, the ram's horn that announced it. Finch's 'Jubilee' is a short season of release for creatures normally under human rule, and 'Tyrant-Man' names that rule bluntly. Critics differ on how far to press the phrase: some read it as a general reflection on human dominion over nature, others (especially feminist readers) as also glancing at male authority, since 'Man' can mean men. The poem itself stays with the animals, but the political vocabulary of tyranny and liberty is striking in a writer whose family lost court office at the Revolution of 1688.

    Leviticus 25:8-13 (King James Version); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'jubilee, n.'; Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction (Finch's life after 1688)

  18. something too high for syllables ll. 39-42

    The couplet moves from description to the spirit: 'sedate Content' (calm satisfaction) and a light that 'reveals' without disturbing lead to 'silent Musings' that seek what cannot be spoken. The idea of the inexpressible has religious roots, as in St Paul's 'unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter' (2 Corinthians 12:4), and the line can be read devotionally. It is also, for later readers, an early instance of the movement from landscape to inward reflection that Wordsworth would make central. The word 'Syllables' keeps the claim modest and concrete: language itself, the poet's medium, falls short.

    2 Corinthians 12:4 (King James Version); Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction (on the poem's anticipation of Wordsworth)

  19. compos'dness l. 43

    'Compos'dness', a rare noun meaning a composed, tranquil state, is recorded by the OED from the seventeenth century. 'Charm'd' carries its older magical sense: the soul is enchanted into calm. 'Free' marks the soul's release from the daytime constraints of 'Cares' and 'Toils' (l. 49). The soul is feminine ('her Own', l. 46), following Latin anima and long English usage, and some readers connect this feminine, freed soul with the speaker herself. The syntax still has not reached its main verb: the soul 'Joys' only in l. 46.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'composedness, n.' and 'charm, v.1'; Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'to charm'

  20. the Elements of Rage disarmed ll. 44-5

    'Elements' can mean the physical elements (wind, water, fire) and also the components of a thing; 'Elements of Rage' covers both the storms confined in l. 2 and the angry passions of the day. 'Disarm'd' continues the military vocabulary of 'Tyrant' and 'Rage'. Over everything 'below' (the earth, seen as if from above) a 'solemn Quiet' has grown. The phrase closes the circle that began with the winds shut in their cavern, so the poem's opening and its spiritual climax answer one another.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'element, n.'; Virgil, Aeneid I.52-63, for the imprisoned winds (The Latin Library)

  21. the inferior world and the alexandrine l. 46

    'Inferiour' means lower in position: the sublunary world beneath the heavens, as opposed to the soul's 'Own', its spiritual home. The soul 'joys in' (takes joy in) the earth because for a moment it resembles that higher world. The line is an alexandrine, with six stresses (JOYS ... inFEriour WORLD ... THINKS ... LIKE ... OWN) where the couplets around it have five, and its extra length, as often in Dryden and Pope, marks a climax and a resting point. The colon then turns to the main clause. Read devotionally, the moment is a foretaste of heaven; read in natural terms, it is a harmony between mind and landscape that later poets made a central theme.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'inferior, adj.' (lower in position); Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), text of the poem

  22. 'let Me abroad remain' and the return of day ll. 47-50

    Only now does the sentence arrive at its main verb: 'let Me abroad remain'. 'Abroad' means out of doors, away from the house. The capital 'Me' is emphatic: the speaker claims the night for herself. The last lines turn the dawn into a fall back into 'confus'd' activity: 'Our Cares, our Toils, our Clamours' and pursuits of pleasure 'seldom reach'd'. The ending thus reverses the usual praise of daylight and order, and it frames the night's calm as temporary, like the animals' 'shortliv'd Jubilee'. Critics have related the wish to Finch's own retired life after 1688 and to her poems on retreat, such as 'The Petition for an Absolute Retreat'.

    Myra Reynolds, ed., The Poems of Anne, Countess of Winchilsea (Chicago, 1903), Introduction (Finch's retirement at Eastwell and the retreat poems); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abroad, adv.' (out of doors)
