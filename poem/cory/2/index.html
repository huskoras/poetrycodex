Death & Dying

Heraclitus

by William Johnson Cory 1823–1892

From Ionica (1905)

Death & Dying

They told me, Heraclitus, they told me you were dead,
They brought me bitter news to hear and bitter tears to shed.
I wept, as I remembered, how often you and I
Had tired the sun with talking and sent him down the sky.

And now that thou art lying, my dear old Carian guest,
A handful of grey ashes, long long ago at rest,
Still are thy pleasant voices, thy nightingales, awake;
For Death, he taketh all away, but them he cannot take.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Cory's 'Heraclitus' and its Greek original l. 1

    Callimachus (third century BC), the scholar-poet of Ptolemaic Alexandria, wrote a six-line elegiac epigram on hearing of the death of his friend Heraclitus of Halicarnassus. Cory (1823-1892), an Eton master, made it into an eight-line lyric in long rhymed couplets, and his version became one of the best-known translations from Greek in English, often anthologised. It expands freely: the Greek says simply 'someone told me of your death', and it is Cory who makes the news plural and bitter. This Heraclitus is not the early philosopher of Ephesus (c. 500 BC), a confusion found even in some modern notes. Compare the literal versions by Paton and Mackail, also in this archive.

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; R. Pfeiffer, ed., Callimachus, vol. 2 (Oxford, 1953), Epigram 2; W. Cory, Ionica (1858; collected edition 1891), 'Heraclitus'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Heraclitus' by William Cory, text from Ionica (1891), with notes; W. R. Paton, trans., The Greek Anthology, vol. 2 (Loeb, 1917), 7.80

  2. bitter news ... bitter tears l. 2

    Callimachus' first couplet is plain and quick: someone told me of your fate, and it brought me to tears. Cory adds the repetition 'They told me ... they told me' in line 1 and the double 'bitter' in line 2, giving the poem a ballad-like, incantatory sorrow. Critics have praised the result as English poetry while noting that it is more openly emotional than the reticent Greek, whose restraint is part of its effect (MacQueen's study of the epigram argues that the Greek deserves reading as a poem in its own right, not only through Cory).

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; J. G. MacQueen, 'Death and Immortality: A Study of the Heraclitus Epigram of Callimachus', Ramus 11 (1982); W. Cory, Ionica (1858; collected edition 1891), 'Heraclitus'

  3. tired the sun with talking and sent him down the sky ll. 3-4

    Callimachus' verb katadyein means 'to make set': the friends talked so long that they saw the sun down. Leschē is a place or occasion of conversation, a club-room for talk. Cory's 'tired the sun with talking' adds a playful personification, as if the sun grew weary of their talk before they did. Gareth Williams has argued that Ovid echoes the motif of talking past sunset in his exile poetry (Classical Quarterly, 1991). The line establishes the friendship as one of conversation, wit and shared literary life.

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; R. Pfeiffer, ed., Callimachus, vol. 2 (Oxford, 1953), Epigram 2; A. W. Mair, trans., Callimachus and Lycophron (Loeb, 1921), Epigram II; G. D. Williams, 'Conversing after Sunset: A Callimachean Echo in Ovid's Exile Poetry', Classical Quarterly 41 (1991)

  4. you ... thou ll. 1, 5

    Stanza 1 uses the everyday 'you' ('they told me you were dead'), stanza 2 the archaic and intimate 'thou', 'thy' ('now that thou art lying', 'thy pleasant voices'). The Greek uses one second-person singular throughout. Cory's change marks a shift in register from the news of death to the formal address to the dead friend, and readers have heard in the 'thou' the language of prayer and epitaph. Some critics have found the shift awkward; others see it as the emotional turn of the poem.

    W. Cory, Ionica (1858; collected edition 1891), 'Heraclitus'; A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Heraclitus' by William Cory, text from Ionica (1891), with notes

  5. Carian guest l. 5

    Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum, Turkey) was a Greek city in Caria, birthplace of Herodotus. Xeinos means 'guest-friend' or 'stranger', a person bound by ties of hospitality, which marks the tie of hospitality between the two men; it need not mean more than 'friend'. Cory's 'Carian' replaces the city with the region, and 'dear old' is his own affectionate addition. Heraclitus was himself an elegiac poet; one epigram ascribed to him survives in the Greek Anthology (7.465).

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; W. R. Paton, trans., The Greek Anthology, vol. 2 (Loeb, 1917), 7.80

  6. a handful of grey ashes, long long ago at rest l. 6

    Spodiē is the ash of a cremation pyre. Tetrapalai, 'four times long ago', is an intensive coinage, rendered by Paton as 'long long ago' and by Mackail as 'long and long ago'; Cory keeps the doubling. The emphasis makes the loss feel final: the friend is already far beyond recall, whether the poet means that he died long ago or is simply exaggerating his sense of distance. Cory adds 'a handful', which makes the ashes smaller and more pitiful, and 'at rest', the language of Christian epitaph.

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; R. Pfeiffer, ed., Callimachus, vol. 2 (Oxford, 1953), Epigram 2; W. R. Paton, trans., The Greek Anthology, vol. 2 (Loeb, 1917), 7.80

  7. thy nightingales l. 7

    Ancient and modern scholars generally take Aedones ('Nightingales') as the title of Heraclitus's collection of poems, so that Callimachus is saying his friend's book survives. The nightingale is also a traditional figure for the poet and for lament (as in the Procne myth), and some critics have argued for the symbolic sense alone or for both together. Paton prints the word with a capital ('thy Nightingales') to mark it as a title; Cory's lower-case 'nightingales' and the added 'pleasant voices' lean towards metaphor. The contrast of ash and living song is the epigram's point: poetry escapes death.

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; J. G. MacQueen, 'Death and Immortality: A Study of the Heraclitus Epigram of Callimachus', Ramus 11 (1982); W. R. Paton, trans., The Greek Anthology, vol. 2 (Loeb, 1917), 7.80; R. Pfeiffer, ed., Callimachus, vol. 2 (Oxford, 1953), Epigram 2

  8. Still are thy pleasant voices ... awake l. 7

    Cory's inversion ('Still are ... awake') puts the emphasis on persistence, and the word 'still' may briefly suggest its other sense (silent) before the line resolves into 'awake'. 'Pleasant voices' glosses the nightingales as songs; 'awake' contrasts with the friend 'lying' asleep in death. The added phrases make the line's meaning explicit for an English reader who does not know that Aedones was a book title.

    W. Cory, Ionica (1858; collected edition 1891), 'Heraclitus'; A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still'

  9. Death, he taketh all away l. 8

    Callimachus' Hades is a robber (harpaktēs, 'snatcher') who seizes everything except the poems. Cory's 'Death, he taketh all away' uses the redundant pronoun ('Death, he') of ballad and the '-eth' verb ending of the Authorised Version, recalling such phrases as 'the LORD hath taken away' (Job 1:21). The closing claim that poetry survives death is an ancient commonplace (compare Horace, Odes 3.30, 'non omnis moriar'), here made tender rather than triumphant, since it is the friend's songs, not the speaker's, that endure.

    A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; Bible (KJV), Job 1:21; Horace, Odes 3.30; A. W. Mair, trans., Callimachus and Lycophron (Loeb, 1921), Epigram II

  10. Metre and rhyme ll. 1-8

    The Greek elegiac couplet pairs a hexameter with a shorter pentameter. Cory chooses a long line of about thirteen or fourteen syllables, with a pause in the middle ('They told me, Heraclitus, / they told me you were dead'), rhyming in couplets (dead/shed, I/sky, guest/rest, awake/take). The rhythm, close to the old 'fourteener' and ballad metre, gives the poem a song-like simplicity that differs from the terse wit of the Greek. The expansion from six lines to eight allows Cory's repetitions and additions.

    W. Cory, Ionica (1858; collected edition 1891), 'Heraclitus'; A. S. F. Gow and D. L. Page, eds., The Greek Anthology: Hellenistic Epigrams (Cambridge, 1965), Callimachus 34, with commentary; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Heraclitus' by William Cory, text from Ionica (1891), with notes
