Love

My galy charged with forgetfulnes

by Sir Thomas Wyatt 1503–1542

From The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat (ed. A. K. Foxwell, 1913)

Love

My galy charged with forgetfulnes,
Thorrough sharpe sees, in wynter nyghtes doeth pas,
Twene Rock and Rock : and eke myn enemy, alas,
That is my Lorde, sterith with cruelnes.

And every owre a thought in redines :
As tho that deth were light in suche a case;
An endles wynd doeth tere the sayll a pase,
Of forced sightes and trusty ferefulnes.

A rayn of teris : a clowde of derk disdain,
Hath done the wered cordes great hinderaunce :
Wrethed with error and eke with ignoraunce.

The starres be hid that led me to this pain :
Drowned is reason that should me comfort :
And I remain dispering of the port.

Study notes by the editors

  1. My galy charged with forgetfulnes: Wyatt and Petrarch l. 1

    Wyatt's sonnet renders Petrarch's Rime 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio' (my ship passes, laden with forgetfulness). It survives in the Egerton manuscript, Wyatt's own collection, and was printed in Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557) under an editorial title saying that the lover compares his state to a ship in a perilous storm. Wyatt keeps Petrarch's sequence of images closely but roughens the music and darkens the ending: Petrarch's speaker begins to despair of the port, Wyatt's simply remains despairing. The 'forgetfulness' with which the galley is loaded is the lover's oblivion of reason and of everything but his passion. Comparing the English with the Italian line by line is the standard way the poem is taught, and the commentaries of Rebholz and of Muir and Thomson make that comparison.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Thomas Wyatt, The Complete Poems, ed. R. A. Rebholz (Penguin, 1978), note on this sonnet; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, ed. A. K. Foxwell (1913), the text used here

  2. galy l. 1

    A galley was a long, low ship driven chiefly by oars, often rowed by slaves or convicts, and used in Mediterranean war and trade. Petrarch has simply 'la nave mia', my ship; Wyatt's choice of galley adds a hint of forced labour and captivity, which suits the lord who steers 'with cruelnes' in line 4 and the oars manned by thoughts in line 5. Wyatt knew the Mediterranean at first hand from his diplomatic journey to Italy in 1527. The spelling 'galy' is that of Foxwell's text, which follows the manuscripts.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'galley'; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, ed. A. K. Foxwell (1913), the text used here

  3. sharpe sees l. 2

    The spelling is the manuscript form of 'seas'. 'Sharp' in the sense of rough, bitter or severe, used of weather and conditions, is well recorded in the period. The winter nights of the same line add darkness and season to the danger: Petrarch's ship passes through a harsh sea at midnight in winter ('per aspro mare, a mezza notte il verno'), and Wyatt follows him almost word for word, only turning the single midnight into a series of winter nights, as if the voyage had no end.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sharp'; The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, ed. A. K. Foxwell (1913), the text used here

  4. Twene Rock and Rock l. 3

    Petrarch names the two monsters of the Straits of Messina, Scylla and Charybdis, between which Odysseus must steer in Odyssey Book 12. Wyatt drops the classical names and leaves only 'Rock and Rock', a plainer and starker phrase. Editors note the change as typical of his way with Petrarch: the learned allusion survives only as a shape, and the danger becomes a matter of bare navigation. 'Twene' is a shortened form of 'between'; 'eke' in the same line means 'also'.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Homer, Odyssey 12 (Scylla and Charybdis); Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet

  5. myn enemy ... my Lorde ll. 3-4

    In Petrarch the steersman is 'il signor mio, anzi 'l mio nemico', my lord, or rather my enemy: that is, Amor, the god of Love, who rules the lover and wrecks him. Wyatt reorders this into the paradox that his enemy is his lord. Many modern readers, mindful of Wyatt's career at court and his imprisonments in 1536 and 1541, have heard a political undertone in the master who steers 'with cruelnes', though nothing external dates the poem to a particular crisis. The commentaries keep the primary sense amorous; the political reading is best offered as a possible second layer, not as a key.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Thomas Wyatt, The Complete Poems, ed. R. A. Rebholz (Penguin, 1978), note on this sonnet; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet

  6. every owre a thought in redines l. 5

    'Owre' is the oar, though the spelling also allows a faint second sense of 'hour', constant thought. Petrarch has 'a ciascun remo un penser pronto et rio', at every oar a ready and wicked thought. Wyatt omits the wickedness and makes the next line his own: the thoughts row on 'As tho that deth were light in suche a case', reckless of the danger, as if death were a small matter. The allegory is precise: the mind's own thoughts drive the ship deeper into the storm.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'oar'

  7. forced sightes and trusty ferefulnes l. 8

    'Sightes' is taken by editors as 'sighs'. The endless wind tears the sail 'a pase', apace or swiftly; Petrarch's wind is likewise of sighs, hopes and desires. 'Forced' means strained, wrung out of the lover; 'trusty ferefulnes' is an oxymoron, fear that can be relied upon because it never leaves him. Petrarchan convention made sighs into wind and tears into rain, and Wyatt carries the convention on into the sestet.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, ed. A. K. Foxwell (1913), the text used here

  8. A rayn of teris ll. 9-11

    The sestet opens by completing the allegory of weather. The rain is the lover's tears, the dark cloud the beloved's disdain. Together they have done 'great hinderaunce' to the 'wered cordes', the worn or wearied ropes. In Petrarch the ropes are wet with tears and twisted of error and ignorance; Wyatt's are 'Wrethed', wreathed or twisted, with the same two strands. The rigging that should hold the ship together stands for the bonds of a rational life, now made of folly.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wreathe'

  9. The starres be hid l. 12

    Sailors steered by the stars, and Petrarch's 'duo mei dolci usati segni', my two sweet accustomed signs, are the beloved's eyes. Wyatt keeps their hiddenness but adds the bitter turn that they 'led me to this pain': the eyes that guided him are also what brought him into the storm. Drowned reason, in the next line, completes the picture of a ship with neither guide nor pilot.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Thomas Wyatt, The Complete Poems, ed. R. A. Rebholz (Penguin, 1978), note on this sonnet; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet

  10. dispering of the port l. 14

    'Dispering' is despairing; the port is the safe harbour of requited love, peace or rest. Petrarch's last line says the speaker begins to despair of the port ('tal ch'i' 'ncomincio a desperar del porto'). Wyatt writes 'And I remain dispering', turning an onset into a permanent condition. Critics often single out this change as the most Wyattian moment of the translation: it is bleaker and more final than the Italian. Readers who take the poem politically or religiously hear in the port a hope of royal favour or of grace.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta (Canzoniere) 189, 'Passa la nave mia colma d'oblio'; Thomas Wyatt, The Complete Poems, ed. R. A. Rebholz (Penguin, 1978), note on this sonnet; Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet

  11. Form: an early English sonnet ll. 1-14

    Wyatt was the first poet to write sonnets in English, and this one shows his usual shape. The octave rhymes on only two sounds, keeping the Italian enclosed pattern, but the sestet (cddc ee) ends in a rhymed couplet, a feature that points toward the later English form used by Surrey and Shakespeare. The rhyme of 'comfort' with 'port' asks for stress on the second syllable of 'comfort'. Wyatt's metre is irregular by later standards; Tottel's editor smoothed many of his lines in 1557, and modern editors debate how far the roughness is deliberate. Here the closing couplet gives the despair the weight of a verdict.

    Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt, ed. Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson (Liverpool UP, 1969), commentary on this sonnet; Thomas Wyatt, The Complete Poems, ed. R. A. Rebholz (Penguin, 1978), note on this sonnet; Tottel's Miscellany (1557-1587), ed. Hyder E. Rollins (Harvard UP, 1928-29)
