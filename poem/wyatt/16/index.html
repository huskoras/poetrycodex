Love

Who so list to hount : I know where is an hynde

by Sir Thomas Wyatt 1503–1542

From The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat (ed. A. K. Foxwell, 1913)

Love

Who so list to hount : I know where is an hynde,
But, as for me : helas, I may no more.
The vayne travail hath werid me so sore,
I ame of theim, that farthest cometh behinde

Yet, may I by no means, my weried mynde
Drawe from the Der; but as she fleeth afore
Faynting I folowe. I leve of therefore :
Sins in a nett I seke to hold the wynde.

Who list her hount : I put him oute of dowbte :
As well as I : may spend his tyme in vain.
And graven with Diamonds in letters plain :

There is written, her faier neck rounde abowte :
Noli me tangere for Cesars I ame
And wylde for to hold : though I seme tame.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Source: Petrarch, Rime 190 l. 1

    Wyatt adapts Petrarch's Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, in which the poet sees at dawn a white hind with golden horns under a laurel; round her neck is written in diamonds and topazes 'Let no one touch me ... it has pleased my Caesar to make me free'; at noon he falls into water and she vanishes. Petrarch's poem is a mystical, visionary sonnet about Laura, often read as anticipating her death. Wyatt keeps the hind and the collar but turns the vision into a hunt with rivals, exhaustion and futility, and changes the meaning of the inscription from freedom to possession. The text here, in Foxwell's 1913 edition, preserves the original spelling of the Egerton manuscript (BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 7v). Tottel's Miscellany (1557) printed a different, regularised text, and the Egerton version was not printed until Nott's edition of 1815.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; A. K. Foxwell, ed., The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, 2 vols (London: University of London Press, 1913), the archive's copy-text

  2. Who so list l. 1

    'List' (OED, list, v.1) means to wish, desire or choose, from Old English lystan; it is the same word as 'listless'. The opening 'Who so list to hount: I know where is an hynde' sounds like an invitation to fellow courtiers, as if the speaker were a huntsman offering information about game. The address to other hunters establishes the sonnet's courtly, competitive world, very different from Petrarch's solitary vision. The phrase returns as 'Who list her hount' at line 9, framing the poem's two halves.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'list, v.1'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  3. hynde (hind) l. 1

    A hind is a female deer, especially of the red deer, the noble quarry of royal hunting. In Petrarch the 'candida cerva' (white hind) is Laura. Love-hunting allegory was conventional, and the pun deer/dear was widely exploited (compare 'Der' at line 6). Some readers also hear a bawdy rhyme in 'hynde ... behinde'. The choice of royal game prepares for the revelation that she belongs to Caesar.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'hind, n.1'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  4. helas l. 2

    'Helas' is the French form of 'alas', appropriate to a courtier and diplomat like Wyatt, who served on embassies to France, Italy and the Emperor. 'May' has its older sense 'can, am able' (Old English magan). The speaker withdraws from the chase in the second line, so the rest of the poem is about his inability to let go despite this declaration.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'may, v.1'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  5. vayne travail l. 3

    'Travail' means painful labour or effort, and the spelling covered both 'labour' and 'journey' in Tudor English. 'Vain' means futile, and the idea returns at line 10, 'may spend his tyme in vain'. Petrarch's speaker abandons all his work to follow the hind (lasciai per seguirla ogni lavoro); Wyatt instead stresses exhaustion and the waste of effort, so that the courtly hunt becomes an image of the futile labour of service, a recurrent concern of his satires and lyrics.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'travail, n.1'

  6. of theim, that farthest cometh behinde l. 4

    The image is of a hunting field, with many riders after one deer and the speaker among the last. Unlike Petrarch's solitary lover, Wyatt's speaker is one of a crowd of competing courtiers. The rhyme 'hynde/behinde' links quarry and failure, and readers attentive to the poem's eroticism have heard a sexual innuendo in the rhyme. The line's irregular movement, with the stresses crowding at its end, mimics the laggard's position in the field.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  7. Der (deer, dear) l. 6

    Foxwell's text preserves the manuscript spelling 'Der', which visually suggests both 'deer' and 'dear'. The construction 'my weried mynde / Drawe from the Der' makes the mind itself a hound or hunter that cannot be called off. The pun on deer/dear is a commonplace of Tudor and Elizabethan love poetry (Shakespeare plays on it in Venus and Adonis). The verb 'Drawe' may also recall drawing hounds off a scent, continuing the hunting vocabulary of the octave.

    A. K. Foxwell, ed., The Poems of Sir Thomas Wiat, 2 vols (London: University of London Press, 1913), the archive's copy-text; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  8. as she fleeth afore / Faynting I folowe ll. 6-7

    The phrasing places 'Faynting I folowe' at the start of line 7 after a run-on from line 6, and then breaks off with 'I leve of therefore', so the rhythm enacts both the stumbling pursuit and the decision to stop. The contradiction between the declared withdrawal (l. 2) and the compulsive following is the psychological core of the octave. In Petrarch the lover's eyes are tired but not sated; Wyatt transfers the weariness to the body.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  9. a nett ... the wynde l. 8

    To catch wind in a net was a classical proverb of impossible labour (Erasmus collects it in the Adagia), and Petrarch uses it of loving Laura: the close of the sestina Rime 239 begins 'In rete accolgo l'aura' ('I catch the breeze in a net'), and Rime 212 speaks of chasing the summer breeze and writing on the wind. Petrarch also puns on l'aura and Laura. Wyatt imports the line into a sonnet that otherwise follows Rime 190, so the octave closes with a Petrarchan figure of futility.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 239 (sestina 'Là ver' l'aurora'), l. 37, and 212, ll. 2-4, A. S. Kline translation; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  10. Who list her hount: the sestet l. 9

    The repetition of 'Who list ... hount' marks the turn (volta) at line 9, where the speaker shifts from his own exhaustion to a warning addressed to other hunters: 'I put him oute of dowbte', I assure him. Any rival will waste his time just as he has. The tone becomes knowing and cautionary, preparing for the revelation of the collar.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  11. graven with Diamonds in letters plain l. 11

    In Petrarch the inscription is written 'di diamanti et di topazi'. Wyatt keeps only the diamonds, a stone associated with hardness and invulnerability (Greek adamas, unconquerable), and so with steadfastness, and also with costly royal ornament. 'Graven' means engraved, and 'letters plain' means clearly legible, so no hunter can claim ignorance. The image of a deer collared with an inscription descends from ancient tales of long-lived stags collared by Alexander or Caesar, recorded by Pliny and later writers.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'diamond, n.'

  12. Noli me tangere l. 13

    Petrarch's collar reads 'Nessun mi tocchi' (let no one touch me). Wyatt substitutes the Vulgate's Latin, Christ's words to Mary Magdalene after the Resurrection (John 20:17), which gives the inscription a scriptural, sacred authority and makes the woman's untouchability seem quasi-divine. The effect is unsettling: a phrase about the boundary between the risen body and human touch is used to mark a woman as a king's property. Some critics read the sacrilege as deliberate irony aimed at royal claims; others see simply a heightening of Petrarch's inscription.

    John 20:17 (KJV): 'Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father' (Vulgate: Noli me tangere); Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  13. for Cesars I ame l. 13

    Petrarch's hind declares that it pleased 'my Caesar' to set her free, usually understood as God, who has freed Laura from earthly bonds. Wyatt reverses the sense: 'for Cesars I ame' declares ownership, echoing 'Render unto Caesar' (Matthew 22:21). Since the sixteenth century the poem has been read biographically as about Anne Boleyn, whom Wyatt is said to have admired before Henry VIII claimed her (a story told later by his grandson George Wyatt). The identification is plausible but unproved, and editors such as Rebholz treat it with caution. Either way, the political danger of desiring what a ruler owns is the poem's point.

    Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation; Matthew 22:21 (KJV): 'Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  14. wylde for to hold, though I seme tame l. 14

    The final line is Wyatt's addition, with no equivalent in Petrarch. 'Wylde for to hold' means hard to hold, impossible to keep. It suggests that the hind's apparent submission is deceptive, which may imply that even Caesar's possession is insecure. The same paradox of tame and wild governs 'They flee from me', where once 'gentle, tame, and meek' creatures become wild. The couplet gives the sonnet a sardonic, epigrammatic end.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  15. Form: the early English sonnet ll. 1-14

    Wyatt, with Surrey, introduced the sonnet into English. This poem keeps Petrarch's two-rhyme octave (hynde/behinde/mynde/wynde against more/sore/afore/therefore) but closes with a sestet rhyming cddc ee, ending in a couplet, a pattern that anticipated the later English (Shakespearean) sonnet's epigrammatic close. The lines are pentameters, though Wyatt's rhythm is often irregular, and the colons in Foxwell's transcription mark mid-line pauses. Compare Petrarch's sestet, which rhymes cdecde.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem; Petrarch, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 190, 'Una candida cerva', Italian text with A. S. Kline's translation
