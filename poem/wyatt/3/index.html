They Flee from Me

by Sir Thomas Wyatt 1503–1542

Love

They flee from me that sometime did me seek,
With naked foot stalking within my chamber:
Once have I seen them gentle, tame, and meek,
That now are wild, and do not once remember
That sometime they have put themselves in danger
To take bread at my hand; and now they range,
Busily seeking in continual change.

Thanked be fortune, it hath been otherwise
Twenty times better; but once especial--
In thin array: after a pleasant guise,
When her loose gown did from her shoulders fall,
And she me caught in her arms long and small,
And therewithal so sweetly did me kiss,
And softly said, ‘Dear heart, how like you this?’

It was no dream; for I lay broad awaking:
But all is turn’d now, through my gentleness,
Into a bitter fashion of forsaking;
And I have leave to go of her goodness;
And she also to use new-fangleness.
But since that I unkindly so am servèd,
‘How like you this?’--what hath she now deservèd?

Study notes by the editors

  1. They flee from me: text and occasion l. 1

    Wyatt (c. 1503-1542) left his poems in manuscript; this lyric survives in British Library Egerton MS 2711, the manuscript collection of his lyrics (copied by several hands and annotated by Wyatt himself), and was first printed after his death in Richard Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557), headed 'The louer sheweth how he is forsaken of such as he sometime enioyed'. Tottel regularised Wyatt's rough metre and altered several words. The archive's text follows the Tottel tradition: it has 'Once have I seen them' (MS 'I have seen them'), 'a bitter fashion' (MS 'a strange fashion'), 'unkindly so am served' (MS 'so kindly am served') and a different last line (MS 'I would fain know what she hath deserved'). Modern editors such as Rebholz and Muir and Thomson print the manuscript text, and students should know that several famous cruxes depend on which version is read. No occasion can be fixed; biographical readings linking the poem to Anne Boleyn are speculative.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Songes and Sonettes [Tottel's Miscellany] (London: Richard Tottel, 1557), where the poem is headed 'The louer sheweth how he is forsaken of such as he sometime enioyed'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on the poem

  2. Rhyme royal st. 1

    The poem is in rhyme royal, the seven-line pentameter stanza rhyming ababbcc that Chaucer used in Troilus and Criseyde and the Parlement of Foules, and that the name (traditionally linked with James I of Scotland's Kingis Quair) associates with courtly love-complaint. Wyatt's choice places the poem in a native tradition rather than the Italian sonnet. In the manuscript the lines are often metrically rough, with feminine endings ('chamber', 'remember', 'danger') and shifting stresses; whether this roughness is artful or the product of an unsettled pronunciation of final -e has long been debated. Tottel's version, printed here, evens out several lines into smoother iambic pentameter. Each stanza moves through a stage of the argument: the general complaint, the remembered night, and the bitter present.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  3. They: the wild creatures ll. 1-7

    The first stanza never says who 'they' are. The language of taming ('gentle, tame, and meek', 'wild', 'take bread at my hand', 'range') makes them sound like half-domesticated animals: deer feeding from the hand, or hawks in the falconer's mews, where 'wild' and 'range' were technical terms. Only in stanza 2 does the plural narrow to one woman, so the metaphor implies that the speaker once had many lovers. The animal figure reverses the usual courtly hunt in which the man pursues: here the creatures came seeking him, entered his private chamber, and have now abandoned him. Commentators also note the sexual frankness of the conceit, which conventional Petrarchan complaint avoids. Compare the hunted hind of Wyatt's 'Who so list to hount'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  4. With naked foot stalking l. 2

    'Stalking' here means treading softly, cautiously, with a stealthy gait (OED, stalk, v.), not striding. The phrase combines the animal image with a human one: bare feet suggest both creatures and women coming secretly, undressed, to a private room. The chamber, in a Tudor great house, was the private bedroom, so the line quietly establishes an erotic setting before stanza 2 makes it explicit. The detail also contrasts with the present, in which the same creatures are 'wild' and 'range' outside. RPO's note glosses 'stalking' as walking carefully in a stealthy way.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stalk, v.'

  5. danger l. 5

    In Middle and early modern English 'danger' (from Old French dangier, ultimately Latin dominium, 'lordship') meant power, jurisdiction or control over another; 'in someone's danger' meant in their power or under obligation to them. In the Roman de la Rose and courtly love literature, Dangier is also the personified aloofness or resistance of the lady. Wyatt's phrase can thus mean that the creatures once submitted themselves to his power, risking capture, in order to feed from his hand; the modern sense of risk is also present. The RPO edition glosses it as under obligation to me, in my debt, or possibly in my power.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'danger, n.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  6. To take bread at my hand l. 6

    The image completes the taming metaphor: wild deer or birds that have learned to eat from a person's hand have accepted his mastery. Read through the human story, it suggests the courtly economy of favour, in which dependants seek benefits from a patron, and so mixes erotic and political dependence, a mixture critics such as Greenblatt find characteristic of Wyatt's court poetry. The reversal is that the speaker, once the giver, is now left with nothing to give that they want.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  7. range ... continual change ll. 6-7

    'Range' (OED, range, v.) means to wander or rove, and was used of hawks and hounds that roam beyond control and, figuratively, of people who stray from fidelity. 'Busily seeking' pointedly echoes 'did me seek' in line 1: the creatures still seek, but now anything new. 'Continual change' anticipates 'new-fangleness' in line 19 and the poem's charge of fickleness. The Egerton manuscript reads 'Busily seeking with a continual change'; Tottel's 'in continual change' is printed here.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'range, v.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  8. Thanked be fortune l. 8

    Fortune, the classical and medieval goddess of chance whose wheel raises and lowers human beings (familiar from Boethius' Consolation of Philosophy, which Chaucer translated), presides over both love and courtly careers. The speaker thanks her that things were once 'otherwise', that is, better; the gratitude is tinged with irony, since Fortune's gifts are by definition unstable and the poem goes on to record her reversal. The word anticipates the 'change' and 'new-fangleness' associated with the woman, who acts like Fortune herself.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  9. Twenty times better l. 9

    The phrase can be read as an intensifier (things were twenty times better) or as a count of occasions (there were twenty times when it went better). RPO's editors list both possibilities. The second reading prepares for 'but once especial', where the speaker singles out one memorable night from many; the first sounds more like conversational boasting. The ambiguity keeps the tone casual and worldly.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  10. once especial l. 9

    'Especial' (here an adverbial use, 'especially, in particular') introduces the narrative heart of the poem. The Egerton manuscript has 'once in special'. The broken syntax and the dash in the archive's text convey the speaker turning from general reflection to a vivid particular memory. The sentence that follows is loose and paratactic, a run of clauses joined by 'When' and 'And', which gives the recollection the feel of spoken narrative rather than formal complaint.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'especial, adj.'

  11. In thin array; pleasant guise l. 10

    'Array' means clothing; 'thin array' suggests a light, scanty garment such as a night-gown. 'Guise' (OED, guise, n.) means manner, style or fashion, including fashion in dress; RPO glosses 'pleasant guise' as pleasing style, behaviour or perhaps livery. The phrase is deliberately understated: the poem's eroticism is conveyed by a few concrete details (the loose gown, the slender arms) rather than Petrarchan blazon. The word 'guise' also anticipates 'fashion' in line 17, linking her style of undress to her later fashion of forsaking.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'guise, n.'

  12. her loose gown did from her shoulders fall l. 11

    The image is remarkable for its physical directness in early Tudor lyric. The gown falls of its own accord, yet the woman is the active party throughout the stanza: she catches him, kisses him, speaks. This reversal of roles, with the woman as seducer, is central to many modern readings that see the poem as about power as much as desire.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  13. long and small l. 12

    In earlier English 'small' (OED, small, adj.) commonly meant slender or slim, especially of the waist, fingers or arms, and is used in praise of feminine beauty in medieval lyric and romance. The doublet 'long and small' is a traditional formula of Middle English verse, and its homely plainness contrasts with the elaborate comparisons of Petrarchan praise. The embrace also echoes the taming image in reverse: she now 'caught' him, as a hunter catches prey.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'small, adj.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  14. Dear heart, how like you this? l. 14

    The woman's words are the only direct speech in the poem and its emotional centre. 'Dear heart' is a common endearment, but RPO's editors note a pun on 'hart', the male deer, which ties the scene to the hunting imagery: now he is the tamed beast. 'How like you this?' is teasing and confident, and its repetition in line 21 turns the question into an accusation: how does she like being treated as he has been? The echo is one of the poem's most discussed effects, since it compresses the movement from intimacy to grievance.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A.   15. It was no dream; broad awaking l. 15

    'Broad waking' means wide awake (RPO). The insistence pointedly rejects a familiar literary convention, the erotic dream or vision of the beloved found in Petrarch and in medieval dream poetry; Petrarch's Rime 190, which Wyatt adapted in 'Who so list to hount', also ends with the vision vanishing. Here reality, not fantasy, is what makes the loss painful. Tottel's 'broad awaking' adds a syllable to the manuscript's 'broad waking'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  16. gentleness l. 16

    'Gentle' and 'gentleness' carry the courtly sense of noble birth and refined, generous behaviour (the gentilesse of Chaucer), and also, in context, the sense of 'tame', echoing 'gentle, tame, and meek' in line 3. The speaker suggests that his courtesy, perhaps his failure to hold her, allowed her to leave; some readers detect self-justification or irony, since he describes himself in the vocabulary of virtue while the poem is about sexual possession.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gentleness, n.'; Stephen Greenblatt, Renaissance Self-Fashioning: From More to Shakespeare (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980), ch. 3 on Wyatt

  17. a bitter fashion of forsaking l. 17

    Egerton reads 'Into a strange fashion of forsaking'; the archive's 'bitter' is Tottel's emendation, which makes the speaker's feeling explicit and loses the sense of something alien or estranging (strange, from Old French estrange, foreign). 'Fashion' means manner or mode, but also a current style; the woman's desertion is presented as fashionable behaviour, linked to 'new-fangleness' two lines later. The choice between readings affects tone: 'strange' is cool and puzzled, 'bitter' openly resentful.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Songes and Sonettes [Tottel's Miscellany] (London: Richard Tottel, 1557), where the poem is headed 'The louer sheweth how he is forsaken of such as he sometime enioyed'; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on the poem

  18. I have leave to go of her goodness l. 18

    'Leave' is formal permission, as given by a superior to a servant or retainer, and 'of her goodness' means by her gracious favour. RPO's editors gloss the phrase as ironic. The speaker presents himself as a dismissed servant of the lady, inverting the language of the opening stanza, where creatures sought his hand. The parallel clause 'And she also to use new-fangleness' makes the arrangement sound like a mutual release, while the tone shows it is nothing of the kind.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  19. new-fangleness l. 19

    'Newfangleness' (from Middle English newefangel, eager for new things) means a liking for novelty and hence inconstancy. Chaucer uses it of fickle lovers: the Squire's Tale says that creatures love 'newefangelnesse' by their very nature, in a story about a deserted falcon, and the lyric 'Against Women Unconstant' opens by reproaching a lady for her 'newefangelnesse', and RPO glosses it as fondness for novelty, fickleness. The word gathers up the earlier images of 'continual change' and 'fashion': the woman's desertion belongs to a culture of restless novelty at court.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'newfangleness, n.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text and commentary on the poem

  20. unkindly so am served l. 20

    This is the poem's best-known textual crux. The Egerton manuscript reads 'But since that I so kindly am served'. 'Kindly' there is ironic ('so nicely treated') and also carries the older sense 'naturally, according to kind' (OED, kindly, adv.): she has behaved as her nature, or as a wild creature, would. RPO's editors gloss it both ways. Tottel, printed here, substituted the plainer 'unkindly', removing the double meaning. Most modern critics prefer the manuscript reading as richer and more characteristic of Wyatt's dry irony.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'They flee from me that sometime did me seek', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (text from BL Egerton MS 2711, fol. 26v; cf. Harrier, The Canon of Sir Thomas Wyatt's Poetry, 1975), line notes; Songes and Sonettes [Tottel's Miscellany] (London: Richard Tottel, 1557), where the poem is headed 'The louer sheweth how he is forsaken of such as he sometime enioyed'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kindly, adv.'; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on the poem

  21. what hath she now deserved? l. 21

    Tottel rewrote the last line to repeat 'How like you this?' from line 14, sharpening the irony; Egerton reads 'I would fain know what she hath deserved'. In both versions the question is left open: does she deserve punishment, or the same treatment she gave him, or nothing at all? Readers divide over whether the ending is vindictive, resigned or self-mocking, and neither version answers the question. Tottel's echo makes the poem more neatly circular but more explicitly accusatory.
