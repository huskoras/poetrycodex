Love

To His Lute

by Sir Thomas Wyatt 1503–1542

Love

My lute, awake! perform the last
Labour that thou and I shall waste,
And end that I have now begun;
For when this song is said and past,
My lute, be still, for I have done.

As to be heard where ear is none,
As lead to grave in marble stone,
My song may pierce her heart as soon:
Should we then sing, or sigh, or moan?
No, no, my lute! for I have done.

The rocks do not so cruelly
Repulse the waves continually,
As she my suit and affectiòn;
So that I am past remedy:
Whereby my lute and I have done.

Proud of the spoil that thou hast got
Of simple hearts thorough Love’s shot,
By whom, unkind, thou hast them won;
Think not he hath his bow forgot,
Although my lute and I have done.

Vengeance shall fall on thy disdain,
That makest but game of earnest pain:
Trow not alone under the sun
Unquit to cause thy lover’s plain,
Although my lute and I have done.

May chance thee lie wither’d and old
The winter nights that are so cold,
Plaining in vain unto the moon:
Thy wishes then dare not be told:
Care then who list! for I have done.

And then may chance thee to repent
The time that thou has lost and spent
To cause thy lover’s sigh and swoon:
Then shalt thou know beauty but lent,
And wish and want as I have done.

Now cease, my lute! this is the last
Labour that thou and I shall waste,
And ended is that we begun:
Now is this song both sung and past--
My lute, be still, for I have done.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Text, title and attribution title; l. 1

    Wyatt (c. 1503-1542) was a courtier and diplomat to Henry VIII. He did not publish his lyrics, which circulated in manuscript, above all in Egerton MS 2711, a collection closely tied to Wyatt that carries corrections thought to be in his hand. The poem was first printed in Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557), which gave it a title and smoothed its metre. A later tradition, recorded in Harington's Nugae Antiquae, gave it to George Boleyn, Lord Rochford, Anne Boleyn's brother. The Egerton copy is the main reason editors keep it with Wyatt. Opening by addressing the instrument is a classical device (Horace speaks to his lyre in Odes 1.32), adapted here for the court song.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Bodleian Digital Miscellanies Index, 'My lute awake perform the last'; Luminarium, 'My lute awake, perform the last' (note on the Rochford attribution in Nugae Antiquae); R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem

  2. The lute l. 5

    At Henry VIII's court poems were often sung to the lute, or imagined as sung. Wyatt makes the instrument a companion who shares the 'labour' and must also fall silent. Nobody knows whether this poem was ever actually set to music, and John Stevens (Music and Poetry in the Early Tudor Court, 1961) warns against assuming that every Wyatt 'song' was sung. The address presents the poem as a rejected lover's last song: a public act of giving up that is also a threat to the lady.

    John Stevens, Music and Poetry in the Early Tudor Court (London, 1961); Horace, Odes 1.32 ('Poscimur'); R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem

  3. labour ... waste l. 2

    The RPO editors note that 'labour' means both effort and petition. 'Waste' anticipates the poem's argument that the song is useless; the lover performs it anyway, partly to accuse her. The first stanza is framed as an ending ('the last', 'I have done'), so the whole poem is a farewell. The verb 'perform' in l. 1 belongs to the language of music and of duty: the lute is asked to do its last service, as a servant would.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'labour, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'waste, v.'

  4. Stanza and refrain st. 1; refrain

    The b-rhyme ('begun', 'done', 'soon', 'won', 'sun', 'moon', 'swoon') runs through all eight stanzas, so each one comes back to the same sound and the same refrain. The refrain's grammar shifts as the poem goes on. First it gives a reason ('for I have done'). Then it becomes a concession that leads into the threats ('Although my lute and I have done'). Finally it becomes 'as I have done' (st. 7), where the lady will suffer as the lover has. 'Done' means finished, but in that last use he is doing something to her by prophecy. The final stanza almost repeats the first, closing the song in a ring.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool, 1969), commentary

  5. Impossibilities ll. 6-8

    This is the figure called adynaton (Latin impossibilia), in which something will happen only when the impossible does. 'As lead to grave in marble stone' means 'as soon as lead could engrave marble', since lead is too soft to cut stone (RPO note). The image also makes her heart marble, hard and cold, a Petrarchan commonplace that the rocks of stanza 3 extend.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Ernst Robert Curtius, European Literature and the Latin Middle Ages (1948; trans. 1953), on the 'world upside down' and impossibilia; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem

  6. Rocks and waves ll. 11-13

    The cruel lady as a rock beaten by the sea comes from Petrarch, whom Wyatt translated, and from classical poetry. Here the lover is the sea, coming back again and again. A 'suit' is a lover's petition, in court terms almost a legal plea. 'Affection' is stressed on its last syllable, marked in the archive with a grave accent, so that it rhymes with 'begun' and 'done'.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool, 1969), commentary; Petrarch, Canzoniere (Rerum vulgarium fragmenta), e.g. 265, on the lady's hard heart

  7. past remedy l. 14

    'Remedy' keeps its medical sense of cure, part of the old picture of love as an illness. Behind 'past remedy ... I have done' stands the proverb 'past cure, past care' (compare Shakespeare, Sonnet 147): since nothing will help, he gives up trying. 'Whereby' means 'for which reason'. The 'past remedy' stanza is the last of the lover's complaints. From the next stanza onward the poem turns outward to accuse the lady and predict her punishment, so the line marks the hinge between grief and anger.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'remedy, n.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem

  8. Love's shot l. 17

    The lady is pictured as a victor proud of her 'spoil'. She won those hearts not by her own merit but because Love's arrows struck her admirers. 'Thorough' is an old form of 'through' and supplies an extra syllable. 'Unkind' in l. 18 means both cruel and unnatural, against 'kind' (nature). The next line warns that Cupid has not forgotten his bow (l. 19): he can shoot her too.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-73 (Cupid's two arrows); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'thorough, prep.'

  9. Cupid's bow l. 19

    Here the poem turns from complaint to a prophecy of revenge. The lover hands his grievance over to Love, who will punish the proud. In Ovid, Cupid punishes Apollo's boasting by making him love Daphne, who runs from him. The classical pattern of the scorner scorned is a likely background to Wyatt's warning. The pronoun 'he' in the line is Love (Cupid), not the lover. The warning is put lightly, almost as a courtier's joke, but it opens the series of threats that runs through the rest of the poem.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (Apollo and Daphne)

  10. Vengeance st. 5

    'Makest but game of earnest pain' (l. 22) sets play against seriousness. She treats as sport what is in earnest for him, which is also a charge against the court's whole game of love. 'Trow' means believe or suppose. 'Unquit' means unpaid, here unpunished. 'Thy lover's plain' means your lover's complaint, since to 'plain' is to lament. The sense is: do not imagine you are the one person on earth who can make a lover lament and get away with it.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trow, v.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unquit, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'plain, v.'

  11. Old age on winter nights ll. 26-28

    In Horace, Odes 1.25, Lydia has grown old and mourns, alone in a back street, the lovers who now scorn her, while the north wind rages on moonless nights. Odes 4.13 mocks Lyce's lost beauty. Wyatt turns the courtly complaint into this classical prophecy of revenge. The RPO text reads 'wethered', glossed 'withered (possibly weathered)'. The Egerton manuscript reads 'night' where the Devonshire manuscript has 'nights' (RPO). In 'Plaining in vain unto the moon', the moon, emblem of chaste Diana and of change, is her only listener.

    Horace, Odes 1.25 and 4.13; Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem

  12. who list l. 30

    'List' comes from Old English lystan, to please, and survives in 'listless'. The tone is a shrug, but his detailed picture of her future misery shows how much he still cares. Her wishes 'dare not be told' because, as an old woman, she will be ashamed to say what she desires. The same tone of mock-indifference ends other Wyatt lyrics, which often close with the lover stepping away from the lady while keeping his grievance.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'list, v.1'

  13. Beauty lent l. 34

    The idea that beauty is borrowed and will be called in by time belongs to the carpe diem tradition, which urges the young to love before their beauty fades (compare Shakespeare, Sonnet 4: 'Nature's bequest gives nothing, but doth lend'). Wyatt uses it not to persuade her but to predict her regret. She will repent the time 'lost and spent' in causing pain.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem; William Shakespeare, Sonnet 4, l. 3; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lend, v.'

  14. The turned refrain l. 35

    Here 'done' stops meaning 'finished' and comes to mean 'endured': the lady's future will repeat the lover's past. The last stanza then goes back almost word for word to the first ('Now cease, my lute! this is the last ...'), and 'ended is that we begun' completes 'end that I have now begun' from l. 3. The ring makes the song a closed performance, its ending acted out in its form.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1978), notes to this poem; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool, 1969), commentary; Representative Poetry Online, 'My Lute Awake', ed. F. D. Hoeniger and Ian Lancashire (from BL Egerton MS 2711, fols. 43v-44)
