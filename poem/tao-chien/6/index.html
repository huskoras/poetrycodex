Nature

I built my hut in a zone of human habitation

by T’ao Ch’ien AD 365–427

Translated by Arthur Waley (1918) · From A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (1918)

Nature

I built my hut in a zone of human habitation,
Yet near me there sounds no noise of horse or coach.
Would you know how that is possible?
A heart that is distant creates a wilderness round it.
I pluck chrysanthemums under the eastern hedge,
Then gaze long at the distant summer hills.
The mountain air is fresh at the dusk of day:
The flying birds two by two return.
In these things there lies a deep meaning;
Yet when we would express it, words suddenly fail us.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Tao Yuanming, 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 title, l. 1

    Tao Qian, also called Tao Yuanming (365-427), served briefly in minor offices and then retired, in 405, to farm near Mount Lu in modern Jiangxi; he became the model of the recluse-poet for all later Chinese literature. 飲酒 Yin jiu, 'Drinking Wine', is a set of twenty five-character poems, which his preface says he wrote when drunk on long autumn evenings. No. 5 is the most famous of them and perhaps of all his poems. Waley, who calls Tao 'the greatest of these recluses', prints it in A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (1918) without a title, using his first line instead, and renders each Chinese line as one long unrhymed English line.

    Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; 'Tao Yuanming', Wikipedia

  2. 'among men' (ren jing 人境) l. 1

    結廬在人境，而無車馬喧, 'I built my hut in the human realm, yet there is no clamour of carriage and horse'. 人境 ren jing, 'the human realm', is literally 'the boundary/region of men', which Waley renders with the bureaucratic-sounding 'zone of human habitation'. The 'carriages and horses' are those of officials and visitors, the traffic of ambition and social life. The paradox, recluse not in the wilds but amid ordinary life, distinguishes Tao from the 'mountain hermits' of earlier tradition: reclusion is a state of mind.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983)

  3. 'when the mind is far, the place becomes remote' l. 4

    問君何能爾？心遠地自偏, 'you ask how this can be? With the heart far away, the place becomes remote of itself'. The question-and-answer form, with the poet addressing himself as 'you' (君), introduces a philosophical note. 心遠 'heart/mind distant' is the key idea, related to Daoist and Buddhist ideals of a mind free from attachment. Waley's 'creates a wilderness round it' is a vivid expansion of 地自偏, 'the place is naturally out of the way'; 'wilderness' adds a sense of wild nature not in the Chinese, where the place is merely secluded.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983)

  4. chrysanthemums under the eastern hedge l. 5

    采菊東籬下, 'plucking chrysanthemums by the eastern hedge'. The chrysanthemum blooms in late autumn, after other flowers fade, and was thought to prolong life; its petals were steeped in wine for the Double Ninth festival (ninth day of the ninth month). Tao's poems mention it repeatedly, and after him the chrysanthemum and the 'eastern hedge' became shorthand for the recluse's life and for Tao himself. The eastern hedge places the flowers in his own garden: the scene is domestic, not wild. The plucking may be for wine, linking the image to the series title.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  5. 'see' (jian 見) or 'gaze at' (wang 望) the Southern Mountain? l. 6

    The received text reads 悠然見南山, 'carefree, I see the Southern Mountain'. Some editions read 望 wang, 'gaze at'. Su Shi (1037-1101) insisted on 見 jian: the mountain comes into view while the poet is picking flowers, so that scene and mind meet without intention; to read 'gaze' would make the poem lose all its spirit. His judgement helped fix the received text, and Xiaofei Tian's study of Tao's manuscript transmission is a good introduction to how readers' images of Tao shaped his text. Waley's 'gaze long' reads 悠然 youran ('leisurely, far-reaching') as 'long', and makes the looking deliberate, the very reading Su Shi rejected.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; Su Shi, colophon on Tao Yuanming's 'Drinking Wine' (Dongpo tiba), quoted in a Tsinghua University essay on the poem; Xiaofei Tian, Tao Yuanming and Manuscript Culture: The Record of a Dusty Table (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 2005); James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary

  6. the Southern Mountain (Nanshan): Mount Lu l. 6

    南山 Nanshan, 'the Southern Mountain', is usually identified with Mount Lu (Lushan), south of Tao's home near Xunyang. The phrase also echoes the Shijing, where 'the Southern Mountain' is a figure of permanence and long life ('like the longevity of the Southern Mountain', Mao 166), so it suits the chrysanthemum's associations of longevity. Waley's 'the distant summer hills' replaces the specific mountain with a generalised landscape; 'summer' is odd, since chrysanthemums bloom in autumn, and may reflect his reading of 南, 'south', associated in Chinese cosmology with summer. 'Distant' renders the sense of 悠然.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  7. evening mountain air l. 7

    山氣日夕佳, 'the mountain air at sunset is fine'. 氣 qi, 'breath, vapour, air', here refers to the hazy atmosphere round the mountain at evening, which Tao sees as beautiful (佳 jia). Waley's 'fresh' changes a visual impression to one of touch or smell. Dusk also marks the end of the day and, by implication, of life, a time for return.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary

  8. birds returning at evening l. 8

    飛鳥相與還, 'flying birds return together'. 相與 means 'together, in company', and Waley's 'two by two' adds an image of pairing not specified in the Chinese. 'Return' (還 huan) links the line to Tao's famous prose-poem 'Homeward Bound' (歸去來兮辭, 405), where the clouds drift out of the hills and 'the birds, weary of flying, know to return'. The birds embody a natural rhythm the poet has rejoined by leaving office. Compare Waley's version of 'Returning to the Fields' (tao-chien/8) in this archive.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983)

  9. 'true meaning' (zhen yi 真意) l. 9

    此中有真意, 'in this there is a true meaning'. 真 zhen, 'genuine, true, authentic', is a key Daoist term, used in Zhuangzi for the original nature that society corrupts; 真意 is the real significance of the scene, or of the life it represents. Waley's 'deep' interprets rather than translates 真. The poem does not say what the meaning is, which is its point.

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; A. R. Davis, T'ao Yuan-ming (AD 365-427): His Works and Their Meaning, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  10. 'I want to explain it but have already forgotten the words' l. 10

    欲辨已忘言, 'I wish to explain it, but have already forgotten the words'. The phrase 忘言 wangyan, 'forget words', recalls Zhuangzi ch. 26: the fish-trap exists for the fish, the snare for the hare, words for meaning; 'once you have got the meaning, you forget the words', and Zhuangzi longs for someone who has forgotten words to talk with. Tao's line thus claims a wordless understanding at the end of a poem. Waley's 'words suddenly fail us' makes it a failure of expression rather than a forgetting after understanding, and his 'us' generalises the poet's 'I' (implied in the Chinese).

    Tao Yuanming, 'Yin jiu' (飲酒二十首), no. 5, Chinese Wikisource; Zhuangzi, ch. 26 'External Things' (外物), Chinese Text Project (Legge trans.); James Robert Hightower, trans., The Poetry of T'ao Ch'ien (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1970), 'Drinking Wine' no. 5 with commentary; Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290
