Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth

by Arthur Hugh Clough 1819–1861

From Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough

History & Politics

Say not the struggle nought availeth,
The labour and the wounds are vain,
The enemy faints not, nor faileth,
And as things have been they remain.

If hopes were dupes, fears may be liars;
It may be, in yon smoke concealed,
Your comrades chase e’en now the fliers,
And, but for you, possess the field.

For while the tired waves, vainly breaking,
Seem here no painful inch to gain,
Far back, through creeks and inlets making,
Comes silent, flooding in, the main,

And not by eastern windows only,
When daylight comes, comes in the light,
In front, the sun climbs slow, how slowly,
But westward, look, the land is bright.

1849

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and publication l. 17 (date line)

    Clough wrote the poem in 1849. It first appeared, as 'The Struggle', in the American art journal The Crayon in August 1855, then untitled in the posthumous Poems of 1862 (Clough died in 1861), and under its first line in The Poems and Prose Remains (1869). The year matters: the revolutions of 1848-9 in France, Italy and elsewhere had ended in defeat, and Clough himself witnessed the French siege of the Roman Republic in 1849, the setting of his Amours de Voyage. Commentators suggest the failed revolutions of 1848-9 may have inspired it, though the poem is not tied to one event and has been read as a poem of religious and personal perseverance too; Clough had resigned his Oxford fellowship in 1848 over religious doubt.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes; Wikipedia, 'Say Not the Struggle Naught Availeth'; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  2. nought availeth l. 1

    To 'avail' is to be of use or profit; 'the struggle nought availeth' means that the effort accomplishes nothing. The archaic third-person '-eth' recalls the King James Bible (e.g. 'The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much', James 5:16). The poem opens by quoting the despairing voice it will answer: 'Say not' introduces four complaints (the struggle is useless, labour and wounds are vain, the enemy does not weaken, nothing changes). The rest of the poem is a reply, so its structure is that of an argument against despair.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'avail'; Bible (KJV), James 5:16

  3. faints not, nor faileth l. 3

    The phrasing echoes Isaiah 40:28: 'the everlasting God, the LORD ... fainteth not, neither is weary'. The irony is that the despairing speaker attributes divine tirelessness to the enemy. Isaiah's chapter continues with the promise that 'they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength ... they shall run, and not be weary' (40:31), the hope Clough's poem goes on to recover. The enemy is never named: it may be political reaction, error, or the world's inertia.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 40:28; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 40:31

  4. as things have been they remain l. 4

    The fourth complaint is that history stands still. In 2 Peter 3:4 the scoffers of the last days say 'all things continue as they were from the beginning of the creation', mocking hope of Christ's coming; the epistle answers that God's time is not ours. Clough's poem gives a secular version of that answer: change may be happening out of sight. The line's heavy, unvaried rhythm suits stasis.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:4; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  5. If hopes were dupes, fears may be liars l. 5

    The line answers the opening with a neat logical turn. A 'dupe' is one who is deceived; hopes that proved 'dupes' were fooled by events. But by the same token fears may be 'liars', deceivers. Clough, famous among his contemporaries for scrupulous doubt, applies scepticism to pessimism itself: if knowledge is uncertain, it cuts both ways. The past tense 'were' (concerning hopes already disappointed) against the present 'may be' (for fears) keeps the future open.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dupe'; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  6. in yon smoke concealed l. 6

    Before smokeless powder, black-powder smoke blanketed battlefields, so that a soldier could rarely judge the course of a fight. 'Yon' (archaic 'that over there') places the speaker on the field, pointing. The stanza imagines comrades 'e'en now' (even now) pursuing 'the fliers', the fleeing enemy, and 'but for you' (were it not for your absence or despair) already in possession of the field. The image recalls the fighting of 1848-9, when Clough was in Rome during the French siege, though the poem keeps it general.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes; Wikipedia, 'Say Not the Struggle Naught Availeth'

  7. the fliers l. 7

    'Fliers' (from 'flee', not 'fly') are fugitives, soldiers in flight from a lost battle. 'E'en' is the poetic contraction of 'even'. 'Possess the field' (l. 8) is the military idiom for victory: the side that holds the battlefield at the end has won. The modern reader may hear 'fliers' as airmen, a sense that gave the poem fresh resonance when Churchill quoted it during the Second World War, although he quoted only the last two stanzas.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flier'; Winston Churchill, broadcast 'Report on the War', 27 April 1941 (transcript, ibiblio)

  8. but for you l. 8

    The phrase turns the argument back on the listener. 'But for you' can mean 'except for you' (the victorious comrades possess the field without you) or 'if it were not for you' (they would possess it, but your defection prevents it). Either way, despair has consequences: the disheartened soldier is missing from his own victory. The second-person address, 'Say not' and 'Your comrades', gives the poem the quality of a friend's exhortation.

    H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  9. the tired waves, vainly breaking ll. 9-10

    The second image comes from the shore. Individual waves break 'vainly', seeming not to gain a 'painful inch', yet the tide is rising: far back, through creeks and inlets, the 'main' floods silently in. The point is that progress may be invisible at the place where one strives. 'Vainly' answers 'vain' in line 2. The sea image may be compared with Matthew Arnold's 'Dover Beach' (published 1867), whose 'Sea of Faith' is ebbing with a 'melancholy, long, withdrawing roar'; Arnold was Clough's close friend and later mourned him in 'Thyrsis'. Clough's tide flows in.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  10. making l. 11

    'Making' here is intransitive: the sea makes (advances, makes its way) through creeks and inlets. The sense survives in nautical phrases such as 'the tide is making' (rising) and 'to make for port'. The participle is delayed to the end of the line, and the subject 'the main' is held back until the end of the stanza, so the syntax itself flows in slowly, enacting the tide's silent advance.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'make'; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  11. the main l. 12

    'The main' (from 'main sea', the principal or open sea) is the ocean in its mass. The contrast is between the visible but futile waves at the front and the whole sea moving silently behind. 'Silent' and 'flooding in' stress that the decisive movement is neither dramatic nor noticed. The stanza's syntax places 'the main' last, as a culminating revelation.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'main'; Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes

  12. not by eastern windows only ll. 13-14

    The final image moves from war and sea to dawn. Watching the east, where the sun 'climbs slow, how slowly', one might think the day hardly begun; but look west and the land is already lit. Light comes in by more than the obvious windows. The emotional emphasis falls on patience ('slow, how slowly' mimics the sun's laborious rise) and on looking in an unexpected direction for evidence of progress. Each of the three images (battle, tide, dawn) shows a hidden or distant gain invisible from where the speaker stands.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)

  13. But westward, look, the land is bright l. 16

    The closing line, with its sudden imperative 'look', is the poem's turn to hope. In his broadcast of 27 April 1941, at a low point of the war, Winston Churchill quoted the last two stanzas as 'appropriate to our fortunes tonight'. In context the 'westward' brightness was widely taken to point toward the United States, whose Lend-Lease aid had begun that spring; the poem was given a political meaning Clough could not have intended. The episode made the poem one of the best known in English. Read in 1849, the West may mean simply the unexpected quarter where change has already arrived.

    Winston Churchill, broadcast 'Report on the War', 27 April 1941 (transcript, ibiblio); Wikipedia, 'Say Not the Struggle Naught Availeth'

  14. Stanza and argument st. 1-4

    The poem's sixteen lines are iambic tetrameter quatrains rhyming abab, akin to the 'long measure' of English hymns. The odd lines mostly have feminine (two-syllable) endings ('availeth/faileth', 'liars/fliers', 'breaking/making', 'only/slowly'), which give the verse a lingering, unemphatic movement, while the even lines close firmly. Structurally the first stanza states the despairing case, the second answers it with the battle, the third with the tide, the fourth with the sunrise. The plain diction and the restraint of the claims ('It may be') are typical of Clough's honest, hesitant optimism.

    Representative Poetry Online (ed. M. F. Nims), 'Say Not the Struggle Nought Availeth', notes; Wikipedia, 'Say Not the Struggle Naught Availeth'; H. F. Lowry, A. L. P. Norrington and F. L. Mulhauser, eds, The Poems of Arthur Hugh Clough (Oxford, 1951; 2nd edn, ed. Mulhauser, 1974)
