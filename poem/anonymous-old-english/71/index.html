Love

Wulf and Eadwacer (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Love

Leodum is minum swylce him mon lac gife;
willað hy hine aþecgan, gif he on þreat cymeð.
Ungelic is us.
Wulf is on iege, ic on oþerre.
Fæst is þæt eglond, fenne biworpen.
Sindon wælreowe weras þær on ige;
willað hy hine aþecgan, gif he on þreat cymeð.
Ungelice is us.
Wulfes ic mines widlastum wenum dogode;
þonne hit wæs renig weder ond ic reotugu sæt,
þonne mec se beaducafa bogum bilegde,
wæs me wyn to þon, wæs me hwæþre eac lað.
Wulf, min Wulf, wena me þine
seoce gedydon, þine seldcymas,
murnende mod, nales meteliste.
Gehyrest þu, Eadwacer? Uncerne earne hwelp
bireð wulf to wuda.
þæt mon eaþe tosliteð þætte næfre gesomnad wæs,
uncer giedd geador.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Wulf and Eadwacer: the poem and its problems ll. 1-19

    The poem is on fols 100v-101r of the Exeter Book, directly after Deor and before the first riddle, and Benjamin Thorpe (1842) printed it as Riddle 1, admitting he could not make sense of it. Henry Bradley (1888) first read it as a dramatic monologue spoken by a woman, and most critics now group it with The Wife's Lament as a Frauenlied or woman's song. The feminine adjective reotugu (l. 10) supports a female speaker. The usual reading has three figures: the speaker, her lover Wulf on another island, and Eadwacer, perhaps her husband or captor, with a child (hwelp). Alternatives include Wulf as her son (Frese 1983), Eadwacer as only an epithet for Wulf (Adams 1958), the Norse Signý story (Schofield 1902), a charm (Fry 1971), and Peter Baker's argument that its ambiguity is designed.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations); Berit Åström, The Politics of Tradition: Examining the History of the Old English Poems The Wife's Lament and Wulf and Eadwacer (Umeå, 2002); Ann Marie Donahue (Medieval Forum, 2004), ''Of this I can make no sense': Wulf and Eadwacer and the Destabilization of Meaning' (survey of readings)

  2. lac: gift, offering, sacrifice l. 1

    Lac is a wide word: 'play, sport', 'battle' (as in beadu-lac), 'gift, present' and especially 'sacrificial offering' (the usual word for the sacrifices of the Old Testament). The speaker's people treat 'him', presumably Wulf, as a gift or prey that has been delivered to them. Leodum is minum, 'to my people', places the speaker among people hostile to Wulf. The first line therefore immediately sets her loyalties against her kin, as in The Wife's Lament, where the lord's kinsmen plot to separate the lovers.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. lác; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations)

  3. aþecgan: 'receive', 'feed' or 'consume'? ll. 2, 7

    Bosworth-Toller glossed a-þecgan 'to receive', while its supplement substitutes 'to take food, consume'; þicgan is 'to take, receive, eat'. The line thus swings between welcome and violence. Many translators choose 'kill' or 'seize', joining the idea of a lac as sacrificial offering; others prefer 'receive' or 'take care of'. Gif he on þreat cymeð, 'if he comes into (or with) a troop', is equally double: þreat may mean a band of men or 'violence, threat'. The repetition of this line in l. 7 makes the danger a refrain.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. a-þecgan; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. á-þecgan; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. þreát; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations)

  4. The refrain and the short lines ll. 3, 8

    Ungelic means 'unlike, different'; the variation between adjective (ungelic) and adverb (ungelice) at l. 8 may be scribal or deliberate. The phrase contrasts the speaker and Wulf with her hostile people, or with each other. Refrains are very rare in Old English poetry, and the nearest parallel is Deor, copied just before. Wulf and Eadwacer also has several half-lines standing alone (ll. 3, 8, 17, 19), which break the regular pattern of paired half-lines. Some scholars see these features as evidence of a foreign or stanzaic model, perhaps Scandinavian, but that remains hypothetical.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. un-gelíc; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations); Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  5. 'Wulf is on one island, I on another' ll. 4-5

    Ieg 'island' survives in place names such as Anglesey and Romsey; eglond (l. 5) is a related form (modern 'island'). An island in the fens need not be at sea: it could be dry ground in a marsh, a natural refuge for outlaws. The name Wulf is ambiguous throughout. As a man's name it was common, but wolf was also an image of the outlaw (the later legal term 'wolf's head', Latin caput lupinum, marked an outlaw who could be killed with impunity). Fæst, 'firm, secure', and biworpen, 'surrounded, cast about', imply a refuge that is also a prison.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. íg; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Bernard J. Muir, ed., The Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry, 2nd edn (Exeter, 2000), commentary

  6. wælreowe: 'slaughter-fierce men' l. 6

    Wælhreow (here spelled without h) is a strong epithet for savage men in battle, used of persecutors in religious verse. It is unclear which island þær refers to: if Wulf's, his companions are fierce; if the speaker's, the fierce men are her own people who are waiting to 'receive' him. The ambiguity is typical of the poem, where deictic words (þær, hit, hine) can point in more than one direction.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wæl-hreów; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations)

  7. dogode: the poem's hapax l. 9

    Wulfes ic mines widlastum wenum dogode: 'I ... my Wulf's far wanderings with hopes'. Widlast means 'a far-stretching track, a wanderer's path', and Bosworth-Toller cites this line. The verb is unique: Bosworth-Toller tentatively gives 'to bear, suffer'; some connect it with a later word for dog and translate 'followed, tracked'; many editors emend to hogode 'thought about, pondered'. The CLASP concordance confirms that dogode has no other occurrence in the corpus. Wenum, 'with expectations, hopes', joins the verb to the waiting that defines the speaker.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. dógian; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wíd-lást; CLASP (A Consolidated Library of Anglo-Saxon Poetry, Oxford), concordance entry for dogode; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  8. reotugu: 'tearful', and the female speaker l. 10

    Reotugu is the feminine nominative singular of reotig, 'mournful, tearful'; this is the only inflection in the poem that marks the speaker's sex. Þonne hit wæs renig weder, 'when it was rainy weather', is a rare touch of weather as mood; in the elegies rain, frost and storm usually belong to the exile's landscape. The two þonne clauses (ll. 10-11) set grief and embrace side by side in time, 'when ... then'.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. reótig; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes

  9. se beaducafa: 'the battle-bold one' l. 11

    Beaducaf means 'battle-quick, bold in battle' (beadu 'battle', caf 'quick, brave'). Bogum is the dative plural of boga 'bow, arch', used here of arms in an embrace. The line is a crux of identity. If the beaducafa is Eadwacer, the embrace is from a husband or captor she does not love, and wæs me hwæþre eac lað, 'yet it was also hateful to me', expresses revulsion; if it is Wulf, the line records a meeting in which love and fear are mixed. Klinck and others note that the poem supports both readings.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. beadu-cáf; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Ann Marie Donahue (Medieval Forum, 2004), ''Of this I can make no sense': Wulf and Eadwacer and the Destabilization of Meaning' (survey of readings)

  10. 'It was a joy to me, yet also hateful' l. 12

    Wyn, 'joy', and lað, 'hateful, loathsome', are opposites placed in two half-lines of identical structure (wæs me ... wæs me). To þon means 'to that extent' or 'in that'. Readers who take the embracer as Wulf hear a woman torn between love and danger; those who take him as Eadwacer hear forced intimacy that is physically pleasurable and morally hateful. The balance of the line is itself the meaning: the speaker cannot separate the two feelings.

    Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations); Berit Åström, The Politics of Tradition: Examining the History of the Old English Poems The Wife's Lament and Wulf and Eadwacer (Umeå, 2002)

  11. seldcymas, meteliste: grief, not hunger ll. 13-15

    Seldcyme is 'a rare visit' (seld 'seldom' and cyme 'coming'); Bosworth-Toller cites this passage. The repeated apostrophe Wulf, min Wulf is the poem's emotional peak. Wena me þine / seoce gedydon: 'my hopes of you have made me sick'. Wen is 'expectation, hope'. The last half-line, nales meteliste, 'not at all lack of food', denies a physical cause with a sharp understatement. It may also hint at the speaker's situation as a captive who is fed but grieves.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. seld-cyme; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. mete-leást; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wén; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  12. 'Do you hear, Eadwacer?' l. 16

    Ead means 'wealth, prosperity, happiness' and wacer 'watchful', so the name suggests a guardian of property. The poem addresses him with a challenge, 'Do you hear?', which turns the lament into speech to a hostile listener. Most critics take Eadwacer as the speaker's husband or captor, distinct from Wulf. J. F. Adams (1958) and others argued that it is not a separate person but an ironic epithet for Wulf himself, the guardian who does not guard her. A recent proposal (Shiels, 2022) links Wulf and Eadwacer to Theodoric and Odoacer; few have followed it.

    Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations); Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Ann Marie Donahue (Medieval Forum, 2004), ''Of this I can make no sense': Wulf and Eadwacer and the Destabilization of Meaning' (survey of readings)

  13. uncerne earne hwelp: 'our wretched cub' l. 16

    Uncer is the dual possessive, 'belonging to us two', so the hwelp 'whelp, cub' (a child, given the wolf imagery) belongs to the speaker and one man. Which man depends on the reading of Eadwacer and the beaducafa. Earne, as the manuscript reads, could be connected with earh 'cowardly, timid' or with earn 'eagle'. Most editors emend to earmne, 'wretched, poor', which this archive text does not do. Hwelp keeps the animal metaphor of Wulf, making the child a wolf-cub. If Wulf is the father, the child of an outlaw is in danger; if the husband is, the word may be scornful.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. hwelp; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. earh; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations)

  14. 'A wolf carries [it] to the wood' l. 17

    The half-line stands alone, like the refrain. Modern editors print the lower-case wulf, but there was no capitalisation of names in the manuscript, so the word may be the name or the animal. If it is Wulf, he has come to take his child away, out of reach. If it is the animal, the child may be dead or exposed, and the line mourns a loss. Dolores Frese (1983) took the whole poem as a mother's lament for her son. The line shows how the poem uses the same word for person and beast.

    Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations); Ann Marie Donahue (Medieval Forum, 2004), ''Of this I can make no sense': Wulf and Eadwacer and the Destabilization of Meaning' (survey of readings)

  15. 'One easily tears apart what was never joined' ll. 18-19

    Toslitan means 'to tear in two, rend asunder'. The line states a bitter paradox: what was never joined (gesomnad 'united') is easily separated, namely uncer giedd geador, 'our song together'. Critics have often compared the marriage formula of Matthew 19:6 (KJV, 'let not man put asunder'), which would make the sentence mean that this union was never a marriage at all, and so is easily broken. Whether the speaker means her bond with Wulf (unsanctioned) or with Eadwacer (unloved) is again open.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. tó-slítan; The Bible (KJV), Matthew 19:6; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  16. giedd: 'song', 'tale' or 'riddle'? l. 19

    Giedd (gid, gyd) means 'song, lay, poem', but also 'saying, proverb, riddle'. Here 'our song together' may be the lovers' shared story, the speaker's present poem, or a riddle (an option favoured by those who emphasise the poem's place next to the riddles). Geador 'together' gives a sound-echo, as if the word for song contained the word for union. The poem thus ends on a reflection on its own form, like Deor's turn to its scop. The Wife's Lament opens with the same word: Ic þis giedd wrece.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. gid; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Wulf and Eadwacer' (summary of manuscript and interpretations)
