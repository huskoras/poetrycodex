The Dream of the Rood (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. G. P. Krapp, vol. II (1932)

Religion

Hwæt! Ic swefna cyst secgan wylle,
hwæt me gemætte to midre nihte,
syðþan reordberend reste wunedon!
þuhte me þæt ic gesawe syllicre treow
on lyft lædan, leohte bewunden,
beama beorhtost. Eall þæt beacen wæs
begoten mid golde. Gimmas stodon
fægere æt foldan sceatum, swylce þær fife wæron
uppe on þam eaxlegespanne. Beheoldon þær engel dryhtnes ealle,
fægere þurh forðgesceaft. Ne wæs ðær huru fracodes gealga,
ac hine þær beheoldon halige gastas,
men ofer moldan, ond eall þeos mære gesceaft.
Syllic wæs se sigebeam, ond ic synnum fah,
forwunded mid wommum. Geseah ic wuldres treow,
wædum geweorðode, wynnum scinan,
gegyred mid golde; gimmas hæfdon
bewrigene weorðlice wealdendes treow.
Hwæðre ic þurh þæt gold ongytan meahte
earmra ærgewin, þæt hit ærest ongan
swætan on þa swiðran healfe. Eall ic wæs mid sorgum gedrefed,
forht ic wæs for þære fægran gesyhðe. Geseah ic þæt fuse beacen
wendan wædum ond bleom; hwilum hit wæs mid wætan bestemed,
beswyled mid swates gange, hwilum mid since gegyrwed.
Hwæðre ic þær licgende lange hwile
beheold hreowcearig hælendes treow,
oððæt ic gehyrde þæt hit hleoðrode.
Ongan þa word sprecan wudu selesta:
"þæt wæs geara iu, (ic þæt gyta geman),
þæt ic wæs aheawen holtes on ende,
astyred of stefne minum. Genaman me ðær strange feondas,
geworhton him þær to wæfersyne, heton me heora wergas hebban.
Bæron me ðær beornas on eaxlum, oððæt hie me on beorg asetton,
gefæstnodon me þær feondas genoge. Geseah ic þa frean mancynnes
efstan elne mycle þæt he me wolde on gestigan.
þær ic þa ne dorste ofer dryhtnes word
bugan oððe berstan, þa ic bifian geseah
eorðan sceatas. Ealle ic mihte
feondas gefyllan, hwæðre ic fæste stod.
Ongyrede hine þa geong hæleð, (þæt wæs god ælmihtig),
strang ond stiðmod. Gestah he on gealgan heanne,
modig on manigra gesyhðe, þa he wolde mancyn lysan.
Bifode ic þa me se beorn ymbclypte. Ne dorste ic hwæðre bugan to eorðan,
feallan to foldan sceatum, ac ic sceolde fæste standan.
Rod wæs ic aræred. Ahof ic ricne cyning,
heofona hlaford, hyldan me ne dorste.
þurhdrifan hi me mid deorcan næglum. On me syndon þa dolg gesiene,
opene inwidhlemmas. Ne dorste ic hira nænigum sceððan.
Bysmeredon hie unc butu ætgædere. Eall ic wæs mid blode bestemed,
begoten of þæs guman sidan, siððan he hæfde his gast onsended.
Feala ic on þam beorge gebiden hæbbe
wraðra wyrda. Geseah ic weruda god
þearle þenian. þystro hæfdon
bewrigen mid wolcnum wealdendes hræw,
scirne sciman, sceadu forðeode,
wann under wolcnum. Weop eal gesceaft,
cwiðdon cyninges fyll. Crist wæs on rode.
Hwæðere þær fuse feorran cwoman
to þam æðelinge. Ic þæt eall beheold.
Sare ic wæs mid sorgum gedrefed, hnag ic hwæðre þam secgum to handa,
eaðmod elne mycle. Genamon hie þær ælmihtigne god,
ahofon hine of ðam hefian wite. Forleton me þa hilderincas
standan steame bedrifenne; eall ic wæs mid strælum forwundod.
Aledon hie ðær limwerigne, gestodon him æt his lices heafdum,
beheoldon hie ðær heofenes dryhten, ond he hine ðær hwile reste,
meðe æfter ðam miclan gewinne. Ongunnon him þa moldern wyrcan
beornas on banan gesyhðe; curfon hie ðæt of beorhtan stane,
gesetton hie ðæron sigora wealdend. Ongunnon him þa sorhleoð galan
earme on þa æfentide, þa hie woldon eft siðian,
meðe fram þam mæran þeodne. Reste he ðær mæte weorode.
Hwæðere we ðær greotende gode hwile
stodon on staðole, syððan stefn up gewat
hilderinca. Hræw colode,
fæger feorgbold. þa us man fyllan ongan
ealle to eorðan. þæt wæs egeslic wyrd!
Bedealf us man on deopan seaþe. Hwæðre me þær dryhtnes þegnas,
freondas gefrunon,
ond gyredon me golde ond seolfre.
Nu ðu miht gehyran, hæleð min se leofa,
þæt ic bealuwara weorc gebiden hæbbe,
sarra sorga. Is nu sæl cumen
þæt me weorðiað wide ond side
menn ofer moldan, ond eall þeos mære gesceaft,
gebiddaþ him to þyssum beacne. On me bearn godes
þrowode hwile. Forþan ic þrymfæst nu
hlifige under heofenum, ond ic hælan mæg
æghwylcne anra, þara þe him bið egesa to me.
Iu ic wæs geworden wita heardost,
leodum laðost, ærþan ic him lifes weg
rihtne gerymde, reordberendum.
Hwæt, me þa geweorðode wuldres ealdor
ofer holmwudu, heofonrices weard!
Swylce swa he his modor eac, Marian sylfe,
ælmihtig god for ealle menn
geweorðode ofer eall wifa cynn.
Nu ic þe hate, hæleð min se leofa,
þæt ðu þas gesyhðe secge mannum,
onwreoh wordum þæt hit is wuldres beam,
se ðe ælmihtig god on þrowode
for mancynnes manegum synnum
ond Adomes ealdgewyrhtum.
Deað he þær byrigde, hwæðere eft dryhten aras
mid his miclan mihte mannum to helpe.
He ða on heofenas astag. Hider eft fundaþ
on þysne middangeard mancynn secan
on domdæge dryhten sylfa,
ælmihtig god, ond his englas mid,
þæt he þonne wile deman, se ah domes geweald,
anra gehwylcum swa he him ærur her
on þyssum lænum life geearnaþ.
Ne mæg þær ænig unforht wesan
for þam worde þe se wealdend cwyð.
Frineð he for þære mænige hwær se man sie,
se ðe for dryhtnes naman deaðes wolde
biteres onbyrigan, swa he ær on ðam beame dyde.
Ac hie þonne forhtiað, ond fea þencaþ
hwæt hie to Criste cweðan onginnen.
Ne þearf ðær þonne ænig anforht wesan
þe him ær in breostum bereð beacna selest,
ac ðurh ða rode sceal rice gesecan
of eorðwege æghwylc sawl,
seo þe mid wealdende wunian þenceð."
Gebæd ic me þa to þan beame bliðe mode,
elne mycle, þær ic ana wæs
mæte werede. Wæs modsefa
afysed on forðwege, feala ealra gebad
langunghwila. Is me nu lifes hyht
þæt ic þone sigebeam secan mote
ana oftor þonne ealle men,
well weorþian. Me is willa to ðam
mycel on mode, ond min mundbyrd is
geriht to þære rode. Nah ic ricra feala
freonda on foldan, ac hie forð heonon
gewiton of worulde dreamum, sohton him wuldres cyning,
lifiaþ nu on heofenum mid heahfædere,
wuniaþ on wuldre, ond ic wene me
daga gehwylce hwænne me dryhtnes rod,
þe ic her on eorðan ær sceawode,
on þysson lænan life gefetige
ond me þonne gebringe þær is blis mycel,
dream on heofonum, þær is dryhtnes folc
geseted to symle, þær is singal blis,
ond me þonne asette þær ic syþþan mot
wunian on wuldre, well mid þam halgum
dreames brucan. Si me dryhten freond,
se ðe her on eorþan ær þrowode
on þam gealgtreowe for guman synnum.
He us onlysde ond us lif forgeaf,
heofonlicne ham. Hiht wæs geniwad
mid bledum ond mid blisse þam þe þær bryne þolodan.
Se sunu wæs sigorfæst on þam siðfate,
mihtig ond spedig, þa he mid manigeo com,
gasta weorode, on godes rice,
anwealda ælmihtig, englum to blisse
ond eallum ðam halgum þam þe on heofonum ær
wunedon on wuldre, þa heora wealdend cwom,
ælmihtig god, þær his eðel wæs.

Codex Note

Study notes by the editors

  1. Hwæt! – the opening call l. 1

    'Hwæt' is the neuter of the interrogative pronoun 'hwa' ('who, what'). As an opening it calls for attention, roughly 'Listen!' or 'So!'. Scholars disagree whether it was an exclamation or a word belonging to the clause that follows, so editors punctuate it differently. Here it introduces 'swefna cyst', 'the best of dreams'. 'Swefna' is the genitive plural of 'swefn' ('sleep, dream'), and the genitive is partitive: 'the choicest among dreams'. The poem uses the dream-vision frame familiar from Latin visionary writing. But it is told in the first person, and the speaker's guilty, ordinary self matters as much as the vision. 'Hwæt' returns at l. 90, where the Rood uses it to open its account of its own exaltation.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. hwæt; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. swefn; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line

  2. Manuscript and the Rood tradition l. 1

    The text printed here is the one in the Vercelli Book (Vercelli, Biblioteca Capitolare, MS CXVII). This is a West Saxon anthology of homilies and poems, copied in the later tenth century, which has been in northern Italy since the Middle Ages. The poem has no title in the manuscript; 'The Dream of the Rood' is a modern editorial title. Parts of the Rood's speech, in an earlier Northumbrian form, are carved in runes on the Ruthwell Cross in Dumfriesshire, usually dated to the eighth century. Lines close to ll. 44 and 48 are also engraved on the Brussels Cross reliquary ('Rod is min nama ...'), which is usually dated to around the early eleventh century. Critics such as Ó Carragáin therefore speak of a 'Dream of the Rood tradition' rather than a single fixed poem. The Vercelli text is often taken as a later expansion of an older core.

    G. P. Krapp, ed., The Vercelli Book, ASPR 2 (New York, 1932), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005); Wikipedia, 'Brussels Cross'; Wikipedia, 'Ruthwell Cross'

  3. reordberend – 'speech-bearers' l. 3

    'Reord' means 'voice, speech, language', and '-berend' is a present participle used as a noun, 'bearer'. Humans are therefore defined by the power of speech. The compound is rare and poetic, a typical epithet of Old English verse. Its use here is pointed. The speech-bearers lie silent in sleep, while a tree, which has no voice, is about to speak (l. 26, 'þæt hit hleoðrode'). The Rood uses the same word at l. 89 ('reordberendum') for the people to whom it opened the way of life. The repetition ties the poem's opening to the Rood's claim. 'Reste wunedon' is idiomatic: literally 'dwelt in rest', that is, slept.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. reordberend; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  4. beacen – sign, standard l. 6

    'Beacen' covers 'sign, token, portent', and also a standard or banner carried into battle. The tree covered in gold and gems recalls the jewelled crosses (crux gemmata) of late antique and Anglo-Saxon art and liturgy. It also recalls the sign Constantine saw in the sky before his victory, a story told at length in Cynewulf's Elene in the same manuscript. The cult of the Cross was strong in Anglo-Saxon England. The Chronicle records that Pope Marinus sent King Alfred a piece of the Cross in the 880s. The word recurs throughout the poem: 'fuse beacen' (l. 21), 'beacna selest' (l. 118), and at l. 83 people now pray 'to þyssum beacne'. 'Begoten mid golde' is literally 'poured over with gold', as if with molten metal.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. beacen; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005); The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, annal for 883 (trans. J. Ingram, Project Gutenberg)

  5. Gems at the earth's corners; five on the cross-beam l. 8

    'Foldan sceatum' is dative plural, 'at the surfaces/corners of the earth'. The cross in the vision is vast and reaches to the ends of the world, an image of its universal reach. 'Eaxlegespann' occurs only here. It combines 'eaxl' ('shoulder') with 'gespann' ('clasp, joining') and seems to mean the crossing point or the cross-beam. The five gems on it have often been read as Christ's five wounds (hands, feet and side). They have also been read as the five jewels set at the centre and ends of a jewelled cross. Editors generally take these hypermetric lines to show both a piece of goldsmith's work and an image of a creation marked out by the cross. The phrase 'foldan sceatum' returns at l. 43, where the Rood says it must not fall 'to foldan sceatum'. The echo ties the tree's cosmic size to its obedient stillness.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. eaxlgespann; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. sceat; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Dickins and A. S. C. Ross, eds., The Dream of the Rood (London: Methuen, 1934), note to the line

  6. Beheoldon þær engel dryhtnes ealle – who is watching? l. 9

    Krapp's ASPR text keeps the manuscript reading 'engel dryhtnes ealle'. The syntax is uncertain. One reading takes 'engel' as a plural (the normal plural is 'englas'): 'all the angels of the Lord beheld [it]'. Some editors emend to 'englas' or explain the form otherwise. The other reading makes 'ealle' the subject and 'engel dryhtnes' the object: 'all beheld there the Lord's angel'. On that reading the cross is God's messenger, which is what Greek 'angelos' means. Swanton and Dickins and Ross discuss the problem in their notes. Either way, the passage builds a widening circle of onlookers: angels, 'halige gastas', 'men ofer moldan' and 'eall þeos mære gesceaft' (l. 12). That last phrase comes back at l. 82, when the Rood says all creation now honours it. 'Forðgesceaft' (l. 10) probably means 'creation (ordained from the beginning)'.

    G. P. Krapp, ed., The Vercelli Book, ASPR 2 (New York, 1932), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Dickins and A. S. C. Ross, eds., The Dream of the Rood (London: Methuen, 1934), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  7. fracodes gealga – a criminal's gallows l. 10

    'Fracod' means 'wicked, vile'; used as a noun, as here in the genitive, it means 'a criminal'. 'Gealga' is 'gallows', the plain word for a place of execution. The poem goes on to call the cross 'gealgan heanne' ('the high gallows', l. 40) and 'gealgtreowe' (l. 146). Old English writers used both 'gealga' and 'rod' for the Cross, and 'gealga' kept the shame of execution. 'Ne wæs ðær huru' ('it was not, indeed') is litotes, a favourite Old English figure in which a denial forcefully asserts the opposite: this was a tree of glory. Compare the Rood's memory at l. 31 that its enemies made it hold up criminals ('wergas').

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. fracod; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. gealga; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  8. synnum fah – stained with sins l. 13

    'Fah' (with a long vowel) can mean 'stained, coloured', and also 'guilty, outlawed, hostile'. Both senses suit a sinner gazing at a holy object. The dative 'synnum' ('with sins') is instrumental. 'Forwunded mid wommum' means 'badly wounded by stains/sins', with 'wamm' meaning 'stain, defilement, sin'. The dreamer describes his sin as a wound, which prepares for the moment when the Cross shows its own wounds (ll. 46-47). Critics often note that the speaker, the Cross and Christ go through the same things in the poem: wounding, trembling, standing fast, and in the end exaltation. The contrast is also visual, gold and gems against stains. 'Sigebeam' ('victory-tree') signals from the start that the Crucifixion is presented as a triumph.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. fah; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. wamm; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; R. Woolf, 'Doctrinal Influences on The Dream of the Rood', Medium Ævum 27 (1958)

  9. Bleeding on the right side l. 20

    'Earmra ærgewin' means 'the former struggle of wretched ones'. 'Earmra' is a genitive plural, and editors disagree about who the wretched ones are: Christ's tormentors, or sufferers more generally. The tree then 'began to sweat' ('swætan') on 'the right side'. 'Swiðra' is the comparative of 'swiþ' ('strong'): 'the stronger, the right'. John 19:34 says only that a soldier pierced Christ's side and blood and water came out. Medieval art and commentary usually placed that wound on the right. 'Swat' is 'sweat', and in verse also 'blood' (compare 'swates gange', l. 23). Line 20 begins a group of hypermetric lines, and the long lines slow the description while the dreamer watches.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 19:34; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. swat; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  10. The changing sign l. 22

    'Wendan wædum ond bleom' means 'change in its coverings and colours', from 'wæd' ('garment') and 'bleo' ('colour, appearance'). 'Hwilum ... hwilum' ('at times ... at times') sets two states against each other. In one the tree is 'bestemed' ('drenched') with moisture and 'beswyled' ('soaked') with the flow of blood. In the other it is 'mid since gegyrwed', adorned with treasure. 'Fuse' ('eager, ready to go, hastening') is hard to pin down. It may mean the sign is vivid and in motion, or that it is ready to depart. Ó Carragáin links the alternation to the liturgy, which honours the Cross as both the instrument of death and the sign of victory. Woolf argued that the poem keeps Christ's suffering and his triumph in balance, as early theology does.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. bleo; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. fus; É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005); R. Woolf, 'Doctrinal Influences on The Dream of the Rood', Medium Ævum 27 (1958); M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line

  11. Prosopopoeia: the tree speaks l. 27

    Lines 28-121 are the Rood's monologue, an example of prosopopoeia, in which an object is given a voice. Critics have long connected it with the riddle tradition. In many Exeter Book riddles, and in the Latin enigmata of Aldhelm and others, an object tells its own life: how it was cut, shaped and put to use. The Rood follows that pattern: felled at the edge of a wood, carried off, set up on a hill. 'Wudu selesta' ('the best of wood') is the subject of 'ongan'. Because of this device, the Crucifixion is told by a witness who also took part, and the Rood can describe from inside a death it was made to inflict. 'Geara iu' ('long ago, years since') and the aside 'ic þæt gyta geman' ('I still remember it') make it sound like an old warrior recalling a battle.

    M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005)

  12. wæfersyne – spectacle l. 31

    'Wæfersyn' (also 'wæfersien') means 'a spectacle, a show'. The first element is uncertain, perhaps linked with 'wafian' ('to wonder at'); the second is 'syn/sien', 'sight'. 'Geworhton him ... to wæfersyne' means 'made (me) into a spectacle for themselves'. 'Wergas' is the accusative plural of 'werg/wearg', 'criminal, outlaw, accursed one'. The word was also a term of abuse, and it is the source of the 'warg' of Germanic legend. 'Heton me heora wergas hebban' means 'commanded me to lift up their criminals', so the Cross remembers executions before Christ's. 'Beorg' (l. 32) is 'hill', meaning Calvary, but the same word also means a burial mound. 'Genoge' ('enough, many') is understatement.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. wæfersyn; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. wearg; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  13. The young hero l. 39

    'Hæleð' is a poetic word for 'hero, warrior, man'. Christ here is not passively led away and nailed up, as in the Gospels. He 'hastens with great courage' (l. 34) and strips himself ('ongyrede hine'), as a warrior strips for combat. In the Gospels it is the soldiers who strip him (Matthew 27:28, 35). He then 'climbs' ('gestah') the high gallows, 'modig' ('brave, proud-hearted'). The aside 'þæt wæs god ælmihtig' insists that the young man is God. Rosemary Woolf argued that the poem divides the Passion between two figures. Christ shows the heroic, divine side, and the Cross bears the suffering of his human nature. Other scholars stress the use of Germanic heroic diction for a Christian subject. Most readers accept some version of both views. 'Lysan' (l. 41) means 'to release, redeem'.

    R. Woolf, 'Doctrinal Influences on The Dream of the Rood', Medium Ævum 27 (1958); M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 27:28

  14. Rod wæs ic aræred – the loyal thegn's paradox l. 44

    'Rod' ('cross, rood', originally 'pole') appears here for the first time in the poem, as the Cross's own name: 'As a rood I was raised up'. The Brussels Cross inscription begins 'Rod is min nama'. In heroic poetry a thegn's duty is to defend his lord or die at his side, as in Maldon. The Rood keeps repeating 'ne dorste' ('I dared not', ll. 35, 42, 45, 47). It could not bow, break, fall, or strike the enemies it could have felled (ll. 37-38). Its loyalty means obeying Christ's will and enduring his death. Critics have compared this inverted heroism with the Germanic code of the comitatus. 'Ahof ic ricne cyning' ('I raised up a mighty king') matches the Brussels lines, 'geo ic ricne cyning bær byfigynde'.

    M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; R. Woolf, 'Doctrinal Influences on The Dream of the Rood', Medium Ævum 27 (1958); Wikipedia, 'Brussels Cross'; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  15. inwidhlemmas – malicious wounds l. 47

    'Dolg' (l. 46) means 'wound, scar'. 'Inwidhlemm' joins 'inwid(d)' ('malice, treachery') to 'hlemm' ('blow, wound'), giving 'wounds made in malice'. The compound is found only here. The Cross tells how dark nails were driven through it ('þurhdrifan') and says the wounds can still be seen ('gesiene') on it now. So the Cross that speaks bears the marks, much as the risen Christ shows Thomas his wounds (John 20:27). The idea links the Cross's wounds to the dreamer's 'forwunded' (l. 14). The same element 'inwid' occurs in Brunanburh's 'eald inwidda' (l. 46), 'the old malicious one'.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. inwidhlemm; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. dolg; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 20:27

  16. unc butu – the dual: 'us two both' l. 48

    Old English kept a dual number in its first- and second-person pronouns: 'wit' ('we two'), 'unc' ('us two'), 'uncer' ('of us two'). The poet uses the dual here, strengthened by 'butu ætgædere' ('both together'), to join Christ and the Cross as fellow victims of mockery ('bysmerian', 'to mock, revile'). The Gospels describe the mocking of Christ (Matthew 27:27-31, 39-44), and the Cross places itself inside that scene. The next line echoes the Brussels Cross ('blode bestemed') and the dreamer's earlier sight of the blood-drenched tree (l. 22). The blood is 'begoten of þæs guman sidan', poured from the man's side, after he had 'sent forth his spirit'. Compare Matthew 27:50, 'yielded up the ghost'.

    B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 27:50

  17. Darkness and weeping creation l. 52

    The Synoptic Gospels record darkness 'over all the land' from the sixth hour to the ninth (Matthew 27:45; Luke 23:44-45). The poem makes the darkness cover 'wealdendes hræw', 'the ruler's corpse'. 'Hræw' is a blunt word for a dead body, also used of the battle-dead in Brunanburh. 'Scirne sciman' ('the bright radiance') stands in apposition, so the corpse is itself light. 'Weop eal gesceaft, / cwiðdon cyninges fyll' means all creation wept and lamented the king's fall. Several scholars compare the Norse myth of Baldr, for whom all things were asked to weep. Others hold that Christian writing, which often shows creation recognising its Lord, explains the image without Norse myth. The question remains open. 'Weruda god' (l. 51), 'God of hosts', renders Latin 'Dominus Sabaoth'.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 27:45; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  18. Crist wæs on rode – the plainest half-line l. 56

    This half-line stands out for its plainness: four words, naming Christ for the first time and stating the fact of the Crucifixion. Until now the poet has used epithets: 'frean mancynnes', 'geong hæleð', 'ricne cyning', 'weruda god'. The runic text of the Ruthwell Cross includes a Northumbrian phrase matching 'Crist wæs on rode'. Comparisons of the two texts are among the main evidence that part of the Rood's speech existed in the eighth century. 'Fuse feorran cwoman / to þam æðelinge' (ll. 57-58) begins the deposition: eager people came from far away to the prince. John 19:38-39 names them as Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus. 'Æðeling' is a heroic word for a prince of royal blood.

    É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005); G. P. Krapp, ed., The Vercelli Book, ASPR 2 (New York, 1932), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; Wikipedia, 'Ruthwell Cross'

  19. moldern and sorhleoð – a hero's burial l. 65

    'Moldern' (l. 65) means 'earth-house', that is, a tomb, from 'molde' ('earth') and 'ærn' ('house'). It is cut 'of beorhtan stane'; compare the new tomb hewn out of the rock in Matthew 27:60. The bearers are 'hilderincas' ('battle-men') and 'beornas'. The body is 'limwerigne' ('limb-weary'), and Christ 'rested a while, weary after the great struggle', so death is shown as sleep after battle. The mourners sing a 'sorhleoð' ('song of sorrow'), as the Geats lament their dead lord at the end of Beowulf. The lament is sung by 'earme', wretched ones, in the evening. 'Gestodon him æt his lices heafdum' means 'stood at the head of his body', with the plural 'heafdum' used idiomatically. The scene is seen through the Cross's eyes: 'on banan gesyhðe', in the sight of his slayer.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. moldærn; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. sorhleoþ; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 27:60

  20. The burial and finding of the Cross l. 75

    'Bedealf' is from 'bedelfan' ('to bury'), and 'seaþ' means 'pit'. The crosses are thrown down and buried. The legend of the Invention (finding) of the Cross tells how Helena, mother of Constantine, found the three crosses at Jerusalem, and the feast was kept on 3 May. Cynewulf retells the story at length in Elene, in the same Vercelli Book. The poem refers to the legend only briefly: 'dryhtnes þegnas, / freondas gefrunon', the Lord's thegns, his friends, learned of [it]. The manuscript gives only one half-line for the second of these lines, and editors assume something is missing. The adornment 'golde ond seolfre' brings back the jewelled cross of the opening, now presented as part of the Cross's history rather than only as a vision.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. bedelfan; G. P. Krapp, ed., The Vercelli Book, ASPR 2 (New York, 1932), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; Wikipedia, 'Elene (poem)'

  21. The Cross and Mary l. 92

    The Rood says the 'Lord of glory' honoured it 'ofer holmwudu', above the trees of the forest. 'Holmwudu' is a difficult compound. In verse 'holm' usually means 'sea'. Some editors take the word as 'wood of the hill', from 'holm', 'rising ground'; others emend. The general sense is clear. The parallel with Mary (ll. 92-94) compares two chosen bearers of Christ: Mary carried him into life, and the Cross carried him in death. Mary is 'blessed among women' (Luke 1:28, 42), and the Cross is blessed above other trees. Patristic and liturgical writing often linked Mary and the Cross. Ó Carragáin connects the comparison with the feasts of the Cross and of the Virgin kept in Anglo-Saxon England.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Luke 1:28; É. Ó Carragáin, Ritual and the Rood: Liturgical Images and the Old English Poems of the Dream of the Rood Tradition (London: British Library, 2005); M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. holmwudu

  22. Judgement Day and the sign of the Son of Man l. 105

    'Domdæg' means 'day of judgement'. The Rood's speech ends by following the Creed: death, resurrection, ascension, and return in judgement (ll. 101-109). Christ's question ('Frineð he ... hwær se man sie') sets the test of discipleship: who would 'taste bitter death' for the Lord's name, as he did 'on the tree'. Compare Matthew 16:24 on taking up the cross. Matthew 24:30 foretells 'the sign of the Son of man' in heaven at the end, which commentators usually understood as the Cross, so the Cross has its place at the Judgement. 'Lænum life' ('this transitory life', l. 109; again l. 138) uses 'læne' ('lent, on loan, fleeting'), a key word of the elegies. 'Unforht' and 'anforht' (ll. 110, 117) set fearlessness against fear. The second word is uncertain, and editors explain or emend it in different ways.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 24:30; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. læne; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  23. The dreamer's response: exile and hope l. 122

    From l. 122 the dreamer speaks again, in the language of the Old English elegies. He is alone ('ana', 'mæte werede'). He has lived through 'langunghwila' ('times of longing'). His friends have gone 'of worulde dreamum' ('from the joys of the world'). As in The Wanderer and The Seafarer, the loss of companions and the shortness of life turn him towards hope of heaven. The Cross becomes his 'mundbyrd' (l. 130), a legal word for protection or patronage. He expects it to 'fetch' him (l. 138) to the place where the Lord's people are 'set at the feast' ('geseted to symle'). The hall-feast that is lost in the elegies is restored in heaven. 'Afysed on forðwege' ('impelled on the journey forth') answers the 'fuse beacen' of l. 21.

    M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. mundbyrd

  24. The ending: the Harrowing of Hell and the Ascension l. 149

    'Bryne' means 'burning, fire', and 'þolian' 'to endure'. 'Those who suffered burning there' are the righteous dead of the Old Testament in hell. After the Crucifixion Christ freed them in the Harrowing of Hell, which the Gospel of Nicodemus narrates and the Creed's 'descended into hell' implies. 'Se sunu wæs sigorfæst on þam siðfate' (l. 150) presents the victory as a journey or expedition. Christ comes home with a host ('manigeo', 'gasta weorode') to his 'eðel' ('homeland'), as a war-leader returns with his troop. Some critics have found the ending a disappointing appendix. Others defend it as the necessary end of the Rood's story, from Crucifixion to triumph, and as the answer to the dreamer's hope of a homeland. 'Heahfædere' (l. 134) refers to God the Father.

    M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; R. Woolf, 'Doctrinal Influences on The Dream of the Rood', Medium Ævum 27 (1958); Wikipedia, 'Harrowing of Hell'

  25. Hypermetric lines l. 20

    A normal Old English line has two half-lines with two main stresses each, joined by alliteration on the first stress of the second half-line. Hypermetric lines have three stresses in each half. The Dream of the Rood has unusually many, in clusters, for example ll. 8-10, 20-23, 30-34, 40-43, 46-49 and 59-70. They tend to come at moments of vision and suffering: the jewelled tree, the bleeding tree, the Crucifixion, the deposition. Here 'Eall ic wæs mid sorgum gedrefed' is the second half of a hypermetric line. It recurs in l. 59, 'Sare ic wæs mid sorgum gedrefed', so the dreamer's grief and the Cross's are expressed in the same words. Scholars debate how hypermetric lines were performed, but their placement suggests the poet used them deliberately for emphasis.

    B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; M. Swanton, ed., The Dream of the Rood (Manchester, 1970; rev. Exeter, 1987), note to the line; G. P. Krapp, ed., The Vercelli Book, ASPR 2 (New York, 1932), text and notes
