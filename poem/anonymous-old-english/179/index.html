The Battle of Maldon (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. E. V. K. Dobbie, vol. VI (1942)

History & Politics

brocen wurde.
Het þa hyssa hwæne hors forlætan,
feor afysan, and forð gangan,
hicgan to handum and to hige godum.
þa þæt Offan mæg ærest onfunde,
þæt se eorl nolde yrhðo geþolian,
he let him þa of handon leofne fleogan
hafoc wið þæs holtes, and to þære hilde stop;
be þam man mihte oncnawan þæt se cniht nolde
wacian æt þam wige, þa he to wæpnum feng.
Eac him wolde Eadric his ealdre gelæstan,
frean to gefeohte, ongan þa forð beran
gar to guþe. He hæfde god geþanc
þa hwile þe he mid handum healdan mihte
bord and bradswurd; beot he gelæste
þa he ætforan his frean feohtan sceolde.
ða þær Byrhtnoð ongan beornas trymian,
rad and rædde, rincum tæhte
hu hi sceoldon standan and þone stede healdan,
and bæd þæt hyra randas rihte heoldon
fæste mid folman, and ne forhtedon na.
þa he hæfde þæt folc fægere getrymmed,
he lihte þa mid leodon þær him leofost wæs,
þær he his heorðwerod holdost wiste.
þa stod on stæðe, stiðlice clypode
wicinga ar, wordum mælde,
se on beot abead brimliþendra
ærænde to þam eorle, þær he on ofre stod:
"Me sendon to þe sæmen snelle,
heton ðe secgan þæt þu most sendan raðe
beagas wið gebeorge; and eow betere is
þæt ge þisne garræs mid gafole forgyldon,
þon we swa hearde hilde dælon.
Ne þurfe we us spillan, gif ge spedaþ to þam;
we willað wið þam golde grið fæstnian.
Gyf þu þat gerædest, þe her ricost eart,
þæt þu þine leoda lysan wille,
syllan sæmannum on hyra sylfra dom
feoh wið freode, and niman frið æt us,
we willaþ mid þam sceattum us to scype gangan,
on flot feran, and eow friþes healdan."
Byrhtnoð maþelode, bord hafenode,
wand wacne æsc, wordum mælde,
yrre and anræd ageaf him andsware:
"Gehyrst þu, sælida, hwæt þis folc segeð?
Hi willað eow to gafole garas syllan,
ættrynne ord and ealde swurd,
þa heregeatu þe eow æt hilde ne deah.
Brimmanna boda, abeod eft ongean,
sege þinum leodum miccle laþre spell,
þæt her stynt unforcuð eorl mid his werode,
þe wile gealgean eþel þysne,
æþelredes eard, ealdres mines,
folc and foldan. Feallan sceolon
hæþene æt hilde. To heanlic me þinceð
þæt ge mid urum sceattum to scype gangon
unbefohtene, nu ge þus feor hider
on urne eard in becomon.
Ne sceole ge swa softe sinc gegangan;
us sceal ord and ecg ær geseman,
grim guðplega, ær we gofol syllon."
Het þa bord beran, beornas gangan,
þæt hi on þam easteðe ealle stodon.
Ne mihte þær for wætere werod to þam oðrum;
þær com flowende flod æfter ebban,
lucon lagustreamas. To lang hit him þuhte,
hwænne hi togædere garas beron.
Hi þær Pantan stream mid prasse bestodon,
Eastseaxena ord and se æschere.
Ne mihte hyra ænig oþrum derian,
buton hwa þurh flanes flyht fyl gename.
Se flod ut gewat; þa flotan stodon gearowe,
wicinga fela, wiges georne.
Het þa hæleða hleo healdan þa bricge
wigan wigheardne, se wæs haten Wulfstan,
cafne mid his cynne, þæt wæs Ceolan sunu,
þe ðone forman man mid his francan ofsceat
þe þær baldlicost on þa bricge stop.
þær stodon mid Wulfstane wigan unforhte,
ælfere and Maccus, modige twegen,
þa noldon æt þam forda fleam gewyrcan,
ac hi fæstlice wið ða fynd weredon,
þa hwile þe hi wæpna wealdan moston.
þa hi þæt ongeaton and georne gesawon
þæt hi þær bricgweardas bitere fundon,
ongunnon lytegian þa laðe gystas,
bædon þæt hi upgang agan moston,
ofer þone ford faran, feþan lædan.
ða se eorl ongan for his ofermode
alyfan landes to fela laþere ðeode.
Ongan ceallian þa ofer cald wæter
Byrhtelmes bearn (beornas gehlyston):
"Nu eow is gerymed, gað ricene to us,
guman to guþe; god ana wat
hwa þære wælstowe wealdan mote."
Wodon þa wælwulfas (for wætere ne murnon),
wicinga werod, west ofer Pantan,
ofer scir wæter scyldas wegon,
lidmen to lande linde bæron.
þær ongean gramum gearowe stodon
Byrhtnoð mid beornum; he mid bordum het
wyrcan þone wihagan, and þæt werod healdan
fæste wið feondum. þa wæs feohte neh,
tir æt getohte. Wæs seo tid cumen
þæt þær fæge men feallan sceoldon.
þær wearð hream ahafen, hremmas wundon,
earn æses georn; wæs on eorþan cyrm.
Hi leton þa of folman feolhearde speru,
gegrundene garas fleogan;
bogan wæron bysige, bord ord onfeng.
Biter wæs se beaduræs, beornas feollon
on gehwæðere hand, hyssas lagon.
Wund wearð Wulfmær, wælræste geceas,
Byrhtnoðes mæg; he mid billum wearð,
his swuster sunu, swiðe forheawen.
þær wearð wicingum wiþerlean agyfen.
Gehyrde ic þæt Eadweard anne sloge
swiðe mid his swurde, swenges ne wyrnde,
þæt him æt fotum feoll fæge cempa;
þæs him his ðeoden þanc gesæde,
þam burþene, þa he byre hæfde.
Swa stemnetton stiðhicgende
hysas æt hilde, hogodon georne
hwa þær mid orde ærost mihte
on fægean men feorh gewinnan,
wigan mid wæpnum; wæl feol on eorðan.
Stodon stædefæste; stihte hi Byrhtnoð,
bæd þæt hyssa gehwylc hogode to wige
þe on Denon wolde dom gefeohtan.
Wod þa wiges heard, wæpen up ahof,
bord to gebeorge, and wið þæs beornes stop.
Eode swa anræd eorl to þam ceorle,
ægþer hyra oðrum yfeles hogode.
Sende ða se særinc suþerne gar,
þæt gewundod wearð wigena hlaford;
he sceaf þa mid ðam scylde, þæt se sceaft tobærst,
and þæt spere sprengde, þæt hit sprang ongean.
Gegremod wearð se guðrinc; he mid gare stang
wlancne wicing, þe him þa wunde forgeaf.
Frod wæs se fyrdrinc; he let his francan wadan
þurh ðæs hysses hals, hand wisode
þæt he on þam færsceaðan feorh geræhte.
ða he oþerne ofstlice sceat,
þæt seo byrne tobærst; he wæs on breostum wund
þurh ða hringlocan, him æt heortan stod
ætterne ord. Se eorl wæs þe bliþra,
hloh þa, modi man, sæde metode þanc
ðæs dægweorces þe him drihten forgeaf.
Forlet þa drenga sum daroð of handa,
fleogan of folman, þæt se to forð gewat
þurh ðone æþelan æþelredes þegen.
Him be healfe stod hyse unweaxen,
cniht on gecampe, se full caflice
bræd of þam beorne blodigne gar,
Wulfstanes bearn, Wulfmær se geonga,
forlet forheardne faran eft ongean;
ord in gewod, þæt se on eorþan læg
þe his þeoden ær þearle geræhte.
Eode þa gesyrwed secg to þam eorle;
he wolde þæs beornes beagas gefecgan,
reaf and hringas and gerenod swurd.
þa Byrhtnoð bræd bill of sceðe,
brad and bruneccg, and on þa byrnan sloh.
To raþe hine gelette lidmanna sum,
þa he þæs eorles earm amyrde.
Feoll þa to foldan fealohilte swurd;
ne mihte he gehealdan heardne mece,
wæpnes wealdan. þa gyt þæt word gecwæð
har hilderinc, hyssas bylde,
bæd gangan forð gode geferan;
ne mihte þa on fotum leng fæste gestandan.
He to heofenum wlat:
"Geþancie þe, ðeoda waldend,
ealra þæra wynna þe ic on worulde gebad.
Nu ic ah, milde metod, mæste þearfe
þæt þu minum gaste godes geunne,
þæt min sawul to ðe siðian mote
on þin geweald, þeoden engla,
mid friþe ferian. Ic eom frymdi to þe
þæt hi helsceaðan hynan ne moton."
ða hine heowon hæðene scealcas
and begen þa beornas þe him big stodon,
ælfnoð and Wulmær begen lagon,
ða onemn hyra frean feorh gesealdon.
Hi bugon þa fram beaduwe þe þær beon noldon.
þær wearð Oddan bearn ærest on fleame,
Godric fram guþe, and þone godan forlet
þe him mænigne oft mear gesealde;
he gehleop þone eoh þe ahte his hlaford,
on þam gerædum þe hit riht ne wæs,
and his broðru mid him begen ærndon,
Godwine and Godwig, guþe ne gymdon,
ac wendon fram þam wige and þone wudu sohton,
flugon on þæt fæsten and hyra feore burgon,
and manna ma þonne hit ænig mæð wære,
gyf hi þa geearnunga ealle gemundon
þe he him to duguþe gedon hæfde.
Swa him Offa on dæg ær asæde
on þam meþelstede, þa he gemot hæfde,
þæt þær modiglice manega spræcon
þe eft æt þearfe þolian noldon.
þa wearð afeallen þæs folces ealdor,
æþelredes eorl; ealle gesawon
heorðgeneatas þæt hyra heorra læg.
þa ðær wendon forð wlance þegenas,
unearge men efston georne;
hi woldon þa ealle oðer twega,
lif forlætan oððe leofne gewrecan.
Swa hi bylde forð bearn ælfrices,
wiga wintrum geong, wordum mælde,
ælfwine þa cwæð, he on ellen spræc:
"Gemunan þa mæla þe we oft æt meodo spræcon,
þonne we on bence beot ahofon,
hæleð on healle, ymbe heard gewinn;
nu mæg cunnian hwa cene sy.
Ic wylle mine æþelo eallum gecyþan,
þæt ic wæs on Myrcon miccles cynnes;
wæs min ealda fæder Ealhelm haten,
wis ealdorman, woruldgesælig.
Ne sceolon me on þære þeode þegenas ætwitan
þæt ic of ðisse fyrde feran wille,
eard gesecan, nu min ealdor ligeð
forheawen æt hilde. Me is þæt hearma mæst;
he wæs ægðer min mæg and min hlaford."
þa he forð eode, fæhðe gemunde,
þæt he mid orde anne geræhte
flotan on þam folce, þæt se on foldan læg
forwegen mid his wæpne. Ongan þa winas manian,
frynd and geferan, þæt hi forð eodon.
Offa gemælde, æscholt asceoc:
"Hwæt þu, ælfwine, hafast ealle gemanode
þegenas to þearfe, nu ure þeoden lið,
eorl on eorðan. Us is eallum þearf
þæt ure æghwylc oþerne bylde
wigan to wige, þa hwile þe he wæpen mæge
habban and healdan, heardne mece,
gar and godswurd. Us Godric hæfð,
earh Oddan bearn, ealle beswicene.
Wende þæs formoni man, þa he on meare rad,
on wlancan þam wicge, þæt wære hit ure hlaford;
forþan wearð her on felda folc totwæmed,
scyldburh tobrocen. Abreoðe his angin,
þæt he her swa manigne man aflymde!"
Leofsunu gemælde and his linde ahof,
bord to gebeorge; he þam beorne oncwæð:
"Ic þæt gehate, þæt ic heonon nelle
fleon fotes trym, ac wille furðor gan,
wrecan on gewinne minne winedrihten.
Ne þurfon me embe Sturmere stedefæste hælæð
wordum ætwitan, nu min wine gecranc,
þæt ic hlafordleas ham siðie,
wende fram wige, ac me sceal wæpen niman,
ord and iren." He ful yrre wod,
feaht fæstlice, fleam he forhogode.
Dunnere þa cwæð, daroð acwehte,
unorne ceorl, ofer eall clypode,
bæd þæt beorna gehwylc Byrhtnoð wrece:
"Ne mæg na wandian se þe wrecan þenceð
frean on folce, ne for feore murnan."
þa hi forð eodon, feores hi ne rohton;
ongunnon þa hiredmen heardlice feohtan,
grame garberend, and god bædon
þæt hi moston gewrecan hyra winedrihten
and on hyra feondum fyl gewyrcan.
Him se gysel ongan geornlice fylstan;
he wæs on Norðhymbron heardes cynnes,
Ecglafes bearn, him wæs æscferð nama.
He ne wandode na æt þam wigplegan,
ac he fysde forð flan genehe;
hwilon he on bord sceat, hwilon beorn tæsde,
æfre embe stunde he sealde sume wunde,
þa hwile ðe he wæpna wealdan moste.
þa gyt on orde stod Eadweard se langa,
gearo and geornful, gylpwordum spræc
þæt he nolde fleogan fotmæl landes,
ofer bæc bugan, þa his betera leg.
He bræc þone bordweall and wið þa beornas feaht,
oðþæt he his sincgyfan on þam sæmannum
wurðlice wrec, ær he on wæle lege.
Swa dyde æþeric, æþele gefera,
fus and forðgeorn, feaht eornoste.
Sibyrhtes broðor and swiðe mænig oþer
clufon cellod bord, cene hi weredon;
bærst bordes lærig, and seo byrne sang
gryreleoða sum. þa æt guðe sloh
Offa þone sælidan, þæt he on eorðan feoll,
and ðær Gaddes mæg grund gesohte.
Raðe wearð æt hilde Offa forheawen;
he hæfde ðeah geforþod þæt he his frean gehet,
swa he beotode ær wið his beahgifan
þæt hi sceoldon begen on burh ridan,
hale to hame, oððe on here crincgan,
on wælstowe wundum sweltan;
he læg ðegenlice ðeodne gehende.
ða wearð borda gebræc. Brimmen wodon,
guðe gegremode; gar oft þurhwod
fæges feorhhus. Forð þa eode Wistan,
þurstanes sunu, wið þas secgas feaht;
he wæs on geþrange hyra þreora bana,
ær him Wigelines bearn on þam wæle læge.
þær wæs stið gemot; stodon fæste
wigan on gewinne, wigend cruncon,
wundum werige. Wæl feol on eorþan.
Oswold and Eadwold ealle hwile,
begen þa gebroþru, beornas trymedon,
hyra winemagas wordon bædon
þæt hi þær æt ðearfe þolian sceoldon,
unwaclice wæpna neotan.
Byrhtwold maþelode bord hafenode
(se wæs eald geneat), æsc acwehte;
he ful baldlice beornas lærde:
"Hige sceal þe heardra, heorte þe cenre,
mod sceal þe mare, þe ure mægen lytlað.
Her lið ure ealdor eall forheawen,
god on greote. A mæg gnornian
se ðe nu fram þis wigplegan wendan þenceð.
Ic eom frod feores; fram ic ne wille,
ac ic me be healfe minum hlaforde,
be swa leofan men, licgan þence."
Swa hi æþelgares bearn ealle bylde,
Godric to guþe. Oft he gar forlet,
wælspere windan on þa wicingas,
swa he on þam folce fyrmest eode,
heow and hynde, oðþæt he on hilde gecranc.
Næs þæt na se Godric þe ða guðe forbeah

Study notes by the editors

  1. A fragment: the lost manuscript and the transcript l. 1

    The poem survives only because someone copied it before the Cotton Library fire at Ashburnham House in 1731, which destroyed its manuscript, Cotton Otho A.xii. That transcript, made around 1724, was printed by Thomas Hearne in 1726 in his edition of John of Glastonbury's chronicle. It was later found in the Bodleian Library (MS Rawlinson B. 203) in the 1930s. The copy has traditionally been credited to David Casley, the library's deputy keeper, though some scholars give it to John Elphinstone. Its text already lacked a beginning and an end, so it opens with the words 'brocen wurde' ('... should be broken'). Its 325 lines are the editors' numbering. Some of the odd spellings in this text (e.g. 'frymdi', 'wordon', 'hælæð') may be a later transcriber's misreadings of Anglo-Saxon letters, not the original scribe's forms. That is why many emendations are proposed.

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), The Battle of Maldon, with notes; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Wikipedia, 'The Battle of Maldon' (manuscript history summary)

  2. The historical battle of 991 l. 17

    The battle is a recorded event. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (versions C, D and E, s.a. 991) says Ipswich was harried and 'very soon after' ealdorman Byrhtnoth was killed at Maldon. It adds that in that year tribute was first paid to the Danes, 10,000 pounds, on the advice of Archbishop Sigeric. Version A, under 993 (a misdating), names the leader Anlaf and gives a fleet of ninety-three ships; some take him to be Olaf Tryggvason. Byrhtferth of Ramsey's Life of St Oswald, written soon after, makes Byrhtnoth a tall, white-haired religious hero. The twelfth-century Liber Eliensis says the Danes carried off his head and that he was buried at Ely; when his bones were found there in 1769 the skull was missing. Later monastic calendars date his death to 10 or 11 August. The poem was therefore written soon after the battle, or within a generation, but how soon is disputed.

    Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, s.a. 991 (C, D, E) and 993 (A), in translation; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; Thijs Porck, 'Chop chop! Three bizarre beheadings in Anglo-Saxon England' (blog, 2017), on the Liber Eliensis and the Ely remains

  3. The hawk let fly to the wood ll. 5-10

    The cniht ('young man, youth') is a kinsman of Offa (l. 5). When he sees that the earl will not put up with cowardice ('yrhðo', l. 6), he releases his 'leofne' (dear) hawk towards the wood and goes to the fight. The poet spells out what it means: from this one 'could know' (oncnawan) that he would not weaken in the battle. Hawking was a sport of the nobility, and releasing the bird is a small, concrete act that marks the move from peaceful aristocratic life to deadly earnest. Critics often see it as a deliberate image of renunciation, done once and for good. It is one of the poem's few images drawn from daily life. The verb 'wacian' (l. 10, 'to weaken, grow slack') foreshadows the cowards later in the poem.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. hafoc

  4. beot: the vow fulfilled ll. 11-16

    Old English 'beot' (Bosworth-Toller: vow, promise, also boast or threat) is the public pledge a warrior makes, often in the hall over mead, to do great deeds for his lord. It is not mere bragging: the vow binds the speaker. Eadric 'gelæste' (fulfilled, carried out) his vow when he had to fight before his lord. The poem is built on the contrast between vows kept and vows broken. Compare Ælfwine's reminder of what was said 'æt meodo' (l. 212), Offa's earlier warning that many boast who will not endure (ll. 198-201), and Offa's own vow (ll. 289-93). The messenger's speech is called 'on beot' (l. 27), a threat; the two senses of the word sit close together.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. beót; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  5. wicinga ar: the Viking messenger ll. 25-28

    'Ar' here means messenger or herald (compare 'ærende', errand, l. 28). He stands 'on stæðe' (on the shore, bank) and speaks 'on beot', threateningly. The Vikings are never named individually; they are 'brimliþendra' (seafarers, genitive plural, l. 27), 'sæmen' (l. 29), 'flotan' (l. 72), and 'wælwulfas' (l. 96). This anonymity contrasts with the English, whose names and kin are listed with care. The scene sets up a formal exchange of speeches across the water, with a challenge and a reply, before any fighting starts, as in heroic tradition.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wícing; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 1 (text, translation, commentary)

  6. gafol: tribute ll. 31-35

    'Gafol' means tax, rent or tribute (Bosworth-Toller). The messenger proposes that the English 'buy off' (forgyldon) this spear-rush ('garræs') with tribute and rings ('beagas wið gebeorge', rings in return for protection). He speaks in a mercantile, almost legal language of deals: 'we willað wið þam golde grið fæstnian' (we will fasten a truce in exchange for the gold). The irony for the first audience was sharp. The Chronicle records that in 991 the English began paying tribute to the Danes, 10,000 pounds, the start of what was later called Danegeld. Byrhtnoth throws the word back in his reply: 'Hi willað eow to gafole garas syllan' (l. 46).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. gafol; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, s.a. 991 (C, D, E) and 993 (A), in translation; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  7. grið: a Norse loanword for 'truce' l. 35

    Old English 'grið' comes from Old Norse 'grið' (truce, peace, sanctuary). It was a legal term that spread into late Old English law codes through Scandinavian settlement. The native word for peace in the next lines is 'frið' (ll. 39, 41). Several critics think the poet gives the messenger a few Norse or legalistic forms on purpose, so that his speech sounds foreign and calculating. Others caution that 'grið' was already established in English by the late tenth century. 'On hyra sylfra dom' (l. 38) means 'on their own terms, at their own judgement'. It is a legal phrase for a settlement whose terms are set by one side alone.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. grið; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  8. Byrhtnoth's reply: the speech formula ll. 42-44

    'Maþelian' (to speak formally, make a speech) is a verb of poetry only; 'X maþelode' is the classic way a heroic speech begins, as often in Beowulf. Byrhtnoth lifts his shield and shakes his slender ash-spear ('wand wacne æsc'). He is 'yrre and anræd' (angry and resolute). The same formula and gestures come back in Byrhtwold's final speech (ll. 309-10), so the poem's two great speeches frame the battle. In his reply Byrhtnoth answers the messenger point by point. He takes up the messenger's own words (gafol, sceattas, scip, gangan) and turns them into weapons. Critics have long admired the speech as a piece of controlled scorn.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. maþelian; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 1 (text, translation, commentary); E. V. Gordon, ed., The Battle of Maldon (London: Methuen, 1937; repr. 1976 with supplement by D. G. Scragg)

  9. heregeatu: war-gear and heriot l. 48

    Bosworth-Toller gives 'military equipment' and also 'heriot'. A heriot was the payment of weapons, horses and money that a nobleman's heirs owed his lord at his death; late Anglo-Saxon wills record such heriots. Byrhtnoth says the English will pay tribute in spears, deadly points and old swords, 'that heriot which will not profit you in battle'. Many editors see a pun. The Vikings will receive weapons, but point first, and the 'heriot' will be paid on their own deaths. The word sums up Byrhtnoth's method of turning the messenger's commercial language into the language of war.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. here-geatu; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  10. Æthelred's land ll. 51-54

    Byrhtnoth calls himself an 'unforcuð eorl' (an earl without reproach, l. 51) who will defend ('gealgean') this homeland ('eþel'). He names it as the land of Æthelred, 'ealdres mines' (my lord). The king is Æthelred II, whose reign saw renewed Viking attacks from the 980s and the tribute payments that began after Maldon. His later nickname 'Unræd' ('ill-counselled') puns on his name ('noble counsel'). The poem names him three times (ll. 53, 151, 203), always as the lord whom Byrhtnoth serves. The chain of loyalty in the poem runs from God through the king and the earl down to the retainers. 'Folc and foldan' (people and land, l. 54) is an alliterating pair typical of verse.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. un-forcúþ; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, s.a. 991 (C, D, E) and 993 (A), in translation; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle

  11. Pante: the River Blackwater l. 68

    The two armies face each other across the tidal water of the Pante, which is the Blackwater estuary; the upper river is still called the Pant. When the tide comes in ('flod æfter ebban', l. 65), the 'lagustreamas' (sea-currents) 'lucon' (locked together, closed in) round the island, so neither side can reach the other except with arrows. E. D. Laborde (1925) identified the Viking base as Northey Island, joined to the mainland at low tide by a causeway. That causeway is the 'bricg' of l. 74. Geological work by George and Susan Petty (1976) supported the identification, though other sites have been proposed. The topography in the poem is precise enough to make this identification widely accepted.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; E. D. Laborde, 'The Site of the Battle of Maldon', English Historical Review 40 (1925); G. R. Petty and S. Petty, 'Geology and The Battle of Maldon', Speculum 51 (1976)

  12. bricg: the causeway ll. 74-83

    The 'bricg' is not a built bridge but the causeway exposed at low tide. Byrhtnoth, the 'hæleða hleo' (protector of warriors, a common formula), sets Wulfstan, son of Ceola, to hold it. Wulfstan kills the first man who steps onto it with his 'franca' (a spear or javelin, perhaps Frankish in origin). He is joined by Ælfhere and Maccus. Because three men can hold the narrow way, the Vikings cannot force a crossing. That leaves them only the trick of asking to be let across (l. 86), and so the poem explains why Byrhtnoth's decision at l. 89 matters so much.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. bricg

  13. Ælfhere and Maccus l. 80

    The defenders of the causeway include Maccus, whose name is not Old English. It is usually explained as Norse or Gaelic (related to forms like Magnus or Gaelic Maccus) and is found in the Danelaw and the Irish Sea world. With Æscferth the Northumbrian hostage (ll. 265-67) and Ælfwine the Mercian (ll. 217-18), Maccus shows that Byrhtnoth's force drew on more than the men of Essex. The poem's cast of named English fighters is unusually long, and many critics think the poet named real men known to his audience. Few of them can be traced in other records.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; E. V. Gordon, ed., The Battle of Maldon (London: Methuen, 1937; repr. 1976 with supplement by D. G. Scragg)

  14. lytegian: to act with guile ll. 84-88

    Bosworth-Toller glosses 'lytigian' as 'to act cunningly', citing this line. The 'laðe gystas' (hateful guests) find that the 'bricgweardas' (guardians of the causeway) are fierce, so they ask that they may have 'upgang' (passage up, a landing) and bring their foot-soldiers ('feþan') across the ford. The word puts the blame for what follows on Viking cunning as well as on Byrhtnoth's response. The phrase 'laðe gystas' is ironic, since guests should be welcomed, and the poem reverses the duties of hospitality throughout.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. lytigian; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 1 (text, translation, commentary)

  15. ofermod: Byrhtnoth's pride l. 89

    Literally 'over-mind, over-spirit', 'ofermod' is glossed by Bosworth-Toller as 'pride, arrogance, over-confidence', and Latin 'superbia'. Elsewhere in Old English it describes Lucifer's pride (e.g. in Genesis B). Tolkien (1953) read the line as the poet's judgement: Byrhtnoth's chivalrous wish for a fair fight sacrificed his men, and the poem honours their loyalty while blaming his rashness. Gneuss (1976) showed that the word is pejorative in all its other uses, and since then 'pride' has been the dominant reading. Others still defend Byrhtnoth. They argue that 'ofermod' can be a heroic 'great spirit', or that a commander had good reason to bring on a battle rather than let the fleet sail off to raid elsewhere. Scholars still disagree on whether the poem condemns him, admires him, or does both.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ofer-mód; J. R. R. Tolkien, 'The Homecoming of Beorhtnoth Beorhthelm's Son', Essays and Studies n.s. 6 (1953), with the appended essay 'Ofermod'; Helmut Gneuss, 'The Battle of Maldon 89: Byrhtnoth's ofermod Once Again', Studies in Philology 73 (1976), 117-37; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  16. landes to fela: too much land l. 90

    'Alyfan' means to allow, grant; 'landes to fela' (too much of land, partitive genitive) refers to the ground the Vikings were allowed to cross and the space to form up on the English side. The phrase is a rare direct comment from the narrator, and its 'to' (too) carries the judgement. Read with 'ofermod', it suggests that Byrhtnoth's generosity was a fault. Some critics hear a further irony: the earl who refused to give the Vikings gold gives them land instead, the very 'eþel' he swore to defend (l. 52). 'Laþere ðeode' means 'to the hateful people', the dative of 'lað' (hateful).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. álýfan; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  17. God alone knows who will hold the field ll. 94-95

    'Wælstow' is the battlefield, literally 'slaughter-place'. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle repeatedly says the winners 'ahton wælstowe geweald' (had control of the field). Byrhtnoth's words that God alone knows who will hold it are pious, and they also show that he thinks the outcome is still open. Critics who blame him read the line as presumption, since he has thrown away a winning defensive position and left the result to chance. Those who defend him see trust in God. The same 'wælstowe' returns in Offa's vow (l. 293), where the place of slaughter has become the place of his death.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wæl-stów; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, s.a. 991 (C, D, E) and 993 (A), in translation; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  18. wihaga: the battle-hedge l. 102

    Bosworth-Toller gives 'wíg-haga', 'a phalanx', citing this line. The compound joins 'wig' (war) with 'haga' (hedge, fence, enclosure, as in 'haw'). Shields set rim to rim make a living fence. The poem uses several terms for this formation: 'bordweall' (l. 277) and 'scyldburh' (shield-fortress, l. 242). Its breaking is the military result of Godric's flight. 'Feohte neh, tir æt getohte' (battle was near, glory in the fight, ll. 103-04) puts the start of the fighting in a pair of half-lines that echo each other.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wíg-haga; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. scild-burh; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  19. The beasts of battle ll. 106-07

    Old English poets often bring in the raven, the eagle and the wolf when battle is about to begin, waiting for corpses. Magoun (1955) called this a recurring 'theme' of oral-formulaic composition; it occurs in Judith, Exodus, Elene, Brunanburh and elsewhere. Here the 'earn æses georn' (eagle eager for carrion) and wheeling 'hremmas' (ravens) appear with the outcry ('hream') of the armies. Maldon uses the theme briefly, as a single image, before the poet turns to the spear-throwing. The wolf is present only in the Vikings themselves, the 'wælwulfas' of l. 96.

    Francis P. Magoun, Jr, 'The Theme of the Beasts of Battle in Anglo-Saxon Poetry', Neuphilologische Mitteilungen 56 (1955); D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  20. Sister's son ll. 113-15

    Tacitus (Germania 20) remarks that among the Germans a sister's son was held in the same honour by his uncle as by his father. The idea recurs in Old English heroic poetry (Beowulf and Hygelac; the Finnsburh story). The poet underlines the relationship ('Byrhtnoðes mæg ... his swuster sunu'), and the next line reports at once that 'wiþerlean' (requital) was given to the Vikings. The pattern of kinship, death and vengeance prepares for the second half of the poem, where vengeance for Byrhtnoth himself becomes the only aim. This Wulfmær should not be confused with Wulfmær 'se geonga', son of Wulfstan (l. 155).

    Tacitus, Germania (Latin text); D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  21. suþerne gar: the southern spear l. 134

    'Suþerne' means 'southern'. Editors have taken it in different ways. It may mean a spear of southern make, perhaps Frankish, from the Rhineland weapon trade, like the 'franca' of ll. 77 and 140. Or it may be a spear of English make from the Viking's northern point of view, or simply a conventional epithet. It is one of several unexplained details in the duel. Byrhtnoth pushes the shaft away with his shield so that it breaks, and then kills two men in turn. The 'særinc' (sea-warrior) here and 'ceorl' (l. 132), a term for a man of lower rank, keep the Viking anonymous and socially below the earl.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; E. V. Gordon, ed., The Battle of Maldon (London: Methuen, 1937; repr. 1976 with supplement by D. G. Scragg); Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. súþerne

  22. Byrhtnoth laughs ll. 146-48

    'Modi' (spirited, brave, also proud; related to 'mod') describes Byrhtnoth as he laughs and thanks the Lord ('metod', the measurer, a poetic name for God) for the 'dægweorc' (day's work) that God has granted him. Laughter in battle is a heroic gesture, found also in Old Norse literature. The thanksgiving makes the warrior's labour a gift from God. The moment is brief: at once a 'dreng' throws a dart that pierces him (ll. 149-51). Critics read the laughter in opposite ways, either as admirable courage or, in the light of 'ofermod', as another sign of the earl's over-confidence.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. módig

  23. Wulfmær the young ll. 152-58

    The poet takes care to describe him as young: 'hyse unweaxen' (an ungrown youth), 'cniht on gecampe' (a boy in the battle). He acts 'full caflice' (very boldly). His father is probably the Wulfstan who held the causeway (l. 75). His deed prepares for the general theme of revenge in the second half. It also sets up the contrast of youth and age running through the poem: the hawk-flying 'cniht' (l. 9), the 'har hilderinc' Byrhtnoth (l. 169), Ælfwine 'wintrum geong' (l. 210), and the old retainer Byrhtwold (l. 310).

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 1 (text, translation, commentary)

  24. har hilderinc: the grey-haired warrior ll. 166-71

    When a Viking cripples his sword-arm, Byrhtnoth's 'fealohilte swurd' (sword with yellowish, i.e. gold-coloured, hilt) falls to the ground. He can no longer hold his 'heardne mece' (hard blade; 'mece' is a poetic word for sword). Still 'har hilderinc' (grey battle-warrior), he encourages his men. Byrhtferth's Life of St Oswald says he had white hair, and he had been ealdorman since 956, so he was probably over sixty. His age adds pathos and links him with Byrhtwold, the 'eald geneat' who speaks the poem's most famous lines. The half-line 'hyssas bylde' (encouraged the young men) fixes his last act as a leader.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. hár

  25. Byrhtnoth's dying prayer ll. 173-80

    The prayer echoes the Christian commendation of the soul at death. He thanks the 'ðeoda waldend' (ruler of peoples) for the joys of his life. He asks the 'milde metod' (gentle Creator) to grant his spirit good, so that his soul may journey ('siðian') into God's keeping, and that the 'helsceaðan' (hell-ravagers, demons) may not harm ('hynan') it. In medieval belief the soul at death could be contested by angels and devils, and the 'þeoden engla' (prince of angels) is asked to protect it. The speech has a hagiographic quality. Some critics compare it to martyr narratives and to the dying Roland's prayer in the Chanson de Roland, while others point to the differences.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Cecily Clark, 'Byrhtnoth and Roland: A Contrast', Neophilologus 51 (1967)

  26. frymdi: entreating l. 179

    Bosworth-Toller enters 'frymdi', 'inquisitive, asking, desirous, suppliant', citing this very line. It relates it to 'frimdig' (desirous) found in Ælfric. 'Ic eom frymdi to þe' thus means 'I am asking of you, I entreat you' that the hell-foes may not harm my soul. The spelling without final -g is unusual, and editors debate whether it reflects late Essex speech, an error in the lost manuscript, or the transcript. Either way the sense is clear. The line ends the prayer in humble petition after the strong verbs of the heroic narrative.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. frymdi; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. frymdig, frymdi; E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), The Battle of Maldon, with notes

  27. Godric's flight on his lord's horse ll. 185-91

    'Hi bugon þa fram beaduwe þe þær beon noldon' (then those who did not wish to be there turned from battle) starts the movement of the cowards. Godric 'forlet' (abandoned) the good man who had often given him a 'mear' (horse), a typical lord's gift. He leapt on the 'eoh' (horse, a rare poetic word) that his lord owned, 'on þam gerædum þe hit riht ne wæs' (in the trappings, which was not right). The theft of the lord's horse makes the betrayal concrete and later deceives the army (ll. 239-42). The three brothers' names alliterate in G (Godric, Godwine, Godwig). The poet's narrating presence is sharp here: he comments on rightness and wrong.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. eoh

  28. Offa's warning at the assembly ll. 198-201

    'Meþelstede' (meeting place, place of speech) and 'gemot' (assembly) refer to a council held earlier on the day of the battle. There Offa observed that many spoke 'modiglice' (bravely, proudly) who would not endure 'æt þearfe' (in time of need). The passage looks back to a scene not in the surviving poem, perhaps once told in the lost opening. It confirms the poem's main moral test: the gap between words and deeds, between the 'beot' and its fulfilment. Offa himself, by contrast, keeps his vow (ll. 289-94).

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. mæþel-stede

  29. To die or to avenge ll. 207-08

    The vow that a retainer should not leave the field alive when his lord has fallen is often traced to Tacitus (Germania 14), who says it was lifelong shame to survive one's chief in battle. Roberta Frank (1991) challenged this assumption. She argued that the ideal of dying with one's lord is rare in earlier Germanic and Old English literature, and that Maldon may reflect a late tenth-century literary fashion rather than ancient custom. Others reply that the motif appears in the Chronicle's Cynewulf and Cyneheard episode (s.a. 755). Either way, the speeches that follow all repeat this choice, each in the speaker's own voice.

    Tacitus, Germania (Latin text); Roberta Frank, 'The Ideal of Men Dying with their Lord in The Battle of Maldon: Anachronism or Nouvelle Vague', in People and Places in Northern Europe 500-1600, ed. I. Wood and N. Lund (1991); D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  30. Remembering the mead-hall vows ll. 212-15

    'Mæl' (speech, saying) here means the formal vows ('beot', l. 213) that the warriors 'ahofon' (raised, made) 'on bence' in the hall 'æt meodo' (at the mead). The ideal of the mead-hall pledge, repaid in battle, is ancient in Germanic heroic verse. Compare Wiglaf's reproach to Beowulf's companions in Beowulf, which uses the same idea of drinking the lord's mead and failing him. 'Nu mæg cunnian hwa cene sy' (now can be tested who is keen) uses the subjunctive 'sy' for an open question. The test of vows is the poem's central theme.

    Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 2 (text, translation, commentary); D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  31. Ælfwine and his Mercian ancestry ll. 209-24

    Ælfwine, 'wiga wintrum geong' (a warrior young in winters), declares his 'æþelo' (noble descent). He is of a great family in Mercia ('on Myrcon'), and his grandfather Ealhelm was a 'wis ealdorman, woruldgesælig' (wise ealdorman, prosperous in the world). An ealdorman named Ealhelm is recorded in Mercia in the 940s. Historians have tentatively identified Ælfwine's father Ælfric with Ælfric 'cild', ealdorman of Mercia, exiled in 985, though this is uncertain. Ælfwine says no thegns among his people shall reproach him ('ætwitan') for going home lordless, because Byrhtnoth 'wæs ægðer min mæg and min hlaford' (was both my kinsman and my lord). Kin and lordship, the two great bonds of the heroic world, are joined in one man.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. æþelu

  32. The army deceived by the horse ll. 237-43

    Offa's speech gives the military cause of the defeat. 'Formoni man' (very many a man) thought, when Godric rode off on the proud steed ('wlancan þam wicge'), that it was their lord. So the army ('folc') was split ('totwæmed') and the 'scyldburh' (shield-fortress) broken. Offa calls Godric 'earh' (cowardly, Bosworth-Toller: 'timorous, fleeing through fear') and curses his action: 'Abreoðe his angin' (may his undertaking fail, perish; optative subjunctive). Unlike the hawk scene, where the poet tells us what the action means, here the meaning comes from a character inside the poem.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. earg; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. scild-burh; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  33. Dunnere, the simple ceorl ll. 255-59

    Bosworth-Toller glosses 'unorne' as 'simple, plain, poor, humble'. A 'ceorl' was a free man of non-noble rank. That a ceorl shakes his dart and cries 'ofer eall' (over all) that no man may hesitate ('wandian') who means to avenge his lord shows the heroic ethic reaching down the social scale. Some critics take this as the poet's idealisation, others as a realistic note about the levy. Earlier the Viking who wounds Byrhtnoth is also called a 'ceorl' (l. 132), in contrast with 'eorl'. Dunnere's short speech is the plainest statement of the vengeance theme.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. un-orne; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  34. Æscferth the Northumbrian hostage ll. 265-72

    A 'gysel' was a hostage given as surety for the good conduct of his kin or people. Æscferth was perhaps a pledge from Northumbria, an area of strong Scandinavian settlement whose loyalty to the West Saxon king was uncertain. That he 'ne wandode na' (did not hesitate at all) but shot arrows ('flan') again and again shows that the household's bond could cover even an outsider. Critics often compare the Chronicle's Cynewulf and Cyneheard story (s.a. 755), where a hostage also fights. 'Hwilon ... hwilon' (sometimes ... sometimes, l. 270) gives his actions a steady rhythm.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon AD 991 (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), essays on the historical battle; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. gísel

  35. cellod bord: an unexplained shield ll. 283-85

    Bosworth-Toller enters 'célod, céllod', from 'ceol' (keel of a ship), and glosses it Latin 'scaphiformis', 'boat-shaped'. Bosworth-Toller cites the same phrase, 'cellod bord', in the Finnsburh Fragment (l. 29), and editors have linked the two passages, though the sense remains uncertain. The word may describe a convex or curved shield. 'Bærst bordes lærig' (the rim of the shield burst) and 'seo byrne sang gryreleoða sum' (the mail-coat sang a song of terror) are a vivid personification, with the ringing of the iron rings turned into a dreadful song.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. célod, céllod; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. cellod; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  36. Offa's vow fulfilled ll. 286-94

    Offa kills a Viking 'sælida' (seafarer), then is cut down himself. The poet says he had accomplished ('geforþod') what he promised his 'beahgifa' (ring-giver, a kenning for lord). Either they would both ride 'on burh' (to the stronghold, home) unharmed ('hale to hame'), or both perish in the army ('on here crincgan') and die of wounds on the 'wælstow' (field of slaughter). 'He læg ðegenlice ðeodne gehende' (he lay like a thegn, close to his lord) is an epitaph in a single line. The alliteration on þ/ð binds 'thegn' and 'lord' together. His vow pairs with Offa's earlier warning (l. 198) and with the hawk of his young kinsman.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. þegenlíce

  37. Byrhtwold, the old retainer ll. 309-11

    'Geneat' (Bosworth-Toller: companion, associate, vassal) is the household companion; 'eald' marks long service. The introduction ('maþelode, bord hafenode ... æsc acwehte') repeats almost word for word the frame of Byrhtnoth's reply to the messenger (ll. 42-43). The echo matters: the last speaker takes the dead lord's place as the voice of the troop. Nothing else is known of Byrhtwold. The poem gives no patronymic for him, only age and loyalty. His speech, spoken 'ful baldlice' (very boldly), 'teaching' ('lærde') the warriors, is the moral climax of the poem as we have it.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-neát; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 2 (text, translation, commentary); D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line

  38. 'Mind must be the harder' ll. 312-13

    Grammar: 'sceal' (must, shall) governs each clause; 'þe heardra', 'þe cenre', 'þe mare' are comparatives preceded by the instrumental 'þe' ('the harder ... the keener'). These are answered by 'þe ure mægen lytlað' ('as [lit. by that which] our strength lessens'). The three key nouns, 'hige' (thought, courage), 'heorte' (heart) and 'mod' (mind, spirit), name the inner self, while 'mægen' (might, physical strength) is the outer power that fails. The lines alliterate on h and m. Critics have compared the sentiment to the Old Norse heroic poem Bjarkamál, known through Saxo Grammaticus. The lines are often quoted as a summary of the northern heroic spirit, the will that refuses defeat when defeat is certain, and Tolkien made them central to his reading. The irony that Byrhtnoth's 'ofermod' caused this situation has often been noted.

    Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 2 (text, translation, commentary); J. R. R. Tolkien, 'The Homecoming of Beorhtnoth Beorhthelm's Son', Essays and Studies n.s. 6 (1953), with the appended essay 'Ofermod'; D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Battle of Maldon

  39. 'I am old in life' ll. 317-19

    'Frod feores' (old in life; genitive of respect) recalls the 'frod fyrdrinc' Byrhtnoth (l. 140). 'Fram ic ne wille' (I will not [go] away) leaves the verb of motion unstated, a common Old English construction. He intends to lie 'be healfe' (by the side) of his lord. 'Licgan þence' (I mean to lie) makes death a deliberate choice. 'A mæg gnornian' (ever may he mourn, l. 315) curses anyone who thinks of turning from this 'wigplega' (war-play, a poetic compound for battle). Frank's argument (1991) that dying with one's lord is a new literary ideal applies above all to this passage. Critics who stress continuity with older tradition read it as the purest expression of the comitatus ethic.

    Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 2 (text, translation, commentary); Roberta Frank, 'The Ideal of Men Dying with their Lord in The Battle of Maldon: Anachronism or Nouvelle Vague', in People and Places in Northern Europe 500-1600, ed. I. Wood and N. Lund (1991); Tacitus, Germania (Latin text)

  40. The other Godric: the poem breaks off ll. 320-25

    Godric son of Æthelgar ('æþelgares bearn') throws his 'wælspere' (slaughter-spear) at the Vikings and goes 'fyrmest' (foremost) until he falls ('gecranc'). The poet adds at once that this was not the Godric who 'ða guðe forbeah' (avoided the battle). The care to separate the two men suggests that the audience knew the names and that a son of Odda's disgrace was a real matter of reputation. It also shows how the poem uses names: identity and lineage decide honour and shame. The text ends here mid-passage, so we do not know how far the lost ending went or whether it followed the fight to the end.

    D. G. Scragg, ed., The Battle of Maldon (Manchester, 1981), note to the line; Cambridge ASNC, 'Spoken Word' Old English: The Battle of Maldon, passage 2 (text, translation, commentary); E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), The Battle of Maldon, with notes
