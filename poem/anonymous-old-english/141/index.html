The Husband's Message (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Relationships

Nu ic onsundran þe secgan wille
...... treocyn ic tudre aweox;
in mec æld... sceal ellor londes
settan ...... sealte streamas
...sse. Ful oft ic on bates
gesohte
þær mec mondryhten min ......
ofer heah hofu; eom nu her cumen
on ceolþele, ond nu cunnan scealt
hu þu ymb modlufan mines frean
on hyge hycge. Ic gehatan dear
þæt þu þær tirfæste treowe findest.
Hwæt, þec þonne biddan het se þisne beam agrof
þæt þu sinchroden sylf gemunde
on gewitlocan wordbeotunga,
þe git on ærdagum oft gespræcon,
þenden git moston on meoduburgum
eard weardigan, an lond bugan,
freondscype fremman. Hine fæhþo adraf
of sigeþeode; heht nu sylfa þe
lustum læran, þæt þu lagu drefde,
siþþan þu gehyrde on hliþes oran
galan geomorne geac on bearwe.
Ne læt þu þec siþþan siþes getwæfan,
lade gelettan lifgendne monn.
Ongin mere secan, mæwes eþel,
onsite sænacan, þæt þu suð heonan
ofer merelade monnan findest,
þær se þeoden is þin on wenum.
Ne mæg him worulde willa gelimpan
mara on gemyndum, þæs þe he me sægde,
þonne inc geunne alwaldend god
...... ætsomne siþþan motan
secgum ond gesiþum s...
næglede beagas; he genoh hafað
fædan goldes
...d elþeode eþel healde,
fægre foldan
...ra hæleþa, þeah þe her min wine...
nyde gebæded, nacan ut aþrong,
ond on yþa geong ...... sceolde
faran on flotweg, forðsiþes georn,
mengan merestreamas. Nu se mon hafað
wean oferwunnen; nis him wilna gad,
ne meara ne maðma ne meododreama,
ænges ofer eorþan eorlgestreona,
þeodnes dohtor, gif he þin beneah
ofer eald gebeot incer twega.
Gecyre ic ætsomne ᛋ ᚱ geador
ᛠ ᚹ ond ᛞ aþe benemnan,
þæt he þa wære ond þa winetreowe
be him lifgendum læstan wolde,
þe git on ærdagum oft gespræconn.

Codex Note

Old English text as in ASPR; hand-keyed e-text via sacred-texts.com.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Husband's Message: manuscript, speaker and damage l. 1

    The poem is on fol. 123r-123v of the Exeter Book, after Riddle 60 and before The Ruin. A diagonal burn across the last folios of the codex has destroyed words in ll. 2-8 and later passages (marked here by dots). The speaker, addressing the woman onsundran 'privately, apart', seems to be the wooden stave on which the message is carved, personified as in the riddles. Some think a human messenger speaks. Riddle 60 (usually solved as a reed) was taken as part of the poem by F. A. Blackburn (1901) and R. W. V. Elliott (1955), and joined with it in a reading of the cross by R. E. Kaske (1967); R. F. Leslie argued for keeping them separate. The poem is in Leslie's Three Old English Elegies (1961).

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; R. E. Kaske, 'A Poem of the Cross in the Exeter Book: Riddle 60 and The Husband's Message', Traditio 23 (1967); Wikipedia, 'The Husband's Message' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  2. treocyn, tudor: the speaking wood l. 2

    Treocyn means 'a kind of tree, a wood'; tudor, 'offspring, progeny', here in the dative tudre, 'as offspring' or 'from offspring'. Aweox 'grew up' (from aweaxan). The burn-hole has removed the beginning of the line. The device of an object telling its own life is common in the Exeter riddles and in The Dream of the Rood, where the cross speaks of being cut from the forest. It invites the Christian reading of Kaske, who read the speaker as the cross.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. treów-cynn; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. túdor; R. E. Kaske, 'A Poem of the Cross in the Exeter Book: Riddle 60 and The Husband's Message', Traditio 23 (1967); Bernard J. Muir, ed., The Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry, 2nd edn (Exeter, 2000), commentary

  3. ceolþel: 'ship's deck' ll. 5-9

    Ceolþelu means 'the deck of a ship' (ceol 'keel, ship' and þel 'plank'). Bates (genitive of bat 'boat') and ofer heah hofu, literally 'over high dwellings' (its sense here is uncertain), describe its journeys. Mondryhten min, 'my lord', is the heroic term for the lord of a band. Cunnan scealt, 'you must learn', addresses the woman directly. The damage in ll. 5-7 leaves only fragments, but the sense of repeated sea journeys on the lord's errands survives.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ceól-þelu; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  4. modlufu, treowe: the lord's heart's love ll. 10-12

    Modlufu means 'heart's love' (mod 'mind, heart' and lufu 'love'). On hyge hycge, 'think in mind', is a typical figura etymologica. Ic gehatan dear, 'I dare promise', makes the stave a guarantor. Treow 'faith, pledge, truth' is the central value of the poem, completed by winetreowe in l. 51. Tirfæste, 'fixed in glory', makes fidelity an honourable quality. The stave thus speaks as a witness who can vouch for the absent lord, the role that the runic oath at the end of the poem formalises.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wine-treów

  5. 'he who carved this wood' l. 13

    Agrafan means 'to engrave, carve', and beam 'tree, beam, piece of wood' is also a word for the cross (in The Dream of the Rood the cross is called beama beorhtost). The image is of a carved message-stick, a practice known from Scandinavian finds (rune-sticks from Bergen) and from Venantius Fortunatus's sixth-century reference to runes on wooden tablets. Hwæt, þec þonne biddan het, 'Listen, he who carved this wood told me to ask you', makes the messenger formal.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. a-grafan; R. E. Kaske, 'A Poem of the Cross in the Exeter Book: Riddle 60 and The Husband's Message', Traditio 23 (1967); John D. Niles, 'The Trick of the Runes in The Husband's Message', Anglo-Saxon England 32 (2003); R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  6. sinchroden: 'treasure-adorned' l. 14

    Sinchroden, 'treasure-adorned', from sinc 'treasure' and hroden 'adorned' (from hreodan), is a heroic term used for halls and noblewomen. On gewitlocan, 'in the enclosure of the mind', uses a kenning, gewitloca 'container of wit', for the mind. Gemunde, the subjunctive of gemunan 'remember', makes this a request: that she should remember the promises. The epithet also implies that she still lives in rank at home, so that the journey asks her to leave a secure position for an exile's new land.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sinc-hroden; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. gewit-loca; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  7. wordbeotung: 'promises in words' ll. 15-16

    Wordbeotung is glossed 'promising, a promise'; Bosworth-Toller cites this line. It is built on beot, the heroic vow (compare beotedan in The Wife's Lament). Git is the dual pronoun 'you two'. On ærdagum, 'in former days', places the vows in a happy past. The repetition of l. 16 as the closing line (l. 53, gespræconn) gives the poem a ring structure: the message begins and ends with the vows.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. word-beótung; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  8. mead-cities and the shared homeland ll. 17-19

    Meoduburg, 'mead-town', is a poetic compound for a lordly settlement with its hall, where mead was drunk; compare meodudreamas, 'mead-joys', in l. 45. Eard weardigan 'occupy a dwelling' and an lond bugan, 'inhabit one land', state the shared past. Freondscype fremman, 'to practise friendship (love)', echoes The Wife's Lament's freondscipe uncer. The picture of shared life in the hall is the happy past against which the exile and the summons are measured; the poem later promises that the hall life will be restored abroad.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. medu; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  9. exile by feud ll. 19-20

    Fæhþo, 'feud', is the cause of his departure; adrifan means 'drive away, expel'. Sigeþeod, 'a victorious or powerful people', describes his own nation. The cause of exile is similar to the situation of The Wife's Lament, where the husband leaves over the sea, and critics including M. J. Swanton (Anglia, 1964) treated the two poems as companions or even parts of one sequence. Most critics prefer to treat them as separate, while acknowledging the similar themes.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. fǽhþ; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sige-þeód; Wikipedia, 'The Husband's Message' (summary of manuscript and scholarship); Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations)

  10. The cuckoo in the grove ll. 21-23

    Geac is 'cuckoo'. In The Seafarer the cuckoo's sad voice also announces summer and stirs the desire to travel by sea (geac monað geomran reorde). Here it signals the safe season for the voyage. Galan geomorne, 'sing sadly', and on hliþes oran, 'on the edge of the slope', set the scene. Lagu drefde, 'stirred the sea', a phrase for rowing or sailing, uses lagu, a poetic word for water. Critics have discussed whether the cuckoo is sad because of the parting it recalls or simply by tradition.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. geác; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  11. sea kennings: 'the gull's homeland', 'sea-ship' ll. 26-28

    Mæw is 'sea-mew, gull', and eþel 'homeland', so mæwes eþel is a kenning for the sea. Sænaca, 'sea-vessel', and merelad, 'sea-way', add compounds of the same type. The commands (Ongin, onsite) are imperatives addressed to the woman; their urgency matches that of the message. Suð heonan, 'south from here', gives the only geographical detail; it suggests the lord now lives in a southern land, but nothing more precise can be said.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. mǽw; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sǽ-naca; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  12. 'let no living man hinder you' ll. 24-25

    Getwæfan, 'separate, hinder', takes the genitive siþes 'of the journey', and gelettan 'hinder' takes lade 'way, course'. Lifgendne monn 'a living man' is the accusative subject of the infinitive after læt. The warning implies that others may wish to stop her, perhaps her kin; this has suggested that, as in The Wife's Lament, there is opposition to the union. The negative imperative Ne læt þu, 'do not let', turns the message from memory to instruction, the main business of the poem's central section.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. lád; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  13. 'if Almighty God grants' ll. 30-34

    Alwaldend, 'ruler of all', is a standard epithet for God. Inc is the dual 'to you two'; geunne is subjunctive of geunnan 'grant'. The damaged lines (33-34) seem to describe the couple distributing treasure to retainers (secgum ond gesiþum), the ideal of a heroic lord and lady. The mention of God sits easily with the secular theme, though allegorists read the whole poem as Christ's message to the soul or the Church.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-síþ; R. E. Kaske, 'A Poem of the Cross in the Exeter Book: Riddle 60 and The Husband's Message', Traditio 23 (1967); R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  14. studded rings and plated gold ll. 35-36

    Næglede, 'nailed, studded', describes rings set with studs or rivets; beagas 'rings' were the currency of lordly gift-giving. Fæted gold (here fædan) means 'plated, ornamented gold'. He genoh hafað, 'he has enough', is a simple statement that has persuaded many readers that the poem is optimistic, distinct from the darker Wife's Lament. Anne Klinck counts it among the elegies but in a hopeful mode.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-nægled; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. fæted; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Husband's Message' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  15. a homeland among foreign people ll. 37-38

    Elþeod means 'foreign people, strangers', and eþel 'native land, homeland'. The paradox, a homeland among strangers, describes the exile's successful resettlement. Fægre foldan, 'a fair country', suggests a pleasant land. The damage leaves gaps around these lines, but the sense of prosperity in exile is clear. Some editors restore words in the gaps from the alliterative pattern, but any reconstruction should be treated as conjecture; students should consult the facsimile or Krapp and Dobbie's notes for the lacunae.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes

  16. 'compelled by need he pushed out his boat' ll. 39-43

    Nyde gebæded, 'compelled by necessity', recalls the necessity of exile. Nacan ut aþrong, 'pushed out the boat', and on yþa geong, 'on the course of the waves', use poetic diction. Flotweg, 'floating-way', is a kenning for the sea, and mengan merestreamas, 'to stir the sea-currents', describes rowing. Forðsiþes georn, 'eager for the going forth', may mean eager to leave or eager to escape. The recollection of his departure prepares the contrast with his present wealth (Nu se mon hafað, l. 43).

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. mere

  17. 'neither horses nor treasures nor mead-joys' ll. 43-47

    The negative list (ne ... ne ... ne) is bound by m-alliteration: meara 'horses', maðma 'treasures', meododreama 'mead-joys'. Gad, 'lack', in nis him wilna gad, 'he has no lack of joys', is followed by genitives. Eorlgestreona, 'noble treasures', completes the list. The condition gif he þin beneah, 'if he may have you' (beneah, from benugan 'to have, enjoy'), makes the woman the one thing missing. Þeodnes dohtor, 'prince's daughter', reveals her rank. Ofer eald gebeot, 'in accordance with the old vow', returns to the vow motif.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Husband's Message' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  18. The runes: an oath in code ll. 49-50

    The rune-names are sigel 'sun', rad 'riding, road', ear 'earth' or 'sea', wynn 'joy' and mon 'man'. Most editions read the last as M, while this text prints the D-rune (dæg 'day'). Gecyre ic, 'I join, put together', is itself uncertain. R. F. Leslie proposed an oath by heaven, earth and the man himself; others read sigelrad 'sun-road' (sky) or seglrad 'sail-road' (sea), earwynn 'joy of earth' or 'sea-joy', and mon. Karl Schneider (1956) proposed personal names, and P. L. Henry (1966) an Irish-style oath by sky, earth, sun and moon. John Niles (ASE 32, 2003) argued that some signs are to be read as runes and others as insular letters, a deliberate trick for the reader. Aþe benemnan, 'declare with an oath', confirms the sense of a vow.

    John D. Niles, 'The Trick of the Runes in The Husband's Message', Anglo-Saxon England 32 (2003); R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Wikipedia, 'The Husband's Message' (summary of manuscript and scholarship); G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes

  19. wær and winetreow: the covenant ll. 51-52

    Wær 'covenant, pledge, agreement' is a legal and religious term (God's covenant with Noah is a wær). Winetreow, 'faith between friends', is glossed by Bosworth-Toller with the note 'between husband and wife'. Be him lifgendum, 'with him living, while he lived', is an absolute construction. Læstan, 'perform, fulfil', is the verb used for keeping vows. The language of covenant, faith and oath gives the secular love-message a quasi-legal solemnity, and allegorical readers have taken it as the language of God's covenant with the soul.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wǽr; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wine-treów; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  20. The closing echo l. 53

    The doubled n of gespræconn (compare gespræcon at l. 16) is a scribal variant, kept by this text. The repetition closes the message by returning to the vows it began with. Such ring composition is common in Old English verse. Critics have used the ending to argue for the poem's optimism: the stave has delivered its message and its promise, and the woman's response is left to the reader.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary
