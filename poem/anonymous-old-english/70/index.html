Death & Dying

Deor (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Death & Dying

Welund him be wurman wræces cunnade,
anhydig eorl earfoþa dreag,
hæfde him to gesiþþe sorge ond longaþ,
wintercealde wræce; wean oft onfond,
siþþan hine Niðhad on nede legde,
swoncre seonobende on syllan monn.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!
Beadohilde ne wæs hyre broþra deaþ
on sefan swa sar swa hyre sylfre þing,
þæt heo gearolice ongieten hæfde
þæt heo eacen wæs; æfre ne meahte
þriste geþencan, hu ymb þæt sceolde.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!
We þæt Mæðhilde monge gefrugnon
wurdon grundlease Geates frige,
þæt hi seo sorglufu slæp ealle binom.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!
ðeodric ahte þritig wintra
Mæringa burg; þæt wæs monegum cuþ.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!
We geascodan Eormanrices
wylfenne geþoht; ahte wide folc
Gotena rices. þæt wæs grim cyning.
Sæt secg monig sorgum gebunden,
wean on wenan, wyscte geneahhe
þæt þæs cynerices ofercumen wære.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!
Siteð sorgcearig, sælum bidæled,
on sefan sweorceð, sylfum þinceð
þæt sy endeleas earfoða dæl.
Mæg þonne geþencan, þæt geond þas woruld
witig dryhten wendeþ geneahhe,
eorle monegum are gesceawað,
wislicne blæd, sumum weana dæl.
þæt ic bi me sylfum secgan wille,
þæt ic hwile wæs Heodeninga scop,
dryhtne dyre. Me wæs Deor noma.
Ahte ic fela wintra folgað tilne,
holdne hlaford, oþþæt Heorrenda nu,
leoðcræftig monn londryht geþah,
þæt me eorla hleo ær gesealde.
þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg!

Codex Note

Study notes by the editors

  1. Deor: the poem, its manuscript and its plan ll. 1-42

    Deor survives only in the Exeter Book (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501, copied c. 960-990), on fols 100r-100v; the title is modern. The scribe marks its sections with capitals, and editors print six stanzas of unequal length, each closed by the refrain (it recurs six times). Lines 1-27 recall figures of Germanic legend (Weland, Beadohild, Mæðhild and Geat, Theodric, Eormanric); lines 28-34 turn to a general sufferer and God's ordering of fortune; lines 35-42 reveal the speaker, a court poet who has lost his place. Critics have called it a consolation, a begging poem and even a charm (Bloomfield, PMLA 1964). Its date is unknown, and Richard North has argued for a ninth-century satirical occasion. Jennifer Lorden (2021) argues that the legendary stanzas follow the career of Widia, Weland's son, rather than being a random list.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Deor' (summary of manuscript and scholarship); Jennifer Lorden, 'Revising the Legendary History of Deor', Medium Ævum 90 (2021), 197-216

  2. be wurman: 'among serpents'? the first crux l. 1

    Wurman looks like a form of wyrm 'serpent, dragon, worm', but the case and sense are unclear. Some editors emend to wurma or keep wurman as a plural and translate 'among serpents', perhaps a snake pit like that of Gunnarr in Norse legend. Kemp Malone kept the manuscript form and took it as a figure for a sword with serpentine pattern-welding, the sort of blade Weland forged. Grein proposed wimman/wifman, 'by a woman', pointing ahead to Beadohild; Rieger and Sedgefield proposed be wornum or be wearnum, 'in abundance', and Klaeber be wynnum, 'by joy'. Bosworth-Toller also records a separate noun wurma, a shellfish or purple dye, which has not won favour here. Translators usually print a sense such as 'among the snakes' with a note.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wyrm; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wurma; Heorot.dk, Deor: notes to the text (survey of editors' readings); Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  3. wræc, cunnian: 'he experienced misery' l. 1

    Wræc (neuter) covers suffering, punishment and banishment; its family includes wrecca 'exile, wretch' and wræcsið 'exile-journey', words central to the elegies. Here it stands in the genitive (wræces) as the object of cunnian 'to try, test, get to know', so the half-line means Weland came to know affliction. The noun returns in l. 4, wintercealde wræce, 'winter-cold misery', a compound that fuses the season with exile as in The Wanderer and The Seafarer. Alliteration in l. 1 binds Welund and wurman on w, with wræces carrying the stress in the b-verse; the poem opens on the sound that also begins the word for woe (wea, l. 4).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wræc; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  4. anhydig eorl: 'the single-minded man' l. 2

    Anhydig is built from an 'one' and hydig 'minded' (from hyge 'mind, thought'), and Bosworth-Toller glosses it 'single-minded, steadfast, firm, stubborn'. The phrase anhydig eorl is also cited by Bosworth-Toller from Exodus and Azarias, so it belongs to the stock of heroic epithets. Applied to Weland, it implies the endurance that will issue in his revenge. Eorl, originally 'nobleman', in verse simply means 'warrior, man', and lifts the craftsman into the heroic register. The b-verse earfoþa dreag, 'endured hardships', uses dreogan 'to suffer, endure', a verb that recurs throughout the elegies (compare The Wife's Lament, fæhðu dreogan).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. án-hydig; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes

  5. sorrow as Weland's companion l. 3

    Gesiþþ (dative gesiþþe) means 'company, companionship', from the same root as gesiþ, the retainer who follows a lord. The line inverts the warrior's ideal: in place of a comitatus, Weland's only companions are sorge ond longaþ, 'sorrow and longing'. Longaþ is the noun of yearning that recurs in The Wife's Lament and The Seafarer. The personification is a recurring elegiac move, also seen in The Wanderer, where cares are the exile's companions. Note also the syntax of the stanza: a series of verbs (cunnade, dreag, hæfde, onfond) piles up experience before the cause is given in ll. 5-6.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-síþ; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. langoþ; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  6. swoncre seonobende: the hamstringing of Weland ll. 5-6

    Seonobend is a compound of seonu 'sinew' and bend 'bond'; Bosworth-Toller glosses it 'a bond made of sinews', and this is its only recorded use. Swoncor means 'pliant, supple, slender'. The phrase is usually taken as a grim kenning for hamstringing: in the Old Norse Völundarkviða King Níðuðr has the smith's sinews cut and keeps him on an island forging for him. On nede legde, 'laid in constraint', describes the captivity. The verb legde governs both the abstract (nede) and the physical (seonobende), so the half-lines echo one another. Weland's revenge, killing the king's sons and violating his daughter, follows in the next stanza.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. seonu-bend; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. swancor; Völundarkviða (Poetic Edda), the Old Norse lay of Völundr, Níðuðr and Böðvildr; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes

  7. syllan monn: 'the better man' l. 6

    Sel has no positive form in Old English; the comparative is selra or sella, here in the weak accusative singular syllan (with y for e). On syllan monn therefore means 'on the better man', and the comparison is implicitly with Niðhad himself. Many critics read the phrase as ironic sympathy: the king's power does not make him the better man. Others take it as a straightforward heroic compliment to Weland. Either way the stanza's moral weight sits on Weland's side, even though the legend makes him a terrible avenger in the next stanza.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sél; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  8. The refrain: 'That passed away; so may this' ll. 7, 13, 17, 20, 27, 42

    Ofergan means 'to pass over, go by'; here it is used impersonally with the genitive: þæs ofereode, 'with respect to that, it passed over'. Þisses (genitive of þes 'this') is the present trouble, and the verb ofergan must be understood after mæg: 'so it can with this'. The refrain comes after each legendary episode and again after the poet's own story, so his loss is placed in the same series as Weland's. Its exact force is disputed. It may be simple consolation (misfortune ends), stoic resignation, or, for Morton Bloomfield, a formula with the cadence of a charm against misfortune. Refrains are almost unknown in Old English poetry; the closest analogue is the 'Ungelic is us' of Wulf and Eadwacer, the next poem in the manuscript.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ofer-gán; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Deor' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  9. Beadohild and her brothers' death ll. 8-12

    In the Norse Völundarkviða the captive smith lures the king's two sons to his smithy, kills them and makes their skulls into cups for the king, then makes Böðvildr (Beadohild) drunk and rapes her before flying away. The Old English poet presents her suffering from the inside: her brothers' death was not as sore on sefan, 'in her mind', as her own situation (hyre sylfre þing). The poem touches the story without retelling it, assuming its audience knows it. The Franks Casket (front panel, eighth century) shows Weland at his forge with the princess, which confirms that the legend was known in England. Her son by Weland was the hero Widia (Wudga), known from Waldere and Widsith.

    Völundarkviða (Poetic Edda), the Old Norse lay of Völundr, Níðuðr and Böðvildr; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Jennifer Lorden, 'Revising the Legendary History of Deor', Medium Ævum 90 (2021), 197-216; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  10. eacen: 'increased', that is, pregnant l. 11

    Bosworth-Toller gives eacen as 'increased, great, vast, powerful' with the Latin gravidus; the compound bearn-eacen, 'increased with a child', makes the sense explicit. Gearolice ongieten hæfde, 'had clearly perceived', marks the moment of realisation. In ll. 11-12 Beadohild could never 'boldly think how it must turn out' (hu ymb þæt sceolde): the poet stresses the anxiety of not knowing. In the legend the outcome was good, the birth of Widia, which gives the refrain its point here: þæs ofereode.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. eácen; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. bearn-eácen; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes

  11. Mæðhild and Geat: the obscure third stanza ll. 14-16

    Most editors read two names: Mæðhild, a woman, and Geat, whose frige 'loves, affections' became grundlease, 'bottomless, without limit', so that sorglufu 'sorrowful love' took away all her sleep. Malone connected the story with Scandinavian ballads about Gauti and Magnhild, in which a woman foresees her drowning. Others divide differently: Grein read mæþ Hilde, 'the violation of Hild', and Holthausen mæþel Hilde, 'the affair of Hild'. Monge has been taken as 'many' (we have heard from many) or as a noun 'intercourse'. The manuscript's hi in l. 16 has also been emended. The stanza shows how much of the legendary world the poet could assume and we have lost.

    Heorot.dk, Deor: notes to the text (survey of editors' readings); Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. frig; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  12. sorglufu: 'sorrowful love' l. 16

    Old English poetry readily makes compounds that join two opposed ideas, and sorglufu is one of the most expressive: love that is itself a grief. Slæp ealle binom, 'took away all sleep', uses beniman 'deprive' with the accusative of the person and the thing. Sleeplessness from love-sorrow is a motif shared with the women's laments, The Wife's Lament and Wulf and Eadwacer, which set female grief in a landscape of waiting. Grundlease (l. 15), 'groundless, bottomless', gives the love the image of an abyss.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sorh-lufu; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  13. Theodric and the Mæringas ll. 18-19

    Theodoric ruled Italy from Ravenna from 493 to 526, roughly thirty years, and Germanic legend made him Dietrich von Bern. The Swedish Rök runestone (ninth century) also names Þjóðríkr as lord of the Mærings (skati Mæringa), which supports reading the Mæringas as his Goths. Whether the thirty winters were a misfortune for Theodric himself (some think of the long exile of Dietrich in German legend) or for those he ruled is unclear; in Christian tradition the historical Theodoric, who imprisoned and executed Boethius, was remembered as a tyrant. Þæt wæs monegum cuþ, 'that was known to many', is a typical oral appeal to common knowledge.

    Wikipedia, 'Rök runestone' (text and translation of the Theodoric strophe); Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Wikipedia, 'Deor' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  14. Eormanric's 'wolfish thought' ll. 21-23

    The historical Ermanaric ruled a Gothic realm north of the Black Sea and died c. 375 when the Huns attacked; Ammianus Marcellinus and Jordanes's Getica (where he is Hermanaricus) record him. In legend he becomes a murderer of kin, and Jordanes tells of his savage killing of Sunilda. Beowulf (ll. 1197-1201) mentions the 'treacherous hostility' of Eormanric, and Widsith lists him. Wylfen, 'wolfish, fierce', is used figuratively; Bosworth-Toller cites this line. We geascodan, 'we have heard tell', like gefrugnon in l. 14, signals traditional knowledge. Ahte wide folc / Gotena rices: he ruled the peoples of the Gothic kingdom far and wide.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wylfen; Jordanes, Getica (Latin text), chapters on Hermanaricus; Widsith (Exeter Book, ASPR 3), the catalogue of heroic kings; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes

  15. The tyrant's subjects: 'many a man sat bound in sorrows' ll. 24-26

    Sorgum gebunden, 'bound with sorrows', is elegiac idiom: in The Wanderer sorrow and sleep together often 'bind' the wretched solitary (gebindað). Wean on wenan, 'in expectation of woe', and wyscte geneahhe, 'wished often', describe the passivity of subjects who can only hope. Cynerice, 'royal realm, kingdom', is in the genitive with ofercumen wære, 'that it might be overcome' (subjunctive), an idiom of 'overcoming with respect to the kingdom'. Here the refrain applies to the end of a tyranny. The image of the man sitting in grief also prepares for the next section, where a nameless sufferer sits (Siteð, l. 28) and the poem turns from history to reflection.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. cyne-ríce; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-neahhe; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  16. The general sufferer and God's ordering of fortune ll. 28-34

    Sorgcearig, 'anxious with sorrow', and sælum bidæled, 'deprived of happiness' (sæl 'good fortune'), describe a man for whom his share of hardship seems endless (endeleas). He may then reflect (Mæg þonne geþencan) that witig dryhten, 'the wise Lord', changes things through the world, giving are 'honour, favour' and wislicne blæd 'certain glory' to many and a share of woes to some. This is the poem's only explicitly Christian passage. Murray Markland (Modern Philology, 1968) and W. F. Bolton (1972) linked it with the Old English Boethius and its teaching on Fortune, though others treat it as an independent gnomic commonplace. The passage turns the legendary examples into a general rule before the poet speaks of himself.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sorh-cearig; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sǽl; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. dryhten; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'Deor' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)

  17. 'I was for a time the poet of the Heodenings' l. 36

    A scop was a court poet who composed and performed at a lord's hall; the word is related to scieppan 'to shape, create'. The Heodenings are the people of Heoden (Norse Heðinn, Middle High German Hetele), whose abduction of Hild, daughter of Hagena, produced the endless battle of the Hjaðningar in Snorri's Skáldskaparmál; Widsith names Heoden among the kings. By attaching himself to this legendary court the speaker becomes himself a figure of story, alongside Weland and Theodric. Hwile, 'for a while', is pointed: the position was not permanent. Dryhtne dyre, 'dear to my lord', expresses the bond between lord and retainer.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. scop; Heorot.dk, Deor: notes to the text (survey of editors' readings); Widsith (Exeter Book, ASPR 3), the catalogue of heroic kings; Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes

  18. 'Deor was my name' l. 37

    Deor as a common noun means 'beast, wild animal' (modern 'deer' has narrowed the sense), and as an adjective deor means 'brave'. Several editors note a play with dyre, 'dear', in the same line, so the poet who was dear to his lord bears a name that sounds like it. Me wæs ... noma, 'to me was the name', is a common idiom for naming. Whether Deor is a real person or a persona invented for the poem is unknown; most critics treat him as a fictional speaker who gives the legendary stanzas a personal frame.

    Heorot.dk, Deor: notes to the text (survey of editors' readings); G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  19. Heorrenda, the rival poet l. 39

    In the Middle High German Kudrun, Horant is a singer whose song enchants listeners and helps win Hild for Hetel; Snorri names a Hjarrandi as Heðinn's father. The poet's choice of rival thus belongs to the same story-complex as the Heodenings. Leoðcræftig monn, 'a man skilled in song', concedes his rival's art without bitterness. The adverb nu, 'now', brings the legendary past into the poet's own present, and the closing refrain extends the consolation of history to his own loss.

    Heorot.dk, Deor: notes to the text (survey of editors' readings); Kemp Malone, ed., Deor (London: Methuen, 1933; later revised edns), introduction and notes; Jennifer Lorden, 'Revising the Legendary History of Deor', Medium Ævum 90 (2021), 197-216

  20. londryht and eorla hleo: the lost estate ll. 38-41

    Londryht means 'right to land, landed estate', and Bosworth-Toller cites this line; geþicgan 'to receive, accept' takes the accusative here. Folgað tilne, 'a good office/service', and holdne hlaford, 'a gracious lord', are what Deor had for fela wintra, 'many years'. Eorla hleo, 'shelter of warriors', is a kenning for a lord found also in Beowulf; hleo is 'shelter, protection'. The lines describe a concrete economic loss, which is why some critics read the poem as a begging poem or a poet's petition. The final refrain applies the poem's consolation to this loss.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. land-riht; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. hleów; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. folgoþ; Wikipedia, 'Deor' (summary of manuscript and scholarship)
