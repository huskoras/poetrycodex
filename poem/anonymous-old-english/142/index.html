Time & Brevity

The Ruin (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Time & Brevity

Wrætlic is þes wealstan, wyrde gebræcon;
burgstede burston, brosnað enta geweorc.
Hrofas sind gehrorene, hreorge torras,
hrungeat berofen, hrim on lime,
scearde scurbeorge scorene, gedrorene,
ældo undereotone. Eorðgrap hafað
waldend wyrhtan forweorone, geleorene,
heardgripe hrusan, oþ hund cnea
werþeoda gewitan. Oft þæs wag gebad
ræghar ond readfah rice æfter oþrum,
ofstonden under stormum; steap geap gedreas.
Wonað giet se ...num geheapen,
fel on
grimme gegrunden
scan heo...
...g orþonc ærsceaft
...g lamrindum beag
mod mo... ...yne swiftne gebrægd
hwætred in hringas, hygerof gebond
weallwalan wirum wundrum togædre.
Beorht wæron burgræced, burnsele monige,
heah horngestreon, heresweg micel,
meodoheall monig ᛗ dreama full,
oþþæt þæt onwende wyrd seo swiþe.
Crungon walo wide, cwoman woldagas,
swylt eall fornom secgrofra wera;
wurdon hyra wigsteal westen staþolas,
brosnade burgsteall. Betend crungon
hergas to hrusan. Forþon þas hofu dreorgiað,
ond þæs teaforgeapa tigelum sceadeð
hrostbeages hrof. Hryre wong gecrong
gebrocen to beorgum, þær iu beorn monig
glædmod ond goldbeorht gleoma gefrætwed,
wlonc ond wingal wighyrstum scan;
seah on sinc, on sylfor, on searogimmas,
on ead, on æht, on eorcanstan,
on þas beorhtan burg bradan rices.
Stanhofu stodan, stream hate wearp
widan wylme; weal eall befeng
beorhtan bosme, þær þa baþu wæron,
hat on hreþre. þæt wæs hyðelic.
Leton þonne geotan
ofer harne stan hate streamas
un...
...þþæt hringmere hate
þær þa baþu wæron.
þonne is
...re; þæt is cynelic þing,
huse ...... burg....

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Old English text as in ASPR; hand-keyed e-text via sacred-texts.com.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening: Wrætlic is þes wealstan l. 1

    The Ruin is copied in the Exeter Book (fols 123b-124b), between The Husband's Message and the second group of riddles. A diagonal burn has destroyed much of the text, so many lines are fragmentary. 'Wrætlic' ('wondrous, curiously made') is a word often used in the Riddles. 'Wealstan' ('wall-stone') is perhaps a single masonry block or the wall. 'Wyrde gebræcon' ('the fates broke it'). The poem was probably composed in the eighth or ninth century; it is one of the few OE poems describing a specific place.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Wikipedia, 'The Ruin' (manuscript, Bath and other identifications); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wrǣtlic'

  2. brosnað enta geweorc l. 2

    'Brosnian' ('to crumble, decay') recurs at l. 28. 'Enta geweorc' ('work of giants') is the traditional formula for Roman or ancient stone construction (see The Wanderer l. 87; Maxims II ll. 1-2; Beowulf l. 2717). 'Burgstede burston' ('the city-places burst apart') is the parallel. The fragmentary l. 16 contains 'orþonc' ('skill, cunning'), which also appears in Maxims II ('orðanc enta geweorc').

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942); R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'brosnian'

  3. Hrofas sind gehrorene: sound and rhyme ll. 3-5

    'Hrof' is 'roof'; 'gehrorene' is the past participle of 'hreosan' ('to fall'). 'Hreorge torras' ('ruinous towers'), from Latin 'turris'. 'Scearde scurbeorge scorene, gedrorene' (l. 5) has rhyming participles ('scorene ... gedrorene'), and the poem often uses internal rhyme ('forweorone, geleorene', l. 7). The rhyme recalls the Exeter Book's Riming Poem. Sound-patterning of this kind, rare elsewhere in Old English verse, is one reason critics stress the poem's craftsmanship.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hrōf'

  4. hrungeat berofen, hrim on lime l. 4

    'Hrungeat' ('rung-gate', perhaps 'barred gate' or 'gate with crossbars') occurs only here; 'berofen' is 'despoiled, stripped' (berēafian). 'Hrim on lime' ('frost on the mortar'): 'lim' is 'lime, mortar', the Roman building material that impressed the Anglo-Saxons. Frost, which in The Wanderer and The Seafarer afflicts the exile, here settles on stone: the ruin itself becomes the sufferer. The line has double alliteration on hr, carried over from l. 3 ('Hrofas ... hreorge').

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hrung-geat'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'līm'

  5. scurbeorge l. 5

    'Scurbeorg' (scur 'shower, storm' + beorg 'protection') occurs only here, usually taken as 'roof'. 'Scearde' is 'gashed, notched'; 'scorene' is 'shorn, cut'; 'gedrorene' is 'fallen'. 'Ældo undereotone' ('undermined by age'). The accumulation of participles suggests the slow work of time. The roof that once sheltered from storms is now open to them, an irony repeated in 'ofstonden under stormum' (l. 11). The run of passive participles also leaves the agents unnamed: no destroyer is seen, only the fact of ruin. Much of the poem's force comes from such accumulations of descriptive compounds.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'scūr-beorg'

  6. Eorðgrap hafað waldend wyrhtan ll. 6-9

    'Eorðgrap' ('earth-grasp') and 'heardgripe hrusan' ('the hard grip of the earth') personify the earth as holding the builders in death. 'Waldend wyrhtan' ('the ruling builders') are the city's makers. 'Forweorone, geleorene' ('decayed, departed') rhyme. 'Oþ hund cnea werþeoda gewitan' ('until a hundred generations of men have passed') shows the great age of the ruin. Some scholars think this may refer to the long span since the Romans.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'eorþ-grāp'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'cnēo'

  7. ræghar ond readfah l. 10

    'Ræghar' ('grey with lichen', 'ræg' = lichen) and 'readfah' ('red-stained') describe the wall's colour. The red has been explained as iron oxide, red sandstone or Roman tiles. 'Rice æfter oþrum' ('one kingdom after another') stresses longevity. 'Steap geap gedreas' ('the high, curved one fell') is often taken as an arch or gable. The standard editions give the readings of the compounds.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'rǣg-hār'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'rēad-fāh'

  8. The burned lines (ll. 12-18) ll. 12-18

    Lines 12-18 are badly damaged by the burn in the Exeter Book. The archive text marks the gaps with dots. Some words can be made out: 'geheapen' ('heaped up'), 'grimme gegrunden' ('cruelly ground'), 'orþonc ærsceaft' ('skilful ancient work'), 'lamrindum beag' ('bent with crusts of clay'). Editors (ASPR, Leslie, Klinck, Muir) differ in their readings and supplements. The ellipses mean that any interpretation should be cautious.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; B. J. Muir, ed., The Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry, 2nd edn (Exeter, 2000), text and commentary

  9. weallwalan wirum wundrum togædre ll. 18-20

    'Hwætred' (perhaps 'quick in counsel'), 'hygerof' ('brave in mind') describe the builder. 'Hringas' ('rings') and 'wirum' ('with wires') suggest the iron clamps and ties used by Roman masons. 'Weallwalan' ('wall-foundations' or 'wall-ridges') occurs only here. The wonder at technical skill is typical of Anglo-Saxon responses to Roman building. Roman masonry with iron cramps set in lead is known from many British sites, and readers have linked the line with such technique, though the damaged context makes the detail uncertain. The 'rings' may also be circular courses or bands of stone.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'weall-wala'

  10. burgræced, burnsele, horngestreon ll. 21-22

    'Burgræced' ('city-buildings'); 'burnsele' ('bath-halls', from 'burna', 'stream, spring'); 'horngestreon' ('wealth of gables' or 'pinnacles', 'horn' meaning gable). 'Heresweg micel' ('great sound of warriors'). The bath-halls support the identification of the city with Bath. The passage pictures the city in its bright days, a contrast with the present. The plural 'monige' and 'monig' give a sense of a large settlement, which suits a Roman town rather than a single villa. The bright past ('Beorht wæron') is set in the preterite, against the largely present-tense description of ll. 1-11, a structure of then and now that runs through the whole poem.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'burn-sele'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'horn-gestrēon'

  11. monig ᛗ dreama full: the 'man' rune l. 23

    The Exeter Book scribe writes the rune ᛗ, whose name in the Old English Rune Poem is 'man' ('mon'). Editors read 'meodoheall monig mon-dreama full' ('many a mead-hall full of the joys of men'). 'Mondream' ('human joy') is a known compound. Runes used as word-signs also appear in the Exeter Riddles and in Cynewulf's signatures. 'Oþþæt þæt onwende wyrd seo swiþe' ('until mighty fate changed that').

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; R. I. Page, An Introduction to English Runes, 2nd edn (Woodbridge, 1999)

  12. Crungon walo wide, cwoman woldagas ll. 24-26

    'Wol' ('pestilence, plague') + 'dagas' ('days'): 'days of pestilence'. 'Walo' is 'slain men'. 'Swylt' is 'death'. The poem suggests that plague, not battle, emptied the city. 'Secgrofra wera' ('of the sword-brave men'). 'Wigsteal' ('war-place' or 'fortifications') became 'westen staþolas' ('waste foundations'). Some readers link the 'days of pestilence' with the plagues recorded in Britain, for example in the 660s, but the poet names no event and the reference may be general. Note the alliteration of l. 25 on c and w ('Crungon walo wide, cwoman woldagas').

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wōl'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wōl-dæg'

  13. Betend crungon hergas to hrusan ll. 27-28

    'Betend' ('repairers, restorers', from 'betan', 'to improve, repair') fell with the armies. 'Hergas' is 'hosts'. 'Forþon þas hofu dreorgiað' ('therefore these buildings become desolate'), 'dreorgian' ('to become dreary'). Without people to repair the city, nature takes over. The causal 'forþon' is clear here, unlike in The Seafarer. The word 'hofu' ('dwellings, courts') is also used of temples. Critics have noted that the poem's sense of decay is impersonal: the buildings are not destroyed by God's judgement, as Babylon is in Revelation 18, but by death, time and neglect. The syntax of the lines is discussed in the standard editions.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'bētend'

  14. teaforgeapa tigelum sceadeð ll. 29-30

    'Teafor' is 'red pigment', 'vermilion'; 'geap' is 'curved, arched'. 'Teaforgeapa' (perhaps 'red-arched' or 'red-curved one') occurs only here. 'Tigelum sceadeð' ('separates from its tiles'); 'hrostbeages hrof' ('roof of the roof-arch' or 'of the ceiling'). Editors have debated whether a vault, a ceiling or a gable is meant, and how 'sceadeð' should be understood. 'Hryre wong gecrong' ('the place fell into ruin').

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'teafor'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hrōst-bēag'

  15. glædmod ond goldbeorht ... wlonc ond wingal ll. 32-34

    'Glædmod' ('glad-minded'), 'goldbeorht' ('gold-bright'), 'gleoma gefrætwed' ('adorned with splendours'). 'Wlonc ond wingal' ('proud and wine-flushed') is the same pair as in The Seafarer (l. 29), where it describes the complacent town-dweller. Here there is no moral criticism; the poet admires the vanished splendour. 'Wighyrstum scan' ('shone in war-trappings'). The comparison shows how a fixed alliterative pair can carry opposite values in different poems. In The Ruin the description is part of the poet's reconstruction of the city's life from its stones, an exercise in historical imagination.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wīn-gāl'

  16. on sinc, on sylfor ... on eorcanstan ll. 35-37

    The anaphora 'on ... on' enumerates the objects the warrior saw. 'Searogimm' ('cunningly worked gem'); 'ead' ('riches'); 'æht' ('possessions'); 'eorcanstan' ('precious stone'). 'Eorcan-' is cognate with Gothic 'airkns' ('holy, pure'). The list ends with 'þas beorhtan burg bradan rices' ('this bright city of a broad kingdom'): the city itself as the treasure. The heaped nouns echo the treasure lists of heroic poetry, such as the dragon's hoard in Beowulf; but here the city's former people, not a dragon, owned them.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'eorcnan-stān'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'eorcnanstān'

  17. The hot springs: Bath? ll. 38-45

    'Stanhofu stodan' ('stone buildings stood'), 'stream hate wearp widan wylme' ('the stream threw out heat in a wide surge'). The hot spring, the baths ('baþu') and the 'hringmere' ('circular pool', l. 45) led Heinrich Leo in 1865 to identify the city with Bath (Aquae Sulis), where Roman baths survive. That identification remains the most widely accepted. Others have proposed Chester, Hadrian's Wall, the Babylon of the Apocalypse, or no specific city.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Wikipedia, 'The Ruin' (manuscript, Bath and other identifications); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hring-mere'

  18. hringmere l. 45

    'Hringmere' ('ring-mere', a circular pool) occurs only here. Some have related it to the circular bath excavated at Bath. 'Leton þonne geotan ofer harne stan hate streamas' ('they let hot streams pour over grey stone'). The next lines are fragmentary because of the burn. Readers have compared it with the circular bath in the Roman bath complex at Bath, but this remains conjecture. The repeated 'þær þa baþu wæron' ('where the baths were', ll. 40, 46) frames the passage; The text from here to the end is very uncertain.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hring-mere'

  19. þæt is cynelic þing l. 48

    'Cynelic' ('royal, fitting for a king') expresses admiration for the city and its baths. The poem ends in fragments ('huse ... burg ...'). Unlike The Wanderer and The Seafarer, The Ruin offers no explicit Christian conclusion in the surviving text; critics discuss whether it is an elegy, an encomium urbis (a praise of cities) or a meditation on fate. Klinck groups it with the elegies but notes its distinct tone.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), The Ruin, notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; B. J. Muir, ed., The Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry, 2nd edn (Exeter, 2000), text and commentary
