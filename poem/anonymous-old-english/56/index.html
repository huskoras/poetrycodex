Death & Dying

The Wanderer (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Death & Dying

Oft him anhaga are gebideð,
metudes miltse, þeah þe he modcearig
geond lagulade longe sceolde
hreran mid hondum hrimcealde sæ,
wadan wræclastas. Wyrd bið ful ared!
Swa cwæð eardstapa, earfeþa gemyndig,
wraþra wælsleahta, winemæga hryre:
"Oft ic sceolde ana uhtna gehwylce
mine ceare cwiþan. Nis nu cwicra nan
þe ic him modsefan minne durre
sweotule asecgan. Ic to soþe wat
þæt biþ in eorle indryhten þeaw,
þæt he his ferðlocan fæste binde,
healde his hordcofan, hycge swa he wille.
Ne mæg werig mod wyrde wiðstondan,
ne se hreo hyge helpe gefremman.
Forðon domgeorne dreorigne oft
in hyra breostcofan bindað fæste;
swa ic modsefan minne sceolde,
oft earmcearig, eðle bidæled,
freomægum feor feterum sælan,
siþþan geara iu goldwine minne
hrusan heolstre biwrah, ond ic hean þonan
wod wintercearig ofer waþema gebind,
sohte sele dreorig sinces bryttan,
hwær ic feor oþþe neah findan meahte
þone þe in meoduhealle min mine wisse,
oþþe mec freondleasne frefran wolde,
weman mid wynnum. Wat se þe cunnað,
hu sliþen bið sorg to geferan,
þam þe him lyt hafað leofra geholena.
Warað hine wræclast, nales wunden gold,
ferðloca freorig, nalæs foldan blæd.
Gemon he selesecgas ond sincþege,
hu hine on geoguðe his goldwine
wenede to wiste. Wyn eal gedreas!
Forþon wat se þe sceal his winedryhtnes
leofes larcwidum longe forþolian,
ðonne sorg ond slæp somod ætgædre
earmne anhogan oft gebindað.
þinceð him on mode þæt he his mondryhten
clyppe ond cysse, ond on cneo lecge
honda ond heafod, swa he hwilum ær
in geardagum giefstolas breac.
ðonne onwæcneð eft wineleas guma,
gesihð him biforan fealwe wegas,
baþian brimfuglas, brædan feþra,
hreosan hrim ond snaw, hagle gemenged.
þonne beoð þy hefigran heortan benne,
sare æfter swæsne. Sorg bið geniwad,
þonne maga gemynd mod geondhweorfeð;
greteð gliwstafum, georne geondsceawað
secga geseldan. Swimmað eft on weg!
Fleotendra ferð no þær fela bringeð
cuðra cwidegiedda. Cearo bið geniwad
þam þe sendan sceal swiþe geneahhe
ofer waþema gebind werigne sefan.
Forþon ic geþencan ne mæg geond þas woruld
for hwan modsefa min ne gesweorce,
þonne ic eorla lif eal geondþence,
hu hi færlice flet ofgeafon,
modge maguþegnas. Swa þes middangeard
ealra dogra gehwam dreoseð ond fealleþ,
forþon ne mæg weorþan wis wer, ær he age
wintra dæl in woruldrice. Wita sceal geþyldig,
ne sceal no to hatheort ne to hrædwyrde,
ne to wac wiga ne to wanhydig,
ne to forht ne to fægen, ne to feohgifre
ne næfre gielpes to georn, ær he geare cunne.
Beorn sceal gebidan, þonne he beot spriceð,
oþþæt collenferð cunne gearwe
hwider hreþra gehygd hweorfan wille.
Ongietan sceal gleaw hæle hu gæstlic bið,
þonne ealre þisse worulde wela weste stondeð,
swa nu missenlice geond þisne middangeard
winde biwaune weallas stondaþ,
hrime bihrorene, hryðge þa ederas.
Woriað þa winsalo, waldend licgað
dreame bidrorene, duguþ eal gecrong,
wlonc bi wealle. Sume wig fornom,
ferede in forðwege, sumne fugel oþbær
ofer heanne holm, sumne se hara wulf
deaðe gedælde, sumne dreorighleor
in eorðscræfe eorl gehydde.
Yþde swa þisne eardgeard ælda scyppend
oþþæt burgwara breahtma lease
eald enta geweorc idlu stodon.
Se þonne þisne wealsteal wise geþohte
ond þis deorce lif deope geondþenceð,
frod in ferðe, feor oft gemon
wælsleahta worn, ond þas word acwið:
"Hwær cwom mearg? Hwær cwom mago? Hwær cwom maþþumgyfa?
Hwær cwom symbla gesetu? Hwær sindon seledreamas?
Eala beorht bune! Eala byrnwiga!
Eala þeodnes þrym! Hu seo þrag gewat,
genap under nihthelm, swa heo no wære.
Stondeð nu on laste leofre duguþe
weal wundrum heah, wyrmlicum fah.
Eorlas fornoman asca þryþe,
wæpen wælgifru, wyrd seo mære,
ond þas stanhleoþu stormas cnyssað,
hrið hreosende hrusan bindeð,
wintres woma, þonne won cymeð,
nipeð nihtscua, norþan onsendeð
hreo hæglfare hæleþum on andan.
Eall is earfoðlic eorþan rice,
onwendeð wyrda gesceaft weoruld under heofonum.
Her bið feoh læne, her bið freond læne,
her bið mon læne, her bið mæg læne,
eal þis eorþan gesteal idel weorþeð!"
Swa cwæð snottor on mode, gesæt him sundor æt rune.
Til biþ se þe his treowe gehealdeþ, ne sceal næfre his torn to rycene
beorn of his breostum acyþan, nemþe he ær þa bote cunne,
eorl mid elne gefremman. Wel bið þam þe him are seceð,
frofre to fæder on heofonum, þær us eal seo fæstnung stondeð.

Codex Note

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening: 'anhaga' and the poem l. 1

    The Wanderer, 115 lines, is copied in the Exeter Book (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501, fols 76b-78a). The book was written c. 965-75 and given to Exeter by Bishop Leofric (d. 1072). The title is modern, from the first editions in the nineteenth century (Benjamin Thorpe printed the Exeter Book in 1842). 'Anhaga' (an 'one' + haga, related to hogian 'to think' or haga 'enclosure') means 'one who dwells or thinks alone'. Bosworth-Toller gives 'a recluse, solitarius'. The variant 'anhogan' returns at l. 40, so the word frames the first movement. In heroic society a man without lord and kin had no protection, no place in the hall and no one to speak for him. The poem turns that social loss into a meditation on the transience of the whole world. Most critics call it an 'elegy', but the label is modern and debated (Klinck).

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'anhaga'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'ān-haga'; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Wikipedia, 'Exeter Book' (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501)

  2. are gebideð: waits for, or experiences, grace? ll. 1-2

    'Ar' means honour, favour, grace, here varied by 'metudes miltse' ('the Measurer's mercy', l. 2). 'Metod', 'measurer, ordainer', is an old poetic word for God or fate. The verb 'gebidan' with the genitive or accusative can mean 'to wait for' or 'to experience, live to see'. Read the first way, the opening is hopeful: the solitary often waits for grace, although he must toil at sea. Read the second way, it is a statement that he often does find it. Leslie and Mitchell-Robinson both discuss the double sense. The question it raises is answered only at the close, where 'are' returns: 'Wel bið þam þe him are seceð' (l. 114). Its concessive clause ('þeah þe he modcearig ... sceolde', 'although he, sad at heart, ... must') holds hope and hardship together from the first sentence.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'gebidan'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'ār'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary

  3. lagulade, hreran mid hondum: the rowing exile ll. 3-5

    'Lagulad' (lagu 'sea, water' + lad 'way, course') is a poetic compound for the sea. 'Hreran mid hondum hrimcealde sæ' ('stir with his hands the rime-cold sea') pictures a man rowing. The detail of bare hands instead of oars stresses toil and isolation, and some critics read it in a Christian light as the exile's labour on the voyage of life. 'Wadan wræclastas' ('tread the tracks of exile') joins sea and land travel; 'wræclast' (wræc 'exile, misery' + last 'track') returns at l. 32 and in The Seafarer l. 57. The emphasis on cold (hrim, 'rime') runs through the whole poem and comes back at ll. 48 and 77. Note the h-alliteration of l. 4 ('hreran ... hondum ... hrimcealde').

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'lagu-lād'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wræc-lāst'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  4. Wyrd bið ful ared l. 5

    'Wyrd' (from weorþan 'to become') is 'what happens, event, fate'. In Christian Old English verse it is usually not a pagan goddess but the course of events under God, as in Boethian writing. 'Ared' is the past participle of arædan, 'to determine, decree', so 'fate is fully determined'. Some editors read it as 'inexorable', and the line's sense is debated. The short, closed half-line acts as a gnomic cadence, the first of many 'bið' sentences that generalise from experience (compare ll. 15, 65 ff., 106-110). Whether the half-line belongs to the narrator or to the wanderer is part of the larger crux of who speaks where. The word returns as 'wyrd seo mære' (l. 100) and 'wyrda gesceaft' (l. 107).

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wyrd'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ārǣdan'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary

  5. Swa cwæð eardstapa: who speaks? ll. 6-7

    'Eardstapa' (eard 'land, dwelling' + stapa 'stepper') occurs only here. 'Swa cwæð' can point backward (ll. 1-5 were his words) or forward (introducing ll. 8 ff.); editors usually take it forward, with an inset speech running at least from l. 8. A second frame at l. 111, 'Swa cwæð snottor on mode', raises the question of whether the wise man of ll. 88-110 is the same man grown old in wisdom or a second speaker. Pope's 1965 essay argued from the pronouns 'min' and 'sylf' for two speeches and an epilogue in each of this poem and The Seafarer; he later withdrew the argument ('Second Thoughts on the Interpretation of The Seafarer', Anglo-Saxon England 3, 1974). Most now read one consciousness passing from sorrow to wisdom, framed by a narrator (Leslie, Klinck). The quotation marks in the archive's text are an editor's choice, not the manuscript's. 'Winemæga hryre' ('the fall of dear kinsmen') names the grief.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; J. C. Pope, 'Dramatic Voices in The Wanderer and The Seafarer', in Franciplegius, ed. J. B. Bessinger and R. P. Creed (New York, 1965); J. C. Pope, 'Second Thoughts on the Interpretation of The Seafarer', Anglo-Saxon England 3 (1974); R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  6. uhtna gehwylce l. 8

    'Uht' (genitive plural 'uhtna') is the time just before dawn, the hour of the monastic office of nocturns or matins ('uhtsang'). 'Uhtna gehwylce', 'each of dawns', means 'every morning before light'. The same time of private grief appears in The Wife's Lament ('uhtceare', l. 7) and in Beowulf ('on uhtan', l. 126), where Grendel's slaughter is discovered. With 'ana' (alone) and 'cwiþan' ('to lament'), the line sets the man's speech as soliloquy: he has no one to whom he dare open his mind ('Nis nu cwicra nan', ll. 9-11).

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ūht'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'gehwilc'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008)

  7. indryhten þeaw: the ethic of silence ll. 11-18

    'Indryhten' means 'very noble, lordly'; 'þeaw' is 'custom, practice'. The 'noble custom' is that a man should bind fast his 'ferðloca' (ferð 'spirit' + loca 'enclosure, lock') and keep his 'hordcofa' ('treasure-chamber'), whatever he thinks. 'Breostcofa' ('breast-chamber', l. 18) follows. These container kennings picture the mind as a locked chest. The restraint has a heroic side and a Stoic or Boethian side, much discussed. The irony is that the speaker breaks the rule even as he states it. Line 15 gives the reason: 'Ne mæg werig mod wyrde wiðstondan' ('a weary mind cannot withstand fate'). The image of binding recurs in fetters (l. 21), the bound waves (l. 24), and sorrow and sleep binding (l. 40).

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ferhþ-loca'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hord-cofa'; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  8. goldwine; hrusan heolstre biwrah ll. 22-23

    'Goldwine' (gold + wine 'friend') is a poetic word for a lord, the giver of gold who binds men to him by gifts. It returns at l. 35, and 'goldgiefan' appears in The Seafarer l. 83. 'Hrusan heolstre biwrah' means 'covered with the darkness (heolstor, 'hiding-place, darkness') of the earth'. 'Biwrah' has no expressed subject: the sense is that the darkness of earth covered his lord long ago, and the new clause 'ond ic hean þonan wod' ('and I, abject, went from there') begins the exile. Editors differ on whether to supply 'they' or 'one' as the agent of burial. 'Geara iu' ('long ago, of yore') stresses that the loss is old. 'Hean' (l. 23), 'abject, humbled', and 'wintercearig' ('winter-sad', l. 24) describe his state as he sets out over the sea.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'gold-wine'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'heolstor'; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line

  9. ofer waþema gebind l. 24 (and l. 57)

    'Waþum' or 'waþem' is a rare poetic word for wave or flood; 'gebind' is 'binding, fastening'. Most editors take the phrase as the sea locked by ice, or the waves' close-knit mass. The repetition at l. 57 ('ofer waþema gebind') frames the passage of dreams and visions. The phrase extends the poem's binding motif: the mind bound fast (ll. 13, 18), the heart in fetters (l. 21), sorrow and sleep binding the wretch (l. 40), and the hail-storm that binds the earth (l. 102, 'hrusan bindeð'). Critics often see binding as one of the poem's linking images.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'waþum'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'gebind'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  10. sinces bryttan; meoduhealle ll. 25-27

    'Sinces brytta' ('distributor of treasure') is a traditional formula for a lord, also found in Beowulf (l. 607, of Hroðgar). 'Meoduheall' is the mead-hall, centre of the warband's life. 'Min mine wisse' is difficult. Most editors take 'mine' as 'love, affection' (myne), giving 'who would know my love' or 'who would show me affection'; other readings exist. 'Weman mid wynnum' (l. 29) is 'entice, entertain with joys'. The passage sums up what exile has lost: lord, hall, gift and fellowship. 'Selesecgas ond sincþege' (l. 34), hall-men and the receiving of treasure, are remembered next.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'brytta'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'medo-heall'; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008); R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary

  11. Warað hine wræclast, nales wunden gold ll. 32-33

    'Warian' is 'to guard, occupy, hold'. The exile-track possesses him, not 'wunden gold' ('twisted gold', the rings a lord gives). The next line repeats the pattern: 'ferðloca freorig, nalæs foldan blæd' ('a frozen mind-locker, not the flourishing of the earth'). 'Blæd' ('glory, prosperity, breath') is a key word in both poems (Seafarer ll. 79, 88). The figure is a balanced antithesis, 'x not y', twice repeated, and shows how the poet uses variation. 'Ferðloca' (compare l. 13) is now cold, a physical image of grief.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'warian'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'blǣd'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  12. The dream of the lord (ll. 41-44) ll. 41-44

    The dream shows a ceremony between lord and retainer: embracing and kissing the lord, laying hands and head on his knee. Scholars compare it with rituals of commendation and homage, in which a man put his hands in his lord's, though how far a real Anglo-Saxon ceremony lies behind it is debated. 'Giefstol' ('gift-seat', the throne from which gifts were given) recurs in Beowulf (l. 168). 'Breac' (brucan 'to enjoy, use') usually takes the genitive, but 'giefstolas' is an accusative plural, so the verb here governs the accusative. The happiness is felt in the mind ('þinceð him on mode') only. The sudden waking at l. 45 ('ðonne onwæcneð eft wineleas guma') makes the loss sharper.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'gief-stōl'; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008)

  13. fealwe wegas, baþian brimfuglas ll. 46-48

    'Fealu' ('fallow, dusky, yellowish-grey') describes the waves. The sea-birds (brimfuglas) bathe and spread their feathers ('brædan feþra'), a bleak replacement for the hall-company he dreamt of. 'Hreosan hrim ond snaw, hagle gemenged' ('rime and snow falling, mingled with hail') echoes the opening cold. Some read the birds in the light of The Seafarer's bird catalogue (ll. 19-25), where bird-cries replace men's laughter. The infinitives 'baþian ... brædan ... hreosan' after 'gesihð' ('sees') give the view as a series of present scenes.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'fealu'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'brim-fugel'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  14. The vision of kinsmen (ll. 50-55) ll. 50-55

    The grammar and the referents of ll. 51-55 are disputed. 'Maga gemynd' ('memory of kinsmen') passes through his mind; he greets them 'gliwstafum' ('joyfully', literally 'with glee-staves'), and eagerly gazes on 'secga geseldan' ('companions of men'). Then 'Swimmað eft on weg!' ('they swim away again'). One reading makes the kinsmen a waking vision that fades. Another, followed by many editors, identifies the 'companions' with the sea-birds of l. 47, who seem to be his kin and then float off. 'Fleotendra ferð' ('the spirit of the floating ones') bring him few familiar words ('cuðra cwidegiedda'). Clemoes compared the passage with patristic ideas of the mind's wandering. 'Cearo bið geniwad' (l. 55) repeats l. 50's 'Sorg bið geniwad' and closes the section.

Clemoes, 'Mens absentia cogitans in The Seafarer and The Wanderer', in Medieval Literature and Civilization, ed. D. A. Pearsall and R. A. Waldron (London, 1969); B. Mitchell and F. C. 'glīw-stæf'

  15. middangeard l. 62 (and l. 75)

    'Middangeard' (also 'middaneard') means 'the middle enclosure', the inhabited world. It is cognate with Old Norse 'Miðgarðr' and Gothic 'midjungards'. In Christian Old English it is simply 'the world', often set against heaven. Here it 'dreoseð ond fealleþ' ('droops and falls') every day. The idea that the world itself was ageing towards its end (the 'senectus mundi') was common in Christian writing from Cyprian and Augustine to Gregory the Great, and the poem shifts from personal grief to this general decline. Tolkien took his 'Middle-earth' from this word.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'middan-geard'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  16. No man is wise until he has winters ll. 64-72

    'Wintra dæl' ('a share of winters', i.e. years) makes experience the condition of wisdom. 'Wita sceal geþyldig' opens a list of the wise man's qualities: not too hot-hearted, nor too hasty of speech, nor too weak a warrior, nor too reckless, too fearful, too glad, too greedy for wealth, nor too eager to boast. The 'sceal' construction ('must, ought to be') is the mode of gnomic verse, as in Maxims I and II. The 'ne to ... ne to' series is a pattern of measure, close to the classical mean. 'Beot' (l. 70) is the warrior's formal vow, which must not be spoken before a man knows where his mind will turn. The passage marks the speaker's change from sorrow to wisdom.

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942); R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'bēot'

  17. The ruined walls (ll. 73-77) ll. 73-77

    'Gæstlic' (l. 73) is 'ghostly, terrible', and the 'wela' ('wealth') of the world will stand waste. 'Missenlice' means 'in various places': the poet points to ruined buildings throughout the land. These were probably the Roman remains that Anglo-Saxons saw everywhere. 'Winde biwaune' ('wind-blown'), 'hrime bihrorene' ('covered, beset with frost'), 'hryðge þa ederas' ('the enclosures storm-beaten'). 'Woriað þa winsalo' ('the wine-halls crumble'). The lords lie dead and the proud troop has fallen beside the wall ('wlonc bi wealle'). Compare The Ruin, whose subject is a single such site.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hrēosan'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'eder'

  18. The 'sum' catalogue and the beasts of battle ll. 80-84

    The passage lists the ways the warriors died, with repeated 'sume ... sumne'. 'Sumne fugel oþbær ofer heanne holm' may mean a bird of prey or carrion carried one off over the deep sea. Some take the 'bird' as a ship or think it a kenning. 'Se hara wulf' ('the grey wolf') echoes the traditional 'beasts of battle' theme (wolf, eagle, raven), described by Magoun. The same 'sum' pattern structures The Fortunes of Men in the Exeter Book. 'Dreorighleor' ('sad-faced') describes the man who buried a comrade in an 'eorðscræf' ('earth-cave', grave).

    F. P. Magoun, 'The Theme of the Beasts of Battle in Anglo-Saxon Poetry', Neuphilologische Mitteilungen 56 (1955); G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  19. eald enta geweorc l. 87

    'Ent' is 'giant'. 'Enta geweorc' ('work of giants') is a fixed formula in Old English verse for ancient buildings, especially Roman stone, which Anglo-Saxons did not build. Maxims II (ll. 1-2, after its opening 'Cyning sceal rice healdan') has: 'Ceastra beoð feorran gesyne, / orðanc enta geweorc' ('cities are visible from afar, the skilful work of giants'). The phrase is also in The Ruin (l. 2) and Beowulf (ll. 2717, 2774). The idea may draw on biblical giants before the Flood (Genesis 6:4), but scholars disagree on how far that is in mind. 'Ælda scyppend' (l. 85, 'creator of men') laid waste the dwelling until the old works stood empty, lacking the noise of citizens ('burgwara breahtma lease').

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ent'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'ent'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008)

  20. Hwær cwom mearg? The ubi sunt ll. 92-96

    The questions 'Where has the horse gone, where the kinsman, where the treasure-giver? Where the seats of the feast? Where are the hall-joys?' follow the Latin 'ubi sunt qui ante nos fuerunt?' pattern. It is found in Isidore of Seville's Synonyma and in Old English homilies. J. E. Cross traced the sources and showed the poet's adaptation to heroic objects. 'Eala' ('alas, O') answers the questions with exclamations: the bright cup, the mailed warrior, the prince's glory. 'Hu seo þrag gewat, genap under nihthelm' ('how the time has passed, grown dark under the helmet of night'). The short half-lines and anaphora make the passage the emotional peak of the poem.

    J. E. Cross, '"Ubi Sunt" Passages in Old English: Sources and Relationships', Vetenskaps-Societetens i Lund Årsbok (1956); R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary

  21. weal wundrum heah, wyrmlicum fah l. 98

    'Wyrmlic' means 'serpent-like' (wyrm 'serpent, worm'); 'fah' means 'stained, coloured, decorated'. The phrase may describe snake-like patterning in the stonework, whether carved decoration or marks of weathering; its exact sense is uncertain. 'Wundrum heah' ('wondrously high') shows awe at ancient stonework, as in The Ruin. The wall 'stondeð on laste' ('stands in the track, after') the dear company: a monument outlasting the people. The line cannot be tied to any known monument.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wyrm-līc'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'fāh'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  22. The winter storm (ll. 101-105) ll. 101-105

    'Stanhleoþu' ('stone slopes') are battered by storms. 'Hrið' ('snowstorm') binds the earth, a final use of the binding image. 'Wintres woma' is 'the tumult of winter'. 'Nipeð nihtscua' ('the night-shadow darkens') is almost the same as The Seafarer l. 31 ('Nap nihtscua'). The shared language may point to common tradition, or one poet's knowledge of the other. 'Norþan' ('from the north') is the quarter of cold and, in some Christian writing, of evil. 'Hæleþum on andan' ('in enmity to men') makes nature hostile.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hrīþ'; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  23. Her bið feoh læne: the 'loan' litany ll. 108-110

    'Læne' ('loaned, transitory', from lǣn 'loan') is the keyword. Four parallel half-lines name wealth, friend, man and kinsman, and the last line sums up: 'eal þis eorþan gesteal idel weorþeð' ('all this foundation of the earth will become void'). 'Idel' may echo the Vulgate's 'vanitas' (Ecclesiastes 1:2). Scholars often compare Hávamál st. 76-77 in the Poetic Edda: 'cattle die, kinsmen die', though in Hávamál fame survives. The Seafarer uses 'læne on londe' (l. 66).

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'lǣne'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Hávamál st. 76-77, The Poetic Edda, trans. H. A. Bellows (1923); Ecclesiastes 1:2 (KJV)

  24. Swa cwæð snottor on mode: the closing gnomic lines ll. 111-115

    'Sundor' is 'apart' and 'run' is 'secret, counsel, mystery' (the same word as for a letter of the runic alphabet). 'Gesæt him sundor æt rune' means 'sat apart by himself in meditation'. Lines 112-115 are metrically heavy, with longer half-lines than the rest of the poem, and some metrists treat them as expanded (hypermetric) verse; the classification is less secure than in The Seafarer. Old English poets used such heavier lines for solemn or gnomic passages, as in the close of The Seafarer (see Bliss on hypermetric verses). 'Til' (l. 112) is 'good'. 'Treowe' is 'faith, loyalty'. A man should not reveal his 'torn' ('grief, anger') too quickly unless he knows the remedy ('bote') beforehand. This recalls the ethic of ll. 11-18.

    A. J. Bliss, The Metre of Beowulf (Oxford, 1958; rev. 1967), on hypermetric verses; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'rūn'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary

  25. Wel bið þam þe him are seceð ll. 114-115

    The final lines say it is well for the one who seeks grace ('ar'), comfort from the Father in heaven, 'þær us eal seo fæstnung stondeð' ('where all our security stands'). 'Fæstnung' ('fastness, firmness, security') contrasts with the 'læne' world. The word 'are' returns from l. 1, forming an envelope that many critics read as the poem's resolution. The heroic speaker's suffering leads to Christian consolation. Earlier critics took this frame as a monkish addition to a pagan core. Most now see the Christian conclusion as integral (Leslie, Klinck), though readers still debate how far it answers the grief.

    R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, 8th edn (Chichester, 2012), The Wanderer, notes and glossary; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'fæstnung'
