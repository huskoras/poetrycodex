The Wife's Lament (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Relationships

Ic þis giedd wrece bi me ful geomorre,
minre sylfre sið. Ic þæt secgan mæg,
hwæt ic yrmþa gebad, siþþan ic up weox,
niwes oþþe ealdes, no ma þonne nu.
A ic wite wonn minra wræcsiþa.
ærest min hlaford gewat heonan of leodum
ofer yþa gelac; hæfde ic uhtceare
hwær min leodfruma londes wære.
ða ic me feran gewat folgað secan,
wineleas wrecca, for minre weaþearfe.
Ongunnon þæt þæs monnes magas hycgan
þurh dyrne geþoht, þæt hy todælden unc,
þæt wit gewidost in woruldrice
lifdon laðlicost, ond mec longade.
Het mec hlaford min herheard niman,
ahte ic leofra lyt on þissum londstede,
holdra freonda. Forþon is min hyge geomor,
ða ic me ful gemæcne monnan funde,
heardsæligne, hygegeomorne,
mod miþendne, morþor hycgendne.
Bliþe gebæro ful oft wit beotedan
þæt unc ne gedælde nemne deað ana
owiht elles; eft is þæt onhworfen,
is nu swa hit no wære
freondscipe uncer. Sceal ic feor ge neah
mines felaleofan fæhðu dreogan.
Heht mec mon wunian on wuda bearwe,
under actreo in þam eorðscræfe.
Eald is þes eorðsele, eal ic eom oflongad,
sindon dena dimme, duna uphea,
bitre burgtunas, brerum beweaxne,
wic wynna leas. Ful oft mec her wraþe begeat
fromsiþ frean. Frynd sind on eorþan,
leofe lifgende, leger weardiað,
þonne ic on uhtan ana gonge
under actreo geond þas eorðscrafu.
þær ic sittan mot sumorlangne dæg,
þær ic wepan mæg mine wræcsiþas,
earfoþa fela; forþon ic æfre ne mæg
þære modceare minre gerestan,
ne ealles þæs longaþes þe mec on þissum life begeat.
A scyle geong mon wesan geomormod,
heard heortan geþoht, swylce habban sceal
bliþe gebæro, eac þon breostceare,
sinsorgna gedreag, sy æt him sylfum gelong
eal his worulde wyn, sy ful wide fah
feorres folclondes, þæt min freond siteð
under stanhliþe storme behrimed,
wine werigmod, wætre beflowen
on dreorsele. Dreogeð se min wine
micle modceare; he gemon to oft
wynlicran wic. Wa bið þam þe sceal
of langoþe leofes abidan.

Codex Note

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Wife's Lament: opening, manuscript and genre l. 1

    The poem is copied on fols 115r-115v of the Exeter Book, between riddles and the homiletic poem Judgment Day I; the title is modern. Giedd wrecan, 'to utter a song, recite a lay', is a formula used in Beowulf and The Seafarer (Mæg ic be me sylfum soðgied wrecan). The speaker, a woman separated from her lord, tells of exile, hostile kin, and a dwelling in an earth-cave under an oak. Like Wulf and Eadwacer it is classed as a Frauenlied, and it is grouped with the Exeter Book elegies. Standard editions are Krapp and Dobbie (1936), Leslie's Three Old English Elegies (1961), Klinck (1992) and Muir; it is in Mitchell and Robinson's Guide.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Wife's Lament

  2. geomorre, minre sylfre: the speaker is female ll. 1-2

    Geomorre is the dative singular feminine of geomor, 'sad, mournful'; minre sylfre (genitive feminine) also marks a woman. Rudolph Bambas (JEGP, 1963) argued nevertheless that the speaker is a male retainer mourning his lord in the manner of The Wanderer, which required treating the endings as scribal error. Most scholars reject this, and the female speaker is now the standard view. Sið (l. 2), 'journey, experience, fate', is the key word of the opening: the poem is the story of her sið, and its plural wræcsiþas recurs in ll. 5 and 38.

    Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations); R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  3. 'new or old, no more than now' ll. 3-4

    Yrmþa (genitive plural of yrmþ 'misery, hardship') depends on hwæt 'what (of)'. Siþþan ic up weox, 'since I grew up', covers her whole life. Niwes oþþe ealdes, 'of new or of old', are genitives qualifying yrmþa. No ma þonne nu, 'never more than now', is concise and emphatic. The phrase claims that her present grief exceeds any past suffering, a typical elegiac superlative.

    Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Wife's Lament

  4. wite wonn: 'I have struggled with torment' l. 5

    A means 'always, ever'. Wite, 'punishment, torment, pain', is the word used for the torments of hell. Wonn is the preterite of winnan, 'labour, strive, endure', taking wite as its object. Wræcsiþa, genitive plural of wræcsið 'journey of exile', defines the torment. The line establishes exile as her permanent state, as in The Wanderer and The Seafarer, but unlike those male speakers she cannot travel in search of a new lord.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wræc; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  5. 'over the tumult of the waves' l. 7

    Gelac (related to lac 'play, contest, offering') means 'commotion, tossing', and yþa gelac occurs in other poems for the rough sea. Gewat heonan of leodum, 'went away from the people', and ærest 'first' mark the beginning of her troubles. Whether the lord left in exile, on a voyage or in disgrace is not said. The Husband's Message, which some critics have connected with this poem, describes a lord driven over the sea by a feud.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. lác; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Bernard J. Muir, ed., The Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry, 2nd edn (Exeter, 2000), commentary

  6. uhtcearu: 'care before dawn' l. 7

    Bosworth-Toller glosses uhtcearu as 'care that comes in the early morning', citing this line. The word joins the uht motif found throughout Old English elegy: The Wanderer's exile mourns ana uhtna gehwylce, and The Wife's Lament later has on uhtan ana gonge (l. 35). Hwær min leodfruma londes wære, 'where in the land my prince might be', uses londes as a partitive genitive of place and wære in the subjunctive of uncertainty.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. úht-cearu; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  7. leodfruma: 'prince of the people' l. 8

    Leodfruma means 'patriarch, prince, chieftain'; it is a poetic word used of leaders and of patriarchs in biblical verse. Its use alongside hlaford (l. 6) and frea (l. 33) gives her husband social rank. Some critics see the poem's terms for the husband as deliberately ambiguous between a lord and a spouse, which has supported both the marital and the allegorical readings (the soul or the Church longing for Christ).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. leód-fruma; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations)

  8. folgað secan, wineleas wrecca: 'to seek service' ll. 9-10

    Folgoð means 'retinue, service, office'; folgað secan normally describes a retainer seeking a lord's service. Applied to a woman, it may mean she followed her husband abroad, sought protection, or (in Bambas's view) that the speaker is male. Wineleas wrecca, 'friendless exile', uses the masculine noun wrecca, the standard word for the exile figure. For minre weaþearfe, 'because of my grievous need' (wea 'woe' and þearf 'need'), gives the reason.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. folgoþ; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. wine-leás; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. weá-þearf; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  9. The kinsmen's secret plot ll. 11-14

    Þæs monnes, 'of that man', refers to her husband, and his magas, 'kinsmen', act þurh dyrne geþoht, 'through secret thought'. Todælden unc, 'might divide us two', uses the dual unc, which recurs through the poem (wit, unc, uncer), stressing the couple as a unit. Gewidost and laðlicost, superlatives meaning 'most widely apart' and 'most hatefully', describe the separation. Mec longade, 'I longed', is impersonal: 'it caused longing in me'. The kinsmen's hostility supplies the poem's only explicit motive for the separation.

    Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes; Bruce Mitchell and Fred C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English, text and notes on The Wife's Lament

  10. herheard: 'a dwelling here' or 'a dwelling in a grove'? l. 15

    Het mec hlaford min ... niman: 'my lord commanded me to take ...'. Most editors divide the manuscript's herheard as her heard (with heard for eard, 'dwelling', or for the adjective 'harsh') and translate 'to take up a dwelling here'. Bosworth-Toller's supplement records hearh-eard, 'a dwelling in a grove', from hearg 'grove, heathen sanctuary'. A. N. Doane (Mediaeval Studies, 1966) built on this a reading of the speaker as connected with a heathen grove or shrine. The command to dwell may come from her husband, which implies he banished her.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. hearh-eard; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  11. 'a well-matched man', with a murderous mind ll. 18-20

    Gemæcne, related to gemæcca 'mate, spouse', means 'fitting, well-matched'. The participles that follow describe him as heardsælig 'ill-fated', hygegeomor 'sad of mind', mod miþende 'concealing his mind' and morþor hycgende 'thinking of murder (or crime)'. Most critics take this man as the husband, perhaps disaffected or guilty of the deed that caused his exile. Some argue for a second man, and Stanley Greenfield (1953) read later lines as a curse on the husband. Morþor means 'murder, deadly sin, great wickedness', so the plot is serious. Translators differ over whether the participles explain the husband's later behaviour or his kin's hostility.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-mæcca; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. heard-sǽlig; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. morþor; Stanley B. Greenfield, 'The Wife's Lament Reconsidered', PMLA 68 (1953); Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  12. beotian: 'we vowed' ll. 21-23

    Beotian, 'to threaten, vow, promise', belongs to the heroic beot, the warrior's boast before battle; here it is a lovers' promise. Bliþe gebæro, 'with cheerful bearing', returns ironically in l. 44, where it describes the mask the young man must wear over his grief. Nemne deað ana / owiht elles, 'except death alone, anything else', sounds like a marriage promise. Eft is þæt onhworfen, 'now that is reversed': onhweorfan 'to change, turn around' marks the reversal.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. beótian; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. on-hweorfan; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  13. A defective line l. 24

    The manuscript gives only is nu before swa hit no wære, and no alliteration links the half-lines, so editors suspect a loss. Some leave the gap, others supply words, but the sense is clear: their friendship (freondscipe uncer, l. 25) is as if it had never been. Students should note that line counts and numbering in the editions follow Krapp and Dobbie even where lines are defective.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds, The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  14. fæhðu dreogan: 'endure the feud' ll. 25-26

    Fæhþ (fæhþu) means 'feud, hostility, vengeance', the formal enmity between kin groups that the law sought to manage with wergild. Mines felaleofan, 'of my much-beloved', probably refers to her husband; the feud is either his towards her or one she suffers because of him. Some readers take it as the enmity she endures as a result of her husband's actions. The Husband's Message (l. 19) uses the same word for the feud that drove the lord abroad.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. fǽhþ; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  15. the grove and the oak ll. 27-28

    Bearu means 'grove', and actreo is 'oak-tree'. The oak and grove have suggested a heathen holy place, as in Doane's reading; for others, they are simply a remote wild spot outside human settlement. Heht mec mon wunian, 'someone (or a man) commanded me to dwell', uses indefinite mon, 'one', which leaves the agent unclear. The repetition of under actreo in l. 36 frames the passage describing her surroundings.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ác-treó; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. bearu; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations)

  16. eorðscræf, eorðsele: cave or grave? ll. 28-29

    Eorðscræf, glossed by Bosworth-Toller 'an earth-cavern, a grave', and eorðsele, 'earth-hall', describe her dwelling. A literal underground chamber, perhaps an ancient barrow, is possible. But the grave sense led Elinor Lench (Comitatus, 1970) and others to read her as one of the 'living dead' speaking from a burial place. Others again see the cave as a prison of banishment or as an image of the soul's exile in the world. Eald is þes eorðsele, 'this earth-hall is old', also suggests an ancient grave mound. Eal ic eom oflongad, 'I am wholly seized with longing'.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. eorþ-scræf; Elinor Lench, 'The Wife's Lament: A Poem of the Living Dead', Comitatus 1 (1970); Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations)

  17. the landscape: dim valleys and briars ll. 30-32

    Dena (plural of denu 'valley') dimme 'dark', and duna uphea 'high hills' form a pair. Burgtunas, 'enclosures, fortified dwellings' (burh 'fort' and tun 'enclosure'), are bitre 'bitter' and brerum beweaxne 'overgrown with briars', a sense of abandoned settlement like that of The Ruin. Wic wynna leas, 'dwelling empty of joys', sums up. The landscape is both physical and emotional, a typical feature of the elegies.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. burh-tún; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  18. fromsiþ frean: 'my lord's departure' ll. 32-33

    Fromsið means 'departure, going away'. Frea is 'lord', used both of secular lords and of God, which keeps the allegorical reading open. Begeat 'seized, came upon' (from begietan) takes mec as object, so the departure is an active force that overwhelms her (wraþe 'cruelly'). The line repeats the opening narrative (ærest min hlaford gewat, l. 6), so the departure that began her troubles is still, in memory, happening again and again (ful oft). The juxtaposition with the next sentence, about lovers who lie together, sharpens the sense of absence.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. from-síþ; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; R. F. Leslie, ed., Three Old English Elegies (Manchester, 1961), text and notes

  19. lovers in their beds ll. 33-34

    Leger means 'a lying, a bed, a couch', but also 'sickness' and 'a grave'. Weardian 'to guard, occupy, keep' gives 'they occupy their bed'. Frynd 'friends' here clearly means 'lovers'. The double sense of leger (bed and grave) supports the living-dead reading, but the main contrast is between others who lie together and the speaker who goes alone (ana gonge) before dawn.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. leger; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; Elinor Lench, 'The Wife's Lament: A Poem of the Living Dead', Comitatus 1 (1970)

  20. 'when I go alone before dawn' ll. 35-41

    On uhtan, 'before dawn', recalls uhtceare (l. 7). Sumorlangne dæg, 'the summer-long day', is a powerful contrast to the winter imagery of the male elegies; the long day lengthens her weeping. Mot and mæg, 'may, can', express both permission and limitation: she may only sit and weep. Modceare and longaþes, genitives after gerestan 'rest from', conclude the narrative section with 'all the longing that has seized me in this life'.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. úht-cearu; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. langoþ; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  21. The gnomic ending: maxim or curse? ll. 42-47

    The subjunctives (scyle, sy) can be read as general statements ('a young man must always be...') or as wishes ('may he be ...'). If gnomic, the passage describes a man who must maintain a cheerful expression despite grief, which may apply to her husband. If optative, it is a curse: may all his joy depend on himself alone, may he be outlawed far away. Stanley Greenfield (1953) argued for a curse, Barrie Ruth Straus (1981) read the end as a speech act of cursing, and John Niles (2003) distinguished gentle from vindictive readings. Sinsorgna gedreag, 'the crowd of perpetual sorrows' (sin 'continual'), and gelong 'dependent' are key terms.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. sin-sorh; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. ge-lang; Stanley B. Greenfield, 'The Wife's Lament Reconsidered', PMLA 68 (1953); John D. Niles, 'The Problem of the Ending of The Wife's Lament', Speculum 78 (2003); Wikipedia, 'The Wife's Lament' (summary of interpretations)

  22. The husband imagined under a rocky slope ll. 47-50

    Stanhliþ, 'stony slope, cliff', storme behrimed 'rimed with frost by storm', and wætre beflowen 'flowed about by water' create a landscape matching hers. Dreorsele (dreor 'blood' or 'dreary', and sele 'hall') is glossed 'a dreary, desolate-looking hall'. Werigmod, 'weary of mind', fits the elegiac exile. The passage mirrors her own hall of earth; both lovers are alone in a hostile place, whether this is a projection of her imagination or the outcome of the curse.

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. stán-hliþ; Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. dreór-sele; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary

  23. The closing gnome ll. 52-53

    He gemon to oft / wynlicran wic, 'he remembers too often a more joyful dwelling', ties the husband's grief to memory. The last gnome, with of langoþe, 'out of longing', and abidan 'wait for', returns to the key word longaþ. Its generality makes it fit either lover; it can also be read as the speaker's own last word. The closing gnome is a common device in the Exeter Book elegies (compare The Wanderer's last lines).

    Bosworth-Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary, s.v. langoþ; Anne L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary; John D. Niles, 'The Problem of the Ending of The Wife's Lament', Speculum 78 (2003)
