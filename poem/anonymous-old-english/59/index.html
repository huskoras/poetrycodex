Time & Brevity

The Seafarer (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, vol. III (1936)

Time & Brevity

Mæg ic be me sylfum soðgied wrecan,
siþas secgan, hu ic geswincdagum
earfoðhwile oft þrowade,
bitre breostceare gebiden hæbbe,
gecunnad in ceole cearselda fela,
atol yþa gewealc, þær mec oft bigeat
nearo nihtwaco æt nacan stefnan,
þonne he be clifum cnossað. Calde geþrungen
wæron mine fet, forste gebunden,
caldum clommum, þær þa ceare seofedun
hat ymb heortan; hungor innan slat
merewerges mod. þæt se mon ne wat
þe him on foldan fægrost limpeð,
hu ic earmcearig iscealdne sæ
winter wunade wræccan lastum,
winemægum bidroren,
bihongen hrimgicelum; hægl scurum fleag.
þær ic ne gehyrde butan hlimman sæ,
iscaldne wæg. Hwilum ylfete song
dyde ic me to gomene, ganetes hleoþor
ond huilpan sweg fore hleahtor wera,
mæw singende fore medodrince.
Stormas þær stanclifu beotan, þær him stearn oncwæð
isigfeþera; ful oft þæt earn bigeal,
urigfeþra; ne ænig hleomæga
feasceaftig ferð frefran meahte.
Forþon him gelyfeð lyt, se þe ah lifes wyn
gebiden in burgum, bealosiþa hwon,
wlonc ond wingal, hu ic werig oft
in brimlade bidan sceolde.
Nap nihtscua, norþan sniwde,
hrim hrusan bond, hægl feol on eorþan,
corna caldast. Forþon cnyssað nu
heortan geþohtas, þæt ic hean streamas,
sealtyþa gelac sylf cunnige;
monað modes lust mæla gehwylce
ferð to feran, þæt ic feor heonan
elþeodigra eard gesece.
Forþon nis þæs modwlonc mon ofer eorþan,
ne his gifena þæs god, ne in geoguþe to þæs hwæt,
ne in his dædum to þæs deor, ne him his dryhten to þæs hold,
þæt he a his sæfore sorge næbbe,
to hwon hine dryhten gedon wille.
Ne biþ him to hearpan hyge ne to hringþege,
ne to wife wyn ne to worulde hyht,
ne ymbe owiht elles, nefne ymb yða gewealc,
ac a hafað longunge se þe on lagu fundað.
Bearwas blostmum nimað, byrig fægriað,
wongas wlitigað, woruld onetteð;
ealle þa gemoniað modes fusne
sefan to siþe, þam þe swa þenceð
on flodwegas feor gewitan.
Swylce geac monað geomran reorde,
singeð sumeres weard, sorge beodeð
bitter in breosthord. þæt se beorn ne wat,
esteadig secg, hwæt þa sume dreogað
þe þa wræclastas widost lecgað.
Forþon nu min hyge hweorfeð ofer hreþerlocan,
min modsefa mid mereflode
ofer hwæles eþel hweorfeð wide,
eorþan sceatas, cymeð eft to me
gifre ond grædig, gielleð anfloga,
hweteð on hwælweg hreþer unwearnum
ofer holma gelagu. Forþon me hatran sind
dryhtnes dreamas þonne þis deade lif,
læne on londe. Ic gelyfe no
þæt him eorðwelan ece stondað.
Simle þreora sum þinga gehwylce,
ær his tid aga, to tweon weorþeð;
adl oþþe yldo oþþe ecghete
fægum fromweardum feorh oðþringeð.
Forþon þæt bið eorla gehwam æftercweþendra
lof lifgendra lastworda betst,
þæt he gewyrce, ær he on weg scyle,
fremum on foldan wið feonda niþ,
deorum dædum deofle togeanes,
þæt hine ælda bearn æfter hergen,
ond his lof siþþan lifge mid englum
awa to ealdre, ecan lifes blæd,
dream mid dugeþum. Dagas sind gewitene,
ealle onmedlan eorþan rices;
næron nu cyningas ne caseras
ne goldgiefan swylce iu wæron,
þonne hi mæst mid him mærþa gefremedon
ond on dryhtlicestum dome lifdon.
Gedroren is þeos duguð eal, dreamas sind gewitene,
wuniað þa wacran ond þas woruld healdaþ,
brucað þurh bisgo. Blæd is gehnæged,
eorþan indryhto ealdað ond searað,
swa nu monna gehwylc geond middangeard.
Yldo him on fareð, onsyn blacað,
gomelfeax gnornað, wat his iuwine,
æþelinga bearn, eorþan forgiefene.
Ne mæg him þonne se flæschoma, þonne him þæt feorg losað,
ne swete forswelgan ne sar gefelan,
ne hond onhreran ne mid hyge þencan.
þeah þe græf wille golde stregan
broþor his geborenum, byrgan be deadum,
maþmum mislicum þæt hine mid wille,
ne mæg þære sawle þe biþ synna ful
gold to geoce for godes egsan,
þonne he hit ær hydeð þenden he her leofað.
Micel biþ se meotudes egsa, forþon hi seo molde oncyrreð;
se gestaþelade stiþe grundas,
eorþan sceatas ond uprodor.
Dol biþ se þe him his dryhten ne ondrædeþ; cymeð him se deað unþinged.
Eadig bið se þe eaþmod leofaþ; cymeð him seo ar of heofonum,
meotod him þæt mod gestaþelað, forþon he in his meahte gelyfeð.
Stieran mon sceal strongum mode, ond þæt on staþelum healdan,
ond gewis werum, wisum clæne,
scyle monna gehwylc mid gemete healdan
wiþ leofne ond wið laþne bealo,
þeah þe he hine wille fyres fulne
oþþe on bæle forbærnedne
his geworhtne wine. Wyrd biþ swiþre,
meotud meahtigra þonne ænges monnes gehygd.
Uton we hycgan hwær we ham agen,
ond þonne geþencan hu we þider cumen,
ond we þonne eac tilien, þæt we to moten
in þa ecan eadignesse,
þær is lif gelong in lufan dryhtnes,
hyht in heofonum. þæs sy þam halgan þonc,
þæt he usic geweorþade, wuldres ealdor,
ece dryhten, in ealle tid.
Amen.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Opening: soðgied wrecan l. 1

    The Seafarer (124 lines) is in the Exeter Book (fols 81b-83a), after The Wanderer and other poems. 'Soðgied' is 'true tale, true song'; 'wrecan' is 'to utter, recite'. The opening resembles The Wife's Lament ('Ic þis giedd wrece / bi me ful geomorre') and Deor, and marks the poem as a personal account. 'Siþas secgan' ('tell my journeys') announces the subject. 'Geswincdagum' ('days of toil') and 'earfoðhwile' ('time of hardship') set the tone. Whether the voyages are literal, allegorical, or both, is the central critical question. Ezra Pound's 1911 version translated only to l. 99 and dropped the Christian ending.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'sōþ-gied'; Wikipedia, 'The Seafarer (poem)' (overview of manuscript and readings)

  2. nearo nihtwaco æt nacan stefnan ll. 6-8

    'Nearo' is 'narrow, anxious, perilous'. 'Nihtwaco' means 'night-watch'. 'Naca' is a poetic word for 'ship' and 'stefn' is 'prow' (modern 'stem'). 'Þonne he be clifum cnossað' ('when it knocks along the cliffs'): the ship pounds near the rocky coast. 'Atol yþa gewealc' ('the terrible rolling of waves') recurs at l. 46. The speaker's experience is concrete; the sea-voyage is described from inside.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'naca'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'stefn'; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  3. Cold fetters and hot sorrow (ll. 8-12) ll. 8-12

    'Calde geþrungen' ('pinched by cold'), 'forste gebunden' ('bound by frost'), 'caldum clommum' ('in cold clamps'): the feet are as if chained. Against this, the cares 'seofedun hat ymb heortan' ('sighed hot about the heart'). 'Seofian' is 'to lament'. The contrast of outer cold and inner heat is a key image of the poem, also used of the mind in l. 64 ('hatran'). 'Hungor innan slat merewerges mod' ('hunger tore within the sea-weary mind'): 'merewerig' occurs only here. The alliteration on c and h reinforces the sensations.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'clam'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'mere-wērig'

  4. A defective line l. 16

    'Winemæg' is 'dear kinsman' and 'bidroren' (from bedreosan) is 'deprived'. The manuscript is written continuously like prose, and editors print 'winemægum bidroren' as a short line with no matching half-line, so some suspect a loss in copying. The theme of loss of kin is the exile's condition, as in The Wanderer ('winemæga hryre', l. 7). Editions differ in how they set out the lines here; consult the apparatus of the edition in use.

    G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wine-mǣg'

  5. The bird catalogue: swan, gannet, curlew, gull ll. 19-22

    The birds stand in place of the hall's music and company. 'Ylfete' is the (whooper) swan; 'ganet' is the gannet; 'huilpe' is probably the curlew; 'mæw' is the gull. Each cry is matched with a hall pleasure: 'to gomene' ('as entertainment'), 'fore hleahtor wera' ('instead of men's laughter'), 'fore medodrince' ('instead of mead-drinking'). This ironic substitution is like the hall dream in The Wanderer. Ornithologists and editors have discussed which species are meant. Ezra Pound's paraphrase made the passage famous.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ylfetu'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'huilpe'

  6. stearn ... isigfeþera; earn ... urigfeþra ll. 23-25

    'Stearn' is probably the tern and 'earn' the eagle. The compounds 'isigfeþera' ('icy-feathered') and 'urigfeþra' ('dewy- or wet-feathered') are near-rhymes and occur only here. The eagle 'bigeal' ('screamed about') is a reminder of the beasts of battle. 'Ne ænig hleomæga feasceaftig ferð frefran meahte' ('no protecting kinsman could comfort the destitute spirit'). Gordon prints the text with comment on the bird identifications.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'īsig-feþera'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'earn'

  7. wlonc ond wingal l. 29

    'Wlonc' is 'proud, splendid' (also 'arrogant'), and 'wingal' is 'wine-flushed, merry with wine'. The man who lives 'in burgum' ('in towns, fortified places') and has had little of 'bealosiþa' ('bitter journeys') cannot believe what the seafarer endured. The same alliterating pair appears in The Ruin (l. 34) of the vanished warriors in the bath-city. In Christian readings the contrast is between worldly comfort and spiritual exile, but it can also be read socially.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wlanc'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'wīn-gāl'; I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  8. Nap nihtscua, norþan sniwde ll. 31-33

    'Nap' is the past tense of 'nipan' ('to grow dark'). The half-line matches The Wanderer l. 104, 'nipeð nihtscua'. 'Hrim hrusan bond' ('frost bound the earth') and 'corna caldast' ('coldest of grains', hail) use the binding image and a riddle-like kenning for hail. The repeated use of 'Forþon' at l. 33 follows, a turning point in the poem. The seasonal sequence then reverses into spring at l. 48.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'nīpan'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line

  9. Forþon: the problem word l. 33 (also ll. 27, 39, 58, 64, 72)

    'Forþon' (also 'forðon') usually means 'therefore' or 'because'. In The Seafarer it occurs six times in the first 72 lines (ll. 27, 33, 39, 58, 64, 72) and twice more in the close (ll. 103, 108), often at key turns, notably here at l. 33. The thoughts of his heart urge him to try the high streams himself, though he has just described the sea's misery. Many editors take some uses as 'indeed' or 'and yet' or as a simple connector. The logic depends on how one reads the speaker's attitude to the sea. Gordon and Klinck discuss the problem; Mitchell's syntax work treats the word in detail.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'for-þon'; B. Mitchell, Old English Syntax, 2 vols (Oxford, 1985), on forþon

  10. elþeodigra eard: peregrinatio ll. 37-38

    'Elþeodig' is 'foreign, from another people'; 'eard' is 'land, homeland'. 'Monað modes lust' ('the desire of the mind urges') the spirit to travel. Dorothy Whitelock (1950) argued that the speaker is a 'peregrinus', a man who undertakes voluntary exile for the love of God, as Irish and Anglo-Saxon monks did. The 'land of foreigners' may be a literal destination or heaven, where all are strangers to earthly life. Earlier critics, beginning with Rieger, thought the poem a dialogue of an old sailor and a young man. Smithers (1957, 1959) argued for a figurative reading of the voyage. Most now read one speaker and a religious meaning, often allegorical.

    D. Whitelock, 'The Interpretation of The Seafarer', in The Early Cultures of North-West Europe, ed. C. Fox and B. Dickins (Cambridge, 1950); G. V. Smithers, 'The Meaning of The Seafarer and The Wanderer', Medium Ævum 26 (1957) and 28 (1959); I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'el-þēodig'

  11. No man so proud (ll. 39-43) ll. 39-43

    The passage uses the repeated 'to þæs' ('so') in a series: not so 'modwlonc' ('proud of mind'), nor so 'god' in gifts, so 'hwæt' ('bold') in youth, so 'deor' ('brave') in deeds, so favoured by his lord ('dryhten', which also means 'the Lord'). The ambiguity of 'dryhten' supports the allegorical reading: 'to hwon hine dryhten gedon wille' ('as to what the Lord will do with him').

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'mōd-wlonc'

  12. No thought of harp or ring-giving ll. 44-47

    'Hringþegu' ('receiving of rings') is the gift from a lord. 'Wife wyn' ('pleasure in a woman'), 'worulde hyht' ('hope in the world'): the man who 'on lagu fundað' ('sets out on the sea') thinks only of 'yða gewealc' (as at l. 6). 'Longunge' ('longing') captures the restless desire that drives him. Critics read the passage as his renunciation of the worldly pleasures praised in heroic poetry.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hring-þegu'

  13. Spring renewal (ll. 48-52) ll. 48-52

    The spring passage contrasts with the winter of ll. 31-33. 'Byrig fægriað' means 'the towns grow fair'. 'Woruld onetteð' ('the world hastens on') suggests both spring's coming and the world's rush towards its end. Spring is the traditional season for voyages and for pilgrimages. Paradoxically, the beauty of the world makes the mind 'fus' ('eager, ready to depart', also used of the dying) for the journey. Some critics read the line as hinting at the end of the world.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'fūs'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ōnettan'

  14. The cuckoo, summer's guardian ll. 53-55

    'Geac' is the cuckoo, called 'sumeres weard' ('guardian, herald of summer'). In Old English poetry its call is sad: The Husband's Message (ll. 22-23) also has the cuckoo crying mournfully, as a signal to sail. In Celtic and Germanic tradition it can be a bird of sorrow. 'Breosthord' ('breast-hoard', the heart or mind) adds a container kenning. The cuckoo has sometimes been taken as the 'anfloga' of l. 62.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'gēac'

  15. The mind leaves its enclosure ll. 58-61

    'Hreþerloca' ('breast-locker') is a variant of The Wanderer's 'ferðloca'. Here the mind ('hyge', 'modsefa') goes out over the sea and comes back. 'Hwæles eþel' ('the whale's homeland') is a kenning for the sea, like 'hwælweg' (l. 63). Clemoes connected the passage to the patristic idea of the mind traveling while the body stays still ('mens absentia cogitans'). 'Eorþan sceatas' ('the corners of the earth') recurs at l. 105.

    P. Clemoes, 'Mens absentia cogitans in The Seafarer and The Wanderer', in Medieval Literature and Civilization, ed. D. A. Pearsall and R. A. Waldron (London, 1969); I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'hreþer-loca'

  16. anfloga: the lone flier l. 62

    'Anfloga' ('lone flier') occurs only here. Some take it as the cuckoo of l. 53, or another bird; others as the mind itself, which has been flying out and returns 'gifre ond grædig' ('greedy and eager'), and now cries. Several critics read the bird as the mind or soul in bird-form, an old image of the soul leaving the body. Clemoes related it to the mind's flight in Latin writing. 'Hweteð on hwælweg hreþer unwearnum' ('urges the heart irresistibly on the whale-way'). The crux is important for how one reads the poem's transition from voyage to eternity.

    P. Clemoes, 'Mens absentia cogitans in The Seafarer and The Wanderer', in Medieval Literature and Civilization, ed. D. A. Pearsall and R. A. Waldron (London, 1969); I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'ān-floga'

  17. dryhtnes dreamas þonne þis deade lif ll. 64-66

    'Hatran' ('hotter', more ardent) recalls the hot cares of l. 11. 'Dream' means 'joy, festivity, music', and the 'dreamas' of heaven contrast with the 'deade lif' (the 'dead life'). 'Læne on londe' ('transitory on land') echoes The Wanderer's 'læne' litany. The word play on 'land' and 'sea' is significant: the land life is dead, while the sea voyage leads to heaven. Many critics see here the hinge of the poem.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'drēam'; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'lǣne'

  18. adl oþþe yldo oþþe ecghete ll. 68-71

    'Simle þreora sum' ('always one of three'), 'to tweon weorþeð' ('becomes a cause of doubt'). The three are 'adl' ('disease'), 'yldo' ('old age') and 'ecghete' ('edge-hate', violence of the sword). Beowulf has a similar list in Hroðgar's sermon (ll. 1763-68). 'Fægum fromweardum' ('the doomed one departing'): 'fæge' means 'fated to die'. The triad is a homiletic commonplace. Its use here makes the sea-voyage a figure for every human life, which must end in one of these ways.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'ecg-hete'

  19. lof lifgendra: fame turned heavenward ll. 72-80

    'Lof' ('praise, fame') is the heroic reward, the 'lastword' ('words after death'). Beowulf (ll. 1387-89) says a man should earn glory before death. The Seafarer redirects this ethic: deeds 'wið feonda niþ' ('against the malice of foes') and 'deofle togeanes' ('against the devil') will earn 'lof ... mid englum' ('fame among the angels'), 'ecan lifes blæd' ('the glory of eternal life'). Critics see here a deliberate Christian conversion of heroic fame.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Beowulf, ed. R. D. Fulk, R. E. Bjork and J. D. Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th edn (Toronto, 2008); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'lof'

  20. cyningas ne caseras ll. 80-90

    'Casere' is the Old English borrowing of Latin 'Caesar', 'emperor'. 'Dagas sind gewitene' ('the days are gone') and 'Gedroren is þeos duguð eal' ('this whole host has fallen'). The weaker ones remain and hold the world. 'Eorþan indryhto ealdað ond searað' ('the earth's nobility ages and withers'), as every man does. The decline of the world ('senectus mundi') and the comparison of the world's age to a man's age is from patristic tradition. The passage parallels The Wanderer's 'ubi sunt'.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'cāsere'; R. F. Leslie, ed., The Wanderer (Manchester, 1966), notes to the line

  21. flæschoma ll. 94-96

    'Flæschoma' ('flesh-covering', 'homa' = covering) is a common Old English poetic word for the body, the garment of the soul. When life ('feorg') is lost, the body cannot taste, feel, move or think. The theme of the body in death is common in Old English homilies and in the Soul and Body poems. 'Gomelfeax' ('grey-haired') describes the old man who mourns his friends 'eorþan forgiefene' ('given to the earth').

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford, 1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. 'flǣsc-hama'; Dictionary of Old English: A to I online (Toronto), s.v. 'flǣsc-hama'

  22. Gold in the grave (ll. 97-102) ll. 97-102

    The text is difficult: 'græf' is 'grave'; 'stregan' is 'strew'; 'byrgan be deadum' ('bury beside the dead'). Gordon and others discuss whether a brother is laying treasure in his brother's grave. The idea is close to Psalm 49:7 (KJV), 'None of them can by any means redeem his brother'. Gold buried for the dead, an echo of pagan burial rites, cannot help the soul 'for godes egsan' ('before God's terror'). The idea of treasure buried with the dead recurs in Beowulf.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; Psalm 49:6-7 (KJV)

  23. The hypermetric homily (ll. 103-124) ll. 103-124

    'Micel biþ se meotudes egsa' ('great is the terror of the Measurer'), 'forþon hi seo molde oncyrreð' ('before whom the earth turns aside'). Lines 103 and 106-108 are hypermetric, with longer half-lines. Some earlier editors and anthologists ended the poem at l. 108 or earlier and treated the rest as a later addition. Modern editors (Gordon, Klinck) keep the whole as one poem. Lines 109-116 are textually difficult, and editors punctuate and divide them differently. The ending has the gnomic 'Dol biþ ... Eadig bið ...' ('Foolish is he ... Blessed is he ...'), close to Maxims I.

    I. L. Gordon, ed., The Seafarer (London, 1960), notes to the line; A. L. Klinck, ed., The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study (Montreal, 1992), commentary to the line; A. J. Bliss, The Metre of Beowulf (Oxford, 1958; rev. 1967), on hypermetric verses; G. P. Krapp and E. V. K. Dobbie, eds., The Exeter Book, ASPR 3 (New York, 1936), text and notes to the line

  24. Uton we hycgan hwær we ham agen ll. 117-124

    'Uton' ('let us') is a typical homiletic formula, used to exhort a congregation. The 'ham' ('home') is heaven, and the 'eadignesse' ('blessedness') that is reached 'in lufan dryhtnes' ('in the love of the Lord'). The poem closes with praise of the Lord and 'Amen', which suggests liturgical or homiletic use. This close completes the transformation of the sea-voyage into the voyage of life to heaven.

C. Fox and B. Dickins (Cambridge, 1950)
