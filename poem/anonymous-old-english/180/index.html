The Battle of Brunanburh (Old English)

by Anonymous (Old English) c. 7th–11th century

From The Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records, ed. E. V. K. Dobbie, vol. VI (1942)

History & Politics

Her æþelstan cyning, eorla dryhten,
beorna beahgifa, and his broþor eac,
Eadmund æþeling, ealdorlangne tir
geslogon æt sæcce sweorda ecgum
ymbe Brunanburh. Bordweal clufan,
heowan heaþolinde hamora lafan,
afaran Eadweardes, swa him geæþele wæs
from cneomægum, þæt hi æt campe oft
wiþ laþra gehwæne land ealgodon,
hord and hamas. Hettend crungun,
Sceotta leoda and scipflotan
fæge feollan, feld dænnede
secga swate, siðþan sunne up
on morgentid, mære tungol,
glad ofer grundas, godes condel beorht,
eces drihtnes, oð sio æþele gesceaft
sah to setle. þær læg secg mænig
garum ageted, guma norþerna
ofer scild scoten, swilce Scittisc eac,
werig, wiges sæd. Wesseaxe forð
ondlongne dæg eorodcistum
on last legdun laþum þeodum,
heowan herefleman hindan þearle
mecum mylenscearpan. Myrce ne wyrndon
heardes hondplegan hæleþa nanum
þæra þe mid Anlafe ofer æra gebland
on lides bosme land gesohtun,
fæge to gefeohte. Fife lægun
on þam campstede cyningas giunge,
sweordum aswefede, swilce seofene eac
eorlas Anlafes, unrim heriges,
flotan and Sceotta. þær geflemed wearð
Norðmanna bregu, nede gebeded,
to lides stefne litle weorode;
cread cnear on flot, cyning ut gewat
on fealene flod, feorh generede.
Swilce þær eac se froda mid fleame com
on his cyþþe norð, Costontinus,
har hilderinc, hreman ne þorfte
mæca gemanan; he wæs his mæga sceard,
freonda gefylled on folcstede,
beslagen æt sæcce, and his sunu forlet
on wælstowe wundun forgrunden,
giungne æt guðe. Gelpan ne þorfte
beorn blandenfeax bilgeslehtes,
eald inwidda, ne Anlaf þy ma;
mid heora herelafum hlehhan ne þorftun
þæt heo beaduweorca beteran wurdun
on campstede cumbolgehnastes,
garmittinge, gumena gemotes,
wæpengewrixles, þæs hi on wælfelda
wiþ Eadweardes afaran plegodan.
Gewitan him þa Norþmen negledcnearrum,
dreorig daraða laf, on Dinges mere
ofer deop wæter Difelin secan,
eft Iraland, æwiscmode.
Swilce þa gebroþer begen ætsamne,
cyning and æþeling, cyþþe sohton,
Wesseaxena land, wiges hremige.
Letan him behindan hræw bryttian
saluwigpadan, þone sweartan hræfn,
hyrnednebban, and þane hasewanpadan,
earn æftan hwit, æses brucan,
grædigne guðhafoc and þæt græge deor,
wulf on wealde. Ne wearð wæl mare
on þis eiglande æfre gieta
folces gefylled beforan þissum
sweordes ecgum, þæs þe us secgað bec,
ealde uðwitan, siþþan eastan hider
Engle and Seaxe up becoman,
ofer brad brimu Brytene sohtan,
wlance wigsmiþas, Wealas ofercoman,
eorlas arhwate eard begeatan.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Her – a poem inside a chronicle l. 1

    The poem has no title in the manuscripts. It is the annal for 937 in four manuscripts of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle: A (the Parker Chronicle, Cambridge, Corpus Christi College 173), B, C and D (British Library, Cotton Tiberius A.vi, B.i and B.iv). A fifth copy, in Cotton Otho B.xi, was badly damaged in the 1731 fire and is known mainly from early transcripts. It opens with the annal formula 'Her', 'here [in this year]', so it is at once verse and record. It is one of several poems entered in the Chronicle in the tenth and eleventh centuries, praising the West Saxon royal house. Campbell's 1938 edition remains the standard commentary, and Livingston's Casebook (2011) collects the sources. Tennyson's verse translation (1880), made from his son Hallam's prose version, made the poem known to Victorian readers.

    E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, annal for 937 (trans. J. Ingram, Project Gutenberg)

  2. Æthelstan, king; 'lord of earls' l. 1

    Æthelstan, son of Edward the Elder, became king of the West Saxons and Mercians in 924-925. In 927 he took York and became the first king to rule the whole of England. The 937 victory confirmed that position against a northern coalition. The poem gives him two traditional epithets in apposition: 'eorla dryhten' ('lord of noblemen') and 'beorna beahgifa' ('ring-giver of warriors'). 'Beahgifa' recalls the generous lord of heroic verse, who rewards his retinue with arm-rings ('beagas'). The style deliberately puts a contemporary West Saxon king into the language of Beowulf and Maldon. The alliteration in the first line falls on vowels: 'æþelstan' / 'eorla'.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. beahgifa; Wikipedia, 'Æthelstan'

  3. Eadmund æþeling – Edmund the prince l. 3

    'Æþeling' means 'prince of the royal house', a man eligible for the throne. Edmund, born around 920-921, was a son of Edward the Elder by his third wife, Eadgifu. He fought alongside his half-brother and succeeded him in 939. The poem pairs the two throughout: 'he and his brother also' (l. 2), and at the end 'cyning and æþeling' going home together (l. 58). 'Ealdorlangne tir' (l. 3) means 'life-long glory', from 'ealdor', 'life' (not here 'chief'), and 'tir', 'glory'. 'Geslogon' means 'won by striking', from 'slean', so the glory is something earned in combat. 'Sweorda ecgum', 'with the edges of swords', is a common formula; a variant, 'sweordes ecgum', returns at l. 68.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. ealdorlang; Wikipedia, 'Edmund I'

  4. Brunanburh – where was it? l. 5

    'Brunanburh' probably means 'Bruna's fortification' or 'the fort by the (river) Brune'. Other sources name the battle differently: 'Wendun' or 'Weondun' (Symeon of Durham), 'Brunandun', 'Vínheiðr' (Egils saga). The identification has been debated since the seventeenth century. The case for Bromborough on the Wirral, where the place-name can be derived from 'Brunanburh', has been put by Paul Cavill and others and is supported by Livingston's Casebook. It fits a Dublin fleet landing in the Mersey estuary (see 'Dinges mere', l. 54). Others have argued for sites in Lancashire, Yorkshire, the Scottish borders and elsewhere, and the question is still open. 'Ymbe' means 'around, near'.

    M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; Wikipedia, 'Battle of Brunanburh'

  5. The shield wall and 'leavings of hammers' l. 5

    'Bordweal' ('board-wall') is the shield wall, the close formation of a Germanic battle line, also found in Maldon. 'Clufan' is the preterite plural of 'cleofan', 'to split'. 'Heaþolinde' means 'war-lindens', that is, shields, since they were made of lime-wood. 'Hamora lafan' is 'the leavings (remnants) of hammers', a kenning for swords: what the smith's hammer leaves behind. 'Laf' ('what is left, a remnant, heirloom') is a key word in the poem. The defeated go home as 'herelafum' ('remnants of the army', l. 47) and 'daraða laf' ('the spears' leavings', l. 54). This repeated use of 'laf' sets the victors' weapons against the vanquished survivors.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. laf; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. bordweall; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  6. afaran Eadweardes – the sons of Edward l. 7

    'Afaran' (also 'eaforan') is a poetic word for 'offspring, sons'. Edward the Elder, son of Alfred, extended West Saxon rule over the Danelaw south of the Humber. 'Swa him geæþele wæs / from cneomægum' means 'as was natural to them from their kinsmen'. 'Geæþele', related to 'æþele', 'noble', here means 'inborn, natural', and 'cneomæg' is 'kinsman of a generation, ancestor'. The idea that courage is inherited through a royal line is central to the poem, which repeats the phrase 'Eadweardes afaran' at l. 52. 'Hord and hamas', 'treasure and homes' (l. 10), is an alliterating pair summing up what they defend. 'Ealgodon' is from 'ealgian', 'to defend'.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. eafora; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. cneomæg; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; Wikipedia, 'Edward the Elder'

  7. Scots and 'ship-sailors' l. 11

    'Sceotta leoda' means 'the people of the Scots'. 'Scipflotan' means 'ship-floaters', that is, seamen or vikings. Here they are the Hiberno-Norse of Dublin. Other sources show that the coalition included Owain of Strathclyde. 'Fæge' (l. 12) means 'fated to die, doomed', a word charged with heroic fatalism. 'Crungun' means 'fell (dead)', from 'cringan'. 'Feld dænnede / secga swate' says the field 'grew wet' (or 'resounded'; the verb is uncertain) with men's blood. The manuscripts spell the verb differently, and Campbell discusses its sense. 'Norþerna' (l. 18) and 'Scittisc' (l. 19) keep the two groups of enemies distinct.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. fæge; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. dennian

  8. The sun, 'God's bright candle' l. 15

    This is the poem's longest descriptive passage (ll. 13-17). 'Mære tungol' means 'glorious star'. 'Godes condel beorht, / eces drihtnes' is 'the bright candle of God, the eternal Lord', a kenning for the sun. 'Sio æþele gesceaft', 'the noble creation', is a further variation. 'Sah to setle' is 'sank to its seat', meaning set. 'Setl' is a seat, and the image is of the sun going to rest like a king to his throne or a man to his bed. The passage gives the battle a frame of a whole day ('ondlongne dæg', l. 21) and sets English victory within God's order. Beowulf calls the sun 'rodores candel' (l. 1572) and 'woruldcandel' (l. 1965), and 'candel' for a heavenly light is a common poetic usage.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. candel; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. tungol; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  9. eorodcistum – in troops; the pursuit l. 21

    'Eorodcist' combines 'eored', 'troop, band (perhaps of horsemen)' (literally 'horse-riding', from 'eoh' + 'rad'), with 'cist', 'company'. It may mean mounted squadrons, which would suit a long pursuit, but editors are cautious. 'On last legdun' means 'laid on the track (of)', that is, followed close behind. 'Heowan herefleman hindan þearle' is 'they hewed the army-fugitives from behind fiercely'. 'Mylenscearp' (l. 24), 'mill-sharp', is a sword sharpened on a grindstone and is unique to this poem. Heroic poetry usually shames flight, so the stress on fugitives cut down from behind belittles the enemy.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. eorodcist; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. mylenscearp; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  10. Myrce ne wyrndon – litotes and 'hand-play' l. 24

    'Myrce' are the Mercians. Naming them next to the West Saxons shows a joint English army, which fits Æthelstan's rule over both kingdoms. 'Ne wyrndon' ('did not refuse', from 'wyrnan') is litotes, typical of heroic style. 'Heardes hondplegan' means 'of hard hand-play'. 'Hondplega' is a kenning for combat, and 'plegan' returns ironically at l. 52 ('plegodan'), where the enemy 'played' with Edward's sons and lost. The poem has a run of such battle-kennings (ll. 49-51): 'cumbolgehnastes' ('clash of banners'), 'garmittinge' ('meeting of spears'), 'gumena gemotes' ('meeting of men'), 'wæpengewrixles' ('exchange of weapons'). Several of them are found only here.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. handplega; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. wyrnan; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes

  11. æra gebland – the sea's tumult l. 26

    'Gebland' is 'mixture, commotion, tumult' (compare 'blandan', 'to mix'). The first word is usually explained from the poetic noun 'ear', 'sea, ocean'. So the phrase means 'the mingling of the waters', that is, the sea in motion. The manuscripts and editions spell it differently. 'On lides bosme' means 'in the ship's bosom', with 'lid' a poetic word for ship, used again at l. 34 ('to lides stefne', 'to the ship's prow'). The invaders came 'fæge to gefeohte', 'doomed to the fight'. The line therefore looks forward to their ruin, and the sea they crossed is the sea they will flee across. 'Gesohtun' means 'sought, came to'.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. ear; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. gebland; E. V. K. Dobbie, ed., The Anglo-Saxon Minor Poems, ASPR 6 (New York, 1942), text and notes; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line

  12. Five young kings and seven earls l. 28

    'Lægun' is 'lay (dead)'. 'Cyningas giunge', 'young kings', are probably lesser Norse or Gaelic kings and sub-kings of the coalition. The poem names none of them, and other sources' lists cannot be reconciled with certainty. 'Sweordum aswefede' means 'put to sleep by swords', a euphemism for killing, from 'aswebban', 'to lull to sleep, kill'. The counting ('fife ... seofene ... unrim', 'countless') reads like an annal, but the euphemism is poetic. 'Campstede' means 'battlefield' ('camp', 'battle', from Latin 'campus'). 'Unrim heriges' means 'a countless number of the army'.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. aswebban

  13. Anlaf, 'chief of the Northmen', and his escape l. 33

    'Anlaf' is the English form of Old Norse 'Óláfr'. He was Óláfr Guthfrithson, king of Dublin, who claimed York. 'Bregu' (also 'brego') is a poetic word for 'prince, chief'. 'Nede gebeded' means 'compelled by necessity'. 'Litle weorode', 'with a little troop', echoes the heroic shame of a lord without followers. 'Cread cnear on flot' means 'the ship pressed out onto the water'. 'Cread' is from 'crudan', 'to press, hasten'. 'Cnear' (compare 'negledcnearrum', 'nail-studded ships', l. 53) is a loan from Old Norse 'knǫrr', a merchant or sea-going ship. 'Feorh generede', 'saved his life', is ironic: the leader survives as his men die. 'Fealene flod' means 'the fallow/dark flood'.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. cnear; Wikipedia, 'Olaf Guthfrithson'

  14. Constantine, the 'old grey warrior' l. 38

    Constantine son of Áed ruled Alba from about 900 until he became a monk at St Andrews in the 940s. He had submitted to Æthelstan in 927 and was invaded by him in 934, and he was the senior member of the 937 coalition. 'Se froda' ('the old, experienced one') and 'har hilderinc' ('grey-haired battle-man') stress his age. The poem then uses irony: 'hreman ne þorfte / mæca gemanan', he had no cause to exult in the fellowship of swords. 'He wæs his mæga sceard' means 'he was deprived of his kinsmen', with 'sceard', 'cut off from, bereft'. His son is 'wundun forgrunden', 'ground to pieces by wounds'.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. sceard; Wikipedia, 'Constantine II of Scotland'

  15. No cause to boast: the poem's taunting l. 44

    'Gelpan' (West Saxon 'gielpan') means 'to boast', and 'gylp' is a warrior's vow or boast in heroic verse. 'Ne þorfte' ('had no need') is used three times (ll. 39, 44, 47), each time about the enemy. It is ironic understatement: they had nothing to boast or laugh about. 'Beorn blandenfeax', 'grey-haired warrior', uses 'blanden-', 'mixed', for hair streaked with grey. 'Bilgeslehtes' means 'clash of swords' ('bill' + 'geslieht'). 'Eald inwidda' means 'old malicious enemy'. 'Hlehhan' ('to laugh', l. 47) refers to the enemy's hopes of victory. The phrase 'þæt heo beaduweorca beteran wurdun' ('that they were the better in battle-work') sums up the boast they cannot make. Critics often read the tone as close to the triumphal mockery of a victory song.

    A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. gielpan; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. blandenfeax

  16. Dinges mere – a crux of place l. 54

    'On Dinges mere' has been read in several ways. Some take it as the open sea ('mere', 'sea, lake'), with 'Dinges' as an obscure name or a corruption. Others see a place-name. Paul Cavill and others have connected it with the Norse assembly site ('þing') at Thingwall on the Wirral and a nearby stretch of water. That would support the identification of Brunanburh with Bromborough. Others remain unconvinced. 'Dreorig daraða laf' means 'the bloody remnant of spears', those whom the spears left alive. 'Darað' is 'spear, dart'. 'Æwiscmode' (l. 56) means 'ashamed, disgraced in mind'. 'Difelin' is Dublin, and 'Iraland' Ireland.

    M. Livingston, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook (Exeter, 2011); A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. æwiscmod; Wikipedia, 'Battle of Brunanburh'

  17. daraða laf – what the spears left l. 54

    'Darað' is a rare poetic word for 'spear, javelin'. It may be connected with Old Norse 'darraðr', though the relation is uncertain. 'Laf' (remnant, leavings) has already appeared in 'hamora lafan', 'leavings of hammers' (l. 6), meaning swords, and in 'herelafum', 'army-remnants' (l. 47). Here the defeated Norse are described as what is left over after the spears have done their work. Repeating the same word gives the poem an ironic symmetry. The English strike with what hammers made, and the enemy go home as what spears left. 'Negledcnearrum' (l. 53) means 'in nail-fastened ships', clinker-built vessels.

    J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. laf; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. daroþ; A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line

  18. The beasts of battle l. 60

    'Hræw bryttian' means 'to divide/enjoy the corpses' ('bryttian', 'to distribute, share out', as a lord shares treasure). The beasts are the black raven, dark-coated ('saluwigpada') and horny-beaked ('hyrnednebba'). Then there is the grey-coated ('hasewanpada') eagle, 'white behind' ('æftan hwit'), which is probably the white-tailed sea eagle, a 'greedy war-hawk' ('guðhafoc'). Last is 'the grey beast, the wolf in the forest'. 'Æses brucan', 'to enjoy the carrion', completes the grim feast. Magoun (1955) identified this trio as a recurring theme in Old English heroic poetry, found in Judith, Exodus, Maldon and elsewhere, and usually placed before or after battle. Here it marks the end of the battle. The descriptive compounds, several rare, are among the most elaborate examples of the theme.

    F. P. Magoun, Jr, 'The Theme of the Beasts of Battle in Anglo-Saxon Poetry', Neuphilologische Mitteilungen 56 (1955); A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. salowigpad; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. hasupad

  19. 'As books tell us': the coming of the English l. 68

    'Bec' are books, and 'ealde uðwitan' are 'old wise men, scholars, philosophers' ('uðwita' translates Latin 'philosophus'). The poet appeals to written history, not oral memory, and this suits a poem in a chronicle. The reference is to the 'Adventus Saxonum' as told by Bede (Historia ecclesiastica I.15) and the Chronicle annals for 449. The Angles and Saxons came 'eastan', from the east, over 'brad brimu', the broad seas, overcame the Britons ('Wealas'), and won the land ('eard begeatan'). They are 'wlance wigsmiþas' ('proud war-smiths') and 'eorlas arhwate' ('nobles eager for glory'). The ending sets Æthelstan's victory in a long history of conquest. Critics have noted the irony that the English, now defending their land against invaders, are celebrated as invaders themselves.

    Bede, Historia ecclesiastica I.15 (Latin text); A. Campbell, ed., The Battle of Brunanburh (London: Heinemann, 1938), note to the line; J. Bosworth and T. N. Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898; Supplement 1921), s.v. uþwita; B. Mitchell and F. C. Robinson, A Guide to Old English (Oxford: Blackwell, various editions), text and notes
