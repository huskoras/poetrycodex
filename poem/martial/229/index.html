Book X, Epigram 47: Ad Julium Martialem

by Martial c. 40–c. 104

Translated by R. Fletcher (1656) · From Ex Otio Negotium (1656)

Social Commentaries

Most pleasant Martial these are they
That make the happyer life and day,
Means not sweat for, but resign'd,
Fire without end, fields still in kinde,
No strife, no office, inward peace,
Free strength, a body sans disease,
A prudent plainesse, equal friends,
Cheap Cates, not scraped from the world's ends,
A night not drown'd, but free from care,
Sheets never sad, and yet chast are,
Sleep that makes short the shades of night,
Art such thou would'st be, if there might
A choice be offer'd, nor dost fear
Nor wish thy last dayes exit here.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Martial 10.47: the poem and this translation ll. 1-2

    Epigram 10.47 is addressed to Julius Martialis, Martial's closest friend, whose villa on the Janiculum he praises in 4.64 and to whom he writes in 1.15 and 5.20. The Latin is thirteen Phalaecean hendecasyllables: a list of nouns describing the happy life, ending with three wishes. Paley and Stone called it an epigram 'of the highest merit' in both form and matter. Post suggests Martial may have been answering a theme set by his friend. The archive gives the version by R. Fletcher in Ex Otio Negotium (1656), in rhymed octosyllabic couplets with seventeenth-century spelling ('happyer', 'plainesse', 'kinde'). Line references follow Fletcher's lines.

    F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library); R. Fletcher, trans., Ex Otio Negotium, or Martiall his Epigrams Translated (London, 1656), EEBO-TCP text

  2. 'the happyer life': a philosophical topic l. 2

    Beatior vita ('a happier life') recalls the philosophers' question about the beata vita, discussed by Cicero (Tusculan Disputations 5) and Seneca (De Vita Beata). Martial's answer borrows from both Epicurean and Stoic ideals: modest independence, a quiet mind, friends and no fear of death. But it is presented as a list of comforts rather than an argument. It was among the earliest of Martial's epigrams to be rendered into English, notably by Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey ('Martial, the things for to attain / The happy life be these, I find'), printed in Tottel's Miscellany (1557). Fletcher adds 'and day', which has no Latin equivalent and is there for the rhyme.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, 'The Means to Attain Happy Life', in Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557), Luminarium; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  3. 'Means not sweat for, but resign'd' l. 3

    The Latin is res non parta labore, sed relicta, 'property not won by labour but left (by inheritance)'. Post glosses res as money or estate, and relicta as 'inherited', from kinsfolk or friends. He cites Plato's Republic (330 B-C), where Socrates observes that those who inherit wealth care less for it than those who have made it. Paley and Stone give the same explanation: self-made men overvalue money and do not enjoy it. Fletcher's 'Means' is apt. 'Resign'd' uses resign in its old sense of 'to hand over, surrender to another' (OED, resign v.), which turns the bequest into a gift.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'resign'

  4. 'Fire without end' l. 4

    The Latin focus perennis means 'a hearth that lasts all year'. Paley and Stone explain it as a hearth well supplied throughout the year, either with fuel or with food, as we speak of keeping the kitchen fire going. Post calls it a metonymy for an unfailing supply of food and of the fuel to cook it, and compares Tibullus 1.1.5-6, where the poet wishes for a modest life with a hearth always alight. Fletcher's 'Fire without end' is literal and slightly grand, but it keeps the image of the burning hearth.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  5. 'fields still in kinde' l. 4

    The Latin is non ingratus ager, 'a not ungrateful field', that is, land that gives a good return. Post compares Cicero, On Old Age 51, on the earth that never refuses command and always repays with interest what it receives. Fletcher's phrase 'still in kinde' is harder. 'Still' means 'always' in older English. 'In kind' probably means 'according to their nature' (kind = nature), so that the fields keep producing as fields should. It could also mean 'in produce', the OED's sense of payment in goods rather than money. 'Kinde' is Fletcher's spelling.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kind'

  6. 'No strife, no office' l. 5

    The Latin is lis numquam, toga rara, mens quieta, 'never a lawsuit, the toga rarely, a quiet mind'. Post glosses lis as lawsuits and notes that the toga was costly, hot and heavy. It had to be worn at the games and, by clients, when they attended their patrons each morning. Martial often complains of the client's toga and praises the country, where it is not needed. Paley and Stone explain toga rara as the toga seldom required, in serving a patron or appearing in the forum. Fletcher replaces the garment with what it stood for: 'no office', no public duty.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  7. 'inward peace' l. 5

    Mens quieta, 'a quiet mind', is glossed by Post simply as 'free from worry'. The phrase recalls the philosophical goal of tranquillity, ataraxia in Epicurean terms or tranquillitas animi in Seneca's essay of that name. In Martial's list it comes after the removal of external troubles, lawsuits and duties, as though peace of mind followed from them. Fletcher's 'inward peace' is close, and it gives the phrase a slightly devotional colour, perhaps natural to an English poet writing in the 1650s, soon after the Civil War.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library); D. R. Shackleton Bailey, ed. and trans., Martial: Epigrams, Loeb Classical Library (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1993), 10.47

  8. 'Free strength, a body sans disease' l. 6

    The Latin is vires ingenuae, salubre corpus, 'free-born strength, a healthy body'. Post explains vires ingenuae as the strength a gentleman needs, not the brute force of a labourer, or possibly 'innate' strength. Paley and Stone render it 'constitutional strength'. Post notes the chiasmus (noun-adjective, adjective-noun) and compares Juvenal's mens sana in corpore sano (10.356). Fletcher's 'Free strength' keeps the social sense of ingenuus ('free-born'), and 'sans' is the French-derived word for 'without', common in English poetry (OED, sans prep.).

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sans'

  9. 'A prudent plainesse' l. 7

    The Latin prudens simplicitas means 'prudent simplicity', or as Paley and Stone put it, openness of character too discreet to give offence by telling unwelcome truths. Post compares Martial's praise of Romana simplicitas (11.20.10) and a 'simple' honest friend in 1.39. The phrase is almost an oxymoron, since simplicitas is guileless and prudens calculating. Fletcher's 'plainesse' (plainness) keeps the idea of plain dealing, and 'prudent' is the cognate of prudens.

    F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  10. 'equal friends' l. 7

    The Latin pares amici may mean friends equal in rank and wealth, on the ancient principle that true friendship exists only between equals. Or, as Post and Paley and Stone prefer, it may mean well-matched, congenial friends. Post cites Cicero's proverb that like readily gathers with like (On Old Age 7) and Horace's dinner invitation in Epistles 1.5, where he will see to it that equal is joined with equal. Fletcher's 'equal friends' keeps the ambiguity. Martial knew the alternative well: much of his work satirises the unequal friendship of patron and client.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  11. 'Cheap Cates, not scraped from the world's ends' l. 8

    The Latin is convictus facilis, sine arte mensa, 'easy company, a table without art'. Post explains this as a plain, old-fashioned dinner, plainly served, like the one Martial describes in the next epigram (10.48). Paley and Stone add that sine arte means without the elaborate creations of artistic cooks. Fletcher omits convictus facilis (good-natured guests) and expands the plain table. 'Cates' are choice foods or provisions (OED, cate n.). 'Not scraped from the world's ends' turns the absence of art into an attack on imported luxuries. That attack is a theme elsewhere in Martial and in Roman satire, but it is Fletcher's addition here.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cate'

  12. 'A night not drown'd, but free from care' l. 9

    The Latin is nox non ebria, sed soluta curis, 'a night not drunken, but loosed from cares'. Post reads this as just enough wine at the after-dinner drinking (comissatio) to forget the burdens of life. Paley and Stone say the cares are relieved by the social cup, not drowned in drink, quoting Propertius on drinking till dawn. Fletcher's 'not drown'd' supplies an image of drowning in wine where the Latin says simply 'not drunken'. 'Free from care' renders soluta curis well.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  13. 'Sheets never sad, and yet chast are' l. 10

    The Latin is non tristis torus, et tamen pudicus, 'a bed not severe, and yet chaste'. Torus ('couch') stands for the marriage-bed, and so for a wife. Paley and Stone gloss it as 'a wife who is virtuous without being a prude', and Post glosses tristis as 'prudish'. Ker's prose translation in the archive (martial/1210) also speaks of a wife. Fletcher keeps the bed ('Sheets') rather than the wife. His 'sad' is used in its older sense of 'serious, grave, sober' (OED, sad adj.), a good match for tristis. 'Chast' is his spelling of 'chaste'.

    F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sad'

  14. 'Sleep that makes short the shades of night' l. 11

    The Latin is somnus, qui faciat breves tenebras, 'sleep that makes the darkness short'. Paley and Stone explain it as a sleep so sound that the dark hours seem to pass quickly. Post calls it sound, unbroken sleep, the opposite of the sleepless nights Martial describes elsewhere, such as the noise of Rome in 12.57. The line closes the list of physical comforts. Fletcher's 'shades of night' is a poetic periphrasis for tenebras ('darkness').

    F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  15. 'Art such thou would'st be, if there might / A choice be offer'd' l. 12

    The Latin is quod sis esse velis nihilque malis, 'wish to be what you are, and prefer nothing'. Post notes the abrupt change from the list of nouns to subjunctives of wish. He argues that a list of the essentials of happiness is really a prayer for them, and compares Juvenal 10.356-62. Paley and Stone render 'to be willing to be what you are, and to prefer no other lot'. Fletcher turns the wish into a description: you already are what you would choose to be if you were offered a choice. The meaning is close, but he loses the imperative force. His syntax ('Art such thou would'st be') is compressed and needs careful reading.

    Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Martial, Epigrams, book 10, Latin text (The Latin Library)

  16. 'nor dost fear / Nor wish thy last dayes exit here' ll. 13-14

    The last line, summum nec metuas diem nec optes, 'neither fear your final day nor wish for it', is the poem's most quoted. Neither fearing death (the Epicurean lesson) nor longing for it (as a release from misery) is the balanced attitude of the sage. Paley and Stone explain nec optes with reference to tormenting diseases that might make a man wish for death. Fletcher's 'thy last dayes exit here' adds 'here', suggesting departure from this world. Surrey's version ends with the parallel advice to neither wish nor fear death.

    F. A. Paley and W. H. Stone, eds, M. Val. Martialis Epigrammata Selecta (London: Bell, 1890), Ep. 544 (10.47), pp. 346-7; Edwin Post, ed., Selected Epigrams of Martial (Boston: Ginn, 1908), notes on 10.47, pp. 251-2; Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, 'The Means to Attain Happy Life', in Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557), Luminarium; D. R. Shackleton Bailey, ed. and trans., Martial: Epigrams, Loeb Classical Library (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1993), 10.47
