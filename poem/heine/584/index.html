Mythology & Folklore

The Loreley

by Heinrich Heine 1797–1856

Translated by James Thomson (B.V.) (1880) · From Attempts at Translation from Heine (1880)

Mythology & Folklore

I KNOW not what evil is coming,
But my heart feels sad and cold ;
A song in my head keeps humming,
A tale from the times of old.

The air is fresh and it darkles,
And smoothly flows the Rhine ;
The peak of the mountain sparkles
In the fading sunset-shine.

I'he loveliest wonderful Maiden
On high is sitting there,
With golden jewels braiden,
And she combs her golden hair.

With a golden comb sits combing,
And ever the while sings she
A marvellous song through the gloaming
Of magical melody.

It hath caught the boatman, and bound him
In the spell of a wild sad love ;
He sees not the rocks around him,
He sees only her above.

The waves through the pass sweep swinging,
But boatman or boat is none ;
And this with her mighty singing
The LoRELEY hath done.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, source and translator l. 1

    Heine wrote the poem in 1824; it appeared as the second poem of the cycle Die Heimkehr ('The Homecoming') and was collected in Buch der Lieder (1827), the book that made his name. It has no title in Heine; 'Die Lorelei' or 'Die Lore-Ley' is a title given by editors and composers. The English version in the archive is by the Scottish poet James Thomson ('B.V.', 1834-82), author of The City of Dreadful Night; it appeared among his 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' in The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (1880). Thomson admired Heine's mixture of lyric grief and irony, and his versions keep Heine's stanza closely.

    'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim; Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  2. what evil is coming l. 1

    Heine opens: 'Ich weiß nicht, was soll es bedeuten, / Daß ich so traurig bin' ('I don't know what it should mean / that I am so sad'). The speaker cannot explain his sadness, and the old tale that haunts him becomes the explanation he half offers. Thomson's 'what evil is coming' adds a sense of foreboding that is not in the German and makes the speaker a prophet of disaster. It also loses the colloquial, faintly self-mocking turn of 'was soll es bedeuten', which is the first hint of Heine's ironic distance from his own Romantic mood. The frame matters: the poem is about a modern man haunted by a 'tale from the times of old', not a simple retelling of a legend.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; S. S. Prawer, Heine: The Tragic Satirist. A Study of the Later Poetry 1827-1856 (Cambridge UP, 1961), on Heine's irony and the Romantic lyric; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  3. A tale from the times of old l. 4

    The 'ancient tale' is less ancient than it claims. The Lorelei rock had long been feared for its currents and its echo, but the enchantress Lore Lay was largely created by Clemens Brentano in a ballad in his novel Godwi (1801), where she is a beautiful woman, wrongly accused of witchcraft, who throws herself from the rock. Later Romantic writers, among them Otto von Loeben (1821), turned her into a siren on the rock, and Heine's poem fixed that image. Heine's 'Märchen' (fairy-tale) and 'uralten' (age-old) thus play the Romantic game of the invented folk tradition, a game he knew well.

    'Lorelei', Wikipedia, on the rock, its name and its legend; 'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim; Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II

  4. darkles l. 5

    Heine writes 'Die Luft ist kühl und es dunkelt' ('the air is cool and it grows dark'). Thomson's 'darkles' imitates the German impersonal verb 'es dunkelt' with an English verb that the OED treats as a back-formation from the adverb 'darkling' ('in the dark'), used by nineteenth-century poets in the senses 'to grow dark' or 'to lie in darkness'. It is a deliberately literary word, giving the line an archaic shimmer while staying close to Heine's construction. Note also that Thomson's 'fresh' softens Heine's 'kühl' (cool, chill), which links the evening air to the speaker's 'cold' heart in line 2 (Heine's 'traurig', sad).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'darkle'; Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  5. the Rhine; the peak of the mountain ll. 6-8

    The rock stands at one of the narrowest and deepest points of the Middle Rhine gorge, where strong currents and submerged rocks wrecked boats for centuries; it was also known for its echo. The name is usually explained from a dialect word for murmuring ('lur') and 'Ley', a Rhineland word for rock, so 'murmuring rock'; another theory links it to 'lauern', to lurk. Heine's calm, smoothly flowing river ('ruhig fließt der Rhein') and the summit lit by the setting sun create a peaceful scene that the last stanza overturns. The area is now part of the Upper Middle Rhine Valley World Heritage site.

    'Lorelei', Wikipedia, on the rock, its name and its legend; 'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim; Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II

  6. The loveliest wonderful Maiden ll. 9-10

    Thomson keeps the oddity of Heine's syntax: 'the most beautiful maiden sits up there wonderfully'. The German 'wunderbar' can describe either the maiden or the manner of her sitting, and it carries its old sense of something marvellous, uncanny. Thomson's piling of 'loveliest' and 'wonderful' before the noun preserves the sense of excess. The archive's text begins the line 'I'he', a scanning error for 'The'; the word is quoted here as it stands. 'Jungfrau' (virgin, maiden) also gives her a fairy-tale innocence that contrasts with her deadly power.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  7. braiden l. 11

    Heine's line gives the jewellery a quick flash of light ('blitzet'). Thomson needed a rhyme for 'Maiden' and coined or revived 'braiden', modelled on old participles in -en such as 'golden', 'oaken' and the archaic 'braided'. It suggests jewels braided into her hair or woven into a band, an image not in the German but consistent with the following combing. The device is typical of Victorian translation, which often used archaic forms to signal that a poem belonged to legend; but it sits a little stiffly beside the simplicity of Heine's diction.

    James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record); Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'braid, v.1'

  8. combs her golden hair ll. 12-13

    The figure combining beauty, gold and a comb belongs to the iconography of mermaids and of German water-spirits (Nixen), who were said to sit on rocks combing their hair. Heine uses 'golden' three times in two lines ('goldnes Geschmeide', 'goldenes Haar', 'goldenem Kamme'), so that the repeated word and the repeated action ('kämmt', 'kämmt es') imitate the slow rhythm of combing and the spell itself. Thomson keeps all three golds and adds 'combing' as a rhyme word. The siren behind the image recalls Homer's Sirens, whose song lures sailors to destruction (Odyssey 12), though Heine's maiden, unlike Homer's, seems unaware of her victim.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; Homer, Odyssey 12.39-54, 12.165-200 (the Sirens); 'Lorelei', Wikipedia, on the rock, its name and its legend

  9. gloaming l. 15

    The OED records 'gloaming' as Scots and northern English for evening twilight, taken into general literary English through Scottish poets such as Burns. Thomson, born at Port Glasgow, uses it as a rhyme for 'combing' and so sets the song at dusk, consistent with Heine's darkening air and the sunset in stanza 2. Heine's lines are different: 'Das hat eine wundersame, / Gewaltige Melodei' ('it has a wondrous, mighty melody'). Thomson's 'marvellous song through the gloaming' thus adds atmosphere where Heine stresses the power of the melody.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gloaming'; Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  10. magical melody l. 16

    The German word 'Melodei' was already archaic in 1824, a form found in folk songs, and Heine uses it to give a popular, sung quality to his line. Its adjective, 'gewaltige', means overwhelming, violent in force, and is related to 'Gewalt' (force); the music is not merely magical but overpowering. Thomson's 'magical' suggests enchantment but loses the sense of brute force; he restores it in the last stanza with 'her mighty singing', which renders Heine's simpler 'mit ihrem Singen'. So Thomson redistributes Heine's emphasis rather than dropping it.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record); 'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim

  11. the boatman ll. 17-20

    The fifth stanza turns from the maiden to her victim. In Heine the song 'seizes' ('ergreift', also 'moves deeply') the boatman in his little boat 'with wild woe'; he does not look at the reefs ('Felsenriffe') but only up to the heights. Thomson's 'bound him / In the spell of a wild sad love' makes the effect an enchantment and adds the word 'love', which Heine leaves implicit. The upward gaze, away from the danger below, is the psychological centre of the poem: the boatman is destroyed by looking at beauty, a figure that Heine's readers took as an image of the lover, and of the Romantic poet himself.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record); S. S. Prawer, Heine: The Tragic Satirist. A Study of the Later Poetry 1827-1856 (Cambridge UP, 1961), on Heine's irony and the Romantic lyric

  12. He sees only her above l. 20

    The poem's space is vertical: maiden 'dort oben' (up there) on the summit lit by sunset; boatman below on the river among reefs. Heine's line says only that he looks 'up into the height', without naming her, which makes the gaze itself the fatal act. Thomson's 'He sees only her above' names her and so makes the line more explicit, but keeps the contrast of 'rocks around him' and the figure above. Students might compare the sirens of Odyssey 12, where Odysseus survives by being bound to the mast; Heine's boatman has no such protection.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  13. But boatman or boat is none ll. 21-22

    Heine's last stanza returns to the speaker: 'I believe the waves will swallow, in the end, boatman and boat; and that, with her singing, the Lore-Ley has done.' The hedging 'Ich glaube' and the flat 'am Ende' (in the end) are characteristic of Heine's irony, a sudden cooling of the Romantic spell into a conversational shrug; critics such as Prawer have made this double tone central to Heine's lyric manner. Others read the poem straight, as a pure folk-song of fatal beauty, which is how it was received when set to music. Thomson's 'The waves through the pass sweep swinging, / But boatman or boat is none' gives a vivid image of empty water and loses the speaker's ironic voice; it reads the poem as tragic rather than ironic.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; S. S. Prawer, Heine: The Tragic Satirist. A Study of the Later Poetry 1827-1856 (Cambridge UP, 1961), on Heine's irony and the Romantic lyric; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  14. with her mighty singing l. 23

    The final couplet makes the Lorelei responsible for the wreck: 'Und das hat mit ihrem Singen / Die Lore-Ley getan' ('and that, with her singing, the Lore-Ley has done'). The grammar echoes stanza 4 ('Das hat eine wundersame, / Gewaltige Melodei'), and Thomson highlights the echo by bringing 'mighty' (gewaltig) forward to the end. Heine's 'getan' (done) is plain, almost legal, as though stating a culprit's guilt, and the poem ends on her name.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  15. The Loreley l. 24

    The name joins 'Lore' (read by Brentano as a woman's name) with 'Ley', a Rhineland word for slate rock; the place-name itself probably meant something like 'murmuring rock'. Brentano's 'Lore Lay' made the rock a woman; Heine's 'Lore-Ley' with hyphen keeps both woman and rock in view. Thomson printed the final word in capitals and small capitals, which the archive reproduces as 'LoRELEY'. Placing the name as the poem's last word gives it the effect of a signature, or an accusation.

    'Lorelei', Wikipedia, on the rock, its name and its legend; 'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record)

  16. Stanza and metre st. 1

    Heine adopts the stanza of the German Volkslied, which the Romantics had revived: lines of three stresses with free unstressed syllables, rhyming abab, with feminine (two-syllable) rhymes in lines 1 and 3 and masculine in 2 and 4 (bedeuten/Zeiten, bin/Sinn). The simple measure makes the poem singable and was one reason for its success as a song. Thomson matches the pattern closely, with feminine rhymes such as 'coming/humming', 'darkles/sparkles', 'combing/gloaming', 'swinging/singing'. The song-like form is itself part of the theme: a poem about a fatal song that is itself a song.

    Heine, Buch der Lieder, ed. Pierre Grappin, Düsseldorfer Heine-Ausgabe (DHA), vol. 1 (Hamburg: Hoffmann und Campe, 1975), commentary to Die Heimkehr II; James Thomson ('B.V.'), The City of Dreadful Night and Other Poems (London: Reeves and Turner, 1880), 'Attempts at Translation from Heine' (Morgan Library record); 'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim

  17. Silcher's song and the Nazi era whole poem

    Friedrich Silcher's simple strophic setting (1837) became one of the most popular of all German songs, and more than forty settings of the text were made in the nineteenth century, among them Liszt (1841) and Clara Schumann (1843). Because Heine was Jewish, it was claimed (by Walter Berendsohn, and repeated by Herbert Eulenberg in 1947) that the Nazis kept the poem in anthologies but attributed it to an unknown poet ('Dichter unbekannt'). The German Wikipedia article on the poem reports that studies of Nazi-era school books and anthologies found no evidence for this, though song collections have not yet been studied. Students should treat the anecdote as a powerful legend about the poem's reception, not an established fact.

    'Die Lore-Ley', Wikipedia (German), on date, Brentano, Silcher and the 'Dichter unbekannt' claim; 'Lorelei', Wikipedia, on the rock, its name and its legend

  18. Heine and the Rhine Romantics st. 1

    Brentano and Achim von Arnim's anthology Des Knaben Wunderhorn (1805-08) and Brentano's own ballads made the Rhine a landscape of legend for German Romanticism. Heine learned much of his lyric idiom from this tradition, yet his poetry often stands slightly outside it, as a modern man who feels its spell and sees through it. The opening admission of unexplained sadness, the 'Märchen aus uralten Zeiten', and the hedging 'Ich glaube' at the end place the legend in the mind of the speaker, and some critics read the poem as about the seductive power of Romantic poetry itself. The archive also holds Thomson's other Heine Rhine poem, 'In the Rhine, in the beautiful river' (heine/587).
