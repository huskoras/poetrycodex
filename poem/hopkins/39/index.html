The Mind

Carrion Comfort

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

The Mind

NOT, I'll not, carrion comfort, Despair, not feast on thee;
Not untwist--slack they may be--these last strands of man
In me ór, most weary, cry I can no more. I can;
Can something, hope, wish day come, not choose not to be.
But ah, but O thou terrible, why wouldst thou rude on me
Thy wring-world right foot rock? lay a lionlimb against
me? scan
With darksome devouring eyes my bruisèd bones? and fan,
O in turns of tempest, me heaped there; me frantic to
avoid thee and flee?

Why? That my chaff might fly; my grain lie, sheer
and clear.
Nay in all that toil, that coil, since (seems) I kissed the rod,
Hand rather, my heart lo! lapped strength, stole joy,
would laugh, chéer.
Cheer whom though? the hero whose heaven-handling
flung me, fóot tród
Me? or me that fought him? O which one? is it each
one? That night, that year
Of now done darkness I wretch lay wrestling with (my
God!) my God.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and the 'terrible sonnets' l. 1

    Hopkins left the poem untitled. Robert Bridges named it from its first line when he published it in 1918. It belongs to the group of sonnets of desolation written in 1885, while Hopkins was Professor of Greek at University College Dublin, isolated, overworked and depressed. On 17 May 1885 he wrote to Bridges that he had after long silence written two sonnets, and that if anything was ever written in blood one of them was. He does not name them, and 'Carrion Comfort' is one of the candidates proposed. 'Carrion comfort' is the comfort of feeding on dead flesh. Despair is personified as a corpse the speaker is tempted to feast on, a 'comfort' that is really death. In Catholic theology despair is a sin against hope.

    Robert Bridges (ed.), Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Humphrey Milford, 1918), poem and notes; C. C. Abbott (ed.), The Letters of Gerard Manley Hopkins to Robert Bridges (Oxford UP, 1935), letter of 17 May 1885; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  2. Form: the long-lined sonnet ll. 1–14

    The poem keeps the Italian sonnet's rhyme scheme and octave–sestet turn. But its lines are stretched to six or more stresses, with extra slack syllables and spill-over (shown in the archive by indented continuations such as 'me? scan'). Hopkins's stress marks (ór, chéer, fóot tród) tell the reader where to put the weight. The jostling, broken syntax of repetitions, questions and parentheses enacts the inner struggle it describes. The volta comes at line 9: 'Why? That my chaff might fly'.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  3. Last strands of man l. 2

    The image is of a rope or cord whose strands hold a man together. Despair would untwist them and leave nothing. The parenthesis '(slack they may be)' admits weakness but holds on. The verb 'untwist' gives the refusal a physical, hand-to-hand quality, preparing for the wrestling of the close. The sequence of negatives at the start ('NOT, I'll not ... Not untwist ... not choose not to be') makes refusal itself an act of resistance.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  4. I can no more. I can l. 3

    'I can no more' is the cry of the exhausted. Hopkins puts it on the speaker's lips only to reverse it in two words: 'I can'. Line 4 then specifies how little can be done: 'Can something, hope, wish day come, not choose not to be'. The double negative of 'not choose not to be' is a refusal of suicide, which recalls Hamlet's 'To be, or not to be' (3.1). The speaker's resistance amounts only to not choosing death, but the poem treats that as a real victory.

    Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1.64 (Folger Shakespeare); Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  5. O thou terrible l. 5

    With 'But ah, but O thou terrible' the address changes. Most critics identify the terrible one as God, whose overwhelming power the speaker experiences as assault, much as in Job. The sestet confirms this when the opponent is named 'my God'. A few readers hear Despair still being addressed in the octave, which would make the poem's identification of the enemy part of its drama. Since the speaker is unsure whether his tormentor is a demon or the God who chastens, he cannot tell despair from purgation.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Michael Tackeff, 'Jacob, The Angel, & Hopkins' (student essay for a Brown University course, 2011), Victorian Web

  6. rude (as verb) l. 5

    The syntax is contorted: 'why wouldst thou rude on me / Thy wring-world right foot rock?' The usual reading is 'why would you roughly rock your world-wringing right foot on me?'. 'Rude' is an adjective made into a verb or adverb, meaning to act rudely, roughly. The heavy alliteration (r, w) and clustered stresses make the line feel like crushing weight. Editors disagree about the exact grammar, and Hopkins's compressions often allow more than one construction.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Carrion Comfort'

  7. Wring-world right foot l. 6

    'Wring-world' is a compound epithet: the foot that wrings, twists or crushes the world. It recalls biblical images of enemies made a footstool and the victor's foot on the neck of the defeated (Joshua 10:24; Psalm 110:1). One student essay on the Victorian Web notes that the foot is likened to a rock. The 'right' foot and 'right' hand carry biblical associations of divine power.

    Michael Tackeff, 'Jacob, The Angel, & Hopkins' (student essay for a Brown University course, 2011), Victorian Web; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 110:1; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  8. Lionlimb l. 6

    Job laments that God hunts him 'as a fierce lion' (Job 10:16), and the prophets compare God to a lion tearing his people (Hosea 13:7–8). Hopkins's compound 'lionlimb' makes the divine attacker a beast whose heavy paw is laid on the speaker. The following questions continue the predatory image: God scans him 'With darksome devouring eyes' and looks at his 'bruisèd bones' (an echo of the Psalmist's 'bones which thou hast broken', Psalm 51:8). The poem's whole octave is a Joblike complaint against God's apparent cruelty.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 10:16; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 51:8; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  9. Fan: the winnowing fan ll. 7–8

    At first 'fan, / O in turns of tempest, me heaped there' sounds like blowing on a heap of something. The next line explains it. A fan is the winnowing fan or basket used to toss threshed grain into the wind so the light chaff blows away. John the Baptist says of Christ, 'Whose fan is in his hand, and he will throughly purge his floor, and gather his wheat into the garner; but he will burn up the chaff' (Matthew 3:12). The speaker lies 'heaped' like grain on a threshing-floor, battered by a tempest of winnowing.

    The Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:12; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fan, v.'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  10. Chaff and grain l. 9

    The sestet begins with the answer to the octave's questions. The suffering has a purpose: 'That my chaff might fly; my grain lie, sheer and clear'. 'Sheer' here means pure, unmixed. The image of winnowing, prepared by 'fan', turns the apparent cruelty into purification, in line with traditional Christian accounts of affliction as God's discipline (Hebrews 12:6). The tone changes from complaint to understanding, though the closing lines show the understanding to be hard-won and unsettled.

    The Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:12; The Bible (KJV), Hebrews 12:6; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  11. Kissed the rod l. 10

    The proverbial expression 'to kiss the rod' meant to accept chastisement meekly, as a child kisses the rod it is beaten with. It appears, for example, in Shakespeare's Two Gentlemen of Verona (1.2). Hopkins's parenthetical '(seems)' makes the submission uncertain, as though he cannot be sure that he has truly accepted God's will. 'Toil' and 'coil' (turmoil, as in Hamlet's 'mortal coil') rhyme internally to describe the struggle.

    Shakespeare, The Two Gentlemen of Verona 1.2 (Folger Shakespeare); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rod, n.'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  12. Hand rather l. 11

    'Hand rather' corrects the previous phrase. Kissing the hand instead of the rod shifts the stress from the instrument of punishment to the person who punishes, and the gesture becomes one of homage or love, like kissing a lord's or a priest's hand. With that the speaker's heart is renewed. It 'lapped strength, stole joy, would laugh, chéer', a cascade of verbs that recovers vitality. 'Lapped' means drank, like an animal lapping water.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'

  13. lapped strength, stole joy l. 11

    'Lapped' means drank by lapping, with an animal eagerness. Some readers recall Gideon's soldiers who 'lapped' water from their hands and were chosen for battle (Judges 7:5–7), though the echo is uncertain. 'Stole joy' suggests a happiness taken almost guiltily in the midst of suffering. The stress mark on 'chéer' prepares for the question in l. 12, 'Cheer whom though?', which turns the word over and examines it.

    The Bible (KJV), Judges 7:5; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  14. Cheer whom though? ll. 12–13

    The question unsettles the moment of joy. Is the heart to applaud 'the hero whose heaven-handling flung me' (God, whose heavenly strength threw him down, 'fóot tród / Me') or 'me that fought him'? The answer, 'is it each one?', gives credit to both sides. This is the paradox of a struggle in which being defeated by God is also victory. Critics compare the Holy Sonnets of Donne, where the speaker asks God to batter and overthrow him. 'Heaven-handling' is a compound coinage for divine grip and dexterity.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Carrion Comfort'

  15. Wrestling with my God ll. 13–14

    In Genesis 32:24–30 Jacob wrestles all night with a mysterious man who turns out to be God ('I have seen God face to face'). He is lamed but blessed. Hopkins's ending recalls this. 'That night, that year / Of now done darkness' places the struggle in the past ('now done'), but 'I wretch' keeps its misery present. The last words are carefully punctuated. The first '(my God!)' is an exclamation of horror, the second 'my God' is the identification of the opponent, God, who is still 'my' God. The pairing is widely seen as the poem's central paradox: the enemy is the beloved Lord.

    The Bible (KJV), Genesis 32:24; Michael Tackeff, 'Jacob, The Angel, & Hopkins' (student essay for a Brown University course, 2011), Victorian Web; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Carrion Comfort'
