Death & Dying

Felix Randal

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Death & Dying

FELIX RANDAL the farrier, O he is dead then? my duty all ended,
Who have watched his mould of man, big-boned and hardy-
handsome
Pining, pining, till time when reason rambled in it and some
Fatal four disorders, fleshed there, all contended?

Sickness broke him. Impatient he cursed at first, but mended
Being anointed and all; though a heavenlier heart began some
Months earlier, since I had our sweet reprieve and ransom
Tendered to him. Ah well, God rest him all road ever he
offended!

This seeing the sick endears them to us, us too it endears.
My tongue had taught thee comfort, touch had quenched thy tears,
Thy tears that touched my heart, child, Felix, poor Felix Randal;

How far from then forethought of, all thy more boisterous years,
When thou at the random grim forge, powerful amidst peers,
Didst fettle for the great grey drayhorse his bright and battering
sandal!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Felix Randal: occasion and date l. 1

    Hopkins was a curate at the Jesuit church of St Francis Xavier in Liverpool from the end of 1879 to 1881, ministering in poor districts. Parish records studied by Alfred Thomas, S.J., identify the man behind the poem as Felix Spencer, a farrier who died, probably of pulmonary tuberculosis, on 21 April 1880; Hopkins had attended him in his illness. The manuscript is dated 28 April 1880. Hopkins altered the surname, perhaps for discretion; the new name also supplies the rhyme with 'sandal' that closes the poem, and Cotter suggests an echo of the ballad 'Lord Randal'. Like nearly all his mature poems it was unpublished in his lifetime, first appearing in Robert Bridges's edition of 1918. The Latin name Felix means 'happy' or 'fortunate', a resonance the poem's last lines seem to exploit.

    James Finn Cotter, article on 'Felix Randal' (Project MUSE), citing Alfred Thomas, S.J., on the parish records of St Francis Xavier's, Liverpool; Alfred Thomas, S.J., Hopkins the Jesuit: The Years of Training (Oxford University Press, 1969); Ian Lancashire, ed., 'Felix Randal', Representative Poetry Online, text from MacKenzie, ed., The Later Poetic Manuscripts of G. M. Hopkins in Facsimile (Garland, 1991); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  2. farrier l. 1

    A farrier (Old French ferrier, from Latin ferrarius, 'of iron') is a blacksmith who specialises in shoeing horses; in the nineteenth century the trade also covered horse-doctoring. In industrial Liverpool, with its huge traffic of carts and dray horses, it was skilled and physically demanding work. The occupation gives the poem its controlling contrast: the powerful craftsman of iron, remembered in the last line, against the wasted invalid of the octave.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. farrier, n.; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020)

  3. O he is dead then? my duty all ended l. 1

    The poem begins in the middle of a conversation, as if the speaker has just been told of the death. 'My duty all ended' recalls that the priest's pastoral obligations to the dying man are now over. Readers differ on the tone: some find it detached, even brusque, a professional's acknowledgement of a case closed; others hear the shock and fatigue of a priest who had watched the man's long decline, and a self-reproach that the poem goes on to correct. The colloquial interjection 'O' and the questioning 'then?' mark it as speech, not formal elegy.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  4. his mould of man, big-boned and hardy-handsome l. 2

    A 'mould' is a form or pattern from which something is cast, and so, by extension, a person's physical build. The phrase presents Felix as a fine specimen of a man, as if cast in a strong pattern. 'Hardy-handsome' is a characteristic Hopkins compound, fusing toughness and beauty. Some readers also hear 'mould' as earth or soil, anticipating the grave, though this sense is not certain. The archive text breaks the compound across a line ('hardy- / handsome'), reflecting the long lines of the manuscript.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. mould, n.3; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  5. Pining, pining, till time when reason rambled l. 3

    'Pining' means wasting away through illness or suffering, and the repetition stretches out the long decline of tuberculosis (the length of Felix Spencer's illness is not firmly documented). 'Reason rambled' describes the delirium of the last stage: the mind wandered within the body ('in it', referring to the 'mould of man'). The alliteration of r and the internal rhyme 'rambled ... some' keep the sound dense even in a line of plain statement.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); James Finn Cotter, article on 'Felix Randal' (Project MUSE), citing Alfred Thomas, S.J., on the parish records of St Francis Xavier's, Liverpool; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  6. some / Fatal four disorders, fleshed there, all contended l. 4

    Hopkins does not name the 'four disorders'. The most literal reading takes them as several fatal illnesses or complications at once, such as tuberculosis with its attendant conditions. Others have proposed a symbolic sense: the four humours of older medicine, whose balance was thought to constitute health, or the four elements of which the body was composed. 'Fleshed there' means established, embedded in the flesh, and may also carry the hunting sense of 'to flesh' a hound, to give it a taste of blood. 'All contended' turns the body into a battlefield, preparing for the next line, 'Sickness broke him'.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. flesh, v.; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  7. Sickness broke him. Impatient he cursed at first l. 5

    The short declarative sentence is a deliberate contrast to the sprawling syntax of the first quatrain. 'Broke' fits both a body and a horse: the man who shod horses has himself been broken, as one breaks in a horse. Felix at first 'cursed' in impatience, then 'mended', not in health but in spirit, after receiving the last rites. The movement from rebellion to acceptance is the spiritual story the priest is able to tell, and it underlies the poem's final note of hope.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  8. Being anointed and all l. 6

    In Catholic practice the dying are anointed with holy oil by a priest, following James 5:14 ('Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord'). In Hopkins's time this was called Extreme Unction. The casual 'and all' imitates popular speech, as if the speaker were explaining matters to a parishioner, and it understates the sacrament's importance. The 'heavenlier heart' had begun even earlier, when he had first received Communion during his illness.

    Bible, King James Version, James 5:14; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  9. our sweet reprieve and ransom l. 7

    The phrase is Hopkins's periphrasis for the Eucharist, which the priest 'tendered' (offered) to the sick man some months before his death. 'Reprieve' is a legal term for the suspension of a death sentence; 'ransom' echoes Christ's saying that the Son of Man came 'to give his life a ransom for many' (Matthew 20:28; Mark 10:45). 'Our' marks it as the shared possession of the faithful, priest and parishioner together. The alliteration and the warm adjective 'sweet' give the sacrament tenderness amid the poem's harder sounds.

    Bible, King James Version, Matthew 20:28; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  10. God rest him all road ever he offended! l. 8

    'All road' (also 'all roads', 'any road') is a northern English dialect expression meaning 'in every way, however'. The prayer thus means 'God rest him, in whatever way he ever offended'. Hopkins took a keen interest in dialect and deliberately adopts the idiom of his Lancashire parishioners here, placing a popular phrase inside the ritual formula 'God rest him'. The effect is both affectionate and humble: the priest speaks the dead man's language.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. road, n. (all roads, any road); Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  11. This seeing the sick endears them to us l. 9

    Line 9 marks the volta of this Petrarchan sonnet (octave abbaabba, sestet ccdccd). The priest generalises: the act of visiting the sick makes them dear to us, and 'us too it endears' makes us dear to them, or makes us better. The inverted repetition (chiasmus) enacts reciprocity. The next line reverses the roles: 'My tongue had taught thee comfort, touch had quenched thy tears, / Thy tears that touched my heart'. The repetition of 'tears' and 'touch' ties the giver and receiver of comfort together. This answers the apparent coldness of 'my duty all ended'.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  12. child, Felix, poor Felix Randal l. 11

    Until line 9 Felix is spoken of in the third person; from line 10 ('My tongue had taught thee comfort') the priest addresses him with the intimate 'thee' and 'thy', and line 11 names him. 'Child' expresses the spiritual fatherhood of the priest ('Father' Hopkins), and it is poignant because the big-boned farrier has become, in illness, as helpless as a child. The repeated name, with its gradual lengthening ('child, Felix, poor Felix Randal'), has the rhythm of lament.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  13. How far from then forethought of, all thy more boisterous years l. 12

    The line is typically condensed. Paraphrased, it says: in all your more boisterous years, how far was this ending from being thought of beforehand. 'Forethought of' is a participle (foreseen) and 'then' refers to the time of his strength. 'Boisterous' suggests noisy, rough, high-spirited energy. The sestet moves back in time from deathbed to the forge, reversing the expected elegiac progression from life to death so that the poem ends on the man's power rather than his decline.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  14. the random grim forge l. 13

    In building, 'random' describes masonry of stones of irregular size and shape, not laid in courses ('random rubble'); Hopkins, alert to technical vocabularies, probably intends this sense of a rough-built smithy. The more common sense, 'haphazard, untidy', is also present, and the word may echo 'Randal'. 'Grim' gives the smoky, dark forge a fierce character. The forge is where Felix was 'powerful amidst peers', the strong man among his fellow workers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. random, adj.; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020)

  15. Didst fettle l. 14

    'Fettle' (from Middle English, of uncertain origin, related to an old word for a girdle) means to make ready, trim or put in good order, and survives in northern English dialect and in the phrase 'in fine fettle'. Here it describes the farrier forging and fitting a shoe. The archaic 'Didst' and 'thou' sit beside the dialect verb, a typical Hopkins mixture of the solemn and the vernacular.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. fettle, v.; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020)

  16. the great grey drayhorse l. 14

    A dray is a strong low cart without sides, used especially by brewers and for dock haulage; the large horses that pulled them were among the most familiar sights of Victorian Liverpool. The alliteration of 'great grey' and 'drayhorse' adds weight to the line. The horse's size and strength mirror Felix's own, and the final image puts the two great bodies together in the work of the forge.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. dray, n.1; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020)

  17. his bright and battering sandal l. 14

    Calling a horseshoe a 'sandal' is surprising: it elevates the horse's iron shoe into something classical or even heroic, and its brightness suggests the light of the poem's close. 'Battering' looks forward to the shoe's work on the cobbled streets. The word supplies the delayed rhyme with 'Randal' (line 11), so that the sestet's c-rhyme closes on the man's name transformed into his craft. Many readers see in the bright final image a resurrection hint: the farrier, like the shoe he forged, is now made 'bright', or remembered in the radiance of his strength.

    Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  18. Sprung rhythm and the long sonnet line l. 1

    Hopkins's 'sprung rhythm', explained in the Author's Preface printed by Bridges in 1918, counts stresses, allowing a variable number of unstressed syllables between them. 'Felix Randal' uses long lines, often of six stresses, closer to the alexandrine than to the pentameter, which allows the colloquial, prose-like movement of the octave. The rhyme scheme is nevertheless strict Petrarchan (abbaabba ccdccd), with feminine and multi-syllable rhymes ('ended / contended', 'some / handsome', 'endears / tears'). The tension between the strict rhymes and the free, speaking rhythm is central to the poem's effect.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), 'Author's Preface' and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary; Oliver Tearle, 'A Short Analysis of Gerard Manley Hopkins's "Felix Randal"', Interesting Literature (2020)
