God's Grandeur

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Religion

THE world is charged with the grandeur of God.
It will flame out, like shining from shook foil;
It gathers to a greatness, like the ooze of oil
Crushed. Why do men then now not reck his rod?
Generations have trod, have trod, have trod;
And all is seared with trade; bleared, smeared with
toil;
And wears man's smudge and shares man's smell:
the soil
Is bare now, nor can foot feel, being shod.

And for all this, nature is never spent;
There lives the dearest freshness deep down things;
And though the last lights off the black West went
Oh, morning, at the brown brink eastward, springs--
Because the Holy Ghost over the bent
World broods with warm breast and with ah!
bright wings.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, place and form l. 1

    Hopkins wrote the poem while studying theology at St Beuno's College, the Jesuit seminary in the Vale of Clwyd, in the year of his ordination (September 1877). Bridges records two autographs, dated 23 February and March 1877, and Hopkins's own description 'Standard rhythm counterpoised', i.e. basically regular iambic pentameter with reversed feet. It was one of the sonnets he sent to his mother for her birthday that March. Like all his mature poems it remained unpublished until Bridges' edition of 1918. The form is a strict Petrarchan sonnet (abbaabba cdcdcd): an octave on God's presence in the world and man's blindness to it, a sestet on nature's inexhaustible renewal.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Gerard Manley Hopkins Society, lecture on 'God's Grandeur' (2016), drawing on Norman White's biography; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary

  2. charged l. 1

    The primary image is electrical: the world is charged as a battery or Leyden jar is charged, holding a force that can discharge suddenly, as it does in the next line ('flame out'). Hopkins was keenly interested in contemporary science, and the line puts Victorian physics at the service of natural theology. A second sense is moral or legal: to be 'charged with' something is to be entrusted with it, so the world is also commissioned to show forth God's glory. Compare Psalm 19:1, 'The heavens declare the glory of God'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'charge, v.'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:1

  3. shook foil l. 2

    'Foil' here is metal leaf, not the fencing sword. When Bridges queried the line, Hopkins replied (letter of 4 January 1883) that he meant foil 'in its sense of leaf or tinsel', and that shaken goldfoil throws out broad glares like sheet lightning and, through its creases and facets, something like fork lightning as well. The image presents divine glory as latent, released in a sudden flash when the world is 'shaken'. 'Shook' as a past participle is archaic or dialectal, chosen for its abrupt sound.

    C. C. Abbott, ed., The Letters of Gerard Manley Hopkins to Robert Bridges (Oxford UP, 1935); Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  4. the ooze of oil / Crushed ll. 3-4

    Where the foil gives instantaneous brilliance, the oil 'gathers to a greatness' slowly and under pressure. The enjambment holds back 'Crushed' to the start of l. 4, enacting the pressure. Oil has strong biblical and sacramental associations (anointing, the olive press of Gethsemane, whose name means 'oil-press'), and commentators have connected the crushing with suffering that yields grace. The two similes together give two modes of God's presence: sudden revelation and slow accumulation.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  5. reck his rod l. 4

    'Reck' survives mainly in 'reckless'; Shakespeare's Ophelia uses it of the preacher who 'recks not his own rede'. The 'rod' is the sceptre of divine rule and the rod of correction: compare Psalm 23:4, 'thy rod and thy staff they comfort me', and Proverbs on the rod of discipline. The question is therefore why men no longer pay attention to God's power and judgement, when the world so plainly shows it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'reck'; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.3.51 ('recks not his own rede'); Bible (KJV), Psalm 23:4

  6. Why do men then now not reck l. 4

    The question piles up short words ('men then now not reck his rod') that resist a smooth iambic reading, and the internal chime of 'men then' and the alliteration of 'reck'/'rod' make the line clotted and emphatic. This is the 'counterpoint' Hopkins describes in his Preface: a regular metre heard underneath, with stresses forced against it. The difficulty of saying the line enacts the moral obtuseness it rebukes.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  7. have trod, have trod, have trod l. 5

    The line is an aural image of endless, heavy walking, and the rhyme sound (-od) joins 'rod' and, later, 'shod'. 'Trod' implies not just passing but trampling down: human history has worn the earth as a path is worn. Hopkins's phrasing may recall Ecclesiastes 1:4, 'One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever', which anticipates the sestet's claim that nature is never spent.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:4; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  8. seared with trade; bleared, smeared with toil l. 6

    'Seared' (scorched, withered; also of a conscience made insensible, 1 Timothy 4:2), 'bleared' (dimmed, as eyes are by rheum) and 'smeared' form a chain of internal rhymes that thickens the line. The North Wales coalfields and the industrial towns of Lancashire were not far from St Beuno's, and Hopkins elsewhere lamented the ugliness of industrial England. 'Smudge' and 'smell' in the next line continue the sensory disgust. Note how the archive text breaks this line typographically after 'with'; it is a single line of the sonnet.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Bible (KJV), 1 Timothy 4:2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blear'

  9. being shod l. 8

    The soil is 'bare' because it has been stripped and exhausted; the feet are covered, so contact is lost on both sides. The image recalls God's command to Moses at the burning bush, 'put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground' (Exodus 3:5): to be shod is to fail to recognise that the earth is holy. The octave ends on this note of insensibility.

    Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:5; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  10. nature is never spent l. 9

    The sestet answers the octave. 'Spent' means used up, exhausted (as money or energy is spent), carrying on the economic language of 'trade'. The turn depends on a theology of continuous creation: God not only made the world but sustains it at every moment, so human misuse cannot finally drain it. The poem's confidence here contrasts with the more anxious ecology of 'Binsey Poplars' (1879).

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spend'

  11. deep down things l. 10

    The omission of 'in' makes 'down' do the work of a preposition, and the run of stressed monosyllables ('deep down things') sounds the depth it describes. The line is often connected with Hopkins's notions of 'inscape' (the distinctive inner form of each thing) and 'instress' (the force that holds it in being and that the beholder feels), terms that recur in his journals. 'Dearest' means both most precious and most loved.

    Humphry House and Graham Storey, eds, The Journals and Papers of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford UP, 1959); Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  12. the black West ... morning ... springs ll. 11-12

    The sentence is inverted, so the main clause 'morning ... springs' arrives only in l. 12, and the reader passes through darkness before reaching dawn. The east (where Christ was traditionally expected to return, and towards which churches face) answers the 'black West'. 'Springs' recalls 'the dayspring from on high' of Luke 1:78. 'Brown brink' gives the dawn horizon the colour of earth, uniting the renewal of light with the soil just described as 'bare'.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 1:78; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  13. the bent / World ll. 13-14

    The word ends l. 13 and its noun waits on the next line, so the sense bends over the line ending. Literally, the earth is round, and the Spirit broods over its curve as a bird over an egg. Morally, the world is warped and fallen, and the redemption the sestet promises is directed at precisely that condition. Commentators generally accept both senses; the pun makes the Fall and the shape of the earth one image.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bent'

  14. the Holy Ghost ... broods l. 14

    In Genesis 1:2 'the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters'; the Hebrew verb (merachefet) has often been rendered 'brooded' or 'hovered', as a bird over its young. Milton made the image famous in the invocation to Paradise Lost, where the Spirit 'Dove-like satst brooding on the vast Abyss'. The dove is also the form in which the Spirit descended at Christ's baptism (Matthew 3:16). Hopkins makes the brooding warm and maternal ('warm breast'), and continuous: the Spirit still hatches new life from the world.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:2; John Milton, Paradise Lost (1674) 1.19-22 ('Dove-like satst brooding on the vast Abyss'); Bible (KJV), Matthew 3:16

  15. ah! bright wings l. 14

    The interjection 'ah!' interrupts the line's syntax and creates a pause before the final image, so the poem ends in an act of wonder rather than a statement. 'Bright' returns to the 'shining' and 'flame' of the opening, completing the poem's movement from latent glory to its revelation. Bridges' 1918 text, which the archive follows, prints the last line broken over two typographical lines.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)
