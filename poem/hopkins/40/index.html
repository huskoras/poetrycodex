The Mind

No worst, there is none

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

The Mind

No worst, there is none. Pitched past pitch of grief,
More pangs will, schooled at forepangs, wilder wring.
Comforter, where, where is your comforting?
Mary, mother of us, where is your relief?
My cries heave, herds-long; huddle in a main, a chief
Woe, world-sorrow; on an age-old anvil wince and sing--
Then lull, then leave off. Fury had shrieked 'No ling-
ering! Let me be fell: force I must be brief'.

O the mind, mind has mountains; cliffs of fall
Frightful, sheer, no-man-fathomed. Hold them cheap
May who ne'er hung there. Nor does long our small
Durance deal with that steep or deep. Here! creep,
Wretch, under a comfort serves in a whirlwind: all
Life death does end and each day dies with sleep.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The 'terrible sonnets' (1885) l. 1

    Hopkins was Professor of Greek at University College Dublin from 1884, isolated, overworked and depressed. On 1 September 1885 he told Bridges that some sonnets had come to him 'like inspirations unbidden and against my will'. In May 1885 he spoke of a sonnet 'written in blood'; Bridges thought this was probably 'Carrion Comfort', and the identification is his conjecture, not Hopkins's word. The manuscript is an undated draft without a title. Hopkins was trained in the Spiritual Exercises of St Ignatius, which describe 'desolation' as darkness of soul and the felt absence of God, and the sonnet reads like a report from such a state. It was printed only in Bridges' 1918 edition.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (London: Humphrey Milford, 1918), editor's notes (Wikisource); C. C. Abbott, ed., The Letters of Gerard Manley Hopkins to Robert Bridges (London: Oxford University Press, 1935; rev. 1955); St Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises, Rules for the Discernment of Spirits, First Week, rule 4 (on desolation), CCEL

  2. No worst l. 1

    The opening denies that grief has a limit: there is no 'worst', since every pain can be exceeded. It closely recalls Edgar's lines in King Lear 4.1, when he meets his blinded father: 'And worse I may be yet: the worst is not / So long as we can say "This is the worst."' The Lear echoes continue through the sonnet (the cliffs, the whirlwind, 'Wretch'). Hopkins's adjective used as a noun ('No worst') is grammatically abrupt, as though the speaker has no breath for a full sentence. Editors take the allusion as certain.

    William Shakespeare, King Lear 4.1.25-28 (MIT Complete Works); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  3. Pitched past pitch of grief l. 1

    'Pitched' has several senses. In music, pitch is the height of a note: the speaker is tuned beyond the highest possible note of grief, which prepares 'wince and sing' in line 6. 'To pitch' also means to throw or fling headlong, as one is hurled from a height; and 'pitch' can mean the highest point or a steep slope. Some readers hear the dark of pitch, as in 'pitch-black'. The repetition 'Pitched past pitch' is a typical Hopkins figure in which a word is set against its own derivative, enacting the escalation it describes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pitch, v.1 and n.2'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  4. schooled at forepangs l. 2

    'Forepangs' is a Hopkins compound, the pangs that came before. The new pangs are 'schooled' at them, that is, trained or educated by the previous ones, so they come with more skill and greater force. The syntax is compressed: 'More pangs will, schooled at forepangs, wilder wring' means 'further pangs, taught by earlier ones, will wring more wildly'. The personification of pain as an apprentice who outdoes its master gives suffering a terrible ingenuity. 'Wring' means twist or squeeze, as hands are wrung in distress or laundry is wrung out.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wring, v.'

  5. Comforter l. 3

    In John 14:16 and 14:26 Christ promises 'another Comforter', the Holy Ghost (Greek paraklētos, advocate, helper). Hopkins addresses the Spirit directly and asks where its promised comfort is, a cry of spiritual desolation rather than unbelief: the speaker believes in the Comforter but cannot feel him. The repetition 'where, where' is a broken lament. Ignatian spirituality expected such periods in which consolation is withdrawn. The line's question is never answered in the poem; the only 'comfort' at the end is sleep and death.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 14:16; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 14:26; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  6. Mary, mother of us l. 4

    Catholic devotion regards Mary as the spiritual mother of the faithful, a role traced to Christ's words from the cross giving her to the beloved disciple: 'Woman, behold thy son! ... Behold thy mother!' (John 19:26-27). Hopkins, a Catholic convert and Jesuit priest, wrote extensively about Mary, notably in 'The Blessed Virgin compared to the Air we Breathe'. Here the appeal mirrors line 3: the speaker asks both the Spirit and the Mother where their help has gone. The address is blunt and unadorned, as if the prayer were spoken without poetic ornament.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 19:26; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  7. My cries heave, herds-long; huddle l. 5

    The compound 'herds-long' pictures the cries moving in a long line, like cattle or sheep driven in herds, and then huddling together in a mass. They 'heave', a verb suggesting both labouring breath and the heaving of a crowd or sea. The image depersonalises grief: the cries are no longer the speaker's own but a herd of beasts he watches. MacKenzie and others note the sound of the line, which crowds stresses together so that the voice too seems to heave and huddle.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  8. a main, a chief / Woe, world-sorrow ll. 5-6

    'Main' as a noun can mean the chief part, a mass, or the open sea; all three fit cries that 'huddle' together into one great body of grief. Bridges noted that at the end of line 5 the draft has marks that look like a hyphen and a comma, so some editors print 'chief- / woe', a compound like 'chief-mourner', while others keep 'chief / Woe'. 'World-sorrow' translates the German Weltschmerz, the Romantic notion of grief for the state of the world; here it becomes the speaker's private grief swollen to universal size, or a share in the sorrow of all humanity since the Fall.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (London: Humphrey Milford, 1918), editor's notes (Wikisource); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Weltschmerz, n.'

  9. on an age-old anvil wince and sing l. 6

    The image is of a smithy: the cries are struck on an 'age-old anvil' and 'wince and sing', flinching under the hammer and ringing as hot metal does. 'Age-old' suggests that the anvil is the perennial human condition on which every generation's sufferings have been beaten. The verb 'sing' returns to the musical sense of 'pitched' in line 1, and readers note the grim irony that the poem itself is the 'song' produced by the blows. The line then breaks with a dash into brief respite.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  10. Then lull, then leave off ... No ling- / ering! ll. 7-8

    After the hammer-blows the pain lulls and 'leaves off', but only so that Fury can shriek again. The violent enjambment 'ling- / ering' breaks the word itself at the line-end, so that the reader is not allowed to linger over it: form enacts meaning. Hopkins used split words of this kind elsewhere (e.g. 'The Windhover', 'king- / dom'). The octave of this Petrarchan sonnet rhymes abbaabba (grief, wring, comforting, relief, chief, sing, ling-, brief), and the broken rhyme 'ling-' / 'sing' shows how far he would strain a rhyme for expressive effect.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Project Gutenberg e-text no. 22403, incl. the Author's Preface on sprung rhythm

  11. Fury l. 7

    The Furies (Greek Erinyes, Latin Furiae) were the avenging spirits who pursued wrongdoers, especially in Aeschylus's Eumenides, a play Hopkins as a classicist knew well. Here 'Fury' is singular and capitalised, a personified rage of pain, perhaps also the speaker's own frenzy. Her shriek, 'No lingering! Let me be fell: force I must be brief', is an order that the torment be cruel and quick. The capitalisation also hints at a demonic tormentor, consistent with the speaker's sense of being abandoned by the Comforter.

    Aeschylus, Eumenides (Perseus Digital Library, trans. H. W. Smyth); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  12. fell; force l. 8

    'Fell' as an adjective means fierce, savage or deadly (Old French fel), surviving in 'at one fell swoop' (Macbeth 4.3). The Fury demands to be cruel because she must also be brief. 'Force' is an elliptical form of 'perforce', 'of necessity': 'force I must be brief' means 'necessarily I must be brief'. Some readers also hear 'force' as a noun in apposition, the Fury naming herself as pure force. The cramped phrasing is typical of Hopkins's late style, where grammar is compressed to the limit of sense.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fell, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'force, adv. (perforce)'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  13. Petrarchan sonnet and the sestet ll. 9-14

    The poem follows the Italian division: an octave of mounting anguish (abbaabba) and a sestet rhyming cdcdcd (fall, cheap, small, creep, all, sleep), set off by an indent. Hopkins's lines are long and heavily stressed, closer to alexandrines in some places, and he exploits enjambment heavily in the sestet ('cliffs of fall / Frightful', 'our small / Durance'). The volta moves from description of pain to generalisation: 'O the mind, mind has mountains'. The repetition of 'mind' resembles the stammering 'where, where' of line 3.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  14. mind has mountains; cliffs of fall ... no-man-fathomed ll. 9-10

    The image makes inner experience into dangerous terrain: mountains, sheer 'cliffs of fall' (cliffs one can fall from), and depths 'no-man-fathomed', never measured by anyone. 'Fathom' is a unit of depth used in sounding the sea, and the compound suggests both unmeasured and incomprehensible. The scene recalls Edgar's description of Dover cliff to the blinded Gloucester in King Lear 4.6, 'How fearful / And dizzy 'tis to cast one's eyes so low!' Hopkins, a keen walker in the Alps and Wales, knew mountains well; his journals record a strong sense of their sublimity and danger.

    William Shakespeare, King Lear 4.6.11-24 (MIT Complete Works); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  15. Hold them cheap / May who ne'er hung there ll. 10-11

    The syntax is inverted: 'May those who have never hung there hold them cheap', that is, only people who have never clung to these mental cliffs can dismiss them as trivial. 'Hold ... cheap' means to regard as of little value. The image of hanging over an abyss is a vivid picture of depression or spiritual terror. The line answers a possible reader's scepticism, and it has made the poem a classic text in discussions of depression and literature. The terse monosyllables ('ne'er hung there') contrast with the compound adjectives just before.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  16. Durance l. 12

    'Durance' means duration or endurance, and also, in its common phrase 'durance vile', imprisonment. All three senses work: our brief time on earth, our small powers of endurance, and our confinement in a mortal body cannot 'deal with' (cope with, or face) 'that steep or deep' for long. The enjambment 'our small / Durance' isolates 'small', stressing human limitation. The rhyming pair 'steep or deep' sums up the cliff and the unfathomed depth of lines 9-10.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'durance, n.'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  17. Here! creep, / Wretch ll. 12-13

    The speaker addresses himself as 'Wretch' and orders himself to 'creep' under whatever shelter is available. The language recalls King Lear's storm scenes: Lear's prayer for 'Poor naked wretches' who 'bide the pelting of this pitiless storm' (3.4), and the hovel where Edgar shelters as Poor Tom. 'Here!' is a sudden imperative, like a call to a dog or a child. The self-address shows the speaker split between sufferer and comforter, the only comforter in the poem being himself.

    William Shakespeare, King Lear 3.4.28-36 (MIT Complete Works); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  18. a comfort serves in a whirlwind l. 13

    The clause is elliptical: 'creep under a comfort [that] serves in a whirlwind', that is, any shelter that will do in a storm. The whirlwind recalls the voice out of which God answers Job (Job 38:1, 'Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind'), a passage about a righteous sufferer who receives not explanation but presence. It also recalls the storm on the heath in King Lear. The diminished 'a comfort' contrasts bitterly with the 'Comforter' of line 3. The rhyme 'all / fall / small' pulls the line toward its grim conclusion.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 38:1; William Shakespeare, King Lear 3.4 (MIT Complete Works); Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  19. all / Life death does end and each day dies with sleep ll. 13-14

    The final line offers two small comforts: all life ends in death, and each day 'dies' in sleep. Macbeth calls sleep 'The death of each day's life, sore labour's bath, / Balm of hurt minds' (Macbeth 2.2), and Hopkins's line seems to recall it. The word order 'all / Life death does end' is inverted, delaying 'death' and making the reader hesitate over whether death is subject or object. Critics differ on whether the ending is a despairing wish for oblivion or a stoic, even faintly hopeful acceptance that suffering has limits in time. The rhyme 'creep / sleep' closes the poem quietly after its storm.

    William Shakespeare, Macbeth 2.2.36-40 (MIT Complete Works); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem
