Pied Beauty

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Religion

GLORY be to God for dappled things--
For skies of couple-colour as a brinded cow;
For rose-moles all in stipple upon trout that swim:
Fresh-firecoal chestnut-falls; finches' wings;
Landscape plotted and pieced--fold, fallow, and
plough;
And àll tràdes, their gear and tackle and trim.

All things counter, original, spare, strange;
Whatever is fickle, freckled (who knows how?)
With swift, slow; sweet, sour; adazzle, dim;
He fathers-forth whose beauty is past change:
Praise him.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Glory be to God l. 1

    The opening adapts the 'Glory be to the Father' of Christian liturgy, and also recalls the Jesuit motto Ad maiorem Dei gloriam ('to the greater glory of God'), which Jesuits traditionally write at the head of their work. The poem belongs to the summer of 1877 at St Beuno's, Tremeirchion, and Hopkins himself classed it as a curtal sonnet in sprung rhythm. The poem is framed as a hymn of praise, beginning with 'Glory' and ending 'Praise him', a structure modelled on the psalms.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary; Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises, 'Principle and Foundation' and 'The Kingdom of Christ' (Second Week)

  2. dappled / pied l. 1

    'Pied' (from the black-and-white magpie) and 'dappled' (spotted) announce the poem's subject: beauty that lies in variety, contrast and irregularity. Hopkins's journals are full of precise notes on the patterns of clouds, water, leaves and animals, and the poem gathers such observations into praise. The celebration of difference is theological: each thing's particular pattern shows the Creator. The adjective 'Pied' in the title is the poem's keynote, a single word for all the particoloured things that follow.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pied'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dapple'; Humphry House and Graham Storey, eds, The Journals and Papers of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford UP, 1959)

  3. couple-colour / brinded l. 2

    'Couple-colour' means two-coloured, the sky patched with cloud and blue. The comparison of the sky to a farm animal is characteristic of Hopkins's yoking of grand and homely. 'Brinded' was archaic by the 1870s, familiar from 'Thrice the brinded cat hath mew'd' in Macbeth. Hopkins's choice of an old word for a common animal is typical: precise rural observation is given a slightly antique dignity. The sky, the cow and the trout of l. 3 make a sequence of air, earth and water, so that the catalogue ranges through the elements before turning to human work.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brinded'; Shakespeare, Macbeth 4.1.1 ('Thrice the brinded cat hath mew'd')

  4. rose-moles ... stipple l. 3

    'Stipple' is the technique of producing shading with dots or small touches rather than lines. Hopkins drew carefully and was familiar with such terms. 'Rose-moles' are the reddish spots on a brown trout. The line brings art and nature together: the fish looks as though painted by a skilled hand. The line also moves the catalogue from sky and land to water, and from large patterns to the minute detail that Hopkins valued. Its stresses fall heavily on the compound nouns.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'stipple'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  5. Fresh-firecoal chestnut-falls l. 4

    When horse-chestnuts fall, the husks split to show glossy brown nuts, which Hopkins likens to coals broken open to show the fire inside (compare the falling embers of 'The Windhover'). The compressed compounds pack the observation into four stressed words. 'Finches' wings', with their bars of colour, follow. The line is a sequence of compressed noun-phrases with no verb, a list of sights rather than a sentence, so the reader moves from one vivid object to the next as the eye does in a landscape.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  6. Landscape plotted and pieced l. 5

    'Plotted' means divided into plots and also mapped; 'pieced' suggests needlework. Hopkins is looking at the patterned fields of the Vale of Clwyd from the hillside of St Beuno's. Unlike 'God's Grandeur', where human work sears and smears, here agriculture adds to the pied beauty of the earth. The phrase moves the catalogue from individual creatures to a whole countryside seen from above, and so to the shaping work of farmers.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  7. fold, fallow, and plough l. 5

    A 'fold' is an enclosure for sheep, and by extension pasture; 'fallow' is ploughed land left to rest for a season; 'plough' is arable land. The triad names the different colours and textures of a farmed landscape: green, brown and freshly turned earth. The alliteration of 'fold, fallow' and the plain monosyllables give the line the rhythm of a farmer's inventory, typical of Hopkins's delight in precise working vocabulary. The terms also suggest the cycle of agricultural seasons, grazing, resting and ploughing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fold, n.1'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fallow'; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  8. gear and tackle and trim l. 6

    All three words have nautical overtones (a ship's tackle; to be 'in trim'), but they extend to every trade's equipment. The archive text marks stresses on 'all' and 'trades' with accents, Hopkins's guide to the reading. The line widens the catalogue from nature to human labour, which is also part of the variety God made possible. The poem therefore includes the made world alongside the natural one.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'trim'; Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes

  9. counter, original, spare, strange l. 7

    'Counter' means opposed or contrasting (as in counterpoint); 'original' means unique, having its own origin; 'spare' means scanty, lean, or rare; 'strange' means unfamiliar or odd. Together they defend beauty that departs from conventional harmony. Critics link this to Duns Scotus, whom Hopkins began reading in 1872, and his doctrine of haecceitas ('thisness'), the principle that makes each individual thing uniquely itself.

    Humphry House and Graham Storey, eds, The Journals and Papers of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford UP, 1959); Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  10. fickle, freckled (who knows how?) l. 8

    'Fickle' (changeable, inconstant) and 'freckled' (spotted) are paired by sound. The parenthesis '(who knows how?)' is a moment of humility: the speaker does not claim to explain variety, only to praise its source. It also prepares the contrast with God, whose beauty is unchanging. The line is spoken with wonder rather than complaint, and 'fickle', normally a fault in a person, becomes a beauty in nature. The run of alliterating f-sounds ('fickle, freckled') carries over to the 'fathers-forth' of l. 10, linking the changing creatures to their changeless Father.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  11. the paired opposites l. 9

    Speed, taste and light are each given in opposed pairs, and the alliteration (swift/slow, sweet/sour) binds the opposites together. 'Adazzle' is a coinage on the pattern of 'ablaze' or 'aglow'. The line is the poem's most compressed statement of pied beauty, a world made of contrasts held together. It also mirrors the poem's own form, built from a sestet and a quatrain held in proportion. Each pair is separated by a semicolon, so the reader pauses between contraries as the eye moves between contrasting patches.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  12. He fathers-forth l. 10

    'Fathers-forth' means begets, brings forth; the hyphen makes a new compound verb. The contrast is between created things, which are 'fickle' and changing, and their Creator, whose beauty is 'past change'. Compare James 1:17, 'the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning', and Malachi 3:6, 'I am the LORD, I change not'. The poem's praise therefore runs from the many to the One.

    Bible (KJV), James 1:17; Bible (KJV), Malachi 3:6; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  13. Praise him. l. 11

    In his Preface Hopkins explained that his 'Curtal-Sonnets' reproduce the proportions of the sonnet (8 + 6) in reduced form (6 + 4) plus a half-line tailpiece; 'Pied Beauty' is one of the few poems he wrote in this form (the others include 'Peace' and 'Ash-boughs'). The two-word close imitates the psalms of praise ('Praise ye him', Psalm 148) and fulfils the purpose that Ignatius's Principle and Foundation assigns to human beings, to praise, reverence and serve God.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Bible (KJV), Psalm 148:1-3; Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises, 'Principle and Foundation' and 'The Kingdom of Christ' (Second Week)
