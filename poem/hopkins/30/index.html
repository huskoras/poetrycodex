Time & Brevity

Spring and Fall

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Time & Brevity

MÁRGARÉT, áre you gríeving
Over Goldengrove unleaving?
Leáves, like the things of man, you
With your fresh thoughts care for, can you?
Áh! ás the heart grows older
It will come to such sights colder
By and by, nor spare a sigh
Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie;
And yet you wíll weep and know why.
Now no matter, child, the name:
Sórrow's spríngs áre the same.
Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed
What heart heard of, ghost guessed:
It is the blight man was born for,
It is Margaret you mourn for.

Codex Note

to a young child

Study notes by the editors

  1. Spring and Fall: to a young child title; l. 1

    Hopkins, then a curate in Liverpool, headed the autograph 'Lydiate, Lancashire, Sept. 7, 1880'. In a letter to Robert Bridges begun on 5 September he described it as a little piece composed since he began the letter, 'not founded on any real incident', and said he was not well satisfied with it. Bridges printed it in the first edition of Hopkins's Poems (1918). The full title, 'Spring and Fall: to a young child', puns on the seasons, on youth and age, and on the Fall of man, which the poem names at its close as 'the blight man was born for'. Margaret (Greek margarites, 'pearl') is an imagined child; the capitals and accents of line 1 stress her name as a heavy, slow invocation.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (London: Humphrey Milford, 1918), editor's notes (Wikisource); C. C. Abbott, ed., The Letters of Gerard Manley Hopkins to Robert Bridges (London: Oxford University Press, 1935; rev. 1955); Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  2. Sprung rhythm and accents ll. 1-15

    Hopkins marked the poem as written in sprung rhythm, in which each foot has one stress and a variable number of slack syllables, so lines can be packed or spare. The acute accents (on 'áre', 'gríeving', 'Leáves', 'wíll', 'Sórrow's') are his performance directions: they place stress where ordinary reading would not, as on 'áre' in line 1 or 'wíll' in line 9, which changes meaning. Bridges explained in 1918 that the accents in the later poems correspond to Hopkins's own marks. The rhymes run in couplets except for the triplet 'sigh / lie / why' (ll. 7-9), which marks the centre of the argument. The short, chant-like lines suit an address to a child.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Project Gutenberg e-text no. 22403, incl. the Author's Preface on sprung rhythm; Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (London: Humphrey Milford, 1918), editor's notes (Wikisource); Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  3. Goldengrove l. 2

    No real 'Goldengrove' has been securely identified; Hopkins coins a place-name that sounds plausible (like Golden Valley or Gorse Grove) while describing a wood whose leaves have turned gold. To the child it is a beloved place; to the reader it suggests the golden age and the garden of Eden before the Fall. Fusing the two words into one gives the name the weight of a proper noun and makes its loss feel like the loss of a whole world. Note the alliteration with 'grieving' and 'grows', binding the grove to grief.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  4. unleaving l. 2

    The participle means shedding leaves, the reverse of 'leaving' in the old sense 'putting out leaves'. Bridges noted in 1918 that 'in line 2 the last word is unleafing in most of the MSS'; the printed 'unleaving' gives a richer double sense, since the grove that is 'unleaving' is also not going away, the cycle of leaf-fall recurring every year while the human life it figures does not. Hopkins's negative prefix turns a word of growth into a word of undoing. The rhyme 'grieving / unleaving' links the child's grief to the leaves before the poem explains why.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (London: Humphrey Milford, 1918), editor's notes (Wikisource); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'leave, v.2 (to put forth leaves)'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  5. Leaves ... you / With your fresh thoughts care for ll. 3-4

    The sentence is inverted for emphasis and rhyme. In normal order: 'Can you, with your young mind, care for the leaves, as if they were human things?' 'Fresh' means young, unspoiled, and also green like new leaves. The adult speaker wonders, half tenderly and half sceptically, at a child's capacity to grieve for something non-human, an emotion adults have learned to suppress. 'The things of man' means human concerns and possessions, which, like leaves, are transient; the simile hints at the poem's answer before the question is finished.

    Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  6. the heart grows older ... colder ll. 5-7

    The speaker predicts that Margaret's spontaneous grief will harden with age: 'it will come to such sights colder / By and by'. The rhyme 'older / colder' links ageing directly to coldness, and 'nor spare a sigh' means the older heart will not even spend a sigh on whole 'worlds' of fallen leaves. The tone is not simply approving: this coldness is itself a loss, the heart's own autumn. The passage recalls Wordsworth's sense of the fading of childhood feeling in the 'Intimations' Ode, a poem Hopkins knew, though the Christian frame here is explicitly the Fall.

    Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807), Poetry Foundation text

  7. wanwood l. 8

    'Wan' in modern English means pale, sickly; its Old English ancestor wann meant dark, dusky, livid. Hopkins, a keen student of English word history, uses both: the leaves are bleached and lifeless, and also dark, rotting on the ground. Compounded with 'wood' it names a whole landscape of pallid, decaying trees, the counterpart to the bright 'Goldengrove'. Forming new compounds from native Anglo-Saxon roots was part of Hopkins's programme of reviving the strength of English; he admired the 'Saxon' resources of the language.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wan, adj.'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  8. leafmeal l. 8

    The suffix '-meal' (Old English -mǣlum, 'by portions') survives in 'piecemeal' and meant 'one at a time'. Hopkins revives it: the leaves lie fallen 'leafmeal', one by one, scattered singly. The word also suggests 'meal' as ground grain, so that the leaves seem pulverised into leaf-mould, a sense many readers hear. The line 'Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie' piles alliteration and compounding into a heavy, falling cadence. Editors cite the word as a characteristic Hopkins coinage built from recovered Old English elements.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. '-meal, suffix'; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  9. you will weep and know why l. 9

    Hopkins's accent on 'wíll' changes the line. Unstressed, it would be a simple future: one day you will weep and know the reason. Stressed, it becomes volitional: you are determined to weep, you will weep whatever I say. Most editors read both: Margaret insists on her grief now, and later she will weep knowingly, understanding what she now only feels. The line completes the triplet rhyme 'sigh / lie / why', giving the 'why' a strong place at the poem's turning point. The next lines explain that knowing the 'why' does not change the cause.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Project Gutenberg e-text no. 22403, incl. the Author's Preface on sprung rhythm; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  10. Now no matter, child, the name l. 10

    Having promised that Margaret will come to know 'why', the speaker sets aside the question of naming. Whether one calls it grief for leaves or grief for oneself, the 'name' of the sorrow does not matter, because 'Sorrow's springs are the same'. The line is gentle in tone ('child'), yet it carries a theological claim: all human sorrow, whatever its apparent object, comes from one root, the fallen condition. Some readers hear an echo of the Catholic doctrine of original sin, whose effects are universal whatever the individual circumstance.

    Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  11. Sorrow's springs l. 11

    'Springs' means sources, as a spring is the source of a stream; all sorrow flows from the same origin. The word also recalls the title, 'Spring and Fall': the season of the child's life is spring, though she grieves at autumn. Hopkins's accents ('Sórrow's spríngs áre') slow the line and stress 'áre', insisting on the identity. The springs of sorrow are also the 'springs' of tears. The single line distils the argument of the poem's second half.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem; Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem

  12. Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed ll. 12-13

    The compressed syntax means: neither your mouth nor your mind had put into words what your heart had heard of and your spirit had guessed. Hopkins distinguishes levels of knowing: articulate speech and rational mind on one side, and the deeper faculties of heart and 'ghost' on the other, which sense truths before they can be stated. The child grieves for something she cannot name. The alliteration (mouth/mind, heart/heard, ghost/guessed) pairs the faculties in twos.

    Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  13. ghost l. 13

    Hopkins uses 'ghost' in its original sense of the human spirit or soul (Old English gāst), preserved in 'Holy Ghost' and 'give up the ghost' (e.g. Mark 15:37). The soul 'guessed' or intuited what the mind had not expressed, the truth of mortality. The archaic word gives the line a native, Anglo-Saxon texture typical of Hopkins, and avoids the abstract Latinate 'spirit'. Some readers also hear a faint modern sense of 'ghost', the dead self Margaret will become.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ghost, n.'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Mark 15:37; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem

  14. the blight man was born for l. 14

    A 'blight' is a disease that withers plants, linking human mortality with the dying leaves. 'Born for' means destined for: since Adam's sin, death is the human inheritance (Genesis 3:17-19; Romans 5:12, 'by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin'). Job's 'man is born unto trouble' gives the phrasing a biblical cadence. As a Jesuit, Hopkins understood the doctrine of original sin as a universal wound; the poem makes it intelligible to a child through the fall of leaves, uniting the two senses of 'Fall' in the title.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 5:7; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 3:17; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Romans 5:12

  15. It is Margaret you mourn for l. 15

    The last line reveals the real object of grief: in mourning the leaves, Margaret mourns her own eventual death and her share in the fallen human condition. The return of her name, which opened the poem, closes the circle, and the parallel 'It is ... / It is ...' structure gives the ending the finality of a verdict. Critics differ on its tone: some find it tender and consoling, the adult understanding the child; others find it harsh, an adult imposing a theological lesson on a child's spontaneous feeling. The feminine rhymes 'born for / mourn for' give the ending a falling cadence, like the leaves.

    Catherine Phillips, ed., Gerard Manley Hopkins: The Major Works, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002), notes on the poem; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary on the poem
