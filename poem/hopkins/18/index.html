Nature

Binsey Poplars

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Nature

MY aspens dear, whose airy cages quelled,
Quelled or quenched in leaves the leaping sun,
All felled, felled, are all felled;
Of a fresh and following folded rank
Not spared, not one
That dandled a sandalled
Shadow that swam or sank
On meadow and river and wind-wandering weed-winding
bank.

O if we but knew what we do
When we delve or hew--
Hack and rack the growing green!
Since country is so tender
To touch, her being só slender,
That, like this sleek and seeing ball
But a prick will make no eye at all,
Where we, even where we mean
To mend her we end her,
When we hew or delve:
After-comers cannot guess the beauty been.
Ten or twelve, only ten or twelve
Strokes of havoc únselve
The sweet especial scene,
Rural scene, a rural scene,
Sweet especial rural scene.

Codex Note

felled 1879

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: 'felled 1879' l. 1

    The poem's subtitle (given in the archive's note field) is 'felled 1879'. In a postscript dated 13 March 1879 to a letter to his friend and former teacher R. W. Dixon, Hopkins wrote that he had walked to Godstow and was sorry to find that the aspens that lined the river were 'everyone felled'. The trees stood along the Thames by Port Meadow, near the hamlet of Binsey, north-west of Oxford, on a walk he had known as an undergraduate in the 1860s. Hopkins was then serving at St Aloysius's church in Oxford. The opening 'MY aspens dear' is a personal possessive: the trees were his, through love and memory. The poem was first published by Bridges in 1918. The aspen (Populus tremula) is itself a poplar, so letter and title agree.

    C. C. Abbott (ed.), The Correspondence of Gerard Manley Hopkins and Richard Watson Dixon (Oxford UP, 1935), letter begun February 1879, postscript of 13 March 1879; Christiane Lang-Graumann, 'Gerard Manley Hopkins Revisiting Binsey', Connotations; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'

  2. Airy cages ll. 1–2

    The tree crowns are seen as open lattices of leaves that catch the sunlight like a wild creature, the 'leaping sun', and tame it. 'Quelled' (subdued, overpowered) is then revised in the next line to 'Quelled or quenched', as though Hopkins were searching for the exact verb in front of us. The light becomes the dappled, moving shade of l. 6–8. The repetition 'quelled, / Quelled' leads straight into the elegiac chime 'felled, felled'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quell, v.1'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  3. All felled l. 3

    Line 3 is almost nothing but the word 'felled', repeated three times and framed by 'All ... all'. Each stress lands like a blow, and the rhyme with 'quelled' turns the trees' gentle power into its violent end. Hopkins's sprung rhythm counts stresses, not syllables, so a line can pile stresses together this way. The poem's lines vary greatly in length, from the short 'Not spared, not one' to the sprawling l. 8, and that irregularity fits a lament that cannot keep a steady form.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  4. Following folded rank l. 4

    A 'rank' is a line of soldiers or objects side by side. 'Following' suggests one tree after another along the bank, and 'folded' suggests the overlapping of their outlines as seen down the line, or their foliage folded together. The heavy f-alliteration (fresh, following, folded) is typical of Hopkins, who drew on Welsh cynghanedd and Old English alliterative verse. The next line's 'Not spared, not one' makes the rank a troop wiped out together.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  5. Dandled a sandalled shadow ll. 6–7

    To dandle is to move a child up and down playfully in one's arms. The trees' shadow is 'sandalled', most readers take this to mean strapped or latticed with bars of light like a sandal, and it 'swam or sank' on meadow and river as the leaves moved. The internal rhyme dandled / sandalled is a deliberate sound-play. The image is of light and shadow as playful, almost living, creatures, and it shows Hopkins's exact attention to the particular look of a scene.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dandle, v.'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'

  6. Wind-wandering weed-winding bank l. 8

    The longest line in the poem runs on past the margin into 'bank'. It is built from two of Hopkins's characteristic compound epithets. 'Wind-wandering' may describe the weed swayed by wind or the bank along which the wind wanders, and 'weed-winding' the bank where weed winds in the current, or the bank that winds with its weeds. The alliteration on w imitates the meandering river. Compound-making of this kind owes much to his study of Old English and to his wish for a native, Saxon-rooted vocabulary.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  7. If we but knew what we do l. 9

    The second stanza turns from lament to reflection, and its first line recalls Luke 23:34. The allusion makes the destruction of the trees an act of unwitting violence against creation. Hopkins, a Jesuit priest, saw nature as expressing God, so harming it has a moral and religious dimension. 'Delve or hew' (dig or cut) names the basic acts of cultivation, as in the proverbial 'When Adam delved', and these are now the means of destruction.

    The Bible (KJV), Luke 23:34; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'delve, v.'; Christiane Lang-Graumann, 'Gerard Manley Hopkins Revisiting Binsey', Connotations

  8. Stress mark 'só' l. 13

    Hopkins often put accent marks in his manuscripts to show where stress falls when it might be missed, here 'só', and later 'únselve'. The archive keeps them. The mark makes 'so' an emphatic intensifier ('so very slender'). Country, personified as 'her', is 'tender / To touch' and fragile, and the rhymes tender / slender / mend her / end her bind the stanza together.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Binsey Poplars' (text from MacKenzie's facsimile of the later manuscripts)

  9. The eye ll. 14–15

    Hopkins compares the countryside to the human eyeball, sleek, alive with sight, and so delicate that 'a prick will make no eye at all'. The comparison is unsettling because it makes the reader imagine an injury to their own eye. It also links landscape and perception. Destroying a scene is like blinding the faculty that sees it, and for Hopkins seeing the 'inscape' of things was a spiritual act. 'This' suggests he is pointing to his own eye.

    Christiane Lang-Graumann, 'Gerard Manley Hopkins Revisiting Binsey', Connotations; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  10. To mend her we end her l. 17

    'Where we, even where we mean / To mend her we end her' admits that harm can come from good intentions. Trees were felled for timber, for river management or to 'improve' land. The near-rhyme of mend / end (one letter apart) puts the paradox in sound. Critics have found in the lines an early ecological awareness, and Hopkins has been claimed as a forerunner of environmental writing. But his grounds are theological and aesthetic, not scientific.

    Christiane Lang-Graumann, 'Gerard Manley Hopkins Revisiting Binsey', Connotations; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  11. the beauty been l. 19

    'After-comers' (people who come after, a native compound in the Old English manner) will have no means of knowing the scene. The past participle 'been' is used as an adjective, 'the beauty [that has] been', a compression typical of Hopkins. Once destroyed, the scene survives only in the memory of those who saw it, and perhaps in the poem itself. This is the reason for the elegy.

    Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  12. Ten or twelve strokes ll. 20–21

    The contrast is between the long growth of the trees and how quickly they are cut down: 'only ten or twelve / Strokes of havoc'. 'Havoc' was originally a military cry calling for pillage and slaughter, which extends the image of the trees as a fallen 'rank'. The repetition ('Ten or twelve, only ten or twelve') imitates the counting of blows, as 'felled, felled' did in stanza 1.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'havoc, n.'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'

  13. únselve l. 21

    'Unselve' is Hopkins's own verb, with the stress marked on the prefix. It means to destroy the 'self' of a scene, the distinctive form and energy of a particular thing that he called 'inscape'. Hopkins drew support for this from the medieval philosopher Duns Scotus, whose idea of haecceitas ('thisness') held that each individual is unrepeatable. Hopkins wrote 'Duns Scotus's Oxford' in the same weeks of 1879. In this sense the 'especial' scene is not replaceable. Lang-Graumann notes that for Hopkins each creature shows an unrepeatable aspect of its maker.

    Christiane Lang-Graumann, 'Gerard Manley Hopkins Revisiting Binsey', Connotations; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unself, v.'

  14. Closing refrain ll. 22–24

    'The sweet especial scene, / Rural scene, a rural scene, / Sweet especial rural scene' takes the words 'sweet', 'especial', 'rural' and 'scene' and recombines them, like bell-changes or an echo dying away. 'Especial' means particular, individual, so it sums up the theme of unselving, since what is lost is not scenery in general but this unique place. The incantatory ending can be read as grief fixing on a lost object, or as the poem's attempt to preserve the scene in words.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970), on 'Binsey Poplars'; Norman H. MacKenzie (ed.), The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), notes on 'Binsey Poplars'
