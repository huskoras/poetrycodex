The Windhover

by Gerard Manley Hopkins 1844–1889

From Poems (1918)

Religion

I CAUGHT this morning morning's minion, king-
dom of daylight's dauphin, dapple-dawn-drawn Fal-
con, in his riding
Of the rolling level underneath him steady air, and
striding
High there, how he rung upon the rein of a wimpling wing
In his ecstacy! then off, off forth on swing,
As a skate's heel sweeps smooth on a bow-bend:
the hurl and gliding
Rebuffed the big wind. My heart in hiding
Stirred for a bird,--the achieve of, the mastery of the
thing!

Brute beauty and valour and act, oh, air, pride, plume, here
Buckle! AND the fire that breaks from thee then, a
billion
Times told lovelier, more dangerous, O my chevalier!

No wonder of it: shéer plód makes plough down
sillion
Shine, and blue-bleak embers, ah my dear,
Fall, gall themselves, and gash gold-vermillion.

Codex Note

Dedication: To Christ our Lord

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and dedication l. 1

    A windhover is the kestrel (Falco tinnunculus), named for its habit of hanging motionless facing into the wind while hunting. Bridges' notes date the poem St Beuno's, 30 May 1877, and quote a letter of 22 June 1879 promising an amended copy, 'as that is the best thing I ever wrote'. The dedication was added in the later manuscript (B), and it directs the poem's ambiguities towards Christ. 'Caught' means both glimpsed and captured in words. Hopkins labelled the metre 'Falling paeonic rhythm, sprung and outriding': a sprung rhythm in which a stress may be followed by up to three slack syllables.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary; 'The Windhover', Wikipedia

  2. minion l. 1

    In older English a 'minion' is a favourite, a darling, often the cherished servant or courtier of a king. The modern pejorative sense (a servile follower) is secondary here. The word opens a run of courtly and chivalric vocabulary: 'king- / dom', 'dauphin', 'chevalier'. The alliteration of m's and the repetition 'morning morning's' are typical of Hopkins's sound-patterning. The bird is thus introduced as a court favourite before it is ever named as a falcon.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'minion'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  3. king- / dom: the split rhyme ll. 1-2

    The octave uses a single rhyme sound throughout (king, riding, striding, wing, swing, gliding, hiding, thing), an extreme form of Petrarchan rhyming, and the split word enacts the bird's swoop over the line end. Hyphenated breaks recur in 'Fal- / con'. The whole poem is in sprung rhythm with 'outrides' (extra unstressed syllables not counted in the scansion), which lets lines swell to great length.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, ed., The Poetical Works of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Clarendon Press, 1990), commentary; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  4. dauphin l. 2

    From 1350 to 1830 'Dauphin' was the title of the French king's eldest son. Applied to the falcon it makes the bird royalty of the dawn, and under the dedication 'To Christ our Lord' it hints at the Son, heir of the Father's kingdom. Like 'chevalier' later, the French word gives the poem its chivalric colouring. The alliteration ('daylight's dauphin, dapple-dawn-drawn') sustains the breathless excitement of the opening sighting.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dauphin'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  5. dapple-dawn-drawn l. 2

    The compound packs several senses: the bird is drawn (attracted, lured out) by the dawn; it is drawn (outlined, delineated) against a dappled sky; and perhaps 'drawn' as a carriage is drawn, continuing the riding imagery. 'Dapple', meaning variegated with spots, is one of Hopkins's favourite words: compare 'Glory be to God for dappled things' in 'Pied Beauty', written the same summer.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  6. riding / Of the rolling level ... air ll. 2-4

    'Riding' combines horsemanship with the nautical sense of a ship riding at anchor or riding the waves. The 'rolling level underneath him steady air' is grammatically difficult; it describes the air as both rolling and level, a steady surface, and the inversion of adjective and noun ('underneath him steady air') holds the reader in suspension like the hovering bird. 'Striding' extends the image of a confident, high-stepping mount.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Poetry Foundation, poem guide to 'The Windhover'

  7. rung upon the rein l. 4

    In the manege, a horse is 'rung' when exercised in a circle at the end of a long rein held by a trainer, which suits the riding imagery of ll. 2-3. In falconry a hawk 'rings' when it climbs in spirals. Some readers also hear the ringing of a bell. The sense is of controlled, circling motion, the bird mastering the wind as a rider masters a horse. The past form 'rung' (for 'ringed') is unusual and may itself be dialectal.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ring, v.2'; Poetry Foundation, poem guide to 'The Windhover'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  8. wimpling l. 4

    A wimple is a cloth folded round the head and neck, worn by medieval women and later by nuns; to 'wimple' is to lie in folds or to ripple. The wing, quivering as the kestrel holds its position, looks crimped or rippled. The religious association of the wimple quietly anticipates the poem's turn to Christ. The word also suits the rippling, tremulous wingbeat that holds the kestrel steady against the wind.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wimple'; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  9. a skate's heel l. 6

    The kestrel breaks off from its hover and swings away in an arc like a skater leaning on the outside edge. 'Bow-bend' suggests the curved shape of a bow, or the curve of a river. The 'hurl and gliding' then 'Rebuffed the big wind': the bird's motion is not passive drifting but active mastery, throwing back the wind's force. The comparison moves from courtly to everyday imagery, a shift the sestet repeats.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  10. My heart in hiding l. 7

    The phrase marks the first appearance of the speaker as subject. His heart is 'in hiding' (sheltered, timid, or deliberately withdrawn), which many readers relate to Hopkins's life as a Jesuit, whose vocation required self-effacement and a sacrifice of ambition, including poetic ambition. Compare Colossians 3:3, 'your life is hid with Christ in God'. The rhyme 'hiding / Stirred for a bird' gives the stirring an almost comic plainness before the outburst 'the achieve of, the mastery of the thing!'

    Bible (KJV), Colossians 3:3; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); William Empson, Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930), ch. 7, on 'The Windhover'

  11. achieve l. 8

    Hopkins uses the verb as a noun, as earlier English occasionally did; the sense is 'the achievement' or 'the accomplishing'. The repeated construction 'the achieve of, the mastery of the thing!' breaks off before its object, as if words fail. 'Achievement' also has a heraldic sense, the full display of a knight's arms, which suits the chivalric vocabulary of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'achieve, n.'; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes

  12. Brute beauty and valour and act l. 9

    'Brute' means animal, physical, without reason, and also raw. 'Act' is probably used in the scholastic sense (Latin actus) of the full realisation of a being's potential, a concept Hopkins knew from Aquinas and Scotus in his theological training. 'Air, pride, plume' adds the bird's element, its spirit and its feathers. 'Here' insists on this place and moment. All these are the subject of the crucial verb 'Buckle'.

    Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  13. Buckle! l. 10

    Grammatically 'Buckle' may be an imperative (the speaker commands these qualities to buckle) or an indicative present (they do buckle here). Its senses are opposed. 'Buckle' can mean to fasten together, as armour is buckled on, so the qualities combine in one act; to buckle to, to apply oneself or grapple; or to give way, crumple, as a wheel or beam buckles under strain. On the last reading, the bird's natural splendour collapses, and from that collapse a fire 'a billion / Times told lovelier' breaks out, an image of self-sacrifice and of Christ's Passion. William Empson treated the poem as an example of ambiguity that reflects a conflict in the writer's mind; most later editors keep several senses in play rather than choosing one.

    William Empson, Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930), ch. 7, on 'The Windhover'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'buckle, v.'

  14. AND l. 10

    Hopkins's capitals give 'AND' great emphasis, signalling consequence: when the qualities buckle, then fire breaks out. 'Thee' is ambiguous: it may be the bird, the speaker's heart, or Christ the 'chevalier'. 'Times told' means times counted (an old sense of 'tell', to count). 'More dangerous' introduces the note of risk and sacrifice that the sestet develops. The fire is at once the light flashing from the bird's turning wings and a spiritual radiance.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tell, v.'

  15. my chevalier l. 11

    'Chevalier' (French, 'horseman, knight') completes the chivalric imagery that began with 'minion' and 'dauphin' and the riding figures of the octave. With the dedication 'To Christ our Lord', most readers hear an address to Christ as knight, a figure with medieval roots and central to Ignatian spirituality: in the Spiritual Exercises the meditation on 'The Kingdom of Christ' imagines Christ as a king calling followers to a campaign. Some readers argue that the bird remains the addressee and the reference to Christ is analogical.

    Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises, 'Principle and Foundation' and 'The Kingdom of Christ' (Second Week); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); 'The Windhover', Wikipedia

  16. sheer plod makes plough down sillion / Shine ll. 12-13

    'No wonder of it' turns to two homely images that support the paradox of l. 10. The first is the ploughshare, worn bright by mere plodding through soil. 'Plough down sillion' is compressed: the plough, (going) down the furrow, shines. The accents in the archive text (on 'sheer' and 'plod') are Hopkins's stress marks, insisting on the heavy beats. The image suits the speaker whose 'heart in hiding' labours obscurely: hidden, repetitive work can shine as gloriously as the falcon's flight.

    Robert Bridges, ed., Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918), Author's Preface and editor's notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981); Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)

  17. sillion l. 12

    'Selion' is an old term for a strip or ridge of ploughed land in the open-field system; Hopkins's spelling 'sillion' brings it close to French sillon, 'furrow'. The word was archaic in 1877 and Hopkins chooses it for its sound (it rhymes with 'billion' and 'vermillion') and its precision. The plough is polished by passing down the furrow. Its rarity also makes the reader pause on the line, as on a heavy stress.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'selion'; W. H. Gardner and N. H. MacKenzie, eds, The Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, 4th edn (Oxford UP, 1967), notes; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  18. blue-bleak embers l. 13

    The second homely image: the embers of a fire, apparently cold, crumble when they fall, and the break exposes the live, intense colour within. The movement repeats the pattern of 'Buckle': collapse produces brilliance. 'Gall' and 'gash' carry overtones of wounding, and the 'gold-vermillion' colour suggests blood; readers widely see an allusion to the wounds of the crucified Christ, whose sacrifice is beauty breaking out through destruction.

    Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970); Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  19. ah my dear l. 13

    In Herbert's 'Love (III)' the speaker, ashamed to be welcomed by Love (Christ), says 'I the unkinde, ungratefull? Ah my deare, / I cannot look on thee'. Hopkins admired Herbert and the echo makes the aside an address to Christ, intimate and affectionate. Some readers take 'my dear' as addressed to the speaker's own heart; either way the tone softens the vocabulary of wounding that follows.

    George Herbert, 'Love (III)', The Temple (1633), l. 9 ('Ah my deare'); F. E. Hutchinson, ed., The Works of George Herbert (Clarendon Press, 1941), 'Love (III)'; Norman H. MacKenzie, A Reader's Guide to Gerard Manley Hopkins (Thames and Hudson, 1981)

  20. gall l. 14

    'Gall' as a verb means to make sore by rubbing, as a harness galls a horse. There is a further echo of the noun 'gall' (bitterness, bile) offered to Christ on the cross (Matthew 27:34), consistent with the poem's Passion imagery. The verbs 'Fall, gall ... gash' form an alliterating and rhyming sequence of increasing violence. The sounds are harsh and cutting, so the final line, like the embers, breaks open before its brilliant last word.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gall, v.1'; Bible (KJV), Matthew 27:34

  21. gold-vermillion l. 14

    Vermilion is a brilliant red pigment (mercuric sulphide), named from Latin vermiculus, 'little worm', after the kermes insect once used for red dye. Combined with gold, it gives the colours of fire and of royalty and, in the Passion reading, of blood shed for redemption. The final word of the poem thus joins the chivalric, the natural and the sacrificial strands.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'vermilion'; Paul L. Mariani, A Commentary on the Complete Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins (Cornell UP, 1970)
