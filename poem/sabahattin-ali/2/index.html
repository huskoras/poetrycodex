Living

Gurbet Hapishanesi

by Sabahattin Ali 1907–1948

Living

Düşünme, gününü doldur
Gurbet hapishanesinde;
Günler yıllara bedeldir
Gurbet hapishanesinde.

Bahtım dağları aşırdı,
Yadelde dama düşürdü.
Yine gözlerim yaşardı
Gurbet hapishanesinde.

Akşam gökler bulutlanır,
Demir kapılar kilitlenir,
Gönül her derde katlanır
Gurbet hapishanesinde.

Halini bilen bulunmaz,
Yüzüne gülen bulunmaz,
Kapıya gelen bulunmaz
Gurbet hapishanesinde.

Geniş ol, göklere bakın,
Çıkacağım günler yakın...
Yar, beni unutma sakın
Gurbet hapishanesinde.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Gurbet Hapishanesi ('The Prison of Exile') title; refrain

    Sabahattin Ali was arrested in Konya in December 1932 over a satirical poem, accused of insulting Mustafa Kemal, held in Konya and then sent to the fortress prison of Sinop on the Black Sea, and released under the amnesty for the Republic's tenth anniversary in October 1933. In 'Bir Şaka' (in Kağnı, 1936), generally read as an account of his own transfer to Sinop, the narrator imagines a lonely prison by the sea in a town where he knows no one and exclaims 'Gurbet hapishanesi!'. The poem's refrain uses the same phrase. The archive's source does not date the poem, so a direct link to Sinop is likely but not proven. Ali wrote many prison poems ('Hapishane Şarkısı' I–V among them), several later sung as popular songs.

    Wikipedia, 'Sabahattin Ali' (imprisonment, Sinop, amnesty; Dağlar ve Rüzgâr, 1934); Sabahattin Ali, 'Bir Şaka', in Kağnı (1936), the story of his transfer to Sinop prison

  2. Folk metre and refrain throughout (e.g. st. 3)

    Unlike the aruz of Ahmet Haşim, Sabahattin Ali uses the syllabic metre of Turkish folk poetry: 'Dü-şün-me, gü-nü-nü dol-dur' and 'Gur-bet ha-pis-ha-ne-sin-de' each have eight syllables, broken 4 + 4. The first stanza alternates, with the refrain as its second and fourth lines; stanzas 2 to 5 each have three lines on one rhyme (aşırdı / düşürdü / yaşardı; bulunmaz three times) and close on the unchanging refrain, the pattern of the türkü and of the folk lament. The repetition gives the effect of days that do not change. The choice of folk form also signals the left-populist sympathies of the poet, who drew on Anatolian folk song throughout his verse.

    Wikipedia, 'Sabahattin Ali' (imprisonment, Sinop, amnesty; Dağlar ve Rüzgâr, 1934); Talât S. Halman, A Millennium of Turkish Literature, ed. Jayne L. Warner (Syracuse University Press, 2011)

  3. gün doldurmak (to serve one's time) l. 1

    The opening advice is the prisoner's survival rule: do not brood, just get through each day. 'Günü doldurmak' means to fill the required time, as in serving a prison sentence or a term of service. The imperative 'Düşünme' (don't think) is spoken to oneself, which makes the poem a dialogue between the prisoner and his own mind. The phrase sets the tone of resigned endurance that the refrain then repeats.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  4. gurbet (exile, being far from home) l. 4

    Gurbet (Arabic ghurba, 'strangeness, absence from one's country') names the condition of the migrant, soldier or exile, and gurbet songs (gurbet türküleri) are a major folk genre; its opposite is sıla, home. The poem joins two kinds of confinement, prison and exile, so that the prison is doubly foreign. The adjective 'garip' (stranger, poor, lonely) comes from the same Arabic root.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  5. bedel (equivalent) l. 3

    Bedel (Arabic badal) means equivalent, substitute, price; 'bir şeye bedel' is 'worth as much as something'. In prison time stretches: each day weighs like a year. The line is an old commonplace of prison and exile poetry, here stated with folk simplicity. It also carries a legal colour suited to prison: the sentence is a price paid in time, and the prisoner pays it many times over, because each day costs him as much as a year would cost a free man.

    James W. Redhouse, A Turkish and English Lexicon (Constantinople, 1890); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  6. baht (fortune, fate) l. 5

    Baht (Persian bakht) is fortune or luck, usually as a force beyond one's control; 'kara baht' is ill fate. In folk poetry the lover or exile often blames his baht. Crossing mountains suggests the journey from inland Anatolia (Konya) to the Black Sea coast, though the poem keeps it general. The word appears in many folk-song formulas, such as 'bahtım kara' (my fate is black), and gives the stanza the voice of an anonymous folk singer rather than of an individual intellectual.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  7. yad el, dam (foreign land; prison) l. 6

    Yad (stranger, alien) and el (land, people) form 'yad el', a foreign land, a folk-poetry phrase close in meaning to gurbet. Dam means a roof or a single-room, flat-roofed house in Anatolian usage; in this context it is the prison cell, though a settled slang sense 'prison' could not be confirmed in the dictionaries consulted. The folk diction places the educated poet among ordinary prisoners in their own language.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  8. Evening lock-up st. 3

    The third stanza sets the sky's evening clouding beside the locking of iron doors, the moment when prisoners are confined to their wards for the night. The line 'Gönül her derde katlanır' (the heart endures every pain) is folk wisdom about resilience. Gönül, the heart as seat of feeling, is the common word of Turkish folk lyric where divan poetry might say dil.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Wikipedia, 'Sabahattin Ali' (imprisonment, Sinop, amnesty; Dağlar ve Rüzgâr, 1934)

  9. The triple 'bulunmaz' st. 4

    Instead of rhyme, the stanza repeats the same word, 'bulunmaz' (is not to be found), three times, a device (redif) common in folk and classical verse. The three absences grow more concrete: understanding, kindness, a visitor at the prison gate. The lack of visitors was a real hardship for prisoners held far from their families, since relatives brought food and news.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük; Sabahattin Ali, 'Bir Şaka', in Kağnı (1936), the story of his transfer to Sinop prison

  10. 'Geniş ol' (be broad-hearted) st. 5, l. 1

    'Geniş olmak' (to be broad, easy in mind) and 'gönlünü geniş tut' are idioms for patience and calm. The archive's text reads 'Geniş ol, göklere bakın', mixing a singular imperative (ol) with a plural one (bakın). It may address the self and then fellow prisoners, or it may be an error of transmission; the rhyme with 'yakın' in the next line explains the form 'bakın'. Students should note the uncertainty. The stanza turns towards hope: 'the days of my release are near'.

    Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük

  11. yâr (beloved) st. 5, l. 3

    Yâr (Persian yār, friend, beloved) is the standard address to the loved one in folk song. Ending a prison or exile song with an appeal not to be forgotten is a convention of the gurbet türküsü. Placed after the hope of release, the line shows the prisoner's real fear: not the walls but being forgotten outside them. Sabahattin Ali's love poems and his novel Kürk Mantolu Madonna share this theme of longing.

    F. Steingass, A Comprehensive Persian-English Dictionary (London, 1892); Türk Dil Kurumu, Güncel Türkçe Sözlük
