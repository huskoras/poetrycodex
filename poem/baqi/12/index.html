Death & Dying

O Thou! Fool-Bounden in the Mesh of Fame

by Bâqî 1526–1600

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

Death & Dying

O thou! fool-bounden in the mesh of fame and glory's snare!
Till when shall last the lust of faithless earth's pursuits and care?

At that first moment, which of life's fair springtide is the last,
'Tis need the tulip-cheek the tint of autumn leaf should wear;

'Tis need that thy last home should be, e'en like the dregs', the dust;
'Tis need the stone from hand of Fate should be joy's beaker's share.

He is a man indeed whose heart is as a mirror clear;
Man art thou? why then doth thy breast the tiger's fierceness bear?

In understanding's eye how long shall heedless slumber bide?
Will not war's Lion-Monarch's fate suffice to make thee ware?

He, Prince of Fortune's Cavaliers! he to whose charger bold,
Whene'er he caracoled or pranced, cramped was earth's tourney-square!

He, to the lustre of whose sword the Magyar bowed his head!
He, the dread gleaming of whose brand the Frank can well declare!

Like tender rose-leaf, gently laid he in the dust his face,
And Earth, the Treasurer, him placed like jewel in his case.

In truth, he was the radiance of rank high and glory great,
A Shah, Iskender-diademed, of Dârâ's armied state:

Before the dust beneath his feet the Sphere bent low its head;
Earth's shrine of adoration was his royal pavilion's gate.

The smallest of his gifts the meanest beggar made a prince;
Exceeding bounteous, exceeding kind a Potentate!

The court of glory of his kingly majesty most high
Was aye the centre where would hopes of sage and poet wait.

Although he yielded to Eternal Destiny's command,
A King was he in might as Doom and puissant as Fate!

Weary and worn by this sad, changeful Sphere, deem not thou him:
Near God to be, did he his rank and glory abdicate.

What wonder if our eyes no more life and the world behold!
His beauty fair, as sun and moon, did earth irradiate!

If folk upon the bright sun look, with tears are filled their eyes;
For seeing it, doth yon moon-face before their minds arise!

Now let the cloud blood drop on drop weep, and its form bend low!
And let the Judas-tree anew in blossoms gore-hued blow!

With this sad anguish let the stars' eyes rain down bitter tears!
And let the smoke from hearts on fire the heavens all darkened show!

Their azure garments let the skies change into deepest black!
Let the whole world attire itself in robes of princely woe!

In breasts of fairies and of men still let the flame burn on —
Of parting from the blest King Süleyman the fiery glow!

His home above the Highest Heaven's ramparts he hath made:
This world was all unworthy of his majesty, I trow.

The bird, his soul, hath, huma-like, aloft flown to the skies,
And nought remaineth save a few bones on the earth below.

The speeding Horseman of the plain of Time and Space was he;
Fortune and Fame aye as his friends and bridle-guides did go.

The wayward courser, cruel Fate, was wild and fierce of pace,
And fell to earth the Shade of God the Lord's benignant Grace.

Through grief for thee, bereft of rest and tearful e'en as I,
Sore weeping let the cloud of spring go wand'ring through the sky!

And let the wailing of the birds of dawn the whole world fill!
Be roses torn! and let the nightingale distressful cry!

Their hyacinths as weeds of woe displaying, let them weep,
Down o'er their skirts their flowing tears let pour — the mountains high!

The odour of thy kindliness recalling, tulip-like,
Within the Tatar musk-deer's heart let fire of anguish lie!

Through yearning for thee let the rose its ear lay on the path,
And, narcisse-like, till the Last Day the watchman's calling ply!

Although the pearl-diffusing eye to oceans turned the world,
Ne'er into being should there come a pearl with thee to vie!

O heart! this hour 'tis thou that sympathiser art with me;
Come, let us like the flute bewail, and moan, and plaintive sigh!

The notes of mourning and of dole aloud let us rehearse;
And let all those who grieve be moved by this our seven-fold verse.

Will earth's King ne'er awake from sleep? — broke hath the dawn of day:
Will ne'er he move forth from his tent, adorned as Heaven's display?

Long have our eyes dwelt on the road, and yet no news hath come
From yonder land, the threshold of his majesty's array:

The colour of his cheek hath paled, dry-lipped he lieth there,
E'en like that rose which from the vase of flowers hath fall'n away.

Goes now the Khusrev of the skies behind the cloudy veil,
For shame, remembering thy love and kindness, one would say.

My prayer is ever: "May the babes, his tears, go 'neath the sod,
Or old or young be he who weeps not thee in sad dismay."

With flame of parting from thee let the sun burn and consume;
And o'er the wastes through grief let darkness of the clouds hold sway.

Thy talents and thy feats let it recall and weep in blood,
Yea, let thy sabre from its sheath plunge in the darksome clay.

Its collar, through its grief and anguish, let the reed-pen tear!
And let the earth its vestment rend through sorrow and despair!

Thy sabre made the foe the anguish dire of wounds to drain;
Their tongues are silenced, none who dares to gainsay doth remain.

The youthful cypress, head-exalted, looked upon thy lance,
And ne'er its lissom twigs their haughty airs displayed again.

Where'er thy stately charger placed his hoof, from far and near
Flocked nobles, all upon thy path their lives to offer fain.

In desert of mortality the bird, desire, rests ne'er;
Thy sword in cause of God did lives as sacrifice ordain.

As sweeps a scimitar, across earth's face on every side,
Of iron-girded heroes of the world thou threw'st a chain.

Thou took'st a thousand idol-temples, turnedst all to mosques;
Where jangled bells thou mad'st be sung the Call to Prayers' strain.

At length is struck the signal-drum, and thou hast journeyed hence;
Lo! thy first resting-place is Eden's flowery, verdant plain.

Praise is to God! for He in the Two Worlds hath blessed thee,
And caused thy glorious name, Hero and Martyr both to be.

Bâqî, the beauty of the King, the heart's delight, behold!
The mirror of the work of God, the Lord of Right, behold!

The dear old man hath passed away from th' Egypt sad, the world;
The youthful Prince, alert and fair as Joseph bright, behold!

The Sun hath risen, and the Dawning grey hath touched its bourne;
The lovely face of yon Khusrev, whose soul is light, behold!

This chase now to the grave hath sent the Behrâm of the Age;
Go, at his threshold serve, King Erdeshir aright, behold!

The blast of Fate to all the winds hath blown Suleymân's throne;
Sultan Selim Khân on Iskender's couch of might, behold!

The Tiger of the mount of war to rest in sleep hath gone;
The Lion who doth now keep watch on glory's height, behold!

The Peacock fair of Eden's mead hath soared to Heaven's parterre;
The lustre of the Huma of high, happy flight, behold!

Eternal may the glory of the heaven-high Khusrev dwell!
Blessings be on the Monarch's soul and spirit: — and farewell!

Codex Note

Terkib-bend. Elegy on Sultan Suleyman I.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Baqi's elegy for Suleyman st. 1

    Baqi (1526-1600), called in his lifetime the 'sultan of poets', had won Suleyman's favour with a qasida in 1555; his elegy for the sultan is generally counted his masterpiece and one of the great poems of Ottoman literature, and Gibb reports that Von Hammer called it the most beautiful poem in the whole range of Ottoman literature. Suleyman, called the Magnificent in Europe and Kanuni ('the Lawgiver') in Turkey, died on the night of 6-7 September 1566 during the siege of Szigetvar. The first strophe, Gibb notes, is addressed to the reader: its first line ('O you, foot-bound in the snare of the trap of fame and shame') is a memento mori. 'Fool-bounden' is Gibb's coinage for a Turkish compound meaning 'fettered at the feet'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Baki'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), chapter on Baqi; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  2. The terkib-bend and its 'seven-fold verse' st. 4

    A terkib-bend is a strophic poem: each strophe (khane) consists of couplets on one rhyme, followed by a couplet with a different rhyme (the vasita or bend, 'tie') that links it to the next. Gibb's English version has seven strophes of seven rhyming couplets, each closed by such a couplet, and his 'seven-fold verse' refers, as his note 206 explains, to the seven rhyming couplets of each strophe. Gibb, who translated 'in the original forms', reproduces the scheme: each strophe has its own rhyme (snare / wear / share; great / state / gate; etc.) and ends on a couplet rhyming together (face / case). The linking couplets carry the most quotable lines of the poem.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  3. The dregs and the dust st. 1

    Gibb's note 197 explains that the Persians throw aside the lees after drinking a cup of wine. The image fits the poem's governing metaphor of life as a feast that must end. 'The tulip-cheek' that must take 'the tint of autumn leaf' is the beautiful young face that turns yellow in age and death; in Ottoman lyric the red tulip is a figure of youth and of the wounded, blood-stained heart. The strophe's argument is that if even Suleyman died, the reader's pursuit of fame is foolish.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  4. The stone in the beaker st. 1

    Gibb's note 198 states that a pebble thrown into a beaker is the signal for a party to break up, and cross-refers to another note in the volume. FitzGerald's first-edition opening of the Rubaiyat uses a related custom ('Morning in the Bowl of Night / Has flung the Stone that puts the Stars to Flight'), which FitzGerald glossed as the desert signal 'To Horse!'. In Baqi the stone also breaks the glass: joy's beaker is shattered by Fate. The image links the opening meditation to the drinking-party (bezm) imagery of Ottoman lyric.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Edward FitzGerald, Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam (1859), stanza I and note

  5. The charger and Rustem's Rakhsh st. 1

    Gibb's note 199 explains that the word translated 'charger', rakhsh, 'lightning', was the name of the famous steed of Rustem, the national hero of Persia, whose adventures fill much of Firdawsi's Shahnama. The sultan is thus made a new Rustem, so mighty that earth's 'tourney-square' was too small for his horse's prancing. The couplet calls him 'Prince of Fortune's Cavaliers', and the one before 'war's Lion-Monarch', the sultan as warrior of the faith, the dominant image of the first half of the elegy.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Firdawsi, Shahnama, the story of Rustem and Rakhsh

  6. The Magyar and the Frank st. 1

    Suleyman's reign was marked by campaigns in central Europe: he took Belgrade (1521), destroyed the Hungarian army at Mohacs (1526), besieged Vienna (1529) and annexed central Hungary with Buda (1541). He died on his thirteenth campaign, at the siege of the Hungarian fortress of Szigetvar. 'Frank' (Turkish Frenk) is the general Ottoman word for western Europeans. The Turkish line plays on the sword's lustre and its 'dread gleaming', which Gibb renders with two different nouns, 'sword' and 'brand' (an archaic word for a sword).

    Halil Inalcik, The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300-1600 (London: Weidenfeld and Nicolson, 1973), on Suleyman's last campaign and the succession; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Suleyman the Magnificent'; E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  7. Earth the Treasurer st. 1

    The couplet that closes the first strophe turns burial into treasure-keeping: Earth is the treasurer who places the sultan in the grave as a jewel is laid in its case. The comparison of the face to a 'tender rose-leaf' laid gently down softens the warrior's portrait just drawn. Gibb renders the Turkish linking couplet with an English rhymed couplet (face / case), marking the formal break between strophes. The jewel image returns in the fourth strophe, where no pearl can ever equal the dead king.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  8. Iskender and Dara st. 2

    In Persian and Ottoman tradition Iskender (Alexander the Great) is the model world-conqueror, and Dara (Darius III) the last king of the ancient Iranian dynasty he defeated; Gibb's note 200 identifies Dara as Darius, the last monarch of the Keyani dynasty. Nizami's Iskendername and the Shahnama made both familiar. Comparing Suleyman to both the conqueror and the conquered emphasises sheer imperial grandeur. In the final strophe 'Iskender's couch of might' becomes the throne to which Selim succeeds.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Nizami, Iskandarnama (Book of Alexander)

  9. Tears at the sun: husn-i ta'lil st. 2

    Husn-i ta'lil, 'beautiful explanation of cause', is the rhetorical figure of giving a poetic false cause for a natural fact. Gibb's note 202 explains: the tears that come when one looks at the sun are here ascribed to sorrow for the loss of the Sultan, whose glorious face the splendour of the sun recalls. It is the closing, linking couplet of the second strophe, and like the others it gathers the strophe's praise of the king's 'beauty fair, as sun and moon' into one memorable conceit.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  10. The Judas-tree st. 3

    The erghavan, the Judas-tree (Cercis siliquastrum), is often mentioned in Oriental poetry; it is common around Istanbul, where its purple-pink flowers cover the Bosphorus hills each spring before the leaves appear. Its colour makes it an emblem of blood and of passionate grief. The strophe is a sustained call for nature to mourn, a kind of pathetic fallacy: cloud weeps blood, stars rain tears, the sky changes its blue robe for black.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Oxford English Dictionary, 'Judas-tree, n.'

  11. Heaven in mourning st. 3

    Black and dark blue were mourning colours in the Ottoman world, and the poet orders the whole cosmos to put them on. The 'robes of princely woe' are the mourning garments of a royal court. The strophe also mentions 'fairies and ... men' (peri and human), the two orders of beings who share the grief. Andrews and Kalpakli read Ottoman courtly poetry as a public performance of relationships of love and loyalty to the sultan; the elegy's universal mourning is the largest expression of such loyalty.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Walter G. Andrews and Mehmet Kalpakli, The Age of Beloveds: Love and the Beloved in Early-Modern Ottoman and European Culture and Society (Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2005)

  12. The huma st. 3

    Gibb's note 203 describes the huma as a fabulous bird often mentioned in Eastern poetry, of the happiest augury: every head it overshadows will one day wear a crown; it also lives entirely on bones, never hurting any living creature. Baqi uses both traits: the sultan's soul flies up like a huma, and 'nought remaineth save a few bones on the earth below', bones being the huma's food. In the last strophe the 'Huma of high, happy flight' is the new sultan, Selim, on whom the bird's shadow now falls.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  13. 'the Shade of God' st. 3

    The title zill Allah, 'the shadow of God (on earth)', was used for caliphs and sultans from the early Islamic centuries, based on a saying attributed to the Prophet that the just ruler is God's shadow on earth, in whom the oppressed take refuge. Ottoman chancery documents applied it to the sultan. The linking couplet of strophe 3 shows Suleyman as a rider thrown when 'the wayward courser, cruel Fate' bolted: the horseman of Time and Space falls, and with him falls the shelter of God's grace. The horse imagery ties back to the charger of strophe 1.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Halil Inalcik, The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300-1600 (London: Weidenfeld and Nicolson, 1973), on Suleyman's last campaign and the succession

  14. Lamenting like the flute st. 4

    The ney, the reed flute, is the classic instrument of lament in the Persianate world, above all because of the opening of Rumi's Masnavi, where the reed cries out for the reed-bed from which it was cut (see the Masnavi prologue in this archive). Baqi's appeal to the heart as fellow-mourner thus makes the elegy a song of separation in the mystical as well as the political sense. The strophe's nature images are precise: hyacinths as 'weeds of woe' (mourning clothes), the rose's petal shaped like an ear (Gibb's note 205), the narcissus, a watchful eye, crying like a night watchman.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Rumi, Masnavi I.1-18 (the Song of the Reed)

  15. The king who does not leave his tent st. 5

    Gibb's note 207 recalls that Sultan Suleyman I died in his camp before Szigeth in Hungary. The historical background sharpens the strophe: the grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha concealed the sultan's death from the army for weeks, until the fortress had fallen and the heir Selim could secure the throne, so the soldiers did wait for a king who would not come out. 'Long have our eyes dwelt on the road, and yet no news hath come' thus records a real anxiety. The pale, dry-lipped king is compared to a rose fallen from the vase.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Halil Inalcik, The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300-1600 (London: Weidenfeld and Nicolson, 1973), on Suleyman's last campaign and the succession; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Suleyman the Magnificent'

  16. 'the Khusrev of the skies' st. 5

    Khusrev (Persian Khusraw), the name of two Sasanian kings and of the royal lover of Nizami's Khusraw and Shirin, became a general word for a great king. Gibb's note 208 identifies the Khusrev of the skies as the sun, which now goes behind a veil of cloud 'for shame', as if it could not bear to shine after the sultan's light has gone. The conceit links back to the sun of the second strophe and forward to the last strophe, where Selim is a 'Khusrev' whose rising replaces the setting of his father.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  17. Tears as babes st. 5

    Gibb's note 209 explains that tears are sometimes compared to babes, being sprung from man, perhaps from merdumek, the 'manikin' of the eye; here those babes are to die and be buried, that is, the unsympathising man is to have cause to soak the ground with bitter tears. The couplet is a curse disguised as a prayer, and it shows how far the poem's conceits depend on Turkish and Persian wordplay that Gibb can only explain in notes. Compare the merdum pun in Fuzuli's ghazel in this archive.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  18. The reed-pen tears its collar st. 5

    Tearing the collar (yaka) of one's shirt was a gesture of extreme grief in Ottoman and Persian culture, as rending garments is in the Hebrew Bible. A reed pen (qalam) is split at the tip, so the poet sees its slit as a torn collar: the very instrument of the elegy mourns. The same strophe makes the sultan's sabre plunge from its sheath into 'the darksome clay', burying itself with its master. This is the linking couplet of the fifth strophe.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  19. Idol-temples turned to mosques st. 6

    Ottoman conquest of a city was symbolised by converting its principal church into a mosque, and church bells, forbidden in Muslim usage, were silenced. To Ottoman poets, churches with their images were butkhane, 'idol-houses'. The sixth strophe praises Suleyman as a ghazi, a warrior for the faith, whose sword 'in cause of God' sacrificed lives. Modern readers will note the triumphalist character of this court poetry; it is the language of the Ottoman ideology of holy war that sustained Suleyman's European campaigns.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Halil Inalcik, The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300-1600 (London: Weidenfeld and Nicolson, 1973), on Suleyman's last campaign and the succession

  20. Ghazi and Shehid st. 6

    Gibb's note 210 explains Gazi ve Shehid as 'Muslim conqueror and martyr': both are pleasing to God, since whoever dies in the field against unbelievers is crowned with martyrdom, while the conqueror is rewarded in the next world. He quotes Prince Cantemir's history: the Turks believed Suleyman a favourite of heaven because he died at Szigeth and so became a martyr, and was also a conqueror. 'Eden's flowery, verdant plain' is the martyr's immediate entry to Paradise (Q 3:169).

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Qur'an 3:169

  21. Selim's strophe and the 'old man' of Egypt st. 7

    Gibb's note 211 says the strophe honours Sultan Selim II, Suleyman's son and successor, and that the third line of his translation is wrong: it should read 'The old Vezir hath passed away from th' Egypt of the world'. The allusion is to the story of Joseph: Zuleykha's husband, the Grand Vizier of Egypt ('Aziz-i Misr), dies, and Joseph rises. Suleyman is the old vizier, Selim the young Joseph, both beautiful and destined to rule. The archive therefore prints Gibb's text with the error he himself identified. Each couplet of the strophe pairs the departed and the new ruler (dawn and sun, Behram and Erdeshir, tiger and lion), with the refrain 'behold!'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Qur'an 12:21-30

  22. Behram Gur and Erdeshir st. 7

    Gibb's note 212 explains that the Sasanian king Behram V (Bahram Gur, r. 420-38) was famous for hunting the gur or wild ass, and legend said he disappeared in a morass while chasing one. Since gur also means 'tomb' in Persian, poets made an equivoque of it: 'this chase (life) hath at length caused the Behram of the age (Suleyman) to reach the gur'. Erdeshir is the Persian Ardashir, whose Greek form is Artaxerxes. Behram here represents Suleyman, Erdeshir Selim.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215; Nizami, Haft Paykar (the story of Bahram Gur)

  23. Suleyman's throne and the wind st. 7

    In the Qur'an God subjects the wind to Solomon (Sulayman), so that it blows at his command (21:81; 38:36), and Islamic legend has Solomon's throne carried through the air on a carpet borne by the wind. Baqi reverses the miracle: 'the blast of Fate' has blown the throne of this Suleyman to the winds. Iskender's 'couch of might' (compare st. 2) now belongs to Sultan Selim Khan. The couplet thus uses the sultan's name, as Ottoman poets constantly did, to fold him into sacred and legendary history.

    Qur'an 21:81 and 38:36; E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215

  24. The Peacock of Eden st. 7

    Gibb's note 213 recounts the legend that before the Fall the peacock was the most beautiful bird in Paradise, its plumage like pearl and emerald, and its voice so melodious that it was appointed to sing God's praises daily in the streets of heaven; in Islamic tales of the Fall the peacock helps Iblis enter the garden and is expelled with Adam. Baqi's image is purely laudatory: the peacock 'hath soared to Heaven's parterre', returning to its first home, and the huma of happy flight (compare st. 3) now overshadows Selim. The final couplet is the poem's blessing and farewell.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems (London: Trubner, 1882), Baqi selection, and notes 196-215
