One Night When All the Battlements Heaven's Castle

by Bâqî 1526–1600

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

History & Politics

One night when all the battlements Heaven's castle doth display,
Illumed and decked were, with the shining lamps, the stars' array,

Amidst the host of gleaming stars the Moon lit up his torch;
Athwart the field of Heaven with radiance beamed the Milky Way.

The Secretary of the Spheres had ta'en his meteor-pen,
That writer of His signature whom men and jinns obey.

There, at the banquet of the sky, had Venus struck her lyre,
In mirth and happiness, delighted, joyed and smiling gay.

Taking the keynote for her tune 'neath in the vaulted sphere,
The tambourinist Sun her visage bright had hid away.

Armed with a brand of gleaming gold had leapt into the plain
The Swordsman of the sky's expanse, of heaven's field of fray.

To give direction to the weighty matters of the earth
Had Jupiter, the wise, lit up reflection's taper's ray.

There raised aloft old Saturn high upon the Seventh Sphere
Sitting like Indian elephant-conductor on did stray.

"What means this decking of the universe?" I wond'ring said;
When, lo! with meditation's gaze e'en whilst I it survey,

Casting its beams on every side, o'er all earth rose the Sun,
O'er the horizons, e'en as Seal of Suleymân's display.

The eye of understanding looked upon this wondrous sight;
At length the soul's ear learned the secret hid in this which lay:

What is it that hath decked earth's hall with splendours such as this,
Saving the might and fortune of the King who earth doth sway?

He who sits high upon the throne above all crowned kings,
The Hero of the battlefield of dread Keyâni fray,

Jemshid of happiness and joy, Darius of the fight,
Khusrev of right and clemency, Iskender of his day!

Lord of the East and West! King whom the kings of earth obey!
Prince of the Epoch! Sultan Süleymân! Triumphant Aye!

Meet 'tis before the steed of yonder Monarch of the realms
Of right and equity, should march earth's rulers' bright array.

Rebelled one 'gainst his word, secure he'd bind him in his bonds,
E'en like the dappled pard, the sky, chained with the Milky Way.

Lord of the land of graciousness and bounty, on whose board
Of favours, spread is all the wealth that sea and mine display;

Longs the perfumer, Early Spring, for th' odour of his grace;
Need hath the merchant, Autumn, of his bounteous hand alway.

Through tyrant's hard oppression no one groaneth in his reign,
And though may wail the flute and lute, the law they disobey.

Beside thy justice, tyranny's the code of Key-Qubâd;
Beside thy wrath, but mildness Qahramân's most deadly fray.

Thy scimitar's the gleaming guide empires to overthrow,
No foe of Islâm can abide before thy sabre's ray.

Saw it thy wrath, through dread of thee would trembling seize the pine;
The falling stars a chain around the heaven's neck would lay.

Amidst thy sea-like armies vast, thy flags and standards fair,
The sails are which the ship of splendid triumph doth display.

Thrust it its beak into the Sphere, 'twould seize it as a grain,
The 'anqa strong, thy power, to which 'twere but a seed-like prey.

In past eternity the hand, thy might, it struck with bat,
That time is this time, for the Sky's Ball spins upon its way.

Within the rosy garden of thy praise the bird, the heart,
Singeth this soul-bestowing, smooth-as-water-running lay

If yonder mouth be not the soul, O heart-enslaver gay,
Then wherefore is it like the soul, hid from our eyes away?

Since in the casket of our mind thy ruby's picture lies,
The mine is now no fitting home for gem of lustrous ray.

Thy tresses fall across thy cheek in many a twisting curl,
"To dance to Hijâz have the Shâmîs tucked their skirts," we'd say.

Let both the youthful pine and cypress view thy motions fair;
The gardener now to rear the willow need no more assay.

The dark and cloudy brained of men thine eyebrows black depict,
While those of keen, discerning wit thy glistening teeth portray.

Before thy cheek the rose and jasmine bowed in sujûd,
The cypress to thy figure in qiyâm did homage pay.

The heart's throne is the seat of that great monarch, love for thee;
The soul, the secret court, where doth thy ruby's picture stay.

The radiance of thy beauty bright hath filled earth like the sun,
The hall, BE! and it is, resounds with love of thee for aye.

The cries of those on plain of earth have risen to the skies,
The shouts of those who dwell above have found to earth their way.

Nor can the nightingale with songs as sweet as Bâqî's sing,
Nor happy as thy star can beam the garden's bright array.

The mead, the world, blooms through thy beauty's rose, like Irem's bower;
On every side are nightingales of sweet, melodious lay.

Now let us pray at Allah's court: "May this for aye endure,
The might and glory of this prospered King's resplendent sway;

Until the lamp, the world-illuming sun, at break of dawn,
A silver candelabrum on the circling skies display,

Oh! may the Ruler of the world with skirt of aid and grace
Protect the taper of his life from blast of doom, we pray!"

Glory's the comrade; Fortune, the cup-bearer at our feast;
The beaker is the Sphere: the bowl, the Steel of gold-inlay!

Codex Note

Qasida (In Praise of Sultan Suleyman I.).

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: Bâkî's qasida for Süleyman couplet 1

    Bâkî (Mahmud Abdülbâki, 1526-1600), the son of a muezzin, became the leading lyric poet of Süleyman's court and was later called 'sultan of poets'. Gibb's heading gives the opening as 'Hengâm-ı şeb ki kungure-i çarh-ı âsmân' ('at the hour of night when the battlements of the wheel of heaven...'). Gibb says this qasida is usually placed first in collections of Bâkî's poems and is 'a perfect example of the form': a long mono-rhymed poem of praise that moves through fixed stages. Here the exordium (nesîb or teşbîb) is a starry night in which the seven planets appear one by one; then comes the turn to the sultan (girizgâh), the praise (medhiye), an inset ghazel (tegazzül), the poet's signature couplet and a closing prayer (duâ). Gibb's second version (1904) differs in many lines and is worth comparing. The archive text is the 1882 version.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 77ff. and notes 170-184, pp. 252-254; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, pp. 147-151, with notes; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Baki'

  2. Heaven as a fortified palace l. 1

    The opening image turns the night sky into a palace (Gibb's 1904 version says 'heaven's sublime seray', glossing seray as 'palace') whose crenellations are hung with lamps. Ottoman cities were illuminated (donanma) for royal victories and festivals, and the conceit makes the whole cosmos keep holiday for the sultan before the poet reveals why. The heavens of the poem are those of Ptolemaic astronomy as Muslim poets knew it: nine concentric spheres, from the sphere of the Moon up to the starless Empyrean. Gibb lists them in his notes and adds the conventional personifications that the next couplets use: the Moon a fair youth or girl, Mercury a scribe, Venus a woman musician, the Sun a sovereign, Mars a warrior, Jupiter a judge or vizier, Saturn an old man.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 84, p. 240 (the nine spheres and the planets personified); E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 148, nn. 1-4

  3. The Milky Way: the 'straw-bearers' road' l. 4; again l. 34

    Gibb's 1904 version keeps the Persian picture: 'the Straw-bearers' Way', and his note explains that keh-keşân, 'the straw-bearers', is the Persian name for what English calls the Milky Way. The image is of chaff dropped along a road by carriers of straw. The same band of light returns later in the panegyric, where the spotted night sky is a leopard held on the chain of the Milky Way. Here, at the start, it simply completes the illuminated field of heaven.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 148, n. 3; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text, p. 77

  4. Mercury, the scribe of heaven ll. 5-6

    In astrological convention Mercury is the planet of scribes and secretaries. Gibb's 1904 version calls him 'The Scribe of heaven' and 'The Cipherer of His decrees', and his note explains that the Cipherer (nişancı) was the Ottoman official who traced the sultan's tuğra, the calligraphic royal monogram, on decrees. So the cosmos is pictured as an Ottoman chancery: God issues commands, Mercury countersigns them with a shooting star for a reed pen, and 'men and jinns obey'. Each of the seven planets is given its traditional office in turn, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, in the order of their spheres.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 148, nn. 4-5; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 84, p. 240

  5. Venus the minstrel, the Sun the tambourine-player ll. 7-10

    Venus (Zühre) is conventionally a woman musician, so at the 'banquet of the sky' she plays the harp or lute. The Sun is usually a king, but Gibb notes that here she is a tambourinist, because the solar disc is round like a tambourine and seems to spin as a player spins her instrument on a finger. The Sun is absent because it is night: she has hidden her face 'neath the vaulted sphere'. Gibb's 1882 note refers the reader to a further note on the Sun's sex when personified. The detail prepares the climax of the exordium, when the Sun rises after all.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 171, p. 252; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 149, n. 1

  6. Mars, Jupiter and Saturn ll. 11-16

    Mars, the Lesser Infortune, is the warrior with a golden blade. Jupiter, the Greater Fortune and most auspicious planet, sits like a vizier or judge pondering affairs by candlelight. Saturn, the Greater Infortune, highest and slowest of the seven, is an old man on the seventh sphere; Gibb notes that he is often called the elephant-driver of the heavens and is figured as an Indian because he is inauspicious and therefore dark. Gibb's 1904 version makes him 'an Indian watchman, old and grey'. The list of planets is complete just as the poet asks what this pageant means.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 78 and 172, pp. 239 and 252; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 148, n. 4; p. 149, n. 2

  7. The rising Sun as Solomon's seal: the turn to the sultan ll. 19-24

    In Islamic legend the prophet-king Solomon (Süleyman) ruled jinn, winds and beasts through a seal-ring engraved with the Name of God (the Qur'an speaks of his command over jinn and birds). The sunrise is compared to that seal, and Gibb notes the double reference to Solomon and to the sultan who bore his name. The couplets that follow are what Gibb calls the guriz-gâh, 'the place of flight', where the poet passes from the exordium to the panegyric: the planets' festival was a display of the 'might and fortune of the King who earth doth sway'. The sequence of night, then dawn, then the king, is a compliment in itself: Süleyman is the sun that the stars were waiting for.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 173, p. 252; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 149, nn. 3-4; The Qur'an, 27:16 -17 (Solomon taught the speech of birds; his hosts of jinn, men and birds); TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  8. Jemshid, Darius, Khusrev, Iskender ll. 27-28

    These are the stock royal exemplars of Persian and Ottoman panegyric, known chiefly through Firdawsī's Shāhnāma and the Alexander romances. Jemshid (Cemşîd), the legendary king of the golden age, owned the world-revealing cup and is the type of royal festivity. Darius (Dârâ) is the last Achaemenid king, defeated by Alexander, here simply the type of a warrior king. Khusrev (Hüsrev) is a Sasanian king; Gibb's 1904 version says 'Kisra', which he identifies as Anushirvan, the type of a just king. Iskender, Alexander the Great, is the world-conqueror. Gibb's note 174 explains Keyâni (from the legendary Kayanian dynasty) as meaning simply 'imperial'. The 1904 version also adds 'Darius of the age' in the next line.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 174, p. 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), p. 149, nn. 5-6; TDV contributor, 'Cemşîd', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; TDV contributor, 'İskender', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; TDV contributor, 'Hüsrev', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  9. Sultan Süleyman I l. 30

    The panegyric piles title on title: Lord of East and West, King of Kings, Prince of the Epoch. Under Süleyman the empire reached its widest extent, and Gibb's notes on this poem remark that Ottoman poetry also reached its highest point in his reign. Bâkî is said to have entered court circles with a qasida presented to the sultan on his return from the Nakhchivan campaign (dated August 1555 in the Turkish encyclopedia entry; some popular sources say 1554); whether that qasida is this one is not stated in the sources consulted here, and readers should not assume it. Bâkî's later Elegy on Sultan Süleyman (also in the archive with its own notes) mourns the same king after his death at Szigetvár in 1566.

    Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Baki'; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 182, p. 253; 'Bâkî', Kure Ansiklopedi (Turkish encyclopedia entry on the poet; date of the Nakhchivan qasida)

  10. The leopard-sky in chains ll. 33-34

    Gibb's note explains that the starry sky is compared to a spotted leopard ('pard' is the old poetic word for leopard), held by the band of the Milky Way as by a chain. The two translations take the couplet differently: in 1882 it is a conditional threat (anyone who rebelled would be bound like the sky), while in 1904 the sky itself 'had made revolt' and been dragged to the sultan as a captive. The difference shows how compressed the original is. Either way the hyperbole is cosmic: even the heavens are subject to the sultan's chain.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), text; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pard'

  11. Spring the perfumer, Autumn the merchant ll. 37-38

    Gibb explains that Autumn is a rich merchant (in 1904, 'householder') who scatters gold, the yellow leaves, on every side, yet even he needs the sultan's generosity. Spring, which makes the world fragrant, is a perfumer who envies the 'odour' of the sultan's grace. Personified seasons are common in Ottoman verse (Gibb mentions the allegory of King Winter and King Spring elsewhere in his notes). The couplet belongs to the medhiye's praise of royal generosity, which follows the praise of justice and precedes that of the sultan's wrath.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 175, p. 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida

  12. Only the music laments ll. 39-40

    The plaintive tones of the reed-flute (ney) and lute were heard as weeping. Bâkî turns this into a compliment: so complete is the sultan's justice that the only 'wailing' left in the land is the music's, and that is an offence against his law. This is the figure Ottoman rhetoric called hüsn-i ta'lîl, inventing a fanciful cause for a natural fact; Gibb points out the same figure in the inset ghazel, where flowers bend in the wind and the poet says they bow in worship.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 180, p. 253 (the same figure at sujûd and qiyâm)

  13. Key-Qubâd and Qahramân ll. 41-42

    Gibb's notes identify Key-Qubâd as the founder of the legendary Kayanian (Keyânî) dynasty of Persia, whose adventures are told in Firdawsī's Shāhnāma, and Qahramân as a legendary hero whom the kings of the fairies asked for help against the demons; his adventures fill the popular Qahramân-Nâma. In 1882 Gibb glosses Key-Qubâd as Dejoces, an identification he drops in 1904. The couplet pairs the two halves of kingship that panegyric always praises together: justice (adl) and terrifying wrath (kahr).

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 176-177, p. 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes 5-6 to the qasida

  14. The 'Anqa of Mount Qaf ll. 49-50

    The 'Anqa (Persian Sîmurg) is the great mythical bird of Near Eastern legend, said to nest on Mount Qaf, the mountain range that rings the world; Gibb's 1882 notes quote the cosmographer Qazwini's claim that it can carry off an elephant. In Sufi poetry, above all Attar's Conference of the Birds, it figures the unreachable divine. Here it is simply the measure of power: to such a bird the whole sphere of heaven is a single grain. Gibb's 1904 version makes the earth 'one bite' for 'the canqa of the Qaf'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 6 on the 'Anqa, pp. 227-228; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida; TDV contributor, 'Ankā', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  15. The sphere as a polo ball ll. 51-52

    Polo was a royal game in Persia, and the ball and stick (gûy u çevgân) are a favourite courtly image. Bâkî says that the sultan's might struck the ball of heaven in 'past eternity' (ezel, the timeless beginning when destinies were fixed), and 'that time is this time': the sky's turning is the continuing effect of that single blow. Gibb calls the idea extravagant and quotes a parallel from Hafiz, in which the sphere circles like a steed urged by the beloved's whip. It is another case of a fanciful cause given to a natural fact.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 178, p. 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida

  16. The inset ghazel (tegazzül) ll. 55-72

    Ottoman qasidas often contain a ghazel, introduced with a new matla' (a couplet in which both lines rhyme). Gibb notes that the ghazel begins here and is addressed to an imaginary being, 'conceived either as an earthly beauty or as a divine ideal'. Its first conceit is the beloved's mouth, so tiny that it is invisible, and so like the soul, which is also hidden and is the source of the lover's life. The bird-heart that 'singeth' this lay in the sultan's garden of praise links the ghazel to the panegyric frame. Gibb marks the end of the ghazel at the couplet about earth's cries rising to the skies.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes on the inset ghazel; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  17. Syrians dancing to Hijaz: a double meaning ll. 59-60

    Gibb's notes untangle a pun typical of Bâkî's fondness for îhâm (a word used in two senses at once). Shâmî means Syrian, and also, through Persian shâm 'evening', dark like evening, so it suits black hair. Hijâz is the region of Arabia with Mecca and Medina, the goal of pilgrims setting out from Syria, and also one of the modes (makam) of Ottoman music. So the curls tumbling over the cheek are Syrians girding up their robes for the journey to Hijaz, or dancers moving to the Hijaz mode. Gibb's 1882 note 85 gives the same musical pun for Iraq and Hijaz in another poem.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 85 and 179, pp. 240 and 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida

  18. Sujûd and qiyâm: the flowers at prayer ll. 65-66

    Sujûd is the position in the Muslim ritual prayer (namaz) in which the forehead touches the ground; qiyâm is the upright standing position. Gibb explains the couplet: the rose and jasmine (the red and white of a lovely cheek) bend when the wind blows, but the poet says they prostrate themselves before a cheek fairer than themselves; the cypress, naturally erect, stands up in homage to a figure more graceful still. The symbols of beauty thus perform the prayer before the beloved. Gibb cites it as another example of hüsn-i ta'lîl. The religious language is bold, and it is one reason readers ask whether the beloved of the inset ghazel is human or divine.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 180, p. 253; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida

  19. 'Be! and it is': the universe l. 70

    The Qur'an says that when God decrees a thing, He says to it only 'Be!' and it is (2:117 and elsewhere). Gibb's note: kun, 'Be!', was God's fiat to creation, so 'the hall, BE! and it is' simply means the universe. The phrase was a commonplace of theology, Sufism and poetry for creation out of nothing. Here it gives the beloved's beauty a cosmic range: like the sun it fills the earth, and the whole of creation echoes with love of it.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 181, p. 253; The Qur'an, 2:117; TDV contributor, 'Kün', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  20. The poet's signature couplet ll. 73-74

    Every ghazel and qasida names its poet by his pen-name in a closing couplet. Bâkî's pen-name means 'Abiding' or 'Everlasting', which gives his boasts an extra edge. In a qasida this self-praise belongs to the fahriyye, the section in which the poet praises his own art, and it often asks for patronage. Gibb notes that this verse is the 'taj or crown', in which the poet mentions his own name, and that from here the sultan is addressed again. The nightingale (bülbül), lover of the rose, is the conventional figure for the poet.

    E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  21. Irem, the lost garden l. 75

    The Qur'an mentions 'Iram of the pillars, the like of which was never created in the lands' (89:6-8) among peoples destroyed for their pride. Later legend made it a terrestrial paradise built by King Shaddad, now lost somewhere in the Arabian desert, and in poetry Irem means simply a garden of perfect beauty. Gibb explains that the 'mead' here is the world of Islam and that the nightingales of the next line are poets, adding that under Süleyman Ottoman poetry reached its highest point.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 182, p. 253; The Qur'an, 89:6 -8; TDV contributor, 'İrem', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  22. The closing prayer and the silver candlestick ll. 77-82

    A qasida ends with a prayer for the person praised (duâ), usually 'so long as' some cosmic order lasts. Here it lasts as long as the dawn sets the sun-lamp on a silver candlestick (the white light of daybreak on the horizon). In 1882 Gibb thought 'some allusion lurks' in the couplet and guessed that Bâkî prays for the sultan to live to old age, until his face (the sun) shows a silver candelabrum, a white beard. He did not repeat the guess in 1904, translating the line plainly. The image of life as a candle threatened by the 'blast of doom' is conventional.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 183, p. 254; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî, Gibb's footnotes to the qasida; TDV contributors (Turkish literature section), 'Kaside', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  23. The sky as gold-inlaid steel l. 84

    Gibb's note explains the final picture: the dark slate-blue night sky studded with stars is sometimes compared to steel inlaid with gold, the luxury metalwork (koftgari or damascening) of Ottoman and Persian workshops. The qasida thus ends where it began, with the night sky, but now the stars that opened the poem as festival lamps have become the decoration of the sultan's own wine-bowl. The banquet (bezm) and battle (rezm) are the two settings of royal praise, and Bâkî closes on the banquet.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 184, p. 254; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), ch. on Bâkî (Gibb's 1904 version: 'the goblet steel-enwrought, of gold inlay')
