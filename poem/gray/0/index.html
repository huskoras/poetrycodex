Death & Dying

Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard

by Thomas Gray 1716–1771

Death & Dying

The curfew tolls the knell of parting day,
The lowing herd wind slowly o'er the lea,
The plowman homeward plods his weary way,
And leaves the world to darkness and to me.

Now fades the glimmering landscape on the sight,
And all the air a solemn stillness holds,
Save where the beetle wheels his droning flight,
And drowsy tinklings lull the distant folds:

Save that, from yonder ivy-mantled tower,
The moping owl does to the moon complain
Of such as, wandering near her secret bower,
Molest her ancient solitary reign.

Beneath those rugged elms, that yew-tree's shade,
Where heaves the turf in many a mouldering heap,
Each in his narrow cell forever laid,
The rude forefathers of the hamlet sleep.

The breezy call of incense-breathing morn,
The swallow twittering from the straw-built shed,
The cock's shrill clarion, or the echoing horn,
No more shall rouse them from their lowly bed.

For them no more the blazing hearth shall burn,
Or busy housewife ply her evening care;
No children run to lisp their sire's return,
Or climb his knees the envied kiss to share.

Oft did the harvest to their sickle yield,
Their furrow oft the stubborn glebe has broke;
How jocund did they drive their team afield!
How bow'd the woods beneath their sturdy stroke!

Let not Ambition mock their useful toil,
Their homely joys, and destiny obscure;
Nor Grandeur hear with a disdainful smile
The short and simple annals of the poor.

The boast of heraldry, the pomp of power,
And all that beauty, all that wealth e'er gave,
Awaits alike th' inevitable hour.
The paths of glory lead but to the grave.

Nor you, ye proud, impute to these the fault,
If Memory o'er their tomb no trophies raise;
Where, through the long-drawn aisle and fretted vault,
The pealing anthem swells the note of praise.

Can storied urn or animated bust
Back to its mansion call the fleeting breath?
Can Honour's voice provoke the silent dust?
Or Flattery soothe the dull cold ear of Death?

Perhaps in this neglected spot is laid
Some heart once pregnant with celestial fire;
Hands, that the rod of empire might have sway'd,
Or wak'd to ecstasy the living lyre:

But Knowledge to their eyes her ample page,
Rich with the spoils of time, did ne'er unroll;
Chill Penury repress'd their noble rage,
And froze the genial current of the soul.

Full many a gem of purest ray serene
The dark unfathom'd caves of ocean bear;
Full many a flower is born to blush unseen,
And waste its sweetness on the desert air.

Some village Hampden, that with dauntless breast
The little tyrant of his fields withstood,
Some mute inglorious Milton here may rest,
Some Cromwell, guiltless of his country's blood.

Th' applause of listening senates to command,
The threats of pain and ruin to despise,
To scatter plenty o'er a smiling land,
And read their history in a nation's eyes,

Their lot forbade: nor circumscrib'd alone
Their growing virtues, but their crimes confin'd;
Forbade to wade through slaughter to a throne,
And shut the gates of mercy on mankind,

The struggling pangs of conscious truth to hide,
To quench the blushes of ingenuous shame,
Or heap the shrine of Luxury and Pride
With incense kindled at the Muse's flame.

Far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife,
Their sober wishes never learn'd to stray;
Along the cool sequester'd vale of life
They kept the noiseless tenor of their way.

Yet even these bones from insult to protect,
Some frail memorial still erected nigh,
With uncouth rhymes and shapeless sculpture deck'd,
Implores the passing tribute of a sigh.

Their name, their years, spelt by th' unletter'd Muse,
The place of fame and elegy supply;
And many a holy text around she strews,
That teach the rustic moralist to die.

For who, to dumb forgetfulness a prey,
This pleasing anxious being e'er resign'd,
Left the warm precincts of the cheerful day,
Nor cast one longing lingering look behind?

On some fond breast the parting soul relies,
Some pious drops the closing eye requires;
Even from the tomb the voice of Nature cries,
Even in our ashes live their wonted fires.

For thee, who, mindful of th' unhonour'd dead,
Dost in these lines their artless tale relate,
If chance, by lonely contemplation led,
Some kindred spirit shall inquire thy fate,

Haply some hoary-headed swain may say,
"Oft have we seen him at the peep of dawn
Brushing with hasty steps the dews away,
To meet the sun upon the upland lawn.

"There at the foot of yonder nodding beech,
That wreathes its old fantastic roots so high,
His listless length at noontide would he stretch,
And pore upon the brook that babbles by.

"Hard by yon wood, now smiling as in scorn,
Muttering his wayward fancies he would rove;
Now drooping, woeful-wan, like one forlorn,
Or craz'd with care, or cross'd in hopeless love.

"One morn I miss'd him on the custom'd hill,
Along the heath, and near his favourite tree;
Another came; nor yet beside the rill,
Nor up the lawn, nor at the wood was he;

"The next, with dirges due in sad array,
Slow through the church-way path we saw him borne.
Approach and read (for thou canst read) the lay
Grav'd on the stone beneath yon aged thorn."

THE EPITAPH.

Here rests his head upon the lap of Earth
A youth, to Fortune and to Fame unknown;
Fair Science frown'd not on his humble birth,
And Melancholy mark'd him for her own.

Large was his bounty, and his soul sincere,
Heaven did a recompense as largely send;
He gave to Misery all he had, a tear;
He gain'd from Heaven ('twas all he wish'd) a friend.

No farther seek his merits to disclose,
Or draw his frailties from their dread abode,
(There they alike in trembling hope repose)
The bosom of his Father and his God.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition, title and the speaker title; l. 4

    The date at which Gray began the poem is disputed. William Mason believed he started it in August 1742, the summer of Richard West's death; Lonsdale argues that its many echoes of verse printed in 1743-7 point rather to a first version of about 1746-7, completed at Stoke Poges in June 1750, when Gray sent it to Walpole as 'a thing with an end to it'. Manuscript copies circulated, and when the Magazine of Magazines threatened to print it, Gray asked Walpole to have Dodsley bring the poem out at once as a quarto pamphlet (February 1751). The Eton College manuscript is headed 'Stanza's wrote in a Country Church-yard'; Mason claimed he persuaded Gray to call it an Elegy. It went through many editions within two years and became one of the most reprinted English poems of the century. The churchyard is usually identified with St Giles, Stoke Poges, where Gray's mother and aunt lay and where he himself was buried in 1771, though editors warn against reading it as a literal portrait. The first stanza ends on 'me': the poem is a solitary speaker's meditation, and that speaker becomes its subject in the closing stanzas.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 103-10 (headnote), 117, note on title and composition (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, Gray's English Poems (Cambridge, 1898; 1922), quoting Mason's Memoirs, note on the title (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. Fairer and C. Gerrard, Eighteenth-Century Poetry: An Annotated Anthology (Blackwell, 1999), note on the title (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  2. curfew l. 1

    Johnson's Dictionary (citing Cowel) defines the curfew as an evening peal at which every man was to cover his fire and put out his light, a custom popularly ascribed to William I. In Gray's day such a bell still rang in many towns, including Cambridge, where Gray lived. The word was already poetic property: Lonsdale notes Milton's 'far-off Curfeu sound' in Il Penseroso and Shakespeare's 'solemn curfew' in The Tempest. The bell 'tolls' as for a funeral, so the opening line folds the end of a working day into the language of death: the day itself is being buried. This double register, ordinary rural time and the time of mortality, governs the whole poem.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 117, note on l. 1 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Samuel Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), s.v. 'curfew'

  3. the knell of parting day: Dante l. 1

    In the 1768 edition of his Poems Gray added a note quoting Purgatorio VIII.5-6, in which the new pilgrim hears a bell 'di lontano / che paia il giorno pianger che si more' (from afar, which seems to weep for the day that dies). He had already admitted the debt in a letter of 1756, perhaps because Norton Nicholls had noticed it. Nicholls also recorded that Gray first wrote 'the knell of dying day' and changed 'dying' to 'parting' to avoid the conceit. The Dantean source matters: Dante's lines describe the evening homesickness of travellers, so Gray's English twilight opens on exile and longing as well as on rural peace. Lonsdale adds that the quatrain also recalls the opening of Inferno II, where the poet alone keeps watch as others rest from their toil.

    Gray's own notes to the Elegy, Poems by Mr. Gray (1768), as given in the Thomas Gray Archive; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 117, note on ll. 1-8 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Dante, Purgatorio VIII.1-6 (Italian text and translation, Digital Dante, Columbia University)

  4. The elegiac (heroic) quatrain ll. 1-4

    Gray's stanza is the alternately rhymed pentameter quatrain that Davenant had chosen for Gondibert (1651) and Dryden for Annus Mirabilis (1667), where Dryden defended it as the most dignified English measure. James Hammond's Love Elegies (1743) had already used it for elegy, and Mason claimed that he urged Gray to call the poem an Elegy because the 'alternate measure' suited the genre. Johnson objected in his Life of Hammond that a stanza Dryden thought the most magnificent in English hardly suited the gentleness of elegy. The Elegy settled the matter in practice: after 1751 the form was so widely imitated that it became known as the elegiac stanza. Its closed, end-stopped quatrains make each stanza feel like an inscription, which suits a poem that ends in an epitaph; line 2, slowed by long vowels and heavy stresses, shows how Gray lets the measure enact the slow movement it describes.

    D. C. Tovey, Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), quoting Mason and Johnson's Life of Hammond, note on the title (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 138-39, note on ll. 117-28 (Hammond's Elegy ix) (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); John Dryden, 'An Account of the Ensuing Poem', prefixed to Annus Mirabilis (1667)

  5. ivy-mantled tower; moping owl ll. 9-12

    'Mantled' means cloaked; Lonsdale compares Milton's 'mantling Vine' (Paradise Lost IV.258) and similar phrases in Mallet and Thomas Warton, whose Pleasures of Melancholy (1747) also has a screech-owl in a ruined, ivy-covered tower. Tovey cautions that although the Stoke Poges church was in Gray's mind, the scene is composed rather than photographed. The owl 'complains' as in Virgil's Aeneid IV.462-3, where a solitary owl on the rooftops laments over Dido; 'moping' may recall Ovid's 'ignavus bubo' (sluggish owl) and Milton's 'moaping Melancholie'. The owl's 'ancient solitary reign' is a small mock-heroic touch: the bird guards its 'bower' like a monarch, and the speaker, wandering near, is the trespasser. First editions misprinted 'secret' as 'sacred', which Gray listed as an erratum.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 119-20, note on ll. 9-12 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), note on l. 9 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Aeneid IV.462-3

  6. narrow cell; rude forefathers ll. 15-16

    The adjective carries its older sense of untaught, simple, unpolished (Latin rudis), so 'rude forefathers' are the uneducated generations of the village. The graves are unmarked mounds where 'heaves the turf', unlike the monuments inside the church that the poem later contrasts with them. Lonsdale cites Parnell's Night-Piece on Death, whose nameless graves 'heave the crumbled Ground' where 'Toil and Poverty repose', and Blair's The Grave, which also supplies a yew and elms. The Eton manuscript reads 'Village' for 'hamlet', deleted. Gray's stanza is the first of several that invest the poor dead with dignity by echoing a long line of mortuary verse while keeping the diction plain.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 120, note on ll. 13-16 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rude, adj.'

  7. glebe l. 26

    Glebe is the earth turned by the plough; in church usage it also meant land assigned to a parish living, which gives the word a faint ecclesiastical colouring in a churchyard poem. Lonsdale traces 'the stubborn glebe has broke' to Virgil, Georgics I.94 ('glaebas qui frangit inertis', who breaks the sluggish clods), and to many English versions of the idea in Dryden, Pope and Gay. The stanza (ll. 25-8) is the poem's georgic moment: harvest, furrow, team and falling woods present labour as heroic, with exclamations ('How jocund...!', 'How bow'd...!') that give the villagers an epic energy in life before the poem returns to their obscurity in death.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 122, note on ll. 25-8 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Georgics I.94; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'glebe, n.'

  8. Ambition, Grandeur and the annals of the poor ll. 29-32

    Annals are chronicles arranged year by year, the form of official history; the poor have them too, but short and uneventful. The capitalised abstractions (Ambition, Grandeur, and later Memory, Honour, Flattery, Knowledge, Penury) are typical of mid-century personification, here used to stage a confrontation between classes. Lonsdale compares Thomson's appeal, in Spring, to those living in 'luxury and ease' not to despise rural themes, and Pope's remark, in his Iliad notes, that farming images had sunk into meanness because farming had become the drudgery of the lowest people. Gray reverses that hierarchy of subject matter. He also notes that the rhymes 'toil'/'smile' were probably exact in Gray's pronunciation.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 122-23, note on ll. 29-32 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); James Thomson, The Seasons, 'Spring' (1728; rev. 1746), ll. 52-4

  9. Awaits ... th' inevitable hour l. 35

    With Gray's reading 'Awaits', the singular 'hour' is the subject and all the grand things of the previous lines are its object: death lies in wait for them. Some editions, including Dodsley's Collection of 1755 and an edition of 1775, print 'Await', which makes boast, pomp, beauty and wealth the subject waiting for death. John Young raised the question in his Criticism on the Elegy (1783); Lonsdale is firm that Gray intended 'Awaits'. The stanza adapts a commonplace that Horace states with famous economy (Odes I.4: pale Death knocks with impartial foot at poor men's huts and kings' towers). Lonsdale suggests the immediate prompt was Richard West's Monody on Queen Caroline, which asks what boasted power can do against 'th'inevitable hour'.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 123-24, note on ll. 33-6 and l. 35 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Horace, Odes I.4.13-14

  10. The paths of glory l. 36

    The line compresses the stanza's moral into a proverb: every road of fame ends at the same place as the ploughman's. Lonsdale compares Pope's Odyssey ('the paths of glory trace') and Wolsey's 'ways of glory' in Henry VIII. The line acquired a famous historical gloss: General James Wolfe is said to have recited the Elegy to his officers on the eve of the attack on Quebec (September 1759), in which he was killed, adding that he would rather have written the poem than beat the French the next day. The anecdote survives through later reports and may have been improved in the telling, so it should be quoted as tradition. The 'storied urn' stanzas that follow continue the argument inside the church, among the monuments of the great.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 124, note on l. 36 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Crofts, ed., Gray: Poetry and Prose (Oxford, 1926; 1948), note on the title (Wolfe anecdote) (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  11. storied urn; animated bust ll. 41-4

    'Storied' means decorated with figured stories or inscriptions (John Young complained in 1783 that it needed explaining); Gray echoes Milton's 'storied Windows richly dight'. 'Animated' means seemingly alive or breathing, the conventional praise of sculpture going back to Virgil's 'spirantia signa' (breathing statues, Georgics III.34); Lonsdale observes that Gray uses the compliment ironically, since the bust only imitates the breath it cannot restore. The setting is the church interior, with its 'fretted' (ornamentally carved) vault and pealing organ. The stanza's four questions all expect the answer no. Honour cannot 'provoke' (in the Latin sense of call forth) the dust, and Flattery cannot soothe 'the dull cold ear of Death', a phrase indebted to Wolsey's 'sleep in dull cold marble' (Henry VIII). Cleanth Brooks took the phrase for the title of his essay on the poem, 'Gray's Storied Urn', in The Well Wrought Urn (1947).

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 125, note on ll. 41-4 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Cleanth Brooks, 'Gray's Storied Urn', in The Well Wrought Urn (New York, 1947)

  12. noble rage; genial current ll. 51-2

    Knowledge never unrolled her 'ample page', imagined as a scroll 'rich with the spoils of time', to the poor, so poverty ('Chill Penury') froze their creative powers. Lonsdale explains that 'noble rage' is the favourable sense of furor, the divinely inspired frenzy of the poet; Dryden used the phrase for anger, but Cowley and Pope used 'rage' of inspiration. 'Genial' carries the Latin sense of genius and generation: the warm, life-giving current of the soul is frozen, as Virgil fears a cold current of blood may block his poetic gifts (Georgics II.484). Lonsdale notes Addison's Spectator 215 and Waller's lines on Caesar as parallels for the idea that education draws out hidden worth, an argument Johnson's age took seriously.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 126-27, note on ll. 49-52 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Georgics II.483-4

  13. Full many a gem ... desert air ll. 53-6

    Lonsdale lists many earlier versions of both images: Joseph Hall's 'many a rich stone laid up in the bowels of the earth', Chamberlayne's flowers wasting scent in deserts, Pope's roses that 'in Deserts bloom and die', and Edward Young's flowers 'by human eyes unseen'; 'the desert air' itself is from Macbeth. 'Desert' means deserted, uninhabited. The stanza became the focus of a famous critique. In Some Versions of Pastoral (1935), Empson argued that by comparing the poor to a gem or flower, which cannot mind being unseen, the poem makes their lack of opportunity seem natural and therefore acceptable, a reassurance he linked to the poem's appeal to its readers. Lonsdale replied that in the poem's first version the poet himself chooses obscurity, which makes Empson's remarks ingenious but misleading. Students should weigh both views.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 127, note on ll. 53-6 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. headnote, note on the structure of the first version (reply to Empson) (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); William Empson, Some Versions of Pastoral (London, 1935), ch. 1, p. 4

  14. Hampden, Milton, Cromwell ll. 57-60

    John Hampden, a Buckinghamshire landowner and MP, became a national hero for refusing to pay ship money in 1636-7, a case tried in 1637-8; he died fighting for Parliament in 1643. The 'little tyrant of his fields' is a local squire or landlord whom a village Hampden might have resisted on a small scale. In the Eton manuscript the three exemplars are Cato, Tully and Caesar; Gray replaced them with English figures of the civil-war period, figures of known nationality that, Bradshaw observes, fix the poem in England. Hampden and Milton both lived in Buckinghamshire, the county of Stoke Poges, and Tovey adds that Cromwell, Hampden's cousin, often visited Great Hampden. The selection is politically balanced and pointed: Hampden is praised outright, while Cromwell is imagined 'guiltless of his country's blood', the village version being innocent precisely because it never had power. Lonsdale notes that Gray wanted examples of greatness that had proved dangerous as well as admirable.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 127-28, note on ll. 57-60 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (Aldine, 1891; 1903), note on ll. 57-60 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'John Hampden'

  15. mute inglorious Milton l. 59

    'Mute' means unable to speak publicly, without a voice in print; 'inglorious' means without glory, unknown, not disgraceful. Lonsdale points to Virgil's 'inglorius' at Georgics II.486, in the famous praise of country life ('O fortunatos nimium...'), where the poet prays to love streams and woods 'inglorious'; this passage underlies the whole central section of the Elegy. Milton was also a local presence: he finished Paradise Lost at Chalfont St Giles, a few miles from Stoke Poges. Shenstone's The Schoolmistress had already imagined a future Milton among village schoolchildren. The phrase has passed into the language as a name for unrealised talent.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 128, note on l. 59 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), note on ll. 57-60 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Georgics II.485-6

  16. wade through slaughter to a throne ll. 65-72

    These stanzas turn the argument: obscurity is not only loss but protection. Lonsdale traces the image of wading through blood to power through Shakespeare (Macbeth III.iv: 'I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far'), Pope's Temple of Fame and Blair's The Grave, and the 'gates of mercy' to Henry V and 3 Henry VI. The model behind the passage is Virgil's contrast of the happy farmer with the ambitious citizen in Georgics II.503-12, who wrecks cities and rejoices drenched in his brothers' blood. Lines 69-72 extend the list of avoided vices to courtly hypocrisy and to poets who burn the 'incense' of their muse at the shrines of Luxury and Pride, a self-implicating glance at patronage verse. 'Ingenuous shame' means the natural, freeborn modesty of an honest mind (Latin ingenuus).

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 129-30, note on ll. 65-72 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, Macbeth III.iv (Folger Shakespeare); Virgil, Georgics II.503-12

  17. The first ending (Eton manuscript) l. 72

    After l. 72 the Eton manuscript contains four stanzas, marked off in the margin, beginning 'The thoughtless World to Majesty may bow'. In them the poet, addressing himself ('And thou, who mindful of the unhonour'd Dead'), resolves to give up 'anxious Cares & endless Wishes' and to seek peace among the graves. Mason reported that this was the original conclusion, and Lonsdale accepts it: the first version was a balanced debate within the speaker's mind, ending in a Christian-Stoic choice of obscure innocence. Gray later cancelled the stanzas, reworked two of their lines as ll. 93-4, and added the second half, with the swain's account and the Epitaph. Much modern criticism of the poem turns on how the two parts fit together.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 130-31, 140-41, note on l. 72 and Appendix (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Crofts, ed., Gray: Poetry and Prose (1926; 1948), note on the title (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  18. madding crowd; noiseless tenor ll. 73-6

    'Madding' is an old form meaning raging or behaving madly; Lonsdale cites Drummond of Hawthornden's 'Far from the madding worldling's hoarse discords' and Milton's use of the word in Paradise Lost VI. The villagers' 'sober wishes' never strayed from their 'sequester'd vale', a secluded valley that is also the 'vale of life' (Cowley's imitation of Virgil has 'life's cool vale'). 'Tenor' means the continuous course or drift of something; 'noiseless' replaced 'silent' in the Eton manuscript, and Lonsdale compares Dryden's wish to live 'noiseless'. Note the syntax: 'Far from' grammatically describes the villagers, but the line also expresses the speaker's own longing. Thomas Hardy borrowed the phrase, ironically, for Far from the Madding Crowd (1874), whose rural world is anything but tranquil.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 131, note on ll. 73-6 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Far from the Madding Crowd'

  19. frail memorial; unletter'd Muse ll. 77-84

    The 'frail memorial' with 'uncouth' (unrefined, awkward) rhymes and 'shapeless sculpture' answers the 'storied urn' of the rich: even the humblest dead want to be remembered. Lonsdale cites Swift's remark that even the vulgar are fond of having an inscription over their grave. The 'unletter'd Muse' is the illiterate village epitaph-maker; the phrase echoes Milton's 'unleter'd Hinds' in Comus. Their names and dates 'supply' the place of fame and elegy (the Eton reading is 'Epitaph'), and the scattered Bible texts teach the 'rustic moralist' how to die, recalling Tickell's elegy for Addison, who 'taught us how to die'. The phrase prepares the epitaph at the poem's end, written by a more lettered muse.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 132, note on ll. 77-84 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); John Milton, Comus (A Masque Presented at Ludlow Castle), l. 174

  20. wonted fires: Petrarch l. 92

    The stanza explains why even the poor want memorials: the dying soul leans on a loving breast, needs 'pious' (dutiful, Latin pius) tears, and nature cries out even from the tomb. 'Wonted' means customary. In 1768 Gray quoted Petrarch's sonnet beginning 'Ch'i veggio nel pensier, dolce mio fuoco' (Rime 170, numbered 169 in older editions), where the poet foresees that after death his cold tongue and closed eyes will still be full of sparks. Bradshaw adds Chaucer's Reeve: 'Yet in our asshen olde is fyr yreke'. The line went through several forms: the Eton manuscript reads 'And buried Ashes glow with social Fires', and the first editions 'Awake, & faithful to her wonted Fires'. Gray's joke about Dodsley's readers taking 'awake' as a verb shows how puzzling that version was.

    Gray's own notes to the Elegy, Poems by Mr. Gray (1768), as given in the Thomas Gray Archive; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 134, note on ll. 89-92 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), p. 225, note on l. 92 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  21. For thee: who is addressed? ll. 93-6

    From l. 93 the poem imagines its own author dead and remembered. Lonsdale argues that in the Eton draft the poet plainly addresses himself, and that nothing in the revision changes this; the shift to the second person is a way of seeing oneself from outside. In the mid-twentieth century several critics, notably Frank H. Ellis in PMLA (1951), proposed that 'thee' is a village stonecutter, the author of the 'uncouth rhymes' in ll. 77-84, or an imagined rustic poet distinct from Gray; Morse Peckham (1956) joined the debate, and J. H. Sutherland (1957) refuted the stonecutter theory. Lonsdale notes that no stonecutter is mentioned and that 'these lines' cannot easily refer back to l. 79. Others have read the youth as Gray's dead friend Richard West. The question affects whether the Epitaph is self-elegy or a portrait of another.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 135, note on l. 93 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Frank H. Ellis, 'Gray's Elegy: The Biographical Problem in Literary Criticism', PMLA 66 (1951), 992-1004 (pages as cited by Lonsdale); Joseph Foladare, 'Gray's "Frail Memorial" to West', PMLA 75.1 (1960), 61-65; M. Peckham, Modern Language Notes 71 (1956), 409-11; J. H. Sutherland, Modern Philology 55 (1957), 11-13 (as cited in Lonsdale's note on l. 93, Thomas Gray Archive)

  22. hoary-headed swain; the youth's day ll. 97-116

    'Swain' is the pastoral word for a rustic or shepherd; 'hoary-headed' means grey-haired with age. Lonsdale suggests Gray's model is Blair's sexton in The Grave, the 'hoary-headed chronicle' who knows every skull's owner. The swain's portrait draws on Milton's Lycidas and L'Allegro ('high Lawns', 'upland' hamlets) and on Jaques in As You Like It, who lies under an oak 'whose antique root peeps out / Upon the brook'; Mason thought Gray's own character resembled Jaques. 'Lawn' means an open grassy space, not a mown garden. Gray dropped an evening stanza after l. 100 and a stanza about violets and a redbreast at the grave before the Epitaph, which Mason printed and admired; according to Mason, Gray thought it too long a parenthesis. The swain's 'for thou canst read' marks the class difference between the illiterate speaker and the reader.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 135-38, note on ll. 97-116 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, As You Like It II.i.30-2 (Folger Shakespeare)

  23. The Epitaph ll. 117-28

    The poem closes with a lapidary inscription in the same stanza, set off by a heading. Lonsdale suggests models in the church inscription in Mallet's The Excursion and the epitaph that ends Hammond's ninth Love Elegy ('Here lies a youth...'). 'The lap of Earth' recalls Virgil's 'gremio telluris' (Aeneid III.509) and Spenser's 'mother Earths deare lap'; 'to Fortune and to Fame unknown' echoes West's Monody. The economy of the second stanza is famous: he gave Misery all he had, a tear, and received from Heaven all he wished, a friend. The ending forbids further inquiry into his merits or frailties, which rest in the 'bosom' of God. Johnson wrote that the poem abounds 'with images which find a mirrour in every mind'.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 138-40, note on ll. 117-28 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', Lives of the English Poets (1781)

  24. Science; Melancholy mark'd him ll. 119-20

    Science (Latin scientia, knowledge) here is knowledge or learning at large, 'fair' because Gray imagines her as a Muse; Lonsdale compares Horace, Odes IV.3, where Melpomene looks with favour on a poet at his birth. The meaning of 'Melancholy' is, in Lonsdale's words, 'crucial to the Epitaph'. He argues it is not gloom caused by thwarted abilities, for then Gray would have written 'But' not 'And'; it is the fashionable mid-century sense of a valuable sensibility, praised in Thomson's 'Philosophic Melancholy' and Warton's Pleasures of Melancholy, that shows itself in benevolence. That reading explains why the next stanza turns to the youth's 'bounty' and tears. To be 'marked' is to be singled out from birth, as by a patron deity.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 139, note on ll. 119-20 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Horace, Odes IV.3.1-2; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'science, n.' (sense 'knowledge, learning')

  25. trembling hope l. 127

    The parenthesis places the dead man's good and bad qualities alike in 'their dread abode', the bosom of God, where they wait in 'trembling hope' for judgment. Gray's 1768 note gave Petrarch's phrase 'paventosa speme' (Rime 115, numbered 114 in older editions) as his source. Lonsdale adds Lucan's 'spe trepido' and Pope's dying Christian, 'Trembling, hoping'. The oxymoron suits the poem's restraint: it neither presumes salvation nor despairs, and the last line, with God as 'Father', closes an elegy that has spoken mostly of human memory with a reference to divine judgment. Compare the earlier claim that 'the paths of glory lead but to the grave': here the grave is no longer the last word.
