Time & Brevity

Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College

by Thomas Gray 1716–1771

Time & Brevity

[Greek: Anthrôpos, hikanê prophasis eis to dustuchein.]--MENANDER.

Ye distant spires, ye antique towers,
That crown the watery glade,
Where grateful Science still adores
Her Henry's holy shade;
And ye, that from the stately brow
Of Windsor's heights th' expanse below
Of grove, of lawn, of mead survey,
Whose turf, whose shade, whose flowers among
Wanders the hoary Thames along
His silver-winding way:

Ah, happy hills! ah, pleasing shade!
Ah, fields belov'd in vain!
Where once my careless childhood stray'd,
A stranger yet to pain!
I feel the gales that from ye blow
A momentary bliss bestow,
As, waving fresh their gladsome wing,
My weary soul they seem to soothe,
And, redolent of joy and youth,
To breathe a second spring.

Say, Father Thames, for thou hast seen
Full many a sprightly race
Disporting on thy margent green
The paths of pleasure trace;
Who foremost now delight to cleave
With pliant arm thy glassy wave?
The captive linnet which enthrall?
What idle progeny succeed
To chase the rolling circle's speed,
Or urge the flying ball?

While some, on earnest business bent,
Their murmuring labours ply
'Gainst graver hours that bring constraint
To sweeten liberty,
Some bold adventurers disdain
The limits of their little reign,
And unknown regions dare descry:
Still as they run they look behind,
They hear a voice in every wind,
And snatch a fearful joy.

Gay hope is theirs by fancy fed,
Less pleasing when possest;
The tear forgot as soon as shed,
The sunshine of the breast:
Theirs buxom health of rosy hue,
Wild wit, invention ever new,
And lively cheer of vigour born;
The thoughtless day, the easy night,
The spirits pure, the slumbers light,
That fly th' approach of morn.

Alas! regardless of their doom,
The little victims play;
No sense have they of ills to come,
No care beyond to-day:
Yet see how all around 'em wait
The ministers of human fate,
And black Misfortune's baleful train!
Ah, show them where in ambush stand
To seize their prey the murtherous band!
Ah, tell them, they are men!

These shall the fury Passions tear,
The vultures of the mind,
Disdainful Anger, pallid Fear,
And Shame that skulks behind;
Or pining Love shall waste their youth,
Or Jealousy with rankling tooth,
That inly gnaws the secret heart;
And Envy wan, and faded Care,
Grim-visag'd comfortless Despair,
And Sorrow's piercing dart.

Ambition this shall tempt to rise,
Then whirl the wretch from high,
To bitter Scorn a sacrifice,
And grinning Infamy.
The stings of Falsehood those shall try,
And hard Unkindness' alter'd eye,
That mocks the tear it forc'd to flow;
And keen Remorse with blood defil'd,
And moody Madness laughing wild
Amid severest woe.

Lo! in the vale of years beneath
A grisly troop are seen,
The painful family of Death,
More hideous than their queen:
This racks the joints, this fires the veins,
That every labouring sinew strains,
Those in the deeper vitals rage:
Lo! Poverty, to fill the band,
That numbs the soul with icy hand,
And slow-consuming Age.

To each his sufferings: all are men,
Condemn'd alike to groan;
The tender for another's pain,
Th' unfeeling for his own.
Yet, ah! why should they know their fate,
Since sorrow never comes too late,
And happiness too swiftly flies?
Thought would destroy their paradise.
No more;--where ignorance is bliss,
'Tis folly to be wise.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and occasion title; l. 1

    Gray's transcript in his Commonplace Book is dated 'at Stoke, Aug: 1742' and titled 'Ode, on a distant Prospect of Windsor, & the adjacent Country'; Eton is not named. It was printed anonymously by Dodsley as a sixpenny folio on 30 May 1747, then under Gray's name in Dodsley's Collection of Poems (1748). The summer of 1742 was a dark one: Richard West, Gray's closest Eton friend, died on 1 June, and Gray was uncertain of his own future. Lonsdale notes that Eton and Windsor could actually be seen from Stoke Poges, so the 'prospect' is literal as well as a view back in time. A letter of May 1742 recalls his schoolfellows as 'dirty boys playing at cricket', and Lonsdale argues that Gray, who had avoided the rough games, could idealise a childhood he watched from a distance.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), pp. 54-6, headnote (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. Fairer and C. Gerrard, eds., Eighteenth-Century Poetry: An Annotated Anthology (Blackwell, 1999), p. 325 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  2. the epigraph from Menander epigraph

    The Greek, transliterated in the archive, reads ánthrōpos: hikanḕ próphasis eis tò dystycheîn, 'a man: a sufficient excuse for being unhappy'. It is a fragment of Menander (c. 342-290 BC), the master of Athenian New Comedy, preserved in later anthologies; editors cite it among his fragments. The motto stands in the margin beside the fifth and sixth stanzas in Gray's manuscripts, near 'Ah, tell them, they are men!' (l. 60), which it glosses, and it was first printed with the poem in 1768 (Poems by Mr. Gray). A comic poet's shrug becomes the text for an elegy on human life.

    H. W. Starr and J. R. Hendrickson, eds., The Complete Poems of Thomas Gray (Oxford, 1966), pp. 203-4 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (Aldine, 1891; 1903), p. 185 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); James Reeves, ed., The Complete English Poems of Thomas Gray (1973), p. 110 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  3. antique towers ... crown the watery glade ll. 1-2

    Bradshaw notes that 'antique' here simply means ancient, the sense it has in Milton's 'antick Pillars' (Il Penseroso 158). Lonsdale gathers the sources of the opening: Comus 934-5 ('May thy lofty head be crown'd / With many a tower'), Pope's Windsor Forest ('Windsor-Domes and pompous Turrets', and 'haunt the watry Glade'), and Dyer's Grongar Hill ('ancient towers crown his brow'). A 'glade' is an open space among trees; 'watery' because the college lies on the Thames meadows. The opening is thus a topographical poem in the tradition of Denham's Cooper's Hill and Pope's Windsor Forest, before it turns personal.

    J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), p. 185, note on l. 1 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 56, notes on ll. 1-2 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); John Milton, Il Penseroso 158 and Comus 934-5 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  4. Science l. 3

    As in the Elegy's 'Fair Science frown'd not on his humble birth', 'Science' means knowledge or learning at large, the older sense of Latin scientia, not natural science. Tovey, Lonsdale and Reeves all gloss it so. Learning is 'grateful' because it owes its home at Eton to Henry VI, and it 'still' (always, to this day) 'adores' his memory. The personification sets the poem's method: abstractions become actors, a technique the ode will push much further in its catalogue of passions.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 56, note on l. 3 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (Cambridge, 1898; 1922), p. 92 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'science, n.' (knowledge, learning)

  5. Henry's holy shade l. 4

    Gray's own note identifies Henry VI as the founder of the college. Lonsdale records that a statue of the king had been placed in School Yard shortly before Gray arrived in 1725, and that Gray elsewhere stresses his sanctity (The Bard, l. 90; Ode for Music); Shakespeare calls him 'holy Henry' in Richard III, and Pope calls him 'the Martyr-King' in Windsor Forest. Henry was murdered in the Tower in 1471 and venerated as a martyr. 'Shade' combines the classical sense of a departed spirit (Latin umbra) with the literal shade of the college groves, so the founder's ghost presides over the landscape.

    Gray's own note to l. 4, Poems by Mr. Gray (1768), as given in the Thomas Gray Archive; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 57, note on l. 4 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, Richard III V.i (Folger Shakespeare)

  6. Windsor's heights ll. 5-7

    The 'ye' of l. 5 addresses the towers of Windsor Castle, which stand on a chalk hill above the river opposite Eton; from their 'stately brow' they survey the wide view below. Gray's first title made Windsor, not Eton, the subject ('a distant Prospect of Windsor, & the adjacent Country'). The Commonplace Book reads 'Of Grove & Lawn & Mead survey'. Lonsdale compares Pope's Summer, where Thames winds through forests and flowery meads; the triple 'whose turf, whose shade, whose flowers' imitates the panoramic sweep of a prospect poem.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 57, notes on ll. 5-10 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); W. Lyon Phelps, ed., Selections from the Poetry and Prose of Thomas Gray (Boston, 1894), p. 130 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  7. the hoary Thames and his silver-winding way ll. 9-10

    Rivers were traditionally imagined as aged gods (Phelps cites Pope's 'Old father Thames' and Spenser's 'full-aged' Thame), hence 'hoary'. Lonsdale points to Virgil's Mincius, which 'wanders in slow windings' (Georgics III.14-15, tardis ingens ubi flexibus errat), and notes that 'silver' is a stock epithet for the Thames in Spenser, Dryden, Pope and Thomson. The verb 'Wanders' prepares the boys who 'stray' and the 'bold adventurers' who range beyond bounds: the river's leisurely course is an image of the carefree time the poem mourns.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 57, note on ll. 9-10 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Georgics III.14-15 (The Latin Library); W. Lyon Phelps, ed., Selections (1894), p. 130 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  8. belov'd in vain l. 12

    Why 'in vain'? Bradshaw explains that the fields no longer give the poet the feelings he had as a 'careless' boy, and adds a reference to West's death in June 1742. Gosse, quoted by Tovey, read the phrase as Gray's half-hidden grief for his lost friend. Lonsdale links the phrase to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West, written the same month, which ends 'And weep the more, because I weep in vain'. Readers who resist biography take the phrase more generally: the love of a place cannot bring back the time spent in it. Lonsdale also cites Gay's Rural Sports, 'Ye happy fields ... where once I us'd to rove', as a model for the apostrophe.

    J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), p. 185 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), p. 92, quoting Gosse (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 57, notes on ll. 11-14 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  9. redolent of joy and youth l. 19

    'Redolent' (Latin redolere, to give off a smell) means fragrant with, and by extension evocative of. Gray's own note cites Dryden's version of Ovid, Metamorphoses XV ('Of the Pythagorean Philosophy'), where honey is 'redolent of spring'. Johnson complained in his Life of Gray that Dryden's phrase was already at the limit of the language and that Gray pushed it further, making gales 'redolent of joy and youth'. The breezes 'seem' to soothe and to breathe 'a second spring': the momentary bliss of l. 16 is already qualified. Lonsdale compares Aaron Hill's Solitude, which welcomes a cool breeze to a mind burnt by cares.

    Gray's own note to l. 19, Poems by Mr. Gray (1768), as given in the Thomas Gray Archive; Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', Lives of the English Poets (1781); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 57, notes on ll. 15-18 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  10. Say, Father Thames l. 21

    Lonsdale traces 'Father Thames' to Dryden and Pope (Windsor Forest), but thinks Gray had in mind Matthew Green's The Grotto: 'Say, father Thames, whose gentle pace / Gives leave to view what beauties grace / Your flowery banks'. Johnson objected that Father Thames 'has no better means of knowing than himself'. Tovey called the objection frivolous: the address to the river, which has seen generations of boys ('Full many a sprightly race'), is a way of setting one life against the continuity of the place. The question also distances Gray from the present boys, whom he can name only as types.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 58, note on l. 21 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', Lives of the English Poets (1781); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), pp. 92-3 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  11. margent l. 23

    Tovey notes that 'margent' is the regular form for 'margin' in Shakespeare and Milton, and that Gray recalls Comus 232, 'By slow Meander's margent green'. Reeves glosses simply 'banks'. Choosing the archaic form and a Miltonic phrase is typical of Gray's diction, which, as Johnson complained, often prefers the poetic to the common word. The boys 'disporting' (amusing themselves) on the bank trace 'the paths of pleasure', a phrase Lonsdale finds in Pope's Essay on Man.

    D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), p. 93 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 58, notes on ll. 23-4 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); John Milton, Comus 232 (Dartmouth Milton Reading Room)

  12. the boys' games ll. 25-30

    Phelps reads the stanza as a list of school sports: swimming ('cleave ... thy glassy wave'), catching or caging birds ('captive linnet'), trundling hoops ('the rolling circle'), and ball games. The Commonplace Book reads 'To chase the Hoop's elusive Speed'; Lonsdale thinks Gray changed it to avoid echoing a phrase from his own Agrippina, while Crofts suggests that 'hoop' seemed too low a word for poetry. The periphrasis is a classic instance of eighteenth-century poetic diction. Lonsdale cites Ovid, Tristia III.xii, where Roman boys play with the ball and the swift hoop, and Pope's Dunciad, 'The Senator at Cricket urge the Ball'.

    W. Lyon Phelps, ed., Selections (1894), p. 131 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 58, notes on ll. 25-30 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); J. Crofts, ed., Gray: Poetry and Prose (Oxford, 1926; 1948), p. 160 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Ovid, Tristia III.xii.19-20 (The Latin Library)

  13. murmuring labours ll. 31-4

    Tovey explains that the boys are 'conning by heart and repeating aloud to themselves the tasks' they must say to their masters in school; Lonsdale quotes him. Their 'earnest business' is homework, and the paradox of the couplet is that they work in their free time against the hours of constraint, 'to sweeten liberty': diligence makes the coming confinement easier, or makes the present freedom sweeter. The stanza sets three types side by side: the players, the workers and the truants.

    D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), p. 94 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 58, note on ll. 32-3 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  14. the bold adventurers ll. 35-9

    Tovey glosses: 'They go out of bounds.' Their 'little reign' is the school's permitted ground. Lonsdale finds 'unknown regions' and 'descries' in Cowley's ode To Mr Hobbes, and for the fearful runners he cites Virgil's Aeneas, escaping Troy, startled by every breeze and sound (Aeneid II.728-9), and Pope's untutored mind that 'hears him [God] in the wind'. 'They hear a voice in every wind' (Phelps: 'the pursuing master') gives the scene a comic surface and a darker undertone, since the fear of pursuit anticipates the 'ambush' of adult misfortune later in the poem.

    D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), pp. 94-5 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), pp. 58-9, notes on ll. 35-40 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Aeneid II.728-9 (The Latin Library)

  15. a fearful joy l. 40

    Lonsdale compares Aeneid I.513-14, where Aeneas and Achates, seeing their lost comrades, are struck with joy and fear together (laetitiaque metuque), and Silius Italicus' laeto pavore, 'joyful dread'. 'Snatch' implies haste and theft: the pleasure is stolen, brief, and taken against the rules. The phrase sums up the stanza and prepares the poem's argument, that childhood joy is real but precarious and already shadowed by fear.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 59, note on l. 40 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Aeneid I.513-14 (The Latin Library)

  16. hope less pleasing when possessed ll. 41-4

    Lonsdale cites Lucretius, De rerum natura III.1082-3: while we lack what we crave, it seems better than everything else; once we have it, we crave something else. Dryden's version gives 'For still we think an absent blessing best; / Which cloys, and is no blessing when possest', which Gray echoes almost word for word. Lonsdale also traces 'The sunshine of the breast' to Pope ('Eternal sunshine of the spotless mind', Eloisa to Abelard) and Thomson. The stanza describes childhood as an Eden of light sleep and easy days, with echoes of Adam's 'Aerie light' sleep in Paradise Lost V.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 59, notes on ll. 41-50 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Lucretius, De rerum natura III.1082-3 (The Latin Library)

  17. buxom l. 45

    'Buxom' comes from Old English bugan, to bow, and first meant pliant, obedient; by Shakespeare's time it had come to mean lively and blithe (Pistol's 'buxom valour', Henry V III.vi), and later plump and comely. Johnson objected that 'buxom health' was not elegant and that Gray seemed not to understand the word; Tovey answered that the primary sense had long been lost and that the transferred sense, prompt and lively, was established. Lonsdale glosses 'lively, healthy', and compares 'rosie red', 'Loves proper hue', in Paradise Lost VIII. Milton's 'buxom, blithe and debonair' (L'Allegro 24) is probably behind Gray's choice.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', Lives of the English Poets (1781); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), p. 95 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 59, note on l. 45 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'buxom, adj.'

  18. the little victims ll. 51-9

    The stanza turns the poem: 'Alas!' Lonsdale compares Dryden's Astraea Redux, 'And Heaven that seem'd regardless of our Fate', and for 'ministers of human fate' cites Ariel's 'ministers of Fate' in The Tempest III.iii, Thomson, Otway and Broome. 'Victims' keeps its Latin sense, animals destined for sacrifice (victima), which play unaware of the knife; the image recalls Pope's lamb that 'licks the hand just rais'd to shed his blood' (Essay on Man I). The 'murtherous band' in ambush was first 'griesly' in the Commonplace Book. Lonsdale cites Spenser's 'ambushment' of hatred and murder.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), pp. 59-60, notes on ll. 51-9 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, The Tempest III.iii (Folger Shakespeare); J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), p. 186, note on 'murtherous' (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  19. Ah, tell them, they are men! l. 60

    Gray wrote the Menander motto in the margin beside the stanzas around this line, and the two say the same thing: to be a man is reason enough for misery. The line is an imperative to an unnamed listener (Father Thames, or the reader) and it is both pitying and grim. The final stanza returns to the phrase, 'all are men, / Condemn'd alike to groan', and then retracts the wish to tell them. Readers have long noticed the tension between the urge to warn and the conclusion that warning is useless.

    J. Bradshaw, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Gray (1891; 1903), p. 185 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 63, notes on ll. 91-100 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  20. the fury Passions and the vultures of the mind ll. 61-2

    'The Fury-passions' is Pope's phrase (Essay on Man III), used after a description of man's lost innocence. The 'vultures of the mind' recall the vultures that feed on Tityos (or the eagle of Prometheus); Lucretius had already read the myth as an allegory of love and anxiety gnawing the living (De rerum natura III.992-4). Lonsdale notes that most English catalogues of this kind descend from Aeneid VI.273-81, where Grief, Cares, Diseases, Old Age, Fear, Hunger and Want lodge at the entrance to the underworld. Gray's procession, Anger, Fear, Shame, Love, Jealousy, Envy, Care, Despair, Sorrow, Ambition, follows that model.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), pp. 60-1, notes on ll. 61-90 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Virgil, Aeneid VI.273-81 (The Latin Library); Lucretius, De rerum natura III.992-4 (The Latin Library)

  21. Grim-visaged comfortless Despair l. 69

    Tovey and Lonsdale agree that Gray combines the opening of Richard III ('Grim-visag'd war hath smooth'd his wrinkled front', I.i.9) with The Comedy of Errors V.i.80, 'grim and comfortless despair'. Lonsdale adds that Mitford found 'grim visadged dispaire' in Robert Yarington's Two Lamentable Tragedies (1601). Nearby, 'Envy wan' and 'faded Care' come from Milton (Sonnet to Henry Lawes; Paradise Lost I.601-2). The stanza shows Gray's mosaic method: almost every personification is made from remembered phrases, which gives the list the weight of tradition.

    D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), pp. 95-6 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 61, notes on ll. 68-9 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, Richard III I.i.9 (Folger Shakespeare)

  22. the painful family of Death ll. 81-4

    Lonsdale and Tovey cite Dryden's State of Innocence, where sickness, care and age, brought by Adam's crime, come 'With all the numerous Family of Death'. Tovey notes that 'family' means attendants, the household (Latin familia), not offspring. The 'vale of years' is Othello's phrase (III.iii), and the 'grisly troop' recalls Comus. The diseases are 'More hideous than their queen', since Death herself (Milton's 'queen of tombs', Lonsdale notes) ends pain while they inflict it. The stanza moves from mental to bodily suffering, ending with Poverty and 'slow-consuming Age'.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 62, notes on ll. 81-90 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), p. 96 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Shakespeare, Othello III.iii (Folger Shakespeare)

  23. where ignorance is bliss, 'tis folly to be wise ll. 99-100

    Lonsdale lists parallels without claiming that Gray used any one: Sophocles' Ajax, telling his son that knowing nothing is the sweetest life; Terence's Hecyra, that the time before we learn of trouble is pure gain; Sir William Davenant's song in The Just Italian, 'Since Knowledge is but sorrow's Spy, / It is not safe to know'; and Prior's 'The only wretched are the Wise'. The lines are often quoted as if they praised ignorance in general. In context the claim is narrow and conditional ('where'): knowledge of future suffering would only bring sorrow early. Some readers find the ending consoling; others think it undercuts the long warning before it, or reflects Gray's own melancholy rather than an argument.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), p. 63, note on ll. 99-100 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); D. C. Tovey, ed., Gray's English Poems (1898; 1922), pp. 96-7 (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); W. C. Eppstein, ed., introduction (1959), p. xx (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive)

  24. stanza and form ll. 11-20

    Each stanza has ten lines rhymed ababccdeed; lines 2, 4 and 10 are trimeters and the rest tetrameters, so each stanza opens with a lyric quatrain and closes with a shorter cadence. The structure is regular and Horatian, unlike the irregular Pindarics of the period. The movement is dramatic: two stanzas of landscape and memory; three of the boys at play; a turn at 'Alas!' (stanza 6); three stanzas of personified evils; and a closing reflection. Exclamation ('Ah, happy hills! ah, pleasing shade!') and apostrophe mark the ode's emotional register. Lonsdale emphasises the fusion of topographical poem and subjective ode.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), pp. 55-6, headnote (as transcribed in the Thomas Gray Archive); Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', Lives of the English Poets (1781)
