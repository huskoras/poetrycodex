Death & Dying

On the Death of a Favourite Cat, Drowned in a Tub of Gold Fishes

by Thomas Gray 1716–1771

Death & Dying

'Twas on a lofty vase's side,
Where China's gayest art had dyed
The azure flowers that blow;
Demurest of the tabby kind,
The pensive Selima, reclin'd,
Gaz'd on the lake below.

Her conscious tail her joy declar'd:
The fair round face, the snowy beard,
The velvet of her paws,
Her coat, that with the tortoise vies,
Her ears of jet, and emerald eyes,
She saw; and purr'd applause.

Still had she gaz'd; but midst the tide
Two angel forms were seen to glide,
The Genii of the stream:
Their scaly armour's Tyrian hue
Through richest purple to the view
Betray'd a golden gleam.

The hapless nymph with wonder saw:
A whisker first, and then a claw,
With many an ardent wish,
She stretch'd in vain to reach the prize.
What female heart can gold despise?
What Cat's averse to fish?

Presumptuous maid! with looks intent
Again she stretch'd, again she bent,
Nor knew the gulf between.
(Malignant Fate sat by, and smil'd.)
The slippery verge her feet beguil'd,
She tumbled headlong in.

Eight times emerging from the flood,
She mew'd to every watery God,
Some speedy aid to send.
No Dolphin came, no Nereid stirr'd:
Nor cruel Tom, nor Susan heard.
A favourite has no friend!

From hence, ye beauties, undeceiv'd,
Know, one false step is ne'er retriev'd,
And be with caution bold.
Not all that tempts your wandering eyes
And heedless hearts is lawful prize,
Nor all that glisters gold.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion title; l. 1

    Walpole, Gray's friend from Eton and his companion on the Grand Tour, told him that one of his cats had drowned in a large china tub of goldfish. Gray's letter of 1 March 1747 began with mock gravity, asking which of Walpole's cats he was to lament, Zara or Selima (or was it Fatima?), before enclosing the ode. It appeared in Dodsley's Collection of Poems by Several Hands (vol. II, 1748). Walpole later had the first stanza inscribed on the pedestal of the tub at Strawberry Hill. The poem is a mock-heroic: the high style of classical and Miltonic poetry applied to a household accident, with a moral for young women at the end.

    Thomas Gray to Horace Walpole, 1 March 1747 (Gray Correspondence); Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  2. China's gayest art ... azure flowers ll. 2-3

    Chinese export porcelain was a mark of taste in eighteenth-century houses, and contemporary satire often linked it with female vanity. Bradshaw and Tovey cite Lady Mary Wortley Montagu's Town Eclogues for the 'azure' china, and Lonsdale cites Gay's The Toilette and Tickell's Kensington Garden. Johnson, in his Life of Gray, singled out 'the azure flowers that blow' as an example of a rhyme resolutely forced, since the words add nothing; Bradshaw reads 'that blow' as 'that are blowing on it', so that the painted flowers seem to be in full bloom.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', in Lives of the English Poets (1781); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blow, v.2'

  3. Selima l. 5

    Gray's letter pretended uncertainty whether the dead cat was Zara or Selima, adding 'or Fatima'. Lonsdale notes that Selima is a heroine in Nicholas Rowe's Tamerlane. 'Demurest of the tabby kind', 'pensive' and 'reclin'd', she is presented as a young lady of fashion; the gazing on 'the lake below' recalls Milton's Eve admiring her reflection in a lake (Paradise Lost IV.456-469), a parallel the editors note, and so introduces the theme of vanity.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Thomas Gray to Horace Walpole, 1 March 1747 (Gray Correspondence); John Milton, Paradise Lost (2nd ed., 1674), IV.456-469

  4. conscious tail ... purr'd applause ll. 7-12

    The tail is 'conscious' in the eighteenth-century sense of sharing knowledge or feeling (Latin conscius). The blazon of the cat's beauty (round face, snowy beard, velvet paws, tortoiseshell coat, jet ears, emerald eyes) parodies the catalogue of a lady's charms in love poetry. She sees her reflection and 'purr'd applause', applauding herself. The double perspective is Gray's main comic device: every phrase is accurate about a cat and also about a vain young woman.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'conscious'; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps)

  5. Two angel forms ... The Genii of the stream ll. 14-15

    In classical belief a genius loci guarded each place, and Lonsdale cites Milton's 'Il Penseroso' ('th'unseen Genius of the Wood') and Matthew Green's The Grotto. The goldfish become divinities of the tub, and their 'scaly armour' turns them into epic warriors. The elevation prepares for the fall: the cat reaches for what she takes to be prey, but the fish are, in the poem's mythology, protected beings.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  6. Their scaly armour's Tyrian hue ll. 16-18

    Tyrian purple, made from the murex shellfish at Tyre, was the colour of Roman imperial robes, so the fish are royal as well as martial. Lonsdale cites Dryden's Aeneid (xi, 'A Robe of Tyrian Dye') and Pope's Windsor-Forest (ll. 142, 144). The 'golden gleam' introduces the word that will matter at the end: 'gold' rhymes through the poem as the lure of riches, joined in stanza 4 with the cat's appetite for fish.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Tyrian'

  7. The hapless nymph l. 19

    In his Life of Gray Johnson objected that Selima is called a nymph 'with some violence both to language and sense', though he admitted that the device paid off in the couplet that follows. 'Hapless' (unlucky) is a stock epic epithet. Lonsdale lists Milton's Eve, Virgil's Camilla, Gay's The Toilette and Ovid's Daphne among the echoes in the portrait of Selima. 'Nymph' was the regular eighteenth-century term for a young lady in polite verse, as in Pope's Rape of the Lock, which the whole poem echoes in its mock-epic treatment of trivial events.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', in Lives of the English Poets (1781); Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  8. What female heart can gold despise? / What Cat's averse to fish? ll. 23-24

    Johnson observed that the first line relates only to the nymph and the second only to the cat, and he thought the device well used. The couplet makes explicit the double allegory: Selima's desire is both natural appetite and avarice. The satire on women's love of gold and finery was conventional (editors cite Addison's Spectator No. 15, which moralises Virgil's Camilla as a type of female love of show), and modern readers may find it dated or misogynistic, though the poem's tone is genial. 'Gold' and 'fish' are united in the goldfish themselves.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', in Lives of the English Poets (1781); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps)

  9. Presumptuous maid! ll. 25-27

    Lonsdale compares Pope's Essay on Man (I.35), where human presumption in questioning God's order is rebuked. The 'gulf between' is both the physical gap between the vase's rim and the fish and, in the heightened style, the abyss that separates mortals from forbidden objects. The stanza moves quickly: 'Again she stretch'd, again she bent', and then the parenthesis of 'Malignant Fate' smiling, before 'She tumbled headlong in'.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man (1733-4), I.35

  10. Malignant Fate sat by, and smil'd l. 28

    Classical epic often shows the gods watching mortal disasters, and Pope's Rape of the Lock and Dunciad parody the device. The bracketed line slows the action at the brink, and 'beguil'd' (deceived) in the next line makes the slippery rim itself a tempter. The language ('headlong') recalls the falls of Milton's rebel angels, who are hurled 'headlong flaming' from heaven (Paradise Lost I.45), in keeping with the poem's comic use of Miltonic grandeur.

    John Milton, Paradise Lost (2nd ed., 1674), I.44-45; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  11. Eight times emerging from the flood l. 31

    Phelps and later editors explain the allusion to the cat's nine lives. Lonsdale also compares Dryden's translation of Homer, where Thetis rises 'emerging from the deep'. Selima mews 'to every watery God', the pagan deities of the sea, but none responds; the mock-epic catalogue of rescuers in the next line confirms the absence. The numerical precision is part of the comedy, since it treats folk superstition as if it were a law of nature, and it makes the drowning seem fated from the start.

    Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  12. No Dolphin came, no Nereid stirr'd l. 34

    Herodotus tells how the singer Arion, thrown overboard by sailors, was carried ashore by a dolphin charmed by his music; the Nereids, daughters of Nereus, were sea-nymphs who helped mariners. Lonsdale also cites Sabrina rising to help the Lady in Milton's Comus. The descent from myth to the domestic 'cruel Tom' and 'Susan', generic servants' names, is the bathos of the mock-heroic. The manuscripts and first edition read 'Harry' for 'Susan'.

    Herodotus, Histories I.23-24 (Arion and the dolphin); Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  13. A favourite has no friend! l. 36

    The line applies to the pampered pet, whom the servants have no reason to rescue, and also to court favourites, a topical point when the poem's recipient was the son of the former prime minister Sir Robert Walpole. Johnson called it 'a melancholy truth'. The thomasgray.org notes record that the Walpole manuscript and Dodsley's 1748 text have the rhetorical question 'What favourite has a friend?'; Gray later made it a statement.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', in Lives of the English Poets (1781); Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  14. Nor all that glisters gold ll. 37-42

    The final stanza addresses 'ye beauties': one false step cannot be undone, so be 'with caution bold'. The last line turns the proverb familiar from Shakespeare's casket scene (Merchant of Venice II.vii.65) and from Chaucer, Spenser and Dryden's Hind and the Panther, all cited by editors. Johnson objected that the poem ends in a pointed sentence unrelated to its purpose: if what glistered had been gold, the cat would still have drowned. Defenders reply that the moral is meant as a comic misfit, part of the mock-solemn manner, and some readers hear in 'false step' a sly sexual warning.

    William Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice, II.vii.65; Samuel Johnson, 'Gray', in Lives of the English Poets (1781); Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes

  15. Form whole poem

    The stanza (aabccb, lines of 8, 8, 6 syllables twice) is a common lyric sixain, a form of tail-rhyme stanza; Gray calls the poem an ode, but it is closer to a Horatian or comic ode than the Pindaric odes he wrote later. Each short third and sixth line gives a snap that the poet uses for the stanza's point, most famously 'What Cat's averse to fish?' and 'A favourite has no friend!'. The structure is narrative: scene, reflection, temptation, fall, drowning, and a moral.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', notes; Thomas Gray Archive (Oxford), 'Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat', text with explanatory and textual notes from editors (Lonsdale, Tovey, Bradshaw, Phelps)
