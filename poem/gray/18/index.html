Death & Dying

Sonnet on the Death of Richard West

by Thomas Gray 1716–1771

From Works, ed. Gosse, vol. I (1884)

Death & Dying

In vain to me the smiling Mornings shine,
And redning Phoebus lifts his golden Fire:
The Birds in vain their amorous Descant joyn;
Or chearful Fields resume their green Attire:
These Ears, alas! for other Notes repine,
A different Object do these Eyes require:
My lonely Anguish melts no Heart but mine;
And in my Breast the imperfect Joys expire.
Yet Morning smiles the busy Race to chear,
And new-born Pleasure brings to happier Men:
The Fields to all their wonted Tribute bear;
To warm their little Loves the Birds complain:
I fruitless mourn to him that cannot hear,
And weep the more because I weep in vain.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date title; l. 1

    Richard West (1716-1742), Gray's friend from Eton, was a poet in poor health who died of consumption at 25. Gray had sent him his 'Ode on the Spring' without knowing he was dying. Gray's fair copy in his Commonplace Book is headed 'Sonnet' and dated 'at Stoke, Aug: 1742'; he did not publish it, and it first appeared in William Mason's edition of Gray's Poems with his Memoirs (1775). Gray also mourned West in his unfinished Latin poem De Principiis Cogitandi, addressed to 'Favonius' (usually identified with West), which breaks off in a lament for him. The text here keeps Gray's own older spellings (redning, joyn, chearful).

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; Edmund Gosse, ed., The Works of Thomas Gray, vol. I (London, 1884), the Sonnet

  2. Rhyme scheme and turn l. 9

    The sonnet was rare in English between Milton and Gray, and Gray's is one of the few of the mid-century. The octave alternates two rhymes throughout (shine / Fire / joyn / Attire / repine / require / mine / expire), an Italian economy of rhyme in an alternating pattern; the sestet alternates two more (chear / Men / bear / complain / hear / vain). The 'Yet' of l. 9 turns from the speaker's grief to the world's indifference. Its design, nature renewing itself while the mourner cannot, belongs to an old elegiac topos found in Petrarch's sonnet 'Zephiro torna' (Rime 310), to which the sonnet has often been compared.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); Joseph Foladare, 'Gray's "Frail Memorial" to West', PMLA 75.1 (1960), 61-65

  3. smiling Mornings l. 1

    Bradshaw (1891) points out three instances of the 'smiling morn' in Paradise Lost (v.124, v.168, xi.175). The personified smiling morning is a stock of eighteenth-century descriptive diction and is precisely the sort of phrase Wordsworth would object to as 'poetic diction'. The plural 'Mornings' makes the joy of nature recur day after day, which heightens the speaker's isolation. The phrase returns in l. 9 ('Yet Morning smiles'), the hinge of the sonnet.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); John Milton, Paradise Lost, v.124, v.168, xi.175; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West

  4. redning Phoebus l. 2

    Phoebus, 'the bright one', is the epithet of Apollo as sun god, a classical periphrasis for the sun. 'Lifts his golden Fire' completes the image of sunrise. 'Redning' is Gray's own spelling of 'reddening'. Wordsworth singled out this kind of mythological ornament as artificial; defenders have answered that Gray deliberately sets the conventional splendour of the octave's first lines against the plain statements of grief that follow.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); Edmund Gosse, ed., The Works of Thomas Gray, vol. I (London, 1884), the Sonnet; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Phoebus'

  5. amorous Descant l. 3

    In music a descant is an ornamental melody sung above a fixed tune (Medieval Latin discantus). Milton's nightingale in Paradise Lost 'all night long her amorous descant sung' (Bradshaw cites iv.603, Tovey iv.602). Tovey adds that 'descant' in Milton means a variation on a plain song, a precision Gray's general application to birdsong loses. 'Joyn' is Gray's spelling of 'join'. The birds' love-song is one of three images (sun, birds, fields) that the sestet takes up again.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); John Milton, Paradise Lost, iv.603; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'descant'

  6. green Attire l. 4

    Bradshaw compares Paradise Lost vii.501, 'Earth in her rich attire'. 'Resume' implies the yearly renewal of nature after winter, which is the root of the poem's contrast between cyclical natural time and the finality of human death. The 'chearful Fields' (Gray's spelling) are personified as capable of cheerfulness, an attribution the speaker cannot share. Gray's three images in the opening quatrain (sun, birds, fields) are deliberately conventional, the common furniture of spring poems, and the sestet will return to each in turn to show that the world's renewal continues for others while the speaker is shut out from it.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); John Milton, Paradise Lost, vii.501; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West

  7. repine l. 5

    The OED gives 'repine' as to feel or express discontent, to fret, and also to long for something with discontent. 'Notes' plays on the birds' notes of l. 3 and the human voice of West, perhaps also his poetry. Ears and eyes in ll. 5-6 shift the poem from the outward scene to the bodily senses of the mourner, which find nothing they want in nature.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'repine'; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West

  8. Wordsworth's critique ll. 6-8, 13-14

    In the Preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800) Wordsworth used the sonnet to attack poetic diction. He set ll. 6-8 and 13-14 in italics and declared that 'the only part of this Sonnet which is of any value' was those lines, whose language differs from prose only in rhyme and in 'fruitless' for 'fruitlessly'. Coleridge replied in Biographia Literaria ch. 18 that the distinction between prose and metrical language is real, and found faults in the italicised lines too. Foladare (1960) re-examined the charge that the sonnet is stilted and asked how well its emotion is actually concealed; the contrast of conventional and plain diction can be read as part of Gray's design, the ornate lines belonging to the world's joy and the plain ones to the mourner.

    William Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (2nd edn, 1800), the passage on Gray's sonnet; S. T. Coleridge, Biographia Literaria (1817), ch. 18; Joseph Foladare, 'Gray's "Frail Memorial" to West', PMLA 75.1 (1960), 61-65; The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922)

  9. imperfect l. 8

    Bradshaw glosses 'imperfect' as incomplete, because the joys cannot be shared with the friend. The scansion requires elision ('th'imperfect'). Rogers compared Dryden's 'And in my ear the imperfect accent dies', which Tovey thought a parallel in rhythm rather than thought. The line closes the octave on 'expire', a word that also means to die, so that the joys share the fate of the friend.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'imperfect'

  10. the busy Race l. 9

    'Race' is the human race; 'busy' suggests both industry and the distraction of the living from death. The phrase anticipates the 'happier Men' of l. 10 and, in Gray's later work, the 'busy' world from which the dead of the Elegy are absent (the housewife 'busy' at her evening care). The sestet thus generalises: the world goes on, and the one person who would share the speaker's sense of loss is gone.

    Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'busy'

  11. wonted Tribute l. 11

    'Wonted' (from the old verb 'won', to dwell, be accustomed) is a poetic archaism in Gray's day. 'Tribute' figures the crops as a payment owed by the fields to mankind, a georgic conception. 'To all' is pointed: the speaker alone receives nothing from nature's generosity. The line recalls the language of Virgil's Georgics and its English imitators, in which the earth pays its yearly dues to the farmer; the economic metaphor stresses regularity and reliability, exactly what death has removed from the speaker's own life.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wonted'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tribute'; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West

  12. complain l. 12

    In amorous poetry 'complain' meant to utter a love-plaint, a sense the OED records; birdsong was imagined as courtship. 'To warm their little Loves' means to make their mates ardent, and the diminutive is affectionate. The line renews the 'amorous Descant' of l. 3, and the plaintive verb 'complain' anticipates the speaker's own lament in the next line, whose object, unlike theirs, 'cannot hear'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'complain'; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922)

  13. fruitless l. 13

    Gray's use of 'fruitless' adverbially is a Latinate, poetic licence. It contrasts with the fields' 'Tribute' just before: the fields bear fruit, the mourner's grief does not. The line's plain statement ('to him that cannot hear') is the emotional centre of the sonnet, and Wordsworth italicised it as such. 'To him that cannot hear' points back to the 'other Notes' of l. 5: the ears that longed for West's voice now address a friend who cannot hear them, so the poem's address itself becomes a sign of loss. Coleridge, by contrast, found even the italicised lines open to criticism.

    William Wordsworth, Preface to Lyrical Ballads (2nd edn, 1800), the passage on Gray's sonnet; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922)

  14. weep in vain l. 14

    Bradshaw and Tovey both cite Colley Cibber's adaptation of Richard III (the two editors give the line in slightly different wordings). Bradshaw also recalls the anecdote of Solon, who, told that weeping for his dead son would avail nothing, replied that he wept precisely for that reason; he notes a similar story of Augustus. Tovey cites Diogenes Laertius for Solon. The last line returns to the poem's first words, 'In vain', closing the sonnet in a ring and turning the futility of nature's joys into the futility of grief.

    The Thomas Gray Archive, 'Sonnet [on the Death of Mr Richard West]': text, manuscript history and notes from Bradshaw (1891) and Tovey (1922); Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Thomas Gray, William Collins, Oliver Goldsmith (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1969), notes to the Sonnet on the Death of Richard West; Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers, I.63 (Solon)
