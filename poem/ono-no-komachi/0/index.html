Nature

The blossom’s tint is washed away

by Ono no Komachi 834–880

Translated by William N. Porter (1909) · From A Hundred Verses from Old Japan (Hyakunin Isshu), poem 9

Nature

The blossom’s tint is washed away
By heavy showers of rain;
My charms, which once I prized so much,
Are also on the wane,—
Both bloomed, alas! in vain.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Ono no Komachi's 'Hana no iro wa', Kokinshu 113; Hyakunin isshu 9 l. 1

    Ono no Komachi (fl. mid-ninth century) is the most famous woman poet of early Heian Japan and one of the 'Six Poetic Immortals' (rokkasen) named in the preface to the Kokinshu (c. 905), the first imperial anthology, where this poem appears as no. 113 among the spring poems. Fujiwara no Teika later chose it as poem 9 of his Hyakunin isshu (c. 1235). The Japanese is a tanka (waka) of thirty-one syllables in five lines (5-7-5-7-7): Hana no iro wa / utsuri ni keri na / itazura ni / waga mi yo ni furu / nagame seshi ma ni. Porter's Victorian version rhymes and adds phrases; his note gives her dates as 834-880, which are traditional and uncertain.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9; 'Hyakunin Isshu', Wikipedia

  2. hana no iro: the colour of the flowers l. 1

    花の色 hana no iro, 'the colour of the flowers'. In the Kokinshu period, hana by itself normally refers to the cherry, whose brief bloom was the supreme emblem of transient beauty. 色 iro means colour, but also outward beauty, sensual charm and amorous love, so 'the flower's colour' is from the start also the poet's attractiveness. Porter's 'tint' is accurate to the literal sense; his 'My charms' in l. 3 makes explicit the second meaning that the Japanese keeps within one word.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9

  3. utsuri ni keri na: 'has faded, alas' l. 1

    移りにけりな utsuri ni keri na, 'has faded, ah!'. 移る utsuru means 'to change, shift, fade' (of colour), and the auxiliary keri marks sudden realisation, with na an exclamation: 'I see now it has faded!'. Porter's 'is washed away' gives a cause, the rain, that in the Japanese comes later through wordplay. Utsuru also suggests the fickleness of love, as in Komachi's other famous poem on the 'flower of the heart' that fades unseen (Kokinshu 797).

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9

  4. pivot-words (kakekotoba): furu and nagame l. 2

    The last two Japanese lines, waga mi yo ni furu / nagame seshi ma ni, read in two ways at once. 経る furu, 'to pass (through life), grow old', puns on 降る furu, 'to fall' (of rain); 眺め nagame, 'gazing, brooding, lost in thought', puns on 長雨 nagame, 'long rains' of spring. So the lines mean both 'while I passed my life in the world, lost in vain brooding' and 'while the long rains fell'. Such pivot-words (kakekotoba) are central to Heian technique. Porter's note recognises the double reading. He separates the senses, giving 'heavy showers of rain' to the flowers and 'on the wane' to the woman, rather than fusing them.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; Robert H. Brower and Earl Miner, Japanese Court Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1961); Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9

  5. the poet's fading beauty l. 3

    わが身 waga mi, 'my self, my body', is the human counterpart of the flower. The Japanese does not say that she prized her charms; Porter adds the phrase, perhaps to fit a legend of Komachi's pride. Later tradition, especially Nō plays such as Sotoba Komachi, made her a proud beauty who rejected lovers and ended as a ragged old woman, and Porter's note mentions her 'unhappy old age'. The poem itself, read in the Kokinshu as a spring poem, concerns the regret of time passing while one is absorbed in thought, which modern scholars prefer to read without the biographical legend.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9; 'Ono no Komachi', Wikipedia; Robert H. Brower and Earl Miner, Japanese Court Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1961)

  6. 'on the wane' l. 4

    'On the wane' (OED: in a state of decline, originally of the moon) is Porter's English cliché for decline, supplying a rhyme with 'rain' and 'vain'. The Japanese has no corresponding phrase: the decline is implied by furu, 'to grow old in the world'. Porter's version makes the parallel between flower and woman explicit with 'also', where Komachi's lines let the two meanings overlap.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface

  7. itazura ni: 'in vain, to no purpose' l. 5

    いたづらに itazura ni, the third line of the Japanese, means 'uselessly, in vain, idly'. Placed in the middle, it can modify the fading of the flowers (they faded without being enjoyed) or the passing of the poet's life (spent in vain brooding), and it hinges the two halves of the poem. Porter's note says the middle line applies to both flower and beauty. His final line, 'Both bloomed, alas! in vain', moves the word to the end and adds 'bloomed' and 'alas', turning reticent regret into an explicit lament. The added fifth rhyming line is his way of imitating the tanka's five-line shape.

    William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; Joshua S. Mostow, Pictures of the Heart: The Hyakunin Isshu in Word and Image (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 1996), poem 9

  8. Ki no Tsurayuki's judgement of Komachi whole poem

    In the Japanese preface (Kana jo) to the Kokinshu, Ki no Tsurayuki described Komachi as belonging to the line of the ancient Princess Sotoori: her poetry was full of feeling but lacked strength, like a beautiful noblewoman suffering from illness. The comparison, partly praise and partly criticism, made her the model of passionate, melancholy women's poetry. Her other poems in the Kokinshu are largely love poems about dreams and longing. Porter's note also records a legend that her verse ended a drought in 866, an example of the belief that great poetry can move heaven and earth, stated in the same preface.

    Helen Craig McCullough, trans., Kokin Wakashu: The First Imperial Anthology of Japanese Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1985), poem 113 and the Kana Preface; 'Kokin Wakashu', Wikipedia; William N. Porter, trans., A Hundred Verses from Old Japan, Being a Translation of the Hyaku-nin-isshiu (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909), no. 9 with note (sacred-texts transcription); Robert H. Brower and Earl Miner, Japanese Court Poetry (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1961)
