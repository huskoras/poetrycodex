Death & Dying

The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Men and Women (1855)

Death & Dying

Vanity, saith the preacher, vanity!
Draw round my bed: is Anselm keeping back?
Nephews--sons mine ... ah God, I know not! Well--
She, men would have to be your mother once,
Old Gandolf envied me, so fair she was!
What 's done is done, and she is dead beside,
Dead long ago, and I am Bishop since,
And as she died so must we die ourselves,
And thence ye may perceive the world's a dream.
Life, how and what is it? As here I lie
In this state-chamber, dying by degrees,
Hours and long hours in the dead night, I ask
"Do I live, am I dead?" Peace, peace seems all.
Saint Praxed's ever was the church for peace;
And so, about this tomb of mine. I fought
With tooth and nail to save my niche, ye know:
--Old Gandolf cozened me, despite my care;
Shrewd was that snatch from out the corner South
He graced his carrion with, God curse the same!
Yet still my niche is not so cramped but thence
One sees the pulpit o' the epistle-side,
And somewhat of the choir, those silent seats,
And up into the aery dome where live
The angels, and a sunbeam's sure to lurk:
And I shall fill my slab of basalt there,
And 'neath my tabernacle take my rest,
With those nine columns round me, two and two,
The odd one at my feet where Anselm stands:
Peach-blossom marble all, the rare, the ripe
As fresh-poured red wine of a mighty pulse.
--Old Gandolf with his paltry onion-stone,
Put me where I may look at him! True peach,
Rosy and flawless: how I earned the prize!
Draw close: that conflagration of my church
--What then? So much was saved if aught were missed!
My sons, ye would not be my death? Go dig
The white-grape vineyard where the oil-press stood,
Drop water gently till the surface sink,
And if ye find ... Ah God, I know not, I! ...
Bedded in store of rotten fig-leaves soft,
And corded up in a tight olive-frail,
Some lump, ah God, of lapis lazuli,
Big as a Jew's head cut off at the nape,
Blue as a vein o'er the Madonna's breast ...
Sons, all have I bequeathed you, villas, all,
That brave Frascati villa with its bath,
So, let the blue lump poise between my knees,
Like God the Father's globe on both his hands
Ye worship in the Jesu Church so gay,
For Gandolf shall not choose but see and burst!
Swift as a weaver's shuttle fleet our years:
Man goeth to the grave, and where is he?
Did I say basalt for my slab, sons? Black--
'T was ever antique-black I meant! How else
Shall ye contrast my frieze to come beneath?
The bas-relief in bronze ye promised me,
Those Pans and Nymphs ye wot of, and perchance
Some tripod, thyrsus, with a vase or so,
The Saviour at his sermon on the mount,
Saint Praxed in a glory, and one Pan
Ready to twitch the Nymph's last garment off,
And Moses with the tables ... but I know
Ye mark me not! What do they whisper thee,
Child of my bowels, Anselm? Ah, ye hope
To revel down my villas while I gasp
Bricked o'er with beggar's mouldy travertine
Which Gandolf from his tomb-top chuckles at!
Nay, boys, ye love me--all of jasper, then!
'T is jasper ye stand pledged to, lest I grieve
My bath must needs be left behind, alas!
One block, pure green as a pistachio-nut,
There 's plenty jasper somewhere in the world--
And have I not Saint Praxed's ear to pray
Horses for ye, and brown Greek manuscripts,
And mistresses with great smooth marbly limbs?
--That's if ye carve my epitaph aright,
Choice Latin, picked phrase, Tully's every word,
No gaudy ware like Gandolf's second line--
Tully, my masters? Ulpian serves his need!
And then how I shall lie through centuries,
And hear the blessed mutter of the mass,
And see God made and eaten all day long,
And feel the steady candle-flame, and taste
Good strong thick stupefying incense-smoke!
For as I lie here, hours of the dead night,
Dying: in state and by such slow degrees,
I fold my arms as if they clasped a crook,
And stretch my feet forth straight as stone can point,
And let the bedclothes, for a mortcloth, drop
Into great laps and folds of sculptor's-work:
And as yon tapers dwindle, and strange thoughts
Grow, with a certain humming in my ears,
About the life before I lived this life,
And this life too, popes, cardinals and priests,
Saint Praxed at his sermon on the mount,
Your tall pale mother with her talking eyes,
And new-found agate urns as fresh as day,
And marble's language, Latin pure, discreet,
--Aha, ELUCESCEBAT quoth our friend?
No Tully, said I, Ulpian at the best!
Evil and brief hath been my pilgrimage.
All lapis, all, sons! Else I give the Pope
My villas! Will ye ever eat my heart?
Ever your eyes were as a lizard's quick,
They glitter like your mother's for my soul,
Or ye would heighten my impoverished frieze,
Piece out its starved design, and fill my vase
With grapes, and add a visor and a Term,
And to the tripod ye would tie a lynx
That in his struggle throws the thyrsus down,
To comfort me on my entablature
Whereon I am to lie till I must ask
"Do I live, am I dead?" There, leave me, there!
For ye have stabbed me with ingratitude
To death--ye wish it--God, ye wish it! Stone--
Gritstone, a-crumble! Clammy squares which sweat
As if the corpse they keep were oozing through--
And no more lapis to delight the world!
Well, go! I bless ye. Fewer tapers there,
But in a row: and, going, turn your backs
--Ay, like departing altar-ministrants,
And leave me in my church, the church for peace,
That I may watch at leisure if he leers--
Old Gandolf--at me, from his onion-stone,
As still he envied me, so fair she was!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Bishop Orders His Tomb: date, setting and publication l. 1

    Browning wrote the poem after visiting Rome in 1844 and sent it for publication early in 1845; it appeared in Hood's Magazine in March 1845 and then in Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845). Its original title added 'Rome, 15--', setting it in the sixteenth century; this text omits that dateline. From 1863 Browning reprinted it in the group 'Men and Women', hence the collection given here. The Bishop and his rival Gandolf are fictional, though the church of Santa Prassede is real. John Ruskin famously praised it in Modern Painters IV, saying that it compressed into a few lines nearly all he had said of the central Renaissance in thirty pages of The Stones of Venice.

    Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981); John Ruskin, Modern Painters, vol. IV (1856), on Browning and the Renaissance spirit; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  2. Vanity, saith the preacher l. 1

    The Bishop opens with the most famous text on worldly futility, Ecclesiastes 1:2: 'Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity' (Latin Vulgate, 'vanitas vanitatum'). As a churchman he slips naturally into scriptural citation. The irony is immediate: a speech that begins by renouncing earthly things turns at once to marble, lapis lazuli, villas, mistresses and the defeat of a rival. Biblical phrases about mortality (from Job and Genesis) recur through the poem, always undercut by the Bishop's materialism.

    Bible, King James Version, Ecclesiastes 1:2; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997)

  3. Dramatic monologue in blank verse l. 2

    The poem is one of the defining examples of Browning's dramatic monologue: one voice, an implied audience (the 'nephews' gathered at the deathbed), and a situation reconstructed from the speech alone. It is in blank verse whose rhythms bend to the Bishop's broken, wandering speech, full of dashes, exclamations and abrupt turns. The listeners never speak, but their responses (whispers, inattention) are registered in his words. Ruskin's praise of the poem's grasp of the Renaissance, its 'worldliness' and taste, has become a commonplace; later critics, including Greenberg, have shown how far it also reflects Victorian polemics about Catholicism and Renaissance art associated with Pugin.

    John Ruskin, Modern Painters, vol. IV (1856), on Browning and the Renaissance spirit; Robert A. Greenberg, 'Ruskin, Pugin, and the Contemporary Context of "The Bishop Orders His Tomb"', PMLA 84.6 (1969); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  4. Nephews--sons mine l. 3

    Priests and bishops vowed to celibacy commonly passed off their natural children as nephews (Italian nipoti); the practice of favouring such relatives in church appointments gave English the word 'nepotism'. The Bishop's slip from 'Nephews' to 'sons mine' exposes the fiction, and he then lapses into uncertainty ('ah God, I know not!'), whether about their paternity or about what he should admit. Throughout the poem he calls them 'sons', 'boys' and 'Child of my bowels', which echoes biblical idioms for begotten children (e.g. 2 Samuel 16:11).

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. nepotism, n.; Bible, King James Version, 2 Samuel 16:11

  5. She, men would have to be your mother once l. 4

    The line is deliberately tangled. 'She, men would have to be your mother' means roughly 'the woman people say was your mother': the Bishop does not openly acknowledge the relationship but attributes it to rumour. Yet he cannot hide his pride that 'Old Gandolf envied me, so fair she was!', which reveals that the rivalry between the two churchmen extended to the woman. The last line of the poem returns to the same phrase, so that the monologue begins and ends with possession and envy. She reappears in memory later as 'Your tall pale mother with her talking eyes'.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981)

  6. Old Gandolf l. 5

    Gandolf is invented by Browning. He was evidently the Bishop's predecessor at Saint Praxed's, and he won the best tomb position, 'that snatch from out the corner South'. The Bishop's speech returns to him obsessively: he wants a tomb that will make Gandolf 'see and burst', he mocks his rival's cheap 'onion-stone' and faulty Latin, and he imagines Gandolf leering at him from his own tomb-top. The rivalry is the poem's dramatic engine, showing how the Bishop's supposed contemplation of death is entirely absorbed into worldly competition. Allingham notes he means to carry it on for eternity.

    Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981); Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  7. Saint Praxed's l. 14

    Browning anglicises Santa Prassede, a basilica on the Esquiline in Rome near Santa Maria Maggiore. A church on the site goes back to early Christian times; the present building was rebuilt by Pope Paschal I in the ninth century and is famous for its mosaics. Saint Praxedes, according to legend, was a second-century Roman virgin who with her sister Pudentiana cared for persecuted Christians and gave her riches to the poor and the Church. The Bishop's lust for luxury in her church is therefore pointedly ironic. His claim that it was 'ever ... the church for peace' sits oddly with the 'tooth and nail' struggle he goes on to describe.

    'Santa Prassede', Wikipedia; Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981); Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997)

  8. the epistle-side l. 21

    In the traditional Latin Mass the Epistle was read from the right-hand side of the altar as the congregation faces it, which in a correctly oriented church is the south side; the Gospel was read from the left (north). The Bishop's niche gives him a view of 'the pulpit o' the epistle-side', the choir, and the dome. He values the position for what he will be able to 'see' as a corpse, a grotesque literalism about life after death that recurs when he imagines hearing the Mass and seeing 'God made and eaten'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. epistle, n.; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  9. Peach-blossom marble; onion-stone l. 29

    The Bishop's tomb is to be ringed by nine columns of a pink and white marble, here called 'peach-blossom'. He describes its colour in sensual terms, 'the rare, the ripe / As fresh-poured red wine of a mighty pulse', mixing the language of fruit, wine and blood. By contrast Gandolf has to make do with 'paltry onion-stone', the Bishop's mocking translation of cipollino (from Italian cipolla, onion), a green-veined marble so called because it splits in layers like an onion. Throughout, kinds of stone serve as status symbols and measures of the Bishop's vanity.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. cipollino, n.

  10. Some lump ... of lapis lazuli l. 42

    Lapis lazuli (Medieval Latin, 'stone of azure') is a deep blue semi-precious stone, ground to make ultramarine, the costly pigment used for the Virgin's robes in paintings. The Bishop's broken confession implies that during a 'conflagration of my church' he secretly saved a great lump of it for himself ('So much was saved if aught were missed!') and buried it in an olive basket in his vineyard. His similes are revealing: 'Big as a Jew's head cut off at the nape' (violent and contemptuous) and 'Blue as a vein o'er the Madonna's breast' (sacred and sensual at once). The juxtaposition sums up the poem's mixture of piety, cruelty and erotic aestheticism.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981)

  11. olive-frail; rotten fig-leaves l. 41

    A frail (Old French fraiel) is a basket woven from rushes, used especially for figs, raisins and olives. The Bishop tells his sons to dig in the vineyard 'where the oil-press stood', pour water gently until the ground sinks, and find the stone packed in fig leaves inside a corded olive-frail. The precise instructions show how carefully he hid his theft. The fig leaves may carry a suggestive echo of Genesis 3:7, where Adam and Eve cover their shame with fig leaves: here they conceal a guilty secret.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. frail, n.1; Bible, King James Version, Genesis 3:7; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  12. Big as a Jew's head cut off at the nape l. 43

    The comparison is one of the poem's most disturbing moments. It measures the precious stone against a severed human head, and the head is specifically a Jew's, reflecting the contempt for Jews common in Renaissance Rome (and in Browning's own day). Some readers also see an allusion to Renaissance painting of decapitated heads, such as Salome with the Baptist's head or Judith with Holofernes'. Browning puts the prejudice in the speaker's mouth as part of his characterisation: the next line's 'Blue as a vein o'er the Madonna's breast' shows that his aesthetic sense makes no moral distinctions between images of violence and of devotion.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997)

  13. God the Father's globe ... the Jesu Church l. 49

    The Chiesa del Gesu, begun in 1568, is the principal Jesuit church in Rome and a model of lavish Baroque decoration ('so gay'). The altar of St Ignatius, designed by Andrea Pozzo and made in 1695-1699, is topped by a sculpted Trinity in which God the Father holds a great globe of lapis lazuli. The Bishop wants his lapis to sit between his effigy's knees like that globe. The chapel's date does not fit a bishop dying in the sixteenth century (the poem's original dateline), an inconsistency that readers have noticed; it may be Browning's licence or slip. Either way, the comparison sets the Bishop's effigy alongside God.

    'Andrea Pozzo, St. Ignatius Chapel, Il Gesu, Rome', Smarthistory; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  14. Swift as a weaver's shuttle ... where is he? l. 51

    Having just gloated that Gandolf 'shall not choose but see and burst', the Bishop lurches back into pious commonplace. 'Swift as a weaver's shuttle fleet our years' paraphrases Job 7:6, 'My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and are spent without hope'. 'Man goeth to the grave, and where is he?' echoes Job 14:10, 'man dieth, and wasteth away: yea, man giveth up the ghost, and where is he?' The abrupt switch shows how scriptural language is for him a habit, not a conviction; within a line he is back to the colour of his slab.

    Bible, King James Version, Job 7:6; Bible, King James Version, Job 14:10; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  15. basalt; antique-black l. 54

    Basalt is a dark volcanic stone. 'Antique-black' translates the Italian nero antico, a fine black marble quarried in antiquity (chiefly in Greece) and reused by Renaissance builders from Roman ruins; it was much more precious. The Bishop corrects himself in panic (''T was ever antique-black I meant!') because the colour must contrast with the bronze bas-relief he wants beneath. The self-correction shows his mind both wandering and obsessively precise about materials.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. antique, adj. and n.

  16. Pans and Nymphs ... and Moses with the tables l. 57

    The Bishop's bronze relief is to include Pans and Nymphs, a tripod (the three-legged vessel of Apollo's oracle at Delphi), a thyrsus (the ivy-wreathed staff of Bacchus), a vase, Christ preaching on the Mount, Saint Praxed in a 'glory' (halo), and Moses with the tablets of the Law, together with a Pan about to 'twitch the Nymph's last garment off'. The eroticism is placed beside the giving of the commandments without any sense of incongruity. This blending of classical sensuality and Christian subject-matter is the 'Renaissance spirit' that Ruskin and later critics saw distilled in the poem, though Greenberg argues it also reflects Victorian polemics against Renaissance art.

    John Ruskin, Modern Painters, vol. IV (1856), on Browning and the Renaissance spirit; Robert A. Greenberg, 'Ruskin, Pugin, and the Contemporary Context of "The Bishop Orders His Tomb"', PMLA 84.6 (1969); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  17. beggar's mouldy travertine l. 66

    Travertine (Italian travertino, from Latin lapis Tiburtinus, 'stone of Tibur', modern Tivoli) is a porous limestone quarried near Rome and used for much of the city's grandest building, including the Colosseum. Its very commonness makes it contemptible to the Bishop, who fears his sons will brick him over in it and spend his money on themselves: 'To revel down my villas while I gasp'. The verb 'gasp' grimly imagines him still conscious inside the tomb, as if he will go on perceiving after death.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. travertine, n.; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  18. jasper ... pure green as a pistachio-nut l. 68

    Jasper is an opaque, often green or red, ornamental quartz. The Bishop's choice of 'One block, pure green as a pistachio-nut' again uses the language of food for stone. There may be a further irony: in Revelation the heavenly city has 'the glory of God: and her light was like unto a stone most precious, even like a jasper stone' (21:11), and its wall is built of jasper (21:18). The Bishop's idea of glory is a jasper monument here and now. He then offers, in return, to pray to Saint Praxed for horses, Greek manuscripts and 'mistresses with great smooth marbly limbs', converting intercession into bribery.

    Bible, King James Version, Revelation 21:11-18; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  19. Tully; Ulpian l. 77

    Marcus Tullius Cicero, known in English as 'Tully', was for Renaissance humanists the supreme model of pure Latin style; strict 'Ciceronians' admitted only words he had used. The Bishop wants his epitaph in 'Choice Latin, picked phrase, Tully's every word'. Domitius Ulpianus (Ulpian, killed c. 223/228 AD) was a great jurist whose writings were preserved in the Digest of Justinian, but his Latin belonged to a later, technical idiom. 'Ulpian serves his need' is a sneer at Gandolf's epitaph. The pedantry is part of the Renaissance portrait: Ruskin listed the period's 'good Latin' among the things the poem catches of the Renaissance spirit.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); John Ruskin, Modern Painters, vol. IV (1856), on Browning and the Renaissance spirit

  20. ELUCESCEBAT quoth our friend? l. 99

    Gandolf's inscription apparently read 'elucescebat', an imperfect of the inceptive verb elucescere, 'to begin to shine forth'. A Ciceronian would have written elucebat, from elucere, 'to shine out, be conspicuous'. The Bishop's triumphant 'No Tully, said I, Ulpian at the best!' recalls an old quarrel in which he mocked the faulty form. The joke shows the depth of his pedantic rivalry: even in his dying hour he remembers a grammatical victory over Gandolf. That he calls Gandolf 'our friend' adds a layer of sarcasm.

    Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  21. see God made and eaten all day long l. 82

    The line refers to the Eucharist and the doctrine of transubstantiation: at each Mass, the priest's consecration makes the bread the body of Christ, which is then received. The Bishop imagines enjoying the endless repetition of this from his tomb, as a spectacle alongside the 'blessed mutter of the mass', the candle-flame and 'Good strong thick stupefying incense-smoke'. The bluntness of 'made and eaten' is often read as Browning's Protestant irony, reducing the sacrament to a sensual show; but it also characterises a man for whom religion is entirely a matter of the senses.

    Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes; Robert A. Greenberg, 'Ruskin, Pugin, and the Contemporary Context of "The Bishop Orders His Tomb"', PMLA 84.6 (1969)

  22. the bedclothes, for a mortcloth l. 89

    A mortcloth (an older word, familiar especially in Scotland) is the cloth spread over a coffin or corpse at a funeral. The Bishop folds his arms 'as if they clasped a crook' (the bishop's crozier), stretches his feet 'straight as stone can point', and lets the bedclothes fall into 'great laps and folds of sculptor's-work'. He is posing as the recumbent effigy that will lie on his tomb, turning himself into art while still alive. The moment is one of the poem's most uncanny, where the line between dying man and stone image dissolves; it prepares for his repeated question, 'Do I live, am I dead?'

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. mortcloth, n.; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  23. Saint Praxed at his sermon on the mount l. 95

    Earlier he had ordered a frieze with 'The Saviour at his sermon on the mount, / Saint Praxed in a glory'. Now, as his thoughts wander with 'a certain humming in my ears', he merges the two: Saint Praxed, a woman, becomes 'his' and is placed on the Mount. Critics read the slip as a sign of approaching death and delirium, and also as an ironic revelation that the patron saint whose church he occupies is merely a name to him. Adam Roberts notes the confusion of gender with Christ.

    Adam Roberts, 'Robert Browning, "The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church. Rome 15--" (1845)' (2024 essay; Roberts edited Browning for Oxford, 1997); Philip V. Allingham, 'The Bishop Orders His Tomb', The Victorian Web (2004), citing John Pettigrew, ed., Robert Browning: The Poems (Yale, 1981); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  24. Evil and brief hath been my pilgrimage l. 101

    In Genesis 47:9 the aged Jacob tells Pharaoh, 'The days of the years of my pilgrimage are an hundred and thirty years: few and evil have the days of the years of my life been'. The Bishop borrows the patriarch's humility for a moment, only to plunge back into threats: 'All lapis, all, sons! Else I give the Pope / My villas!' As with the earlier echoes of Ecclesiastes and Job, Scripture works in the poem as an automatic reflex, never as a guide to conduct.

    Bible, King James Version, Genesis 47:9; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes

  25. a visor and a Term ... a lynx l. 108

    In classical decoration a visor (or vizard) is a mask, often the theatrical mask associated with Bacchus. A Term, or terminal figure, is a human bust that merges into a square tapering pedestal, named after Terminus, the Roman god of boundary stones. The lynx (or panther) was one of the beasts that drew Bacchus's chariot, and the Bishop wants one tied to the tripod, struggling so that it 'throws the thyrsus down'. These ornaments, added to grapes in the vase, make his 'impoverished frieze' ever more pagan and Bacchic.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. term, n.; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds, Robert Browning: The Poems, vol. 1 (Yale University Press / Penguin, 1981), notes
