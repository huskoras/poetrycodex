The Mind

Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845)

The Mind

My first thought was, he lied in every word,
That hoary cripple, with malicious eye
Askance to watch the working of his lie
On mine, and mouth scarce able to afford
Suppression of the glee, that pursed and scored
Its edge, at one more victim gained thereby.

What else should he be set for, with his staff?
What, save to waylay with his lies, ensnare
All travellers who might find him posted there,
And ask the road? I guessed what skull-like laugh
Would break, what crutch 'gin write my epitaph
For pastime in the dusty thoroughfare,

If at his counsel I should turn aside
Into that ominous tract which, all agree,
Hides the Dark Tower. Yet acquiescingly
I did turn as he pointed: neither pride
Nor hope rekindling at the end descried,
So much as gladness that some end might be.

For, what with my whole world-wide wandering.
What with my search drawn out through years, my hope
Dwindled into a ghost not fit to cope
With that obstreperous joy success would bring,--
I hardly tried now to rebuke the spring
My heart made, finding failure in its scope.

As when a sick man very near to death
Seems dead indeed, and feels begin and end
The tears, and takes the farewell of each friend,
And hears one bid the other go, draw breath
Freelier outside, ("since all is o'er," he saith,
"And the blow fallen no grieving can amend;")

While some discuss if near the other graves
Be room enough for this, and when a day
Suits best for carrying the corpse away,
With care about the banners, scarves and staves:
And still the man hears all, and only craves
He may not shame such tender love and stay.

Thus, I had so long suffered in this quest,
Heard failure prophesied so oft, been writ
So many times among "The Band"--to wit,
The knights who to the Dark Tower's search addressed
Their steps--that just to fail as they, seemed best,
And all the doubt was now--should I be fit?

So, quiet as despair, I turned from him,
That hateful cripple, out of his highway
Into the path he pointed. All the day
Had been a dreary one at best, and dim
Was settling to its close, yet shot one grim
Red leer to see the plain catch its estray.

For mark! no sooner was I fairly found
Pledged to the plain, after a pace or two,
Than, pausing to throw backward a last view
O'er the safe road, 'twas gone; gray plain all round:
Nothing but plain to the horizon's bound.
I might go on; naught else remained to do.

So, on I went. I think I never saw
Such starved ignoble nature; nothing throve:
For flowers--as well expect a cedar grove!
But cockle, spurge, according to their law
Might propagate their kind, with none to awe,
You'd think: a burr had been a treasure trove.

No! penury, inertness and grimace,
In some strange sort, were the land's portion. "See
Or shut your eyes," said Nature peevishly,
"It nothing skills: I cannot help my case:
'Tis the Last Judgment's fire must cure this place,
Calcine its clods and set my prisoners free."

If there pushed any ragged thistle-stalk
Above its mates, the head was chopped; the bents
Were jealous else. What made those holes and rents
In the dock's harsh swarth leaves, bruised as to balk
All hope of greenness? 'tis a brute must walk
Pashing their life out, with a brute's intents.

As for the grass, it grew as scant as hair
In leprosy; thin dry blades pricked the mud
Which underneath looked kneaded up with blood.
One stiff blind horse, his every bone a-stare,
Stood stupefied, however he came there:
Thrust out past service from the devil's stud!

Alive? he might be dead for aught I know,
With that red gaunt and colloped neck a-strain,
And shut eyes underneath the rusty mane;
Seldom went such grotesqueness with such woe;.
I never saw a brute I hated so;
He must be wicked to deserve such pain.

I shut my eyes and turned them on my heart.
As a man calls for wine before he fights,
I asked one draught of earlier, happier sights,
Ere fitly I could hope to play my part.
Think first, fight afterwards--the soldier's art:
One taste of the old time sets all to rights.

Not it! I fancied Cuthbert's reddening face
Beneath its garniture of curly gold,
Dear fellow, till I almost felt him fold
An arm in mine to fix me to the place,
That way he used. Alas, one night's disgrace!.
Out went my heart's new fire and left it cold.

Giles then, the soul of honor--there he stands
Frank as ten years ago when knighted first.
What honest man should dare (he said) lie durst.
Good--but the scene shifts--faugh! what hangman hands
Pin to his breast a parchment? His own bands
Read it. Poor traitor, spit upon and curst!

Better this present than a past like that;
Back therefore to my darkening path again!
No sound, no sight as far as eye could strain.
Will the night send a howlet or a bat?
I asked: when something on the dismal flat
Came to arrest my thoughts and change their train.

A sudden little river crossed my path
As unexpected as a serpent comes.
No sluggish tide congenial to the glooms;
This, as it frothed by, might have been a bath
For the fiend's glowing hoof--to see the wrath
Of its black eddy bespate with flakes and spumes.

So petty yet so spiteful! All along,
Low scrubby alders kneeled down over it;
Drenched willows flung them headlong in a fit
Of mute despair, a suicidal throng:
The river which had done them all the wrong,
Whate'er that was, rolled by, deterred no whit.

Which, while I forded,--good saints, how I feared
To set my foot upon a dead man's cheek,
Each step, or feel the spear I thrust to seek
For hollows, tangled in his hair or beard!
--It may have been a water-rat I speared,
But, ugh! it sounded like a baby's shriek.

Glad was I when I reached the other bank.
Now for a better country. Vain presage!
Who were the strugglers, what war did they wage,
Whose savage trample thus could pad the dank
Soil to a plash? Toads in a poisoned tank,
Or wild cats in a red-hot iron cage--

The fight must so have seemed in that fell cirque.
What penned them there, with all the plain to choose?
No footprint leading to that horrid mews,
None out of it. Mad brewage set to work
Their brains, no doubt, like galley-slaves the Turk
Pits for his pastime, Christians against Jews.

And more than that--a furlong on--why, there!
What bad use was that engine for, that wheel,
Or brake, not wheel--that harrow fit to reel
Men's bodies out like silk? with all the air
Of Tophet's tool, on earth left unaware,
Or brought to sharpen its rusty teeth of steel.

Then came a bit of stubbed ground, once a wood,
Next a marsh, it would seem, and now mere earth
Desperate and done with: (so a fool finds mirth,
Makes a thing and then mars it, till his mood
Changes and off he goes!) within a rood--
Bog, clay and rubble, sand and stark black dearth.

Now blotches rankling, colored gay and grim,
Now patches where some leanness of the soil's
Broke into moss or substances like boils;
Then came some palsied oak, a cleft in him
Like a distorted mouth that splits its rim
Gaping at death, and dies while it recoils.

And just as far as ever from the end!
Naught in the distance but the evening, naught
To point my footstep further! At the thought,
A great black bird, Apollyon's bosom-friend,
Sailed past, nor beat his wide wing dragon-penned
That brushed my cap--perchance the guide I sought.

For, looking up, aware I somehow grew,
'Spite of the dusk, the plain had given place
All round to mountains--with such name to grace
Mere ugly heights and heaps now stolen in view.
How thus they had surprised me,--solve it, you!
How to get from them was no clearer case.

Yet half I seemed to recognize some trick
Of mischief happened to me, God knows when--
In a bad dream perhaps. Here ended, then,
Progress this way. When, in the very nick
Of giving up, one time more, came a click
As when a trap shuts--you're inside the den!

Burningly it came on me all at once,
This was the place! those two hills on the right,
Crouched like two bulls locked horn in horn in fight;
While to the left, a tall scalped mountain ... Dunce,
Dotard, a-dozing at the very nonce,
After a life spent training for the sight!

What in the midst lay but the Tower itself?
The round squat turret, blind as the fool's heart,
Built of brown stone, without a counterpart
In the whole world. The tempest's mocking elf
Points to the shipman thus the unseen shelf
He strikes on, only when the timbers start.

Not see? because of night perhaps?--why, day
Came back again for that! before it left,
The dying sunset kindled through a cleft:
The hills, like giants at a hunting, lay,
Chin upon hand, to see the game at bay,--
"Now stab and end the creature--to the heft!"

Not hear? when noise was everywhere! it tolled
Increasing like a bell. Names in my ears,
Of all the lost adventurers my peers,--
How such a one was strong, and such was bold,
And such was fortunate, yet each of old
Lost, lost! one moment knelled the woe of years.

There they stood, ranged along the hillsides, met
To view the last of me, a living frame
For one more picture! in a sheet of flame
I saw them and I knew them all. And yet
Dauntless the slug-horn to my lips I set,
And blew. "Childe Roland to the Dark Tower came."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and the opening st. 1, l. 1

    In King Lear Edgar, disguised as the madman Poor Tom, sings a scrap of ballad: 'Child Rowland to the dark tower came'. Browning took this line, which has no known story behind it, and invented the quest leading to it. He wrote the poem on 2 January 1852 in Paris, having resolved to write a poem every day; according to DeVane and later accounts, he said it came upon him 'as a kind of dream'. It was published in Men and Women (1855). In his later collected editions Browning regrouped his shorter poems, and this one came to sit among the 'Dramatic Romances', which may explain the archive's label; it was not in the 1845 Dramatic Romances and Lyrics. The opening 'My first thought' implies thoughts after it: the knight is recalling the journey.

    Shakespeare, King Lear 3.4 (Edgar as Poor Tom: 'Child Rowland to the dark tower came'); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'; 'Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came', Wikipedia (composition date, Browning's comments, critical readings)

  2. the hoary cripple st. 1, l. 2

    'Hoary' means grey or white with age. The cripple is a familiar romance figure, the wayside guide, made sinister. Roland reads malice into his eye and his half-suppressed glee, yet does what he says. The paradox sets up the poem's psychology: the quester expects deceit and failure and goes on anyway. Since everything is filtered through Roland's suspicion, some critics ask whether the cripple really lies at all, since the path does lead to the Tower. The 'staff' and 'crutch' that 'gin write my epitaph' (st. 2) turn the guide into a figure of death.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  3. Stanza and metre st. 1, l. 5

    Each stanza rhymes abbaab (word / eye / lie / afford / scored / thereby). The first rhyme sound returns twice, enclosing the middle, so each stanza circles back on itself as the knight's journey does. Browning often runs sentences across stanza breaks (sts. 2-3, 5-6, 20-21), so the form pulls one way and the syntax another, a restless forward movement inside closed units. The rhymes are often harsh or grotesque (stanza 25's 'mirth' / 'earth' / 'dearth'), matching the landscape. Thirty-four stanzas make 204 lines.

    Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  4. the dying man's overheard funeral st. 5, l. 25

    The two-stanza simile is bitterly comic. The dying man hears his friends discuss graves, banners and the best day for 'carrying the corpse away', and his only wish is not to embarrass them by surviving. Roland's quest has become something like that: he has heard failure foretold so often that failure seems the fitting end. The simile turns the heroic quest into an image of shame and social expectation. Bloom reads such moments as signs of Roland's self-destructive wish; others see a stoic acceptance of death.

    Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  5. 'The Band' st. 7, l. 39

    The quotation marks make 'The Band' sound like a formal title, a roll-call of failed questers. Roland's ambition has turned around: 'just to fail as they, seemed best'. The idea of a company of knights questing for a secret place recalls Arthurian Grail romance. In the last stanzas these lost knights, 'my peers', reappear ranged along the hills to watch his end (st. 34). Several critics, notably Bloom, read the Band as poetic precursors, earlier poets whose failures the latecomer both honours and fears.

    Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  6. estray st. 8, l. 48

    'Estray' comes from law: a valuable tame animal found wandering, which could be claimed by the lord of the manor. Browning makes Roland himself the 'estray' and the plain its new owner. The sunset is personified as giving 'one grim / Red leer' of satisfaction. The legal word makes the capture sound official and impersonal. The next stanza makes the trap literal: as soon as Roland is 'pledged to the plain', the road behind him vanishes.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'estray, n.'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  7. cockle, spurge st. 10, l. 58

    Corn-cockle grows among wheat and its seeds are poisonous. In the KJV Job swears, 'let thistles grow instead of wheat, and cockle instead of barley' (Job 31:40). Spurge (Euphorbia) has an irritant, milky juice. The land is 'starved' and 'ignoble'; flowers are as unlikely as a cedar grove, and a burr would be 'treasure trove'. Browning's grotesque nature is a deliberate anti-pastoral. Bloom, following DeVane, connects it with Gerard de Lairesse's Art of Painting, which Browning read as a boy and which has a chapter on 'things deformed and broken'.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 31:40; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cockle, n.1'; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971)

  8. 'It nothing skills' and the Last Judgment's fire st. 11, l. 64

    'Skill' as an impersonal verb ('it skills not') meant 'it matters' or 'it makes a difference'; The older impersonal use survives in Browning's day mainly in verse. Here a petulant Nature admits she cannot help her case. Only the 'Last Judgment's fire' can 'calcine its clods', that is, burn them to powder or quicklime, as in chemistry, and 'set my prisoners free'. The image draws on 2 Peter 3:10, where 'the elements shall melt with fervent heat'. A suffering creation waiting for release also recalls Romans 8:22.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'skill, v.1'; The Bible (KJV), 2 Peter 3:10; The Bible (KJV), Romans 8:22

  9. bents ... Pashing st. 12, l. 72

    Bent grass is the wiry grass of heaths and dunes. Browning imagines the thistle's head 'chopped' because the bents 'were jealous', so even the weeds act out envy. The dock's 'swarth' (dark) leaves are torn and 'bruised'. 'Pash' is an old word, probably imitative, meaning to strike violently or smash; Shakespeare's Ajax threatens to 'pash' Achilles o'er the face (Troilus and Cressida 2.3). Roland infers 'a brute' with 'a brute's intents', inventing malice where he sees only damage. This habit is typical of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pash, v.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bent, n.1'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  10. the stiff blind horse st. 13, l. 76

    The horse is said to derive from a figure in a tapestry that hung in Browning's Florence home (now at Balliol College, Oxford). 'Colloped' neck means thick with folds of flesh: 'collops' are slices of meat, and Job 15:27 speaks of the wicked man who 'maketh collops of fat on his flanks'. 'Stud' is a stable of horses kept for breeding. The striking moral turn comes at the close of st. 14: 'He must be wicked to deserve such pain.' Roland reverses cause and effect, a reflex that critics read as a sign of his warped perception or of a cruel universe that makes suffering look like guilt.

    Eton College Library catalogue, MS 682, photograph of the tapestry that partly inspired 'Childe Roland'; The Bible (KJV), Job 15:27; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  11. Cuthbert st. 16, l. 91

    Roland, like a soldier calling for wine before battle, tries to draw courage from 'earlier, happier sights'. The first, golden-haired Cuthbert linking arms with him, collapses at once with 'one night's disgrace', never explained. The name belongs to St Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, the Northumbrian saint, which sharpens the irony of the shame. Browning leaves the disgrace untold. The unexplained hints are part of what makes the poem dreamlike and open to interpretation.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  12. Giles and the traitor's parchment st. 17, l. 97

    Giles' motto is reported in a deliberately tangled line: 'What honest man should dare (he said) he durst.' He would dare anything an honest man should dare. Then the 'scene shifts' to his public disgrace: hangman's hands pin a 'parchment' (a written charge) to his breast. Condemned men were sometimes paraded with their crime written on a placard. 'His own bands' (his own company) read it. Both memories show honour turning to shame. Roland concludes, 'Better this present than a past like that.'

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  13. the sudden little river st. 19, l. 109

    The river appears 'as unexpected as a serpent comes', a hint of Eden's tempter. It is 'petty yet so spiteful', with alders kneeling and willows flinging themselves in 'mute despair, a suicidal throng'. The landscape is again moralised as cruelty and suffering. 'Bespate' means spattered. Crossing a river is a traditional threshold in quest and underworld journeys (compare Styx and Acheron). The water-rat with 'a baby's shriek' is the poem's most shocking moment of uncertainty: Roland cannot tell what he has killed.

    Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  14. fell cirque ... horrid mews st. 23, l. 133

    'Fell' means fierce or deadly. 'Cirque' (from Latin circus) is an arena, and 'mews' were the cages where hawks were kept while moulting (Old French mue, from muer, 'to moult'). Roland pictures 'toads in a poisoned tank' or 'wild cats in a red-hot iron cage'. The simile then turns to Turkish galleys, where slaves were supposedly set to fight 'Christians against Jews' for the master's sport. The scene has no footprints in or out, so the violence is sealed off from any cause, which matches the dream logic of the poem.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cirque, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mew, n.2'; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  15. Tophet's tool st. 24, l. 143

    In the Old Testament, Tophet in the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem was where children were 'burned in the fire' as sacrifices (Jeremiah 7:31; 2 Kings 23:10). It became a byword for hell, as in Milton's Paradise Lost 1.404-5. Roland sees an 'engine', a wheel, a 'brake' (an instrument of torture), a 'harrow fit to reel / Men's bodies out like silk'. He thinks it might be a hellish tool left on earth, or brought up 'to sharpen its rusty teeth of steel'. The image recalls the medieval 'harrowing of hell' and instruments of martyrdom.

    The Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 7:31; The Bible (KJV), 2 Kings 23:10; John Milton, Paradise Lost 1.404-5

  16. rood st. 25, l. 149

    'Rood' as a measure meant a rod or perch of land, and also a quarter of an acre; the same word means the Cross ('Holy Rood'). The point is the speed of the degradation: within a few yards the land runs through every kind of waste, ending in 'stark black dearth'. The parenthesis before it compares nature to a fool who makes a thing and then mars it until his mood changes, an image of a capricious creator. Critics have related this to Browning's later grotesque theologies, such as 'Caliban upon Setebos'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rood, n.'; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  17. Apollyon's bosom-friend st. 27, l. 160

    Revelation 9:11 names the king of the bottomless pit 'in the Hebrew tongue Abaddon, but in the Greek tongue... Apollyon', meaning Destroyer. In Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress (1678) Christian fights Apollyon in the Valley of Humiliation: a monster with scales like a fish and 'wings like a dragon'. Hence the bird's 'wide wing dragon-penned' (with dragon's feathers or pinions). Roland's quest has often been compared to Christian's pilgrimage, though here the landscape gives no promise of the Celestial City. The bird's brushing pass may be 'the guide I sought'.

    The Bible (KJV), Revelation 9:11; John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), Christian's fight with Apollyon; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'

  18. 'came a click / As when a trap shuts' st. 29, l. 173

    The mountains have 'stolen in view', and Roland half-recognises the trick from 'a bad dream perhaps'. The 'click' is a striking modern, mechanical image in a romance landscape. The trap shutting turns the long search into sudden arrival, and arrival into capture. 'In the very nick / Of giving up' means at the exact critical moment. Compare 'Estray' (st. 8), where the plain caught its stray, and the road behind that vanished.

    Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971)

  19. Dunce, / Dotard st. 30, l. 178

    Humanists and reformers mocked the followers of the scholastic theologian John Duns Scotus as 'Dunsmen' or 'dunses', and the word came to mean a dull-witted person. 'Dotard' is one in his dotage; 'at the very nonce' means at the very moment. The self-reproach breaks off mid-line as the hills are described ('a tall scalped mountain ... Dunce'), so the syntax registers the shock of recognition. 'Burningly' opening the stanza makes the realisation feel like fire.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dunce, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nonce, n.'

  20. the Tower itself st. 31, l. 182

    After the epic build-up the Tower is anticlimactic: round, squat, brown stone, 'without a counterpart / In the whole world'. 'Blind as the fool's heart' recalls Psalm 14:1, 'The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God'. The simile of the 'tempest's mocking elf' who points the sailor to the 'unseen shelf' (a sandbank or reef) only as 'the timbers start' (spring apart) makes recognition and ruin happen at once. Critics have related the Tower to death, to the poet's self, or to the unknowable object of any quest; Browning refused to explain.

    The Bible (KJV), Psalm 14:1; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'shelf, n.2'

  21. the hills like giants at a hunting st. 32, l. 190

    Roland now becomes the hunted 'creature', the animal brought to bay. The giants' cry 'Now stab and end the creature—to the heft!' (the heft is the handle or hilt of a knife) puts the hunting ritual of the kill into a cosmic frame. The dying sunset returns to light the scene, so nothing is hidden: 'Not see? ... Not hear?' The questions answer an imagined objection, as if the reader suggested that Roland might have missed the Tower in the dark. The landscape's watching faces prepare for the assembled Band.

    Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E (The Victorian Age), notes to 'Childe Roland'; Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971)

  22. the lost adventurers, 'a living frame' st. 33, l. 195

    Sound swells 'like a bell', a death knell made of names. The lost knights were strong, bold or fortunate, 'yet each of old / Lost, lost!' Then they appear 'ranged along the hillsides', a 'living frame / For one more picture'. The language of painting (frame, picture, sheet of flame) makes Roland's last act a scene to be watched. Bloom reads the peers as precursor poets; others as the communion of failed heroes whom Roland joins in a final act of courage. The flame recalls the Last Judgment's fire of stanza 11.

    Harold Bloom, 'Browning's Childe Roland: All Things Deformed and Broken', in The Ringers in the Tower (U of Chicago P, 1971); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd ed. (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Childe Roland'

  23. slug-horn st. 34, l. 203

    'Slughorn' was an old Scots spelling of 'slogan', from Gaelic sluagh-ghairm, the battle-cry of a host. Thomas Chatterton, in his pseudo-medieval Rowley poems (Battle of Hastings), used 'slughorne' as if it were a trumpet, and the OED records this as an erroneous sense. Browning takes it over, so Roland 'blows' a horn. The word thus combines two meanings: a horn, as Roland sounds in the Chanson de Roland, and, by origin, a defiant war-cry. That is just what he utters: the poem's own title.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'slughorn, n.'; Thomas Chatterton, 'The Battle of Hastings' (Rowley poems, 1777); 'Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came', Wikipedia (composition date, Browning's comments, critical readings)

  24. Childe Roland st. 34, l. 204

    'Childe' (as in Byron's Childe Harold) marks a candidate for knighthood. Roland is the nephew of Charlemagne in the Chanson de Roland, who at Roncesvalles refuses to blow his horn until too late, then blows it so hard that he bursts his temples. Edgar's song in Lear connects the name with the 'dark tower' of a lost ballad, which some have linked to a Scottish tale of Childe Rowland rescuing his sister from Elfland (retold by Joseph Jacobs in English Fairy Tales, 1890). Browning's ending makes the title also the poem's last words: the knight names himself in the third person, as if signing the story.

    Shakespeare, King Lear 3.4 (Edgar as Poor Tom: 'Child Rowland to the dark tower came'); 'Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came', Wikipedia (composition date, Browning's comments, critical readings); Joseph Jacobs, English Fairy Tales (1890), 'Childe Rowland'

  25. What does the ending mean? st. 34, l. 203

    Browning said the poem came upon him 'as a kind of dream' and refused to supply an allegory. W. L. Phelps listed three readings: success through failure, the futility of the quest, and the Tower as damnation. DeVane traced the scenery to Lairesse's Art of Painting and to Browning's reading. Harold Bloom calls the poem a 'loving critique' of Shelley and reads Roland as questing for his own failure. Others stress the courage of the gesture: 'Dauntless' he sounds the horn knowing the end. The poem breaks off before any combat, so it ends in the act of announcing, and readers must decide whether that counts as victory.
