Arts & Sciences

A Toccata of Galuppi's

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Lyrics (1842)

Arts & Sciences

Oh Galuppi, Baldassare, this is very sad to find!
I can hardly misconceive you; it would prove me deaf and blind;
But although I take your meaning, 't is with such a heavy mind!

Here you come with your old music, and here 's all the good it brings.
What, they lived once thus at Venice where the merchants were the
kings,
Where St. Mark's is, where the Doges used to wed the sea with rings?

Ay, because the sea's the street there; and 't is arched by ... what
you call
... Shylock's bridge with houses on it, where they kept the carnival:
I was never out of England--it 's as if I saw it all.

Did young people take their pleasure when the sea was warm in May?
Balls and masks begun at midnight, burning ever to mid-day,
When they made up fresh adventures for the morrow, do you say?

Was a lady such a lady, cheeks so round and lips so red,--
On her neck the small face buoyant, like a bell-flower on its bed,
O'er the breast's superb abundance where a man might base his head?

Well, and it was graceful of them--they 'd break talk off and afford
--She, to bite her mask's black velvet--he, to finger on his sword,
While you sat and played Toccatas, stately at the clavichord?

What? Those lesser thirds so plaintive, sixths diminished, sigh on
sigh,
Told them something? Those suspensions, those solutions--"Must we
die?"
Those commiserating sevenths--"Life might last! we can but try!"

"Were you happy?"--"Yes."--"And are you still as happy?"--"Yes. And
you?"
--"Then, more kisses!"--"Did I stop them, when a million seemed so
few?"
Hark, the dominant's persistence till it must be answered to!

So, an octave struck the answer. Oh, they praised you, I dare say!
"Brave Galuppi! that was music! good alike at grave and gay!
I can always leave off talking when I hear a master play!"

Then they left you for their pleasure: till in due time, one by one,
Some with lives that came to nothing, some with deeds as well undone,
Death stepped tacitly and took them where they never see the sun.

But when I sit down to reason, think to take my stand nor swerve,
While I triumph o'er a secret wrung from nature's close reserve,
In you come with your cold music till I creep through every nerve.

Yes, you, like a ghostly cricket, creaking where a house was burned:
"Dust and ashes, dead and done with, Venice spent what Venice earned.
The soul, doubtless, is immortal--where a soul can be discerned.

"Yours for instance: you know physics, something of geology,
Mathematics are your pastime; souls shall rise in their degree;
Butterflies may dread extinction,--you 'll not die, it cannot be!

"As for Venice and her people, merely born to bloom and drop,
Here on earth they bore their fruitage, mirth and folly were the crop:
What of soul was left, I wonder, when the kissing had to stop?

"Dust and ashes!" So you creak it, and I want the heart to scold.
Dear dead women, with such hair, too--what's become of all the gold
Used to hang and brush their bosoms? I feel chilly and grown old.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and speaker st. 1

    The archive's 'Dramatic Lyrics (1842)' label is mistaken. The poem first appeared in Men and Women (1855), vol. 1, and was probably written between 1847 and 1853, while the Brownings lived in Italy. A toccata (Italian toccare, 'to touch') is a keyboard piece meant to display the player's touch. Baldassare Galuppi (1706–85) was a Venetian composer, known for comic operas and as maestro di cappella of St Mark's. The speaker is a nineteenth-century English amateur of science. He addresses Galuppi as if the music were the composer's voice and finds its 'meaning' hard to bear ('very sad to find'). The monologue blends social satire of eighteenth-century Venice with the speaker's own unease.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); 'A Toccata of Galuppi's', Wikipedia (on the music and Galuppi); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  2. Which piece? st. 1

    In 1887 Browning noted that he owned two manuscript volumes of Galuppi's toccata-pieces, so he knew the music directly. Scholars have not been able to match the poem's harmonic details to any surviving piece. In Galuppi's time 'toccata' and 'sonata' were used loosely for keyboard works, and several sonatas have been proposed. The general view is that Browning combined typical features of the style. A minority of musicologists have tried to show that the poem's own structure imitates a musical form.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); 'A Toccata of Galuppi's', Wikipedia (on the music and Galuppi)

  3. Metre and stanza st. 1–15

    Each three-line stanza holds a single rhyme (find / blind / mind; brings / kings / rings). The lines are trochaic octameter, usually catalectic (the last unstressed syllable dropped), so each ends firmly on a stress. The archive breaks a few long lines in two to fit the page. The insistent, regular beat and the rhyme repeated three times give a mechanical, pattering motion, often compared to a toccata's busy keyboard figures. It also lets Browning slide easily between dialogue, description and reflection.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  4. Wedding the sea st. 2

    In the Sposalizio del Mare, celebrated on Ascension Day, the Doge sailed out in the state barge, the Bucintoro, and dropped a gold ring into the Adriatic, declaring the city's marriage to the sea. 'Where the merchants were the kings' alludes to Venice as a mercantile republic ruled by an oligarchy of merchant families. The Republic ended in 1797, about a decade after Galuppi's death, which gives the speaker's picture of a pleasure-loving city a sense of approaching doom.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  5. Shylock's bridge st. 3

    The speaker cannot recall the name of the bridge ('what you call') and identifies it through Shakespeare, as an untravelled Englishman would. The Rialto bridge over the Grand Canal carries rows of shops, hence 'with houses on it'. In The Merchant of Venice Shylock speaks of being berated 'In the Rialto' (1.3), meaning the exchange district. The line 'I was never out of England' that follows makes the joke explicit. The whole Venice of the poem is put together from literature and music.

    Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice 1.3 (Folger Shakespeare); The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  6. Imagined Venice st. 3

    The admission ironically undercuts the speaker's authority. The scenes that follow, balls, masks, flirtation, are what he imagines the music 'says', not historical knowledge. The poem thus examines how music calls up meaning. It is a drama of the listener's projection as much as of the composer's art. Browning, who lived in Italy, writes ironically about a provincial Englishman, but also with sympathy, since the speaker's response to the music is real.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  7. Carnival st. 4

    Eighteenth-century Venice was known across Europe for its long carnival season, in which masks allowed anonymity and freedom across classes. The festivities lasting from 'midnight' to 'mid-day', and the 'fresh adventures for the morrow', compress that reputation for pleasure and intrigue. The lady 'bite[s] her mask's black velvet' in stanza 6, a detail of the moretta or black velvet mask. The speaker's questions ('do you say?') show him reading all this into the music.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  8. The lady st. 5

    The fifth stanza makes the music into a woman's body. The round cheeks and red lips, the small face bobbing like a campanula on its stem, and 'the breast's superb abundance where a man might base his head' are sensuous and slightly comic in their frankness. 'Base' here means rest or lay down. In a poem full of musical terms the word may also hint at the musical bass, though this is only a possibility. The portrait prepares the ending, where 'Dear dead women' with golden hair become the poem's central image of mortality.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  9. clavichord st. 6

    The clavichord was a small domestic keyboard instrument, very soft in tone, in which metal tangents strike the strings. It suits the poem's intimate scene, though Galuppi's public music would more often have been played on the harpsichord. The picture is ironic. The composer sits 'stately' while the lovers break off talk only for a moment, she to bite her mask, he to finger his sword (a hint of duels and intrigue). The music is a background to pleasure.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clavichord, n.'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  10. Lesser thirds, diminished sixths, suspensions, solutions st. 7

    The speaker now 'reads' the music's harmony as speech. 'Lesser thirds' are minor thirds, traditionally heard as plaintive. 'Sixths diminished' are dissonant intervals that sound like a sigh. 'Suspensions' are notes held over from one chord into the next, which produce tension, and 'solutions' are their resolutions (an older term). 'Commiserating sevenths' are seventh chords, dissonances that call for resolution. Each device is given a voice: 'Must we die?', 'Life might last! we can but try!' Browning was musically trained, and the technical language is accurate, but the 'translations' are the speaker's own.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); 'A Toccata of Galuppi's', Wikipedia (on the music and Galuppi)

  11. The dominant's persistence st. 8–9

    In tonal harmony the dominant (the fifth degree of the scale), especially as a dominant seventh, creates the strongest expectation of return to the tonic, the home key. The 'persistence' that 'must be answered to' is a sustained or repeated dominant. The answer, 'an octave struck', is the final resolution. In stanza 8 the musical tension is turned into the lovers' dialogue of kisses ('a million seemed so few'), and the cadence falls like a conversation's end. The music's resolution is also an image of death, which is the answer to every life.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  12. tacitly st. 10

    'Tacitly' means silently, unspoken, from Latin tacere ('to be silent'). The word is unexpectedly Latinate in a line of monosyllables. It may also suggest the musical direction 'tacet' ('it is silent'), by which a part falls silent, the music stopping as the lives end. The stanza's summary of the Venetians, 'Some with lives that came to nothing, some with deeds as well undone', is moral judgement given almost casually. The phrase 'where they never see the sun' recalls classical and biblical pictures of the dark underworld.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tacitly, adv.'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  13. The speaker's science st. 11, 13

    When the speaker sits down 'to reason' and triumphs over 'a secret wrung from nature's close reserve', he speaks for nineteenth-century scientific confidence. The music's ghostly voice later mocks his learning: 'you know physics, something of geology, / Mathematics are your pastime'. The irony is aimed at a faith in intellect as a guarantee of the soul's worth. Geology in particular (Lyell's Principles of Geology, 1830–33) had unsettled traditional ideas of time and creation in the decades before the poem.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  14. Ghostly cricket st. 12

    The simile 'like a ghostly cricket, creaking where a house was burned' gives the harpsichord-like tinkle of the old music an eerie quality. It is a small dry sound surviving among ruins. Folklore treated the cricket on the hearth as a domestic spirit (as in Dickens's The Cricket on the Hearth, 1845), and here the hearth is gone. The image suits the poem's view of eighteenth-century Venice as a civilisation burned out and remembered only through its music.

    The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  15. Dust and ashes st. 12, 15

    Abraham calls himself 'but dust and ashes' (Genesis 18:27), and Job says 'I am become like dust and ashes' (Job 30:19) and repents 'in dust and ashes' (42:6). In the poem the phrase is put into the music's mouth as a sardonic verdict: 'Venice spent what Venice earned'. It returns at the start of the last stanza as 'So you creak it'. The repetition gives the moralising its shape, in which a pleasure-loving society reaps the consequences. The speaker cannot answer it ('I want the heart to scold', meaning he lacks the heart).

    The Bible (KJV), Genesis 18:27; The Bible (KJV), Job 30:19; The Bible (KJV), Job 42:6

  16. The soul, doubtless, is immortal st. 12

    This is one of the poem's main cruxes. The ghostly voice grants immortality only to those who have souls worth the name. Read straight, the scientist's serious mind will survive ('you'll not die, it cannot be!'), while the Venetians, 'merely born to bloom and drop', had nothing left 'when the kissing had to stop'. Most critics take the lines ironically. The music mocks the speaker's assumption that intellect earns immortality, and his final chill shows that he has felt the mockery. The poem never settles whether Browning endorses any part of the judgement.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)

  17. Butterflies and extinction st. 13

    In Greek psyche means both 'soul' and 'butterfly', and the butterfly emerging from its chrysalis was a traditional emblem of the soul's resurrection. Here the butterflies stand for the frivolous Venetians, who 'may dread extinction'. The word has a pointed modern meaning, since pre-Darwinian geology had made the extinction of species a public topic (Chambers's Vestiges, 1844). The speaker is mockingly assured that he, unlike the butterflies, cannot die. The irony works because his science is the very thing that made extinction thinkable.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'extinction, n.'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'

  18. Dear dead women st. 15

    The last stanza moves from the music's moralising to a wistful, physical image. The hair that 'Used to hang and brush their bosoms' recalls the lady of stanza 5. Critics have read the ending in several ways. In one reading it is genuine elegy, the speaker mourning the lost beauty he had dismissed. In another it is self-revelation, his complacent superiority dissolving into a sense of his own ageing and mortality. In a third, Browning gives the last word to sensuous life over abstract argument. 'I feel chilly and grown old' brings the 'cold music' of stanza 11 into the speaker's own body.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on 'A Toccata of Galuppi's'; The Victorian Web, "Robert Browning's 'A Toccata of Galuppi's' (1855)" (notes drawing on Pettigrew's edition)
