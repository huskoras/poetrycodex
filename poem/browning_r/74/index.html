Love

Porphyria's Lover

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845)

Love

The rain set early in to-night,
The sullen wind was soon awake,
It tore the elm-tops down for spite,
And did its worst to vex the lake:
I listened with heart fit to break.
When glided in Porphyria; straight
She shut the cold out and the storm,
And kneeled and made the cheerless grate
Blaze up, and all the cottage warm;
Which done, she rose, and from her form
Withdrew the dripping cloak and shawl,
And laid her soiled gloves by, untied
Her hat and let the damp hair fall,
And, last, she sat down by my side
And called me. When no voice replied,
She put my arm about her waist,
And made her smooth white shoulder bare
And all her yellow hair displaced,
And, stooping, made my cheek lie there,
And spread, o'er all, her yellow hair,
Murmuring how she loved me--she
Too weak, for all her heart's endeavor,
To set its struggling passion free
From pride, and vainer ties dissever,
And give herself to me forever.
But passion sometimes would prevail,
Nor could to-night's gay feast restrain
A sudden thought of one so pale
For love of her, and all in vain:
So, she was come through wind and rain.
Be sure I looked up at her eyes
Happy and proud; at last I knew
Porphyria worshipped me; surprise
Made my heart swell, and still it grew
While I debated what to do.
That moment she was mine, mine, fair,
Perfectly pure and good: I found
A thing to do, and all her hair
In one long yellow string I wound
Three times her little throat around,
And strangled her. No pain felt she;
I am quite sure she felt no pain.
As a shut bud that holds a bee,
I warily oped her lids: again
Laughed the blue eyes without a stain.
And I untightened next the tress
About her neck; her cheek once more
Blushed bright beneath my burning kiss:
I propped her head up as before,
Only, this time my shoulder bore
Her head, which droops upon it still:
The smiling rosy little head,
So glad it has its utmost will,
That all it scorned at once is fled,
And I, its love, am gained instead!
Porphyria's love: she guessed not how
Her darling one wish would be heard.
And thus we sit together now,
And all night long we have not stirred,
And yet God has not said a word!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and 'Madhouse Cells' l. 1

    This is Browning's earliest short dramatic monologue and his first study of a disordered mind. It appeared, signed 'Z', in W. J. Fox's Monthly Repository in January 1836. In Dramatic Lyrics (1842) Browning placed it beside 'Johannes Agricola in Meditation' under the joint title 'Madhouse Cells', which told readers that the speaker is insane; the present title dates from the 1863 collected Poetical Works, where the label was dropped (the archive's heading cites the later 1845 volume). Removing the frame shifts the burden of judgment onto the reader, who must infer madness from the speaker's calm, orderly account.

    'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; 'Madhouse Cells, Part II' ['Porphyria's Lover'], University of Virginia Anthology of Victorian poetry, text and notes

  2. Metre and stanza ll. 1-60

    The poem falls into twelve five-line units rhymed ababb, but they are run together without breaks and the sentences often overrun the units (for example ll. 10-11, 'from her form / Withdrew the dripping cloak'). The doubled b-rhyme gives each unit a heavy close; critics have compared the effect to a mind circling a fixed idea. The regular four-beat line and the plain narrative syntax contrast grimly with the content, making the murder sound like one domestic action among others ('I found / A thing to do').

    'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Harlan Henthorne Hatcher, The Versification of Robert Browning (1928)

  3. The storm ll. 1-4

    The wind tears down the elm-tops 'for spite' and does 'its worst to vex the lake', attributing malice to nature. Ruskin would name this attribution of human feeling to nature the 'pathetic fallacy' in Modern Painters III (1856). Here it serves the dramatic monologue: we see the weather through the speaker's resentful mood, before he tells us that he sat 'with heart fit to break'. Porphyria brings warmth and light into this setting ('Blaze up, and all the cottage warm'), which makes her the active and the speaker the passive figure until the murder.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; John Ruskin, Modern Painters, vol. III (1856), ch. 12, 'Of the Pathetic Fallacy'

  4. Porphyria l. 6

    Purple (Greek porphyra, the costly dye from the murex shellfish) was the colour of imperial and aristocratic dress; Byzantine princes were 'porphyrogenitus', born in the purple. The name thus marks Porphyria's higher social standing, which the poem implies when it speaks of her 'pride, and vainer ties' and of the 'gay feast' she has left. Some critics have connected the name with the disease porphyria (Popowich 1999), reading the woman as an embodiment of illness and the killing as the speaker's recovery; this is one interpretation among several, and the poem's own text does not demand it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'porphyry, n.'; 'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Barry L. Popowich, 'Porphyria is Madness', Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999), 59-66

  5. glided in l. 6

    The inversion ('glided in Porphyria') delays her name to the end of the clause and gives her entrance a dreamlike quality. She then performs a sequence of practical acts: she shuts out the storm, kneels to light the fire, removes her wet cloak and shawl, unties her hat and lets down her hair. Critics note that the speaker records each act with obsessive precision, and that he does not respond when she calls him (l. 15). The sexual frankness of her undressing and her initiative in embracing him unsettled Victorian conventions of female modesty.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72

  6. soiled gloves l. 12

    On the literal level Porphyria's gloves are muddied from her walk through 'wind and rain'. Because gloves were a mark of a lady's propriety, critics have taken the detail as part of the speaker's covert judgment of her as sullied or as stepping down to him; Ross reads it, with the hair and the 'rosy little head', as part of the poem's sexual subtext. The speaker's later insistence that, at the moment of murder, she was 'Perfectly pure and good' suggests a need to fix her in a purity he otherwise doubts.

    Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72; 'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999))

  7. vainer ties dissever ll. 21-25

    The speaker's account of Porphyria's 'struggling passion' implies a social barrier: her 'pride' and other ties (family, rank, perhaps a suitor) prevent marriage. 'Vainer' means more empty or worthless, as the speaker judges them; 'dissever' is a rather literary word for 'sever' (OED). The syntax of ll. 21-25 ('she / Too weak ... ') is the speaker's interpretation, not her words; the poem never lets us hear Porphyria directly, a point emphasised in feminist readings of the monologue.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dissever, v.'; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  8. one so pale l. 28

    Porphyria has left a 'gay feast' because of 'A sudden thought' of her lover, pale with unrequited love. The lovesick lover's pallor is a commonplace of Petrarchan and Romantic convention. Critics who stress the speaker's passivity have read the line as evidence of illness, invalidism or impotence, explaining his inertia in the first half and his violent seizing of control; the Wikipedia summary of criticism records this line of interpretation, but it remains inference from scant evidence.

    'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72

  9. Porphyria worshipped me ll. 31-35

    The speaker looks up at her eyes and concludes 'at last I knew / Porphyria worshipped me'. The religious verb anticipates the poem's final reference to God and shows the speaker placing himself in the position of a deity. His 'surprise / Made my heart swell' and he 'debated what to do': the murder is presented as a deliberate response to a moment of gratified self-esteem. Langbaum's influential account of the dramatic monologue argues that such poems hold us between sympathy and judgment; we enter the speaker's logic even as we recoil from it.

    Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  10. The preserved moment ll. 36-37

    'That moment she was mine, mine, fair, / Perfectly pure and good' gives the speaker's motive: the wish to arrest time at its point of perfection. The desire to make a moment eternal recurs in Browning ('The Last Ride Together', 'My Last Duchess', where the Duke prefers his wife as a painting). Critics compare the result to a tableau vivant or a work of art: the living woman is turned into a fixed image. The repeated 'mine, mine' exposes the possessive logic beneath the romantic language.

    Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2; 'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  11. A thing to do ll. 37-41

    The banal phrase 'I found / A thing to do' leads into the strangling, delayed across the rhyme: her hair 'In one long yellow string I wound / Three times her little throat around, / And strangled her.' The thrice-wound hair has a ritual or magical air. The plainness of the verb 'strangled', placed at the start of a line after the build-up of rhyme, gives a shock. Using her own hair, earlier spread over him as a sign of intimacy, turns the token of love into a weapon, a detail critics read as fetishistic.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72; 'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999))

  12. Sources: Wilson and Procter l. 39

    Editors note two Romantic-period sources. John Wilson's 'Extracts from Gosschen's Diary', in Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine (1818), is a lurid account of a murder, usually named as a possible source for the poem. Bryan Waller Procter ('Barry Cornwall'), a friend of Browning, acknowledged basing 'Marcian Colonna' (1820) on it, adding the detail of the killer sitting up all night with his victim. Browning's originality lies in giving the murderer his own voice without authorial comment; the influence of Wilson is generally treated as probable rather than proved.

    'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955)

  13. Unreliable narration ll. 41-42

    The speaker states the same claim twice in successive lines, the second time with an emphatic 'quite sure'. Such over-assurance is how the dramatic monologue lets readers see more than the speaker does. Ross observes that the speaker's 'romantic egotism' leads him to assume Porphyria's feelings throughout, even after death, culminating in his belief that her head is 'glad' to have its 'utmost will'.

    Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  14. As a shut bud that holds a bee ll. 43-44

    'Warily' and the image of the bee suggest that the speaker half fears what he will find, a living reproach in her eyes. 'Oped' is an archaic form of 'opened'. The natural image of a closed bud recalls Romantic flower-imagery of virginity and innocence, now attached to a corpse. The next line, 'Laughed the blue eyes without a stain', projects happiness onto the dead eyes; 'without a stain' recalls his wish that she be 'pure', and may also hint, grimly, that the strangling has left no visible mark.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  15. Blushed bright l. 48

    Loosening the tress from her neck allows blood to return to the face, producing colour that the speaker interprets as a response to his kiss. The alliteration on b ('Blushed bright beneath my burning') gives a heightened, quasi-erotic tone to a necrophiliac scene. Here and in 'The smiling rosy little head' (l. 52), Browning lets us see the gap between the physical facts and the speaker's interpretation of them.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72

  16. Her utmost will ll. 51-55

    The tense shifts to the present ('droops upon it still', 'has its utmost will'), revealing that the speaker is narrating while still sitting with the body. He reinterprets the murder as fulfilment of Porphyria's 'darling one wish', to give herself to him forever (l. 25), and imagines that everything she 'scorned' (the social ties) 'is fled'. The reversal of positions, her head now on his shoulder where his was on hers (ll. 19, 49-50), completes his assertion of control.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  17. God has not said a word l. 60

    Critics divide over the final line. On one reading, the speaker takes God's silence as approval, completing his self-justification; on another, the 'yet' betrays anxiety, as if he is waiting for punishment; a third reading sees Browning raising the problem of a God who does not intervene in human evil. The pairing with 'Johannes Agricola', an antinomian who believes himself saved whatever he does, supports a reading that the speaker presumes divine sanction. The night-long vigil echoes Procter's 'Marcian Colonna'.

    'Porphyria's Lover', Wikipedia (citing Karlin 2004; Ross, Explicator 60 (2002); Popowich, Studies in Browning and His Circle 22 (1999)); Catherine Ross, 'Browning's Porphyria's Lover', The Explicator 60.2 (2002), 68-72; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2
