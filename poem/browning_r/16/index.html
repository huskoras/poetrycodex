Love

Meeting at Night

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Lyrics (1842)

Love

The gray sea and the long black land;
And the yellow half-moon large and low;
And the startled little waves that leap
In fiery ringlets from their sleep,
As I gain the cove with pushing prow,
And quench its speed i' the slushy sand.

Then a mile of warm sea-scented beach;
Three fields to cross till a farm appears;
A tap at the pane, the quick sharp scratch
And blue spurt of a lighted match,
And a voice less loud, through its joys and fears,
Than the two hearts beating each to each!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and companion poem ll. 1–12

    The archive's collection label (Dramatic Lyrics, 1842) is not right for this poem. It first appeared in Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845) as the first part of a two-part poem, 'Night and Morning'. In Poems (1849) Browning separated the parts as 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'. The four-line sequel has the lover leave at dawn for 'the need of a world of men'. The pronouns of the sequel make the traveller a man. This matters for interpretation. Read alone, 'Meeting at Night' ends in rapture; read with its pair, the rapture is a single episode in a larger life of work. Some modern critics call it the most sensual poem Browning had written up to that date and link it to his feelings for Elizabeth Barrett.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'; 'Meeting at Night', Wikipedia (publication history); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Parting at Morning'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  2. Form: two sestets without a main verb ll. 1–12

    Each stanza rhymes abccba (land / low / leap / sleep / prow / sand; beach / appears / scratch / match / fears / each), so the stanza closes in on itself as the journey closes in on the meeting. The lines have four stresses with a mix of iambs and anapaests, which gives a rocking, propulsive movement. Grammatically the poem has no finite main verb. It is a list of sensations joined by 'And' and 'Then', with verbs only in subordinate clauses ('As I gain the cove'). The syntax imitates perception in a hurry, impressions arriving one after another before any reflection. The meeting itself is reached only in the last line.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'

  3. Gray, black, yellow ll. 1–2

    Line 1 is heavily stressed ('gray sea', 'long black land'), and so the poem opens slowly and broadly before the quickening of line 3. The palette is sparse. Against it the 'yellow half-moon large and low' (a moon near the horizon looks enlarged) is the first point of warmth. Colour later sharpens into the 'fiery ringlets' of the waves and the 'blue spurt' of the match, so the poem moves from dim expanse to points of light.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  4. Fiery ringlets ll. 3–4

    Small waves disturbed by the boat light up as 'fiery ringlets'. The usual explanation is marine phosphorescence (bioluminescent plankton glowing when disturbed), though the moonlight on curling ripples may be meant as well. The waves are personified: 'startled', woken 'from their sleep', they 'leap'. 'Ringlets' are also curls of hair, and the word looks forward to the woman waiting at the end. The nervous excitement of the waves anticipates the 'joys and fears' of line 11.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ringlet, n.'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  5. Pushing prow, slushy sand ll. 5–6

    'Gain' means reach, arrive at. The boat's 'pushing prow' enters the cove and its speed is quenched 'i' the slushy sand' (the elided 'i'' for 'in' is typical Browning colloquialism). The physical vigour of the lines, with the plosives of 'pushing prow', is plain. Many critics also find an erotic subtext, the journey's thrust and its quenching anticipating the lovers' meeting. Others warn against over-reading and stress the realism of a night landing on a beach. Either way, the first stanza ends with arrival by sea and the second begins the journey on land.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'

  6. Shift of senses l. 7

    The first stanza is mostly visual. In the second, as the speaker comes nearer, the senses become intimate: warmth and scent ('warm sea-scented beach'), then hearing ('A tap', 'scratch', 'a voice'), then the felt heartbeat. The compound 'sea-scented' keeps the sea with him on land. The distances shrink, from 'a mile' of beach to 'Three fields' to a window pane, a kind of zoom that suits the poem's single forward motion.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  7. Tap at the pane l. 9

    The lover does not knock at the door. He taps at the window, which suggests a clandestine meeting and perhaps a prearranged signal. The farmhouse ('till a farm appears') places the woman in a rural, domestic setting. The response is immediate, a match struck inside, so the reader learns she has been waiting. The lines give no names and no explanation, which is typical of Browning's dramatic lyric. The situation is caught in the middle of the action.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  8. Scratch and blue spurt of a match ll. 9–10

    'Quick sharp scratch / And blue spurt of a lighted match' is strongly onomatopoeic. The short vowels and clustered consonants (sk, tch) imitate the strike, and 'spurt' catches the flare's sudden burst of blue before it settles. Friction matches had been on sale in England only since the late 1820s, so the detail is pointedly modern. It brings an unpoetic object into a love lyric. The tiny flame is the climax of the poem's movement from darkness towards light.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'match, n.2'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes

  9. Two hearts beating ll. 11–12

    The final lines compare a 'voice less loud, through its joys and fears' with the 'two hearts beating each to each'. The whispered greeting is fainter than the sound of the lovers' pulses, and the poem ends at the moment of meeting, without describing it. 'Each to each' suggests mutual answering, two rhythms in step. Whose voice it is (hers, or both) is left open. The ending's self-sufficient rapture is qualified by the poem's original pairing. In 'Parting at Morning' the man turns away from love to the world of men. Critics disagree on whether that sequel undercuts the night's experience or completes it.

    William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955), entry on 'Meeting at Night' and 'Parting at Morning'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'Parting at Morning'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins (eds), Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), vol. 1, notes
