How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Lyrics (1842)

History & Politics

I sprang to the stirrup, and Joris, and he;
I galloped, Dirck galloped, we galloped all three;
"Good speed!" cried the watch, as the gate-bolts undrew;
"Speed!" echoed the wall to us galloping through;
Behind shut the postern, the lights sank to rest,
And into the midnight we galloped abreast.

Not a word to each other; we kept the great pace
Neck by neck, stride by stride, never changing our place;
I turned in my saddle and made its girths tight,
Then shortened each stirrup, and set the pique right,
Rebuckled the cheek-strap, chained slacker the bit,
Nor galloped less steadily Roland a whit.
'T was moonset at starting; but while we drew near
Lokeren, the cocks crew and twilight dawned clear;
At Boom, a great yellow star came out to see;
At Düffeld, 't was morning as plain as could be;
And from Mecheln church-steeple we heard the half-chime,
So Joris broke silence with, "Yet there is time!"

At Aershot, up leaped of a sudden the sun,
And against him the cattle stood black every one,
To stare through the mist at us galloping past,
And I saw my stout galloper Roland at last,
With resolute shoulders, each butting away
The haze, as some bluff river headland its spray:

And his low head and crest, just one sharp ear bent back
For my voice, and the other pricked out on his track;
And one eye's black intelligence,--ever that glance
O'er its white edge at me, his own master, askance!
And the thick heavy spume-flakes which aye and anon
His fierce lips shook upwards in galloping on.

By Hasselt, Dirck groaned; and cried Joris, "Stay spur!
Your Roos galloped bravely, the fault 's not in her,
We'll remember at Aix"--for one heard the quick wheeze
Of her chest, saw the stretched neck and staggering knees,
And sunk tail, and horrible heave of the flank,
As down on her haunches she shuddered and sank.

So, we were left galloping, Joris and I,
Past Looz and past Tongres, no cloud in the sky;
The broad sun above laughed a pitiless laugh,
'Neath our feet broke the brittle bright stubble like chaff;
Till over by Dalhem a dome-spire sprang white,
And "Gallop," gasped Joris, "for Aix is in sight!"

"How they'll greet us!"--and all in a moment his roan
Rolled neck and croup over, lay dead as a stone;
And there was my Roland to bear the whole weight
Of the news which alone could save Aix from her fate,
With his nostrils like pits full of blood to the brim,
And with circles of red for his eye-sockets' rim.

Then I cast loose my buffcoat, each holster let fall,
Shook off both my jack-boots, let go belt and all,
Stood up in the stirrup, leaned, patted his ear,
Called my Roland his pet-name, my horse without peer;
Clapped my hands, laughed and sang, any noise, bad or good,
Till at length into Aix Roland galloped and stood.

And all I remember is--friends flocking round
As I sat with his head 'twixt my knees on the ground;
And no voice but was praising this Roland of mine,
As I poured down his throat our last measure of wine,
Which (the burgesses voted by common consent)
Was no more than his due who brought good news from Ghent.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and occasion title; l. 1

    The poem appeared in Bells and Pomegranates VII, Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845); the archive's '1842' label confuses it with Dramatic Lyrics. Browning later wrote that 'There is no historical incident whatever commemorated in the poem': he meant it as a general impression of the sieges and warfare of the Low Countries. He is said to have written it at sea, on the voyage to Italy in 1838, seven years before it was printed. Despite the disclaimer, an editor suggested the Pacification of Ghent (1576), the alliance of the Netherlands provinces against Spain, as a possible background. The 'news' is never stated.

    Wikipedia, 'How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix' (publication, Browning's letter, route); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on the poem; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  2. Galloping metre ll. 1-2

    Each line has four main stresses with mostly two unstressed syllables before each ('I GALloped, DIRCK GALloped, we GALloped all THREE'), the rhythm known as anapaestic tetrameter. The couplets rush forward, and the repeated 'galloped' supplies onomatopoeia. Browning varies the beat with sudden stops ('So Joris broke silence'), spondees, and heavy consonants in passages of exhaustion. The six-line stanza groupings are irregular in sense, and some run over. Commentators have praised the poem as one of the great English imitations of motion in verse; it was much recited in schools.

    Wikipedia, 'How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix' (publication, Browning's letter, route); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  3. the watch; gate-bolts; postern ll. 3-5

    The opening places us inside a walled city at night: the 'watch' (town guard) wishes them 'Good speed!', the bolts are drawn ('undrew'), and the wall itself seems to echo 'Speed!' as the horses pass through. The 'postern' shuts behind them and 'the lights sank to rest'. The vocabulary is late-medieval or early modern and suits a vaguely sixteenth- or seventeenth-century Flanders. The three riders set off at midnight, moving into a darkness that will brighten through the stages of dawn and morning.

    Oxford English Dictionary; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  4. pique; girths; cheek-strap; bit ll. 9-11

    The lines show expert horsemanship: tightening the saddle's 'girths' (the belly-straps), shortening the stirrups, setting the 'pique' right, rebuckling the 'cheek-strap' of the bridle, and letting the bit hang slacker so the horse can stretch its head. The meaning of 'pique' has puzzled readers; editors usually take it as a variant of 'peak', the raised front of the saddle, and it is one of the poem's few obscure words. The point is that all this is done at full gallop, 'Nor galloped less steadily Roland a whit', and without a word.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'; Oxford English Dictionary

  5. The route: Lokeren to Dalhem ll. 14-41

    The stages run roughly from west to east across present-day Belgium, from Ghent to Aachen (Aix-la-Chapelle) in Germany, well over a hundred miles. The towns are Lokeren, Boom, 'Düffeld' (Düffel), 'Mecheln' (Mechelen or Malines), 'Aershot' (Aarschot), Hasselt, Looz (Borgloon), Tongres (Tongeren) and Dalhem. The incident is invented, but the route is geographically plausible, and each town is linked to a moment of the night: cock-crow, a yellow star (the morning star) at Boom, morning at Düffeld, the half-chime of the great church-tower at Mechelen (St Rumbold's), sunrise at Aarschot. Time and space advance together.

    Wikipedia, 'How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix' (publication, Browning's letter, route); John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  6. a great yellow star l. 15

    Personifications of watching nature recur: the star 'came out to see', the cattle at Aershot 'stood black' against the sun 'To stare through the mist at us', and later 'The broad sun above laughed a pitiless laugh'. The universe watches the riders but does not help them. The progression from moonset (l. 13) to star, morning, sunrise and full sun is carefully managed, and the sun's growing heat in the later stanzas accompanies the exhaustion of the horses.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  7. Roland ll. 12, 22

    Roland, Charlemagne's nephew in the Old French epic, is the type of loyal, self-sacrificing heroism, and the name fits the horse who alone completes the ride. There is also a hint of the destination: Aix-la-Chapelle was Charlemagne's capital. The description of Roland in stanzas 4 and 5 is the poem's most vivid writing, with 'resolute shoulders' butting away the haze 'as some bluff river headland its spray' and one ear bent back for the rider's voice while 'the other pricked out on his track'. The eye's 'black intelligence', glancing 'O'er its white edge' at the rider, suggests a partnership between horse and man.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  8. spume-flakes; aye and anon ll. 29-30

    Roland's lips throw 'spume-flakes' upward as he gallops, a sign of extreme exertion. 'Aye and anon' combines 'aye' (always) and 'anon' (again, presently), an archaic doublet emphasising repetition. The physical detail anticipates the collapse of the other horses: Dirck's mare Roos, with her 'quick wheeze', 'stretched neck and staggering knees', 'sunk tail, and horrible heave of the flank', sinks on her haunches at Hasselt.

    Oxford English Dictionary

  9. Roos; the roan ll. 32, 43-44

    The horses fall one by one. Roos sinks at Hasselt, and Joris promises 'We'll remember at Aix'. Joris's roan, just as Aix comes into sight, 'Rolled neck and croup over, lay dead as a stone'. The 'croup' is the rump of a horse. The suddenness of the death, in the middle of Joris's cry 'How they'll greet us!', is one of the poem's sharpest effects, and it leaves Roland 'to bear the whole weight / Of the news which alone could save Aix from her fate'.

    Oxford English Dictionary; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  10. the pitiless sun ll. 39-40

    The sun is now cloudless and cruel, an indifferent spectator. 'Brittle bright stubble like chaff' places the ride at harvest-time; the biblical image of chaff (e.g. Psalm 1:4, 'like the chaff which the wind driveth away') suggests worthlessness and dispersal, though Browning uses it chiefly for sound. The 'dome-spire' that 'sprang white' at Dalhem marks the first sight of the goal.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'; Bible (KJV), Psalm 1:4

  11. buffcoat; holster; jack-boots ll. 49-50

    A 'buffcoat' was a thick coat of buff leather (ox-hide) worn by soldiers in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; 'jack-boots' were heavy riding boots reaching above the knee; 'holsters' held pistols at the saddle. The details again point to the period of the Dutch Revolt or the Thirty Years' War. Shedding them, the rider stands in the stirrups and coaxes the horse with his 'pet-name', claps, laughs and sings, 'any noise, bad or good', in a desperate improvisation.

    Oxford English Dictionary; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  12. Aix l. 54

    Aachen, called Aix-la-Chapelle in French after Charlemagne's palace chapel, was the coronation city of German kings for centuries. 'Stood' is a deliberately flat, final verb: the horse has done all that can be done. The poem never tells us what the news was or from whom it came, which concentrates attention on the ride itself. Browning's refusal to supply history fits his comment that the poem has no historical incident behind it.

    Wikipedia, 'How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix' (publication, Browning's letter, route); John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'

  13. The ending: Roland's reward ll. 55-60

    The narrator remembers only friends flocking round and himself holding the horse's head between his knees, pouring wine down its throat. The 'burgesses' (citizens with civic rights) 'voted by common consent' that this was 'no more than his due'. The bureaucratic touch is gently comic, and the final word 'Ghent' completes the frame of the title. The 'last measure' is the riders' own last wine, given up for the horse. The poem's sympathy is for the horse; the riders' human motives remain vague.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1991), notes to 'How They Brought the Good News'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes
