My Last Duchess

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845)

Relationships

That's my last Duchess painted on the wall,
Looking as if she were alive. I call
That piece a wonder, now: Frà Pandolf's hands
Worked busily a day, and there she stands.
Will't please you sit and look at her? I said
"Frà Pandolf" by design, for never read
Strangers like you that pictured countenance,
The depth and passion of its earnest glance,
But to myself they turned (since none puts by
The curtain I have drawn for you, but I)
And seemed as they would ask me, if they durst,
How such a glance came there; so, not the first
Are you to turn and ask thus. Sir, 't was not
Her husband's presence only, called that spot
Of joy into the Duchess' cheek: perhaps
Frà Pandolf chanced to say, "Her mantle laps
Over my lady's wrist too much," or "Paint
Must never hope to reproduce the faint
Half-flush that dies along her throat:" such stuff
Was courtesy, she thought, and cause enough
For calling up that spot of joy. She had
A heart--how shall I say?--too soon made glad,
Too easily impressed: she liked whate'er
She looked on, and her looks went everywhere.
Sir, 't was all one! My favor at her breast,
The dropping of the daylight in the West,
The bough of cherries some officious fool
Broke in the orchard for her, the white mule
She rode with round the terrace--all and each
Would draw from her alike the approving speech,
Or blush, at least. She thanked men,--good! but thanked
Somehow--I know not how--as if she ranked
My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name
With anybody's gift. Who'd stoop to blame
This sort of trifling? Even had you skill
In speech--(which I have not)--to make your will
Quite clear to such an one, and say, "Just this
Or that in you disgusts me; here you miss,
Or there exceed the mark"--and if she let
Herself be lessoned so, nor plainly set
Her wits to yours, forsooth, and made excuse,
--E'en then would be some stooping; and I choose
Never to stoop. Oh sir, she smiled, no doubt,
Whene'er I passed her; but who passed without
Much the same smile? This grew; I gave commands;
Then all smiles stopped together. There she stands
As if alive. Will't please you rise? We'll meet
The company below, then. I repeat,
The Count your master's known munificence
Is ample warrant that no just pretence
Of mine for dowry will be disallowed;
Though his fair daughter's self, as I avowed
At starting, is my object. Nay, we'll go
Together down, sir. Notice Neptune, though,
Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity,
Which Claus of Innsbruck cast in bronze for me!

Study notes by the editors

  1. My Last Duchess: publication and speaker l. 1

    The poem first appeared in Dramatic Lyrics (1842) under the heading 'Italy and France. I. Italy'; it took its present title in 1849. The speaker is identified in the original headnote as 'Ferrara', and Louis S. Friedland (1936) showed that Browning probably drew on Alfonso II d'Este, fifth Duke of Ferrara, whose first wife, Lucrezia de' Medici, married him in 1558 while still a young teenager and died in 1561, amid rumours of poison. Alfonso then negotiated to marry Barbara of Austria, daughter of the Emperor Ferdinand I and sister of Ferdinand II, Count of Tyrol. 'Last' means 'most recent' and 'final': the Duke is already arranging the next. The poem is the classic example of the dramatic monologue.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Louis S. Friedland, 'Ferrara and My Last Duchess', Studies in Philology 33 (1936), 656-684; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman, 1991), headnote to 'My Last Duchess'; 'My Last Duchess', Wikipedia (historical background, citing Woolford and Karlin)

  2. couplets that hide themselves ll. 2-3

    The poem is written in twenty-eight heroic couplets, but the sentences run across line-ends ('I call / That piece a wonder') and pause in mid-line, so the rhymes rarely close a unit of sense. The effect is of natural, controlled speech that is nevertheless strictly patterned, a formal image of the Duke himself: courteous on the surface, rigidly in control beneath. Ian Lancashire's RPO commentary analyses how the stresses and verbal habits build the speaker's idiolect.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; 'My Last Duchess', Wikipedia (historical background, citing Woolford and Karlin)

  3. as if she were alive l. 2

    On the surface the Duke compliments the painter: the picture looks alive. But 'as if' insists that she is not. When he repeats 'There she stands / As if alive' (ll. 46-47) right after 'all smiles stopped together', the phrase gains a chilling double meaning: the portrait is the Duchess he prefers, one who smiles only when he draws the curtain. RPO's commentary notes that the murder is never stated, only implied by such phrasing.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  4. Frà Pandolf l. 3

    No painter of this name is securely identified, and he is usually taken as Browning's invention; being a friar, he is an unlikely seducer. The title 'Frà' (from frate, brother) was used for members of religious orders, like the real painter Fra Filippo Lippi, whom Browning later made a speaker in 'Fra Lippo Lippi' (1855). That the portrait took a single day ('a day') emphasises the painter's skill, and the Duke stresses that he named him 'by design': he wants the visitor to know the picture is a prized work, and perhaps to raise the subject of the Duchess's blush.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman, 1991), headnote to 'My Last Duchess'

  5. the curtain l. 10

    The parenthesis '(since none puts by / The curtain I have drawn for you, but I)' reveals a jealous ownership of the image. Whereas the living Duchess smiled on everyone, the painted Duchess is shown only when the Duke decides. Robert Langbaum's influential reading of the dramatic monologue uses this poem to show how a reader is drawn into a degree of sympathy with a speaker even while judging him: the Duke's charm and confidence hold us as they hold the envoy.

    Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2; F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire

  6. the listener l. 7

    We learn who the listener is only at the end (l. 49): the agent of 'the Count your master', sent to negotiate a marriage. RPO identifies him, following Friedland, as presumably Nikolaus Madruz (also spelled Mardruz), who led the Count of Tyrol's entourage in the 1560s. His silence is part of the form: everything we infer comes through the Duke's reactions ('Will't please you sit', 'Will't please you rise?', 'Nay, we'll go / Together down'), which hint that the envoy is uneasy and perhaps tries to leave first.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Louis S. Friedland, 'Ferrara and My Last Duchess', Studies in Philology 33 (1936), 656-684; 'My Last Duchess', Wikipedia (historical background, citing Woolford and Karlin)

  7. that spot / Of joy ll. 14-15

    The 'spot of joy' is a flush of pleasure in her cheek. The Duke imagines the painter's compliments ('Her mantle laps / Over my lady's wrist too much', 'Paint / Must never hope to reproduce the faint / Half-flush that dies along her throat') and condemns her for responding to mere 'courtesy'. A mantle (RPO) is a loose sleeveless cloak. The repeated word 'spot' (ll. 14, 21) carries a faint suggestion of a stain: the Duke turns innocent warmth into a fault.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mantle' n.

  8. A heart--how shall I say?--too soon made glad l. 22

    The interjection performs delicacy, but the charge it introduces is self-condemning: she was 'too easily impressed', she 'liked whate'er / She looked on'. The Duke's account reads as an excuse for a grievance that is really about his own vanity. The asides, dashes and self-corrections ('Somehow--I know not how') are habits of speech by which Browning lets the speaker expose more than he means to.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  9. My favor at her breast ll. 25-29

    A 'favour' (RPO) is a token of love, such as a ribbon or jewel, worn as a sign of attachment. The catalogue that follows puts the Duke's gift on the same level as natural beauty ('The dropping of the daylight in the West'), a present from 'some officious fool', and her mule. 'Officious' here means over-eager to please, meddlesome, though the older sense of 'dutiful' is also available (OED). The Duke's outrage at this levelling reveals his belief that everything she gave should have been graded by rank.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'favour' n. and 'officious' adj.

  10. a nine-hundred-years-old name l. 33

    The House of Este was among the oldest noble houses in Italy, whereas Lucrezia's family, the Medici, had risen from banking and only recently acquired ducal rank (Cosimo I became Duke of Florence in 1537). RPO points out the contrast and observes that the Duke's figure of nine hundred years is an exaggeration. The hyphenated compound turns the name into a possession, a 'gift' that he feels his wife valued no more than 'anybody's gift'.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Louis S. Friedland, 'Ferrara and My Last Duchess', Studies in Philology 33 (1936), 656-684

  11. skill / In speech--(which I have not) ll. 35-36

    This disclaimer is central to a long critical debate. B. R. Jerman ('Browning's Witless Duke', PMLA 1957) argued that the Duke blunders, revealing more than he intends to a stranger. Laurence Perrine ('Browning's Shrewd Duke', PMLA 1959) replied that the revelation is deliberate: the Duke is warning the envoy, and through him the Count, what he expects of his next wife. Either way, the false modesty is itself a rhetorical device, and the speech that follows ('Just this / Or that in you disgusts me') is a precise rehearsal of the reproof he refused to give.

    B. R. Jerman, 'Browning's Witless Duke', PMLA 72 (1957); Laurence Perrine, 'Browning's Shrewd Duke', PMLA 74 (1959); Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  12. miss ... exceed the mark; lessoned ll. 38-41

    RPO identifies 'miss' and 'exceed the mark' as terms from archery, overshooting the target. The Duke imagines correcting his wife as one corrects a pupil's aim. 'Lessoned' (instructed) may pun on 'lessened', since such correction would diminish her, and on his own diminution if he had to argue with her: 'nor plainly set / Her wits to yours' imagines her answering back as an equal. 'Forsooth' (in truth) is scornful.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lesson' v. and 'mark' n.1

  13. I choose / Never to stoop ll. 42-43

    'Stoop' appears three times in a few lines (ll. 34, 42, 43), the key word of the Duke's self-image. To explain his displeasure would be beneath him, so he chooses another course, which the next sentence implies. The phrase is in the present tense ('I choose'), a reminder that this principle still governs him and will govern his next marriage. Perrine reads such statements as a deliberate message to the envoy; Jerman as self-exposure.

    Laurence Perrine, 'Browning's Shrewd Duke', PMLA 74 (1959); B. R. Jerman, 'Browning's Witless Duke', PMLA 72 (1957); F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire

  14. I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together ll. 45-46

    The Duke never says what the commands were. Asked whether they meant she was put to death, Browning is reported to have answered that they were that she should be put to death, 'or he might have had her shut up in a convent' (recorded by Hiram Corson, and summarised by RPO and Wikipedia). Most readers hear murder, reinforced by 'There she stands / As if alive'. The flat, short sentences after the long periods of complaint make the moment chilling; then the Duke turns at once to business ('Will't please you rise?').

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; 'My Last Duchess', Wikipedia (historical background, citing Woolford and Karlin); Hiram Corson, An Introduction to the Study of Robert Browning's Poetry (Boston, 1886)

  15. The Count your master l. 49

    RPO presumes the Count to be Ferdinand II, Count of Tyrol, whose court was at Innsbruck; strictly, the bride, Barbara of Austria, was his sister and a daughter of the Emperor Ferdinand I, so the identification is approximate. Browning need not have intended an exact historical match. 'Munificence' (generosity) and 'Is ample warrant that no just pretence / Of mine for dowry will be disallowed' mean: the Count's well-known generosity guarantees that any reasonable dowry I ask will be granted. The Duke negotiates money in the same breath as he disclaims it.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; 'My Last Duchess', Wikipedia (historical background, citing Woolford and Karlin); John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman, 1991), headnote to 'My Last Duchess'

  16. his fair daughter's self ... is my object ll. 52-53

    Officially the Duke says he wants the daughter herself, not her dowry: 'object' in the sense of purpose (OED). But after his account of the last Duchess, the word cannot escape its other sense, a thing to be acquired and displayed, like the portrait and the bronze. 'As I avowed / At starting' shows the speech is a stage in a negotiation that began earlier.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'object' n.; Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  17. Nay, we'll go / Together down, sir ll. 53-54

    'Nay' suggests the envoy has moved to go downstairs first, perhaps from deference, perhaps from discomfort. Etiquette would let the lower-ranking visitor follow; by insisting they go 'together', the Duke is either gracious or unwilling to let the guest escape his talk. RPO's commentary notes how much of the listener's behaviour must be inferred from such small signals. The moment also prepares the final gesture, in which the Duke, still walking, points out one more possession.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; Laurence Perrine, 'Browning's Shrewd Duke', PMLA 74 (1959)

  18. Neptune taming a sea-horse ll. 54-56

    Neptune, the Roman god of the sea, was often shown with sea-horses (hippocamps) drawing his chariot (RPO). The Duke's closing remark turns from the dead wife to another treasure, and the subject of the bronze, a god subduing a wild creature, glosses his own marriage. No sculptor 'Claus of Innsbruck' is securely identified; Innsbruck links him with the Count's court, so the gift may also hint at the alliance. The final line ends, as the poem began, with possession: 'for me'.

    F. E. L. Priestley, ed., 'My Last Duchess', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), line notes; commentary by Ian Lancashire; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 2: 1841-1846 (Longman, 1991), headnote to 'My Last Duchess'
