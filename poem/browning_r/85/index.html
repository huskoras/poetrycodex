Andrea del Sarto

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Men and Women (1855)

Arts & Sciences

But do not let us quarrel any more,
No, my Lucrezia; bear with me for once:
Sit down and all shall happen as you wish.
You turn your face, but does it bring your heart?
I 'll work then for your friend's friend, never fear,
Treat his own subject after his own way,
Fix his own time, accept too his own price,
And shut the money into this small hand
When next it takes mine. Will it? tenderly?
Oh, I 'll content him,--but to-morrow, Love!
I often am much wearier than you think,
This evening more than usual, and it seems
As if--forgive now--should you let me sit
Here by the window with your hand in mine
And look a half-hour forth on Fiesole,
Both of one mind, as married people use,
Quietly, quietly the evening through,
I might get up to-morrow to my work
Cheerful and fresh as ever. Let us try.
To-morrow, how you shall be glad for this!
Your soft hand is a woman of itself,
And mine the man's bared breast she curls inside.
Don't count the time lost, neither; you must serve
For each of the five pictures we require:
It saves a model. So! keep looking so--
My serpentining beauty, rounds on rounds!
--How could you ever prick those perfect ears,
Even to put the pearl there! oh, so sweet--
My face, my moon, my everybody's moon,
Which everybody looks on and calls his,
And, I suppose, is looked on by in turn,
While she looks--no one's: very dear, no less.
You smile? why, there 's my picture ready made,
There 's what we painters call our harmony!
A common grayness silvers everything,--
All in a twilight, you and I alike
--You, at the point of your first pride in me
(That 's gone you know),--but I, at every point;
My youth, my hope, my art, being all toned down
To yonder sober pleasant Fiesole.
There 's the bell clinking from the chapel-top;
That length of convent-wall across the way
Holds the trees safer, huddled more inside;
The last monk leaves the garden; days decrease,
And autumn grows, autumn in everything.
Eh? the whole seems to fall into a shape
As if I saw alike my work and self
And all that I was born to be and do,
A twilight-piece. Love, we are in God's hand.
How strange now looks the life he makes us lead;
So free we seem, so fettered fast we are!
I feel he laid the fetter: let it lie!
This chamber for example--turn your head--
All that 's behind us! You don't understand
Nor care to understand about my art,
But you can hear at least when people speak:
And that cartoon, the second from the door
--It is the thing, Love! so such thing should be--
Behold Madonna!--I am bold to say.
I can do with my pencil what I know,
What I see, what at bottom of my heart
I wish for, if I ever wish so deep--
Do easily, too--when I say, perfectly,
I do not boast, perhaps: yourself are judge,
Who listened to the Legate's talk last week,
And just as much they used to say in France.
At any rate 't is easy, all of it!
No sketches first, no studies, that 's long past:
I do what many dream of all their lives,
--Dream? strive to do, and agonize to do,
And fail in doing. I could count twenty such
On twice your fingers, and not leave this town,
Who strive--you don't know how the others strive
To paint a little thing like that you smeared
Carelessly passing with your robes afloat,--
Yet do much less, so much less, Someone says,
(I know his name, no matter)--so much less!
Well, less is more, Lucrezia: I am judged.
There burns a truer light of God in them,
In their vexed beating stuffed and stopped-up brain,
Heart, or whate'er else, than goes on to prompt
This low-pulsed forthright craftsman's hand of mine.
Their works drop groundward, but themselves, I know,
Reach many a time a heaven that 's shut to me,
Enter and take their place there sure enough,
Though they come back and cannot tell the world.
My works are nearer heaven, but I sit here.
The sudden blood of these men! at a word--
Praise them, it boils, or blame them, it boils too.
I, painting from myself and to myself,
Know what I do, am unmoved by men's blame
Or their praise either. Somebody remarks
Morello's outline there is wrongly traced,
His hue mistaken; what of that? or else,
Rightly traced and well ordered; what of that?
Speak as they please, what does the mountain care?
Ah, but a man's reach should exceed his grasp,
Or what 's a heaven for? All is silver-gray
Placid and perfect with my art: the worse!
I know both what I want and what might gain,
And yet how profitless to know, to sigh
"Had I been two, another and myself,
Our head would have o'erlooked the world!" No doubt.
Yonder's a work now, of that famous youth
The Urbinate who died five years ago.
('T is copied, George Vasari sent it me.)
Well, I can fancy how he did it all,
Pouring his soul, with kings and popes to see,
Reaching, that heaven might so replenish him,
Above and through his art--for it gives way;
That arm is wrongly put--and there again--
A fault to pardon in the drawing's lines,
Its body, so to speak: its soul is right,
He means right--that, a child may understand.
Still, what an arm! and I could alter it:
But all the play, the insight and the stretch--
Out of me, out of me! And wherefore out?
Had you enjoined them on me, given me soul,
We might have risen to Rafael, I and you!
Nay, Love, you did give all I asked, I think--
More than I merit, yes, by many times.
But had you--oh, with the same perfect brow,
And perfect eyes, and more than perfect mouth,
And the low voice my soul hears, as a bird
The fowler's pipe, and follows to the snare--
Had you, with these the same, but brought a mind!
Some women do so. Had the mouth there urged
"God and the glory! never care for gain.
The present by the future, what is that?
Live for fame, side by side with Agnolo!
Rafael is waiting: up to God, all three!"
I might have done it for you. So it seems:
Perhaps not. All is as God overrules.
Beside, incentives come from the soul's self;
The rest avail not. Why do I need you?
What wife had Rafael, or has Agnolo?
In this world, who can do a thing, will not;
And who would do it, cannot, I perceive:
Yet the will 's somewhat--somewhat, too, the power--
And thus we half-men struggle. At the end,
God, I conclude, compensates, punishes.
'T is safer for me, if the award be strict,
That I am something underrated here,
Poor this long while, despised, to speak the truth.
I dared not, do you know, leave home all day,
For fear of chancing on the Paris lords.
The best is when they pass and look aside;
But they speak sometimes; I must bear it all.
Well may they speak! That Francis, that first time,
And that long festal year at Fontainebleau!
I surely then could sometimes leave the ground,
Put on the glory, Rafael's daily wear,
In that humane great monarch's golden look,--
One finger in his beard or twisted curl
Over his mouth's good mark that made the smile,
One arm about my shoulder, round my neck,
The jingle of his gold chain in my ear,
I painting proudly with his breath on me,
All his court round him, seeing with his eyes,
Such frank French eyes, and such a fire of souls
Profuse, my hand kept plying by those hearts,--
And, best of all, this, this, this face beyond,
This in the background, waiting on my work,
To crown the issue with a last reward!
A good time, was it not, my kingly days?
And had you not grown restless ... but I know--
'T is done and past; 't was right, my instinct said;
Too live the life grew, golden and not gray,
And I 'm the weak-eyed bat no sun should tempt
Out of the grange whose four walls make his world.
How could it end in any other way?
You called me, and I came home to your heart.
The triumph was--to reach and stay there; since
I reached it ere the triumph, what is lost?
Let my hands frame your face in your hair's gold,
You beautiful Lucrezia that are mine!
"Rafael did this, Andrea painted that;
The Roman's is the better when you pray.
But still the other's Virgin was his wife"--
Men will excuse me. I am glad to judge
Both pictures in your presence; clearer grows
My better fortune, I resolve to think.
For, do you know, Lucrezia, as God lives,
Said one day Agnolo, his very self,
To Rafael ... I have known it all these years ...
(When the young man was flaming out his thoughts
Upon a palace-wall for Rome to see,
Too lifted up in heart because of it)
"Friend, there 's a certain sorry little scrub
Goes up and down our Florence, none cares how,
Who, were he set to plan and execute
As you are, pricked on by your popes and kings,
Would bring the sweat into that brow of yours!"
To Rafael's!--And indeed the arm is wrong.
I hardly dare ... yet, only you to see,
Give the chalk here--quick, thus the line should go!
Ay, but the soul! he 's Rafael! rub it out!
Still, all I care for, if he spoke the truth,
(What he? why, who but Michel Agnolo?
Do you forget already words like those?)
If really there was such a chance, so lost,--
Is, whether you 're--not grateful--but more pleased.
Well, let me think so. And you smile indeed!
This hour has been an hour! Another smile?
If you would sit thus by me every night
I should work better, do you comprehend?
I mean that I should earn more, give you more.
See, it is settled dusk now; there 's a star;
Morello 's gone, the watch-lights show the wall,
The cue-owls speak the name we call them by.
Come from the window, love,--come in, at last,
Inside the melancholy little house
We built to be so gay with. God is just.
King Francis may forgive me: oft at nights
When I look up from painting, eyes tired out,
The walls become illumined, brick from brick
Distinct, instead of mortar, fierce bright gold,
That gold of his I did cement them with!
Let us but love each other. Must you go?
That Cousin here again? he waits outside?
Must see you--you, and not with me? Those loans?
More gaming debts to pay? you smiled for that?
Well, let smiles buy me! have you more to spend?
While hand and eye and something of a heart
Are left me, work 's my ware, and what 's it worth?
I 'll pay my fancy. Only let me sit
The gray remainder of the evening out,
Idle, you call it, and muse perfectly
How I could paint, were I but back in France,
One picture, just one more--the Virgin's face.
Not yours this time! I want you at my side
To hear them--that is, Michel Agnolo--
Judge all I do and tell you of its worth.
Will you? To-morrow, satisfy your friend.
I take the subjects for his corridor,
Finish the portrait out of hand--there, there,
And throw him in another thing or two
If he demurs; the whole should prove enough
To pay for this same Cousin's freak. Beside,
What 's better and what 's all I care about,
Get you the thirteen scudi for the ruff!
Love, does that please you? Ah, but what does he,
The Cousin! what does he to please you more?

I am grown peaceful as old age to-night.
I regret little, I would change still less.
Since there my past life lies, why alter it?
The very wrong to Francis!--it is true
I took his coin, was tempted and complied,
And built this house and sinned, and all is said.
My father and my mother died of want.
Well, had I riches of my own? you see
How one gets rich! Let each one bear his lot.
They were born poor, lived poor, and poor they died:
And I have labored somewhat in my time
And not been paid profusely. Some good son
Paint my two hundred pictures--let him try!
No doubt, there 's something strikes a balance. Yes,
You loved me quite enough, it seems to-night.
This must suffice me here. What would one have?
In heaven, perhaps, new chances, one more chance--
Four great walls in the New Jerusalem,
Meted on each side by the angel's reed,
For Leonard, Rafael, Agnolo and me
To cover--the three first without a wife,
While I have mine! So--still they overcome
Because there 's still Lucrezia,--as I choose.

Again the Cousin's whistle! Go, my Love.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion l. 1

    Browning drew on Vasari's Life of Andrea (Vasari had been Andrea's pupil), which praises his technically flawless work but blames his wife and his timid nature for his failure to equal Raphael and Michelangelo. A story recorded by Mrs Andrew Crosse (1900) says John Kenyon asked the Brownings for a copy of the Pitti double portrait thought to show Andrea and his wife; unable to obtain a good copy, Browning sent the poem instead. Historians treat the tale cautiously, and the portrait's identification is itself doubtful. The poem opens in the middle of a conversation, after a quarrel, a typical monologue device.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; Robert Browning, Men and Women (1855), vol. 2, 'Andrea del Sarto (Called "The Faultless Painter")' (Wikisource); Mrs Andrew Crosse, 'John Kenyon and his Friends', Temple Bar (April 1900), repeated in Edward Dowden, The Life of Robert Browning (1904); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955); 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002)

  2. Blank verse and the silent listener ll. 1-3

    Unlike the rhymed, enjambed couplets of 'My Last Duchess', this monologue uses a slow, conversational blank verse, full of pauses, dashes and broken sentences, matching Andrea's weariness. Lucrezia's reactions (turning her face, smiling, listening for the Cousin's whistle) are registered only through his words, a technique that lets the reader see what Andrea half-refuses to acknowledge. Langbaum's theory of the monologue, balancing sympathy and judgment, has often been illustrated from this poem, where Andrea's self-excuses are both moving and evasive.

    Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002); Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  3. Lucrezia l. 2

    Vasari, who disliked her, describes Lucrezia as a beautiful but proud and jealous woman who dominated Andrea and alienated his friends and pupils. Browning adopts this view but complicates it: Andrea's own weakness, not merely his wife, is exposed. The poem assumes she is meeting a lover, the 'Cousin', and that her 'friend's friend' commissions pictures as part of that arrangement. Several critics also hear in Andrea's dependence on an uncomprehending wife a deliberate inversion of the Brownings' own marriage of two poets.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002); William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955)

  4. Fiesole l. 15

    Fiesole, the Etruscan and Roman Faesulae, stands on a hill overlooking Florence. In the poem the view becomes an emblem of Andrea's temperament and art: 'My youth, my hope, my art, being all toned down / To yonder sober pleasant Fiesole' (ll. 39-40). The convent wall, the bell and the 'last monk' leaving the garden (ll. 41-44) complete a scene of dusk and enclosure.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  5. my everybody's moon ll. 29-32

    The image works on several levels. The moon reflects borrowed light and shines indifferently on all; Lucrezia's beauty is public, and Andrea suspects she is 'looked on by' other men in turn. The moon is also traditionally changeable and associated with inconstancy. Andrea's tone wavers between tenderness and bitter insight, ending lamely, 'very dear, no less'. The passage illustrates how the monologue reveals Andrea's knowledge of her infidelity, which he will not openly confront.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  6. A common grayness ll. 34-35

    'Harmony' is the painter's term for the balanced relation of tones and colours. Andrea's own work was admired for its soft, blended tonality and delicate modelling. The 'grayness' recurs as a leitmotif ('All is silver-gray / Placid and perfect with my art', ll. 98-99; 'the gray remainder of the evening', l. 227), set against the 'golden' days in France (l. 168). Grey suggests technical refinement but also emotional twilight, lack of passion and resignation.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter); Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  7. autumn in everything ll. 44-45

    The details of the scene, the clinking chapel bell, the convent wall holding the trees, the last monk leaving the garden, 'days decrease', build a mood of ending. Browning gives Andrea a painter's sense of composition: the scene 'seems to fall into a shape' (l. 46). The passage is an example of the monologue's use of setting to externalise the speaker's state of mind; the time of day and year match his weariness ('I am grown peaceful as old age to-night', l. 244), though historically Andrea was under forty.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  8. A twilight-piece l. 49

    Andrea views 'my work and self / And all that I was born to be and do' as a single composition. Immediately he turns to fatalism: 'Love, we are in God's hand' and 'So free we seem, so fettered fast we are!' (ll. 49-51). Critics note that his invocations of God and fate tend to excuse his lack of will; he accepts the 'fetter' rather than resisting. The self-pictorialising also shows his habit of turning experience into aesthetic surface.

    Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002)

  9. cartoon l. 57

    In Renaissance workshops a 'cartoon' (Italian cartone, from carta, paper) was a full-size drawing whose outlines were pricked or traced onto the plaster or panel (OED). The modern sense of a humorous drawing dates from the 1840s. Andrea points with pride to a Madonna design, and boasts that he can do 'with my pencil' (brush or drawing instrument) whatever he conceives, 'easily' and 'perfectly'. The boast is the poem's ironic premise: technical mastery without the striving of greater spirits.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cartoon, n.'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  10. less is more ll. 76-78

    Someone (unnamed: 'I know his name, no matter') says the struggling painters 'do much less' than Andrea; Andrea turns this round: 'less is more', their imperfect works are worth more because they express a 'truer light of God'. The phrase was later made famous as a motto of modernist architecture (Mies van der Rohe), with a very different meaning (simplicity as virtue). Here it expresses Browning's recurrent doctrine of the value of imperfection, which the poem states at l. 97. 'I am judged' means both 'I am condemned' and 'my case is decided'.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002)

  11. Morello l. 93

    Monte Morello is the highest hill close to Florence. Andrea imagines a critic objecting that the mountain's outline in a painting is 'wrongly traced', and asks 'what does the mountain care?' (l. 96): the real mountain is indifferent to praise or blame, as Andrea claims to be. At dusk Morello disappears from view ('Morello's gone', l. 209), marking the progress of the evening across the monologue.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  12. reach and grasp ll. 97-98

    The lines state a central Browningesque idea, the 'glory of the imperfect': human worth lies in striving beyond what can be attained, and the gap between aspiration and achievement implies a fulfilment beyond this life. Ironically it is Andrea, who reaches only what he can grasp, who utters it; the maxim condemns him. The rest of the line, 'All is silver-gray / Placid and perfect with my art: the worse!', applies it at once. Compare Browning's 'A Grammarian's Funeral' and 'Rabbi Ben Ezra' ('What I aspired to be, / And was not, comforts me').

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002); Robert Langbaum, The Poetry of Experience: The Dramatic Monologue in Modern Literary Tradition (1957), ch. 2

  13. The Urbinate ll. 104-106

    Raphael died in Rome in April 1520, so the dramatic date is about 1525. The painting is a copy sent by 'George Vasari', the art historian Giorgio Vasari (1511-74), who studied with Andrea and later wrote the Lives on which the poem draws; the anglicised 'George' is Browning's touch. Andrea, a superb draughtsman, can see that an arm in Raphael's picture is 'wrongly put', yet recognises that its 'soul is right' (ll. 111-113). Raphael (spelled 'Rafael') thus stands for inspired imperfection against Andrea's faultlessness.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter)

  14. the fowler's pipe ll. 124-125

    A fowler is a hunter of wild birds, who used a pipe or call to imitate birdsong and draw birds into a snare (OED). The image recalls the biblical 'snare of the fowler' (Psalm 91:3, 124:7). Andrea's soul 'hears' her voice and 'follows to the snare': he presents himself as the willing victim of her beauty, though the poem invites us to see the passivity as his own.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fowler, n.'; Psalm 124:7 (KJV); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  15. Agnolo l. 130

    Browning uses the Tuscan form Agnolo for Angelo. Andrea imagines Lucrezia urging him on 'side by side with Agnolo' and 'up to God, all three'. Later (ll. 183-193) he recalls Michelangelo telling Raphael that a 'sorry little scrub' in Florence (Andrea) would make him sweat if given equal commissions. I have not found that compliment in Vasari's Life of Andrea, and its source is uncertain; Browning may have shaped it from Florentine tradition. Andrea values it above everything, yet cannot act on it.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter)

  16. All is as God overrules ll. 132-141

    Having imagined that a more ambitious wife might have spurred him to greatness, Andrea immediately retracts: 'Perhaps not', 'incentives come from the soul's self', and finally 'God, I conclude, compensates, punishes'. The aphorism 'In this world, who can do a thing, will not; / And who would do it, cannot' (ll. 137-138) defines the 'half-men' who have either will or power but not both. Critics such as Roma King see Andrea's oscillation between self-assertion and acknowledged weakness as the poem's psychological core.

    'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002); Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; Roma A. King, Jr., The Bow and the Lyre: The Art of Robert Browning (1957/1964)

  17. Francis I and Fontainebleau ll. 149-165

    According to Vasari, Andrea went to France at the invitation of Francis I (reigned 1515-47), the great patron who also brought Leonardo to France. He was honoured and well paid, but returned to Florence at Lucrezia's urging, with money the king had entrusted to him to buy works of art, which he spent instead on a house. Fontainebleau was the king's palace south-east of Paris. Browning's lines on the king's 'golden look', the 'jingle of his gold chain' and 'such frank French eyes' give the memory a radiance that contrasts with the 'gray' present.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  18. the other's Virgin was his wife ll. 177-179

    Vasari records that Andrea rarely painted a woman's face without using Lucrezia as model. Andrea pictures future viewers saying that Raphael's picture is better 'when you pray', but that Andrea had the consolation of a beautiful wife. The irony is that the wife he idealises as the Virgin is unfaithful. 'The Roman' is Raphael, who worked chiefly in Rome. Andrea's longing to paint 'the Virgin's face' again, 'Not yours this time!' (ll. 230-231), hints at a wish to escape her.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  19. scrub l. 189

    'Scrub' in this sense means an undersized or contemptible person (OED). The speech attributed to Michelangelo says that this obscure Florentine, if 'pricked on' by popes and kings as Raphael is, would 'bring the sweat into that brow of yours'. Andrea then corrects Raphael's drawing in chalk, only to stop himself: 'Ay, but the soul! he's Rafael! rub it out!' (l. 197). The gesture enacts the poem's argument in miniature.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'scrub, n.2'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  20. the arm is wrong ll. 194-197

    The flaw in Raphael's drawing, first noticed at l. 111, returns. Andrea briefly asserts his superiority and calls for chalk, then erases his correction. Browning dramatises the distinction, central to Victorian art criticism (notably Ruskin), between technical finish and imaginative or spiritual expression. Andrea's competence is real; what he lacks is the 'play, the insight and the stretch' (l. 116).

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002)

  21. cue-owls l. 210

    The scops owl, a small Mediterranean owl, has a clear, repeated call rendered 'kew' or 'chiù', from which its Italian name derives. Wright's English Dialect Dictionary records 'cue-owl' as an English rendering; the Scottish National Dictionary treats the word as an imitative coinage and suggests other owls, so the species is not certain. The detail marks nightfall ('settled dusk', l. 208) and gives the monologue its precise sense of time passing.

    Joseph Wright, English Dialect Dictionary, s.v. 'cue-owl' (citing Howard Saunders, Manual of British Birds, 1889); Scottish National Dictionary, s.v. 'cue-owl'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem

  22. gold ... cement ll. 214-218

    Vasari reports that Andrea spent money Francis I had given him to buy pictures on building himself a house in Florence. Here the walls, when Andrea's eyes are tired, seem lit up, the mortar replaced by 'fierce bright gold'. The image fuses guilt with the golden radiance of his French period. It anticipates the house as 'the melancholy little house / We built to be so gay with' (ll. 212-213) and the frank confession 'I took his coin, was tempted and complied' (l. 248).

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter)

  23. The Cousin ll. 220-222

    The 'Cousin', whose whistle summons Lucrezia, is clearly her lover. Andrea knows of the 'loans' and 'gaming debts' and offers to pay them by taking on more commissions ('let smiles buy me!'). The 'friend' of l. 5 and l. 234 may be the same person or a patron connected to him. The 'thirteen scudi for the ruff' (l. 241) is the price of a pleated collar; scudi (Italian scudo, 'shield') were coins. The poem ends on 'Again the Cousin's whistle! Go, my Love', a final surrender.

    John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem; 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002)

  24. The New Jerusalem ll. 261-266

    In Revelation 21 the heavenly city, the New Jerusalem, lies 'foursquare', and an angel measures it with 'a golden reed' (21:15-16). Andrea fancies each great painter covering one wall, and himself with an advantage, since the other three are 'without a wife' while he has his. The final irony is double: he still thinks Lucrezia an asset, and his imagined heavenly 'chance' is only another competition he will lose to them. 'Leonard' is Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519). 'Meted' means measured.

    Revelation 21:10-17 (KJV); John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems, 2 vols (Penguin, 1981), notes to the poem; Daniel Karlin, ed., Robert Browning: Selected Poems (Penguin, 2004), notes to the poem

  25. 'The faultless painter' ll. 69-82

    Vasari's Life presents Andrea as a painter of almost flawless technique whose timid spirit kept him from the grandeur of his rivals; the epithet 'senza errori' summarises his reputation. Browning's subtitle, 'Called "The Faultless Painter"', is ironic: the poem argues that perfect execution without aspiration falls short of the flawed but soaring work of others whose 'works drop groundward' while 'themselves' reach 'a heaven that's shut to me' (ll. 83-84). Critics such as Hawlin connect this to Andrea's emotional and sexual timidity.

    Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects (1550, rev. 1568), 'Life of Andrea del Sarto', trans. G. du C. de Vere (1912-15), vol. 5; Robert Browning, Men and Women (1855), vol. 2, 'Andrea del Sarto (Called "The Faultless Painter")' (Wikisource); 'Andrea del Sarto (poem)', Wikipedia (citing Vasari; King, The Bow and the Lyre, 1964; Hawlin, Robert Browning, 2002); 'Andrea del Sarto', Wikipedia (the painter)
