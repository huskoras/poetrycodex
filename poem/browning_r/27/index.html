Nature

Home-Thoughts, from Abroad

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Lyrics (1842)

Nature

Oh, to be in England
Now that April's there,
And whoever wakes in England
Sees, some morning, unaware,
That the lowest boughs and the brush-wood sheaf
Round the elm-tree bole are in tiny leaf,
While the chaffinch sings on the orchard bough
In England--now!

And after April, when May follows,
And the whitethroat builds, and all the swallows!
Hark, where my blossomed pear-tree in the hedge
Leans to the field and scatters on the clover
Blossoms and dewdrops--at the bent spray's edge--
That's the wise thrush; he sings each song twice over,
Lest you should think he never could recapture
The first fine careless rapture!
And though the fields look rough with hoary dew,
All will be gay when noontide wakes anew
The buttercups, the little children's dower
--Far brighter than this gaudy melon-flower!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Home-Thoughts, from Abroad: date and publication l. 1

    The poem first appeared in Dramatic Romances and Lyrics (1845), number VII of Browning's pamphlet series Bells and Pomegranates. There it was the first of three pieces under the shared title 'Home-Thoughts, from Abroad'; the second ('Here's to Nelson's memory') was later moved elsewhere, and the third became 'Home-Thoughts, from the Sea'. From 1863 Browning reprinted it under the heading 'Dramatic Lyrics', which is why some editions file it there. William DeVane believed Browning wrote it in England in April 1845, recalling his second Italian journey (1844), so the 'abroad' of the title may be imagined rather than actual. The speaker is not necessarily Browning: like most of his lyrics, it is a dramatic utterance.

    Jacqueline Banerjee, 'Robert Browning, "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"', The Victorian Web (2026), drawing on DeVane's Browning Handbook and Woolford and Karlin; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955); John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes; J. D. Robins, ed., 'Home-Thoughts, from Abroad', Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

  2. Now that April's there l. 2

    The deictic 'there' (rather than 'here') sets up the whole poem's structure of longing at a distance. Spring is imagined, not seen. The present tense of 'wakes' and 'sees' in the following lines is therefore a present of imagination: the speaker pictures what an Englishman would see on an April morning. April has a long association with the opening of English poetry (Chaucer's General Prologue begins with it), and with the return of birds, a theme the poem develops. Critics have debated whether the exclamatory opening is simple nostalgia or a more anxious, even ironic utterance by an exile.

    Jacqueline Banerjee, 'Robert Browning, "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"', The Victorian Web (2026), drawing on DeVane's Browning Handbook and Woolford and Karlin; Alison Chapman, 'Robert Browning's Homesickness', Victorian Poetry 50.4 (2012), 469-484; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  3. some morning, unaware l. 4

    'Unaware' modifies the person who wakes: he sees, suddenly and without having watched for it, that the trees are in leaf. The word captures the way English spring seems to happen overnight. It also hints at a contrast between the unreflecting native, who takes this for granted, and the exiled speaker, who is all too aware of what he misses. The vague 'whoever' and 'some morning' make the experience general rather than personal.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes; Alison Chapman, 'Robert Browning's Homesickness', Victorian Poetry 50.4 (2012), 469-484

  4. brush-wood sheaf ... elm-tree bole l. 6

    'Bole', a word of Old Norse origin, means the trunk of a tree. English elms often sprouted dense suckers and small twiggy growth around the base of the trunk, which Browning calls a 'sheaf' of brushwood, as if it were bundled like corn. It is these lowest twigs that first come into 'tiny leaf'. The precision of the observation is typical of the poem, which builds its England out of small, exact rural details rather than grand landscape. The elm was a defining tree of the English countryside until Dutch elm disease destroyed most of them in the twentieth century.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. bole, n.1; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  5. chaffinch l. 7

    The chaffinch (Fringilla coelebs) is one of the commonest British birds, a resident whose bright, descending song begins in late winter and becomes general in spring. The poem's England is made largely of birdsong: the chaffinch in stanza one, then the whitethroat, the swallows and the thrush in stanza two. Each is a familiar, unexotic bird, which supports the poem's preference for the modest English scene over the 'gaudy' south at its close.

    'Home Thoughts from Abroad', Wikipedia; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  6. Irregular lines and the exclamation l. 8

    The poem does not use a fixed stanza: the first verse paragraph has eight lines, the second twelve, and the line lengths vary from two stresses to five. Rhymes are present but irregularly placed (there / unaware, with 'England' repeated rather than rhymed; sheaf / leaf; bough / now). The dash and the exclamation 'now!' give the effect of breath and impulse, and the repeated 'England' frames the stanza. The form thus enacts spontaneous longing, though, as the thrush lines will suggest, its spontaneity is carefully made.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes; Alison Chapman, 'Robert Browning's Homesickness', Victorian Poetry 50.4 (2012), 469-484

  7. whitethroat; swallows l. 10

    The common whitethroat (Sylvia communis) is a summer visitor to Britain, wintering south of the Sahara and arriving in April, when the male sings and builds courtship nests in hedgerows and brambles. Swallows, too, return from Africa in April and May. Both are migrants: like the speaker, they have been abroad, but unlike him they come home with the season. The poem moves here from April to May, from first leaves to nest-building and blossom.

    'Home Thoughts from Abroad', Wikipedia; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  8. my blossomed pear-tree in the hedge l. 11

    With 'my' the poem moves from the general English scene to a remembered personal place: a pear tree growing in a hedgerow, its blossoming branches bending over the meadow. The petals and dewdrops falling 'at the bent spray's edge' are seen with a painter's closeness. The imperative 'Hark' introduces a sound-picture as well, so that sight and hearing combine in the act of memory. Whether the tree is remembered from Browning's own surroundings (his family then lived at New Cross, on the semi-rural edge of London) or belongs only to the speaker is not stated.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1955)

  9. That's the wise thrush l. 14

    Ornithologists have long noted that the song thrush (Turdus philomelos) sings in short phrases, each typically repeated two or more times. Browning turns this natural fact into a witty conceit: the thrush repeats itself so that listeners will not think its first, spontaneous burst a happy accident that it could never repeat. The bird is 'wise' because it knows its art. The lines respond to Romantic celebrations of natural, unpremeditated song, such as Shelley's skylark with its 'unpremeditated art'.

    John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes; Jacqueline Banerjee, 'Robert Browning, "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"', The Victorian Web (2026), drawing on DeVane's Browning Handbook and Woolford and Karlin

  10. The first fine careless rapture l. 16

    The phrase is the most quoted in the poem. 'Careless' means free from care and unstudied, and 'rapture' is ecstatic joy. The thrush sings twice to show that its rapture can be recovered at will; the speaker, by contrast, can only recapture England through memory. Readers have taken the lines in different ways: as a celebration of natural song; as a comment on the art of the lyric, which repeats and shapes what seems spontaneous; or, in Alison Chapman's reading, as part of the poem's anxiety about whether a lyric voice abroad can ever make its home-thoughts real. The alliteration of f and r gives the phrase its own lilting rapture.

    Alison Chapman, 'Robert Browning's Homesickness', Victorian Poetry 50.4 (2012), 469-484; Jacqueline Banerjee, 'Robert Browning, "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"', The Victorian Web (2026), drawing on DeVane's Browning Handbook and Woolford and Karlin; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  11. hoary dew l. 17

    'Hoary', from Old English har (grey, white-haired), describes something whitened as if with age or frost. In the early hours the dew gives the fields a rough, greyish look, but the speaker promises that by noon, when the sun has burnt it off, all will be 'gay', bright and cheerful. 'Noontide' is an old poetic word for midday. The lines move the imagined day forward from dawn to noon, completing the poem's progression from April morning into full May.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. hoary, adj.; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  12. The buttercups, the little children's dower l. 19

    A 'dower' is a portion or endowment, originally the property given with a bride or the share a widow received. The buttercups are the children's natural inheritance: golden flowers that cost nothing and that every child may pick, a wealth that belongs to them by right. The metaphor contrasts humble native riches with the showy beauty of the south in the next line. The golden colour of the buttercup also prepares for the comparison with the yellow melon-flower.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. dower, n.1; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes

  13. this gaudy melon-flower l. 20

    Only at the very end does the poem show where the speaker is: 'this' melon-flower is before his eyes, probably in Italy (the poem itself names no place). 'Gaudy' means showy, garish, over-bright. The comparison of yellows (the large melon blossom and the small buttercup) concludes the poem's preference for the modest English scene over the opulent south. The judgement is plainly partial, and some critics hear irony in it, as of an exile defensively disparaging his surroundings. Victorian Web notes how readings have shifted from simple nostalgia (Symons called it 'a beautiful poem') to Chapman's sense of anxiety and irony.

    Jacqueline Banerjee, 'Robert Browning, "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"', The Victorian Web (2026), drawing on DeVane's Browning Handbook and Woolford and Karlin; Alison Chapman, 'Robert Browning's Homesickness', Victorian Poetry 50.4 (2012), 469-484; John Woolford and Daniel Karlin, eds, The Poems of Browning, vol. 2 (Longman; reissued Routledge, 2014), headnotes and line notes
