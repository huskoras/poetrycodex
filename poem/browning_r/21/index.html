Love

Love Among the Ruins

by Robert Browning 1812–1889

From Dramatic Lyrics (1842)

Love

Where the quiet-colored end of evening smiles
Miles and miles
On the solitary pastures where our sheep
Half-asleep
Tinkle homeward through the twilight, stray or stop
As they crop--
Was the site once of a city great and gay,
(So they say)
Of our country's very capital, its prince
Ages since
Held his court in, gathered councils, wielding far
Peace or war.

Now,--the country does not even boast a tree,
As you see,
To distinguish slopes of verdure, certain rills
From the hills
Intersect and give a name to, (else they run
Into one,)
Where the domed and daring palace shot its spires
Up like fires
O'er the hundred-gated circuit of a wall
Bounding all,
Made of marble, men might march on nor be pressed,
Twelve abreast.

And such plenty and perfection, see, of grass
Never was!
Such a carpet as, this summer-time, o'erspreads
And embeds
Every vestige of the city, guessed alone,
Stock or stone--
Where a multitude of men breathed joy and woe
Long ago;
Lust of glory pricked their hearts up, dread of shame
Struck them tame;
And that glory and that shame alike, the gold
Bought and sold.

Now,--the single little turret that remains
On the plains,
By the caper overrooted, by the gourd
Overscored,
While the patching houseleek's head of blossom winks
Through the chinks--
Marks the basement whence a tower in ancient time
Sprang sublime,
And a burning ring, all round, the chariots traced
As they raced,
And the monarch and his minions and his dames
Viewed the games.

And I know, while thus the quiet-colored eve
Smiles to leave
To their folding, all our many-tinkling fleece
In such peace,
And the slopes and rills in undistinguished gray
Melt away--
That a girl with eager eyes and yellow hair
Waits me there
In the turret whence the charioteers caught soul
For the goal,
When the king looked, where she looks now, breathless, dumb
Till I come.

But he looked upon the city, every side,
Far and wide,
All the mountains topped with temples, all the glades'
Colonnades,
All the causeys, bridges, aqueducts,--and then,
All the men!
When I do come, she will speak not, she will stand,
Either hand
On my shoulder, give her eyes the first embrace
Of my face,
Ere we rush, ere we extinguish sight and speech
Each on each.

In one year they sent a million fighters forth
South and North,
And they built their gods a brazen pillar high
As the sky,
Yet reserved a thousand chariots in full force--
Gold, of course.
Oh heart! oh blood that freezes, blood that burns!
Earth's returns
For whole centuries of folly, noise and sin!
Shut them in,
With their triumphs and their glories and the rest!
Love is best.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Love Among the Ruins: date and placing title; l. 1

    Browning placed this lyric first in Men and Women (November 1855), the two-volume collection of his marriage years. The date of composition is uncertain: it is usually placed in 1852 or early 1853, and Johnstone Parr argued for 1853. It does not belong to Dramatic Lyrics (1842). The title sets love, the private present, against 'ruins', the public past, and the poem alternates between the two in every stanza: the quiet pasture of now, the vanished capital of then. The speaker is a shepherd or herdsman walking his sheep home at dusk to meet a girl in the one surviving turret, and the poem moves from landscape to history to the meeting and its moral.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on the poem; Johnstone Parr, 'The Site and Ancient City of Browning's Love Among the Ruins', PMLA 68.1 (1953), 128-137

  2. Stanza and metre ll. 1-12

    Each stanza has six couplets in which a long trochaic line of six stresses (the last foot shortened, ending on a stress: 'WHERE the QUIet-COLored END of EVEning SMILES') is answered by a brief two-stress line ('MILES and MILES'). The effect is of a long view followed by a short comment or after-thought, and the short lines often carry irony or deflation ('So they say', 'Gold, of course'). The pattern is sustained with great virtuosity through seven stanzas, all on paired rhymes, giving the poem a ballad-like regularity that contrasts with the sprawling grandeur it describes.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  3. quiet-colored end of evening smiles l. 1

    Browning's compound 'quiet-colored' is synaesthetic, attributing silence to the colours of twilight, and the personified evening 'smiles' over the landscape. The image returns in the fifth stanza ('the quiet-colored eve / Smiles to leave / To their folding, all our many-tinkling fleece'), framing the poem's present as tranquil and pastoral. 'Tinkle' (l. 5) and 'many-tinkling fleece' (l. 51) refer to sheep-bells; the sheep 'crop' (graze) and 'stray or stop', as unhurried as the evening.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; Oxford English Dictionary

  4. the ruined capital ll. 7-12

    Browning names no place. DeVane thought the poem 'a vivid recollection of the Italian scene', and several critics placed it on the Roman Campagna, where sheep grazed among ancient remains. Johnstone Parr (1953) surveyed rival claims, among them Babylon and Jerusalem as well as the Campagna, and set the poem against the vogue for excavation of Nineveh and Babylon after Layard's popular accounts (1849, 1853). The details are a mosaic: Homer's 'hundred-gated' Thebes, Babylonian walls broad enough for chariots, a hippodrome, temples on mountains. The composite city stands for worldly greatness as such, whose 'prince' once 'wielding far / Peace or war' has left nothing.

    Johnstone Parr, 'The Site and Ancient City of Browning's Love Among the Ruins', PMLA 68.1 (1953), 128-137; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on the poem; John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'

  5. hundred-gated l. 21

    In the Iliad (9.381-384), Akhilleus, refusing Agamemnon's gifts, says he would not be moved even by all the wealth of Egyptian Thebes, the city 'of a hundred gates', from each of which two hundred men drive out with horses and chariots. Browning's 'hundred-gated circuit of a wall' borrows the epithet (Greek hekatompylos). The allusion fits the poem: in Homer, too, the greatest wealth is rejected in favour of a personal claim, and the city's gates pour out chariots, as Browning's city does.

    Homer, Iliad 9.381-384 (Egyptian Thebes with its hundred gates), Perseus Digital Library; John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'

  6. marble walls: 'Twelve abreast' ll. 23-24

    Herodotus (1.178-179) describes the walls of Babylon as so wide that a four-horse chariot could be turned on top of them, a detail repeated in many later accounts of the ancient city. Browning's men who 'might march on nor be pressed, / Twelve abreast' echo this kind of marvel. The short line drops the fact with deadpan precision, and the next stanza reveals that 'Every vestige of the city' is now covered by grass, the measured greatness reduced to guesswork ('guessed alone').

    Herodotus, Histories 1.178-179 (the walls of Babylon), Perseus Digital Library; Johnstone Parr, 'The Site and Ancient City of Browning's Love Among the Ruins', PMLA 68.1 (1953), 128-137

  7. Stock or stone l. 30

    'Stock or stone' is a traditional alliterative pair (as in the biblical idolaters who worship 'stocks and stones', Jeremiah 2:27), meaning any material remnant at all. The grass 'o'erspreads / And embeds' every vestige, and the city survives only as conjecture. The stanza then turns to moral history: 'Lust of glory pricked their hearts up, dread of shame / Struck them tame', and both glory and shame were 'Bought and sold' with gold. Browning compresses the whole psychology of empire into a couplet.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 2:27

  8. caper; gourd; houseleek ll. 39-43

    The caper (Capparis spinosa) is a Mediterranean shrub that roots in old walls, and its presence suggests a southern, Italian or Near Eastern setting. The 'gourd' is a climbing vine that 'overscores' (scrawls over) the stonework; readers may recall the gourd that shades Jonah and then withers (Jonah 4:6-7). The 'houseleek' (Sempervivum), traditionally grown on roofs, 'winks' its blossom through the chinks, a playful personification. Nature is not hostile here, only indifferently and cheerfully reclaiming the work of kings.

    Oxford English Dictionary; John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; Bible (KJV), Jonah 4:6

  9. the turret ll. 37-48

    The turret is the hinge of the poem: it once overlooked the 'burning ring' of a racecourse, 'all round', where chariots raced and 'the monarch and his minions and his dames' watched the games. 'Burning' suggests both the friction-heat of wheels and the passion of the contest. In the fifth stanza the same turret holds 'a girl with eager eyes and yellow hair', who looks where the king looked, waiting 'breathless, dumb / Till I come'. Public spectacle and private love occupy the same vantage point, and the poem asks which is worth more.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  10. folding l. 51

    The 'quiet-colored eve' leaves the sheep 'To their folding', that is, to be gathered into the fold. The word has pastoral and biblical associations (the good shepherd and his fold, John 10:16), and the hour of folding is traditionally the hour of lovers' meetings in pastoral poetry. As the slopes and rills 'Melt away' 'in undistinguished gray', the landscape loses its distinctions, as the city has lost its own, and the speaker's attention turns to the girl.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Bible (KJV), John 10:16

  11. charioteers caught soul / For the goal ll. 57-58

    To 'catch soul' is to be inspired, as from a glance. The 'goal' is the turning-post or finishing point of an ancient racecourse (Latin meta). Browning sets up an analogy: as the king's gaze spurred the racers, so the girl's gaze draws the speaker home. But there is a contrast too. The king 'looked upon the city' and saw temples, colonnades, aqueducts, 'All the men!', a vision of power; the girl looks for one man only.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; Oxford English Dictionary

  12. causeys l. 65

    The catalogue of public works ('causeys, bridges, aqueducts') is a list of imperial engineering, the infrastructure of Rome or Babylon. 'Causey' is the older English form from which 'causeway' was made (from Old French caucie). The headlong list builds to the climax 'All the men!', the multitude ruled by the king. The next lines answer with intimacy: the girl will not speak but will set 'Either hand / On my shoulder', and 'give her eyes the first embrace / Of my face'.

    Oxford English Dictionary

  13. extinguish sight and speech / Each on each ll. 71-72

    This is Browning's discreet but intense image of the lovers' union. Sight and speech, the faculties through which the king surveyed and ruled his city, are 'extinguished', each lover silencing and blinding the other in the embrace. The verb echoes the earlier fire imagery (spires 'Up like fires', 'a burning ring') and suggests a different kind of burning, sensual rather than martial. The meeting is described in the future tense, so the poem ends before it happens: the climax remains anticipated.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes

  14. brazen pillar; 'Gold, of course' ll. 75-78

    The last stanza piles up military and religious display: a million fighters sent 'South and North', a bronze ('brazen') pillar 'As the sky' for the gods (perhaps recalling the Tower of Babel, Genesis 11:4, 'whose top may reach unto heaven'), and a reserve of a thousand chariots. The two-stress line 'Gold, of course' is one of Browning's sharpest deflations: the speaker's offhand tone exposes the vanity of wealth. 'Brazen' also carries a moral sense, shameless.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; Bible (KJV), Genesis 11:4; Oxford English Dictionary

  15. 'Love is best': the ending ll. 79-84

    'Oh heart! oh blood that freezes, blood that burns!' contains the speaker's two responses: horror at the past, passion for the present. 'Earth's returns' (the yield or profit of centuries of 'folly, noise and sin') are to be shut away, and 'Love is best.' Many readers take this at face value, a lyric affirmation matching the volume's love poems. Others note that the final short line, like 'Gold, of course', has the same deflating rhythm, and that the speaker's passion echoes the 'lust of glory' of the ancients; his love too will pass into ruins. The tension between the two readings is part of the poem's power.

    John Woolford, Daniel Karlin and Joseph Phelan, eds., The Poems of Browning, vol. 3: 1847-1861 (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2007), notes to 'Love Among the Ruins'; William Clyde DeVane, A Browning Handbook, 2nd edn (1955), entry on the poem; John Pettigrew and Thomas J. Collins, eds., Robert Browning: The Poems (Penguin, 1981), notes
