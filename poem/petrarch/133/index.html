Sonnet CIV (In Life): Love's Inconsistency

by Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) 1304–1374

Translated by Sir Thomas Wyatt (in Bohn, 1879) · From To Laura in Life

Love

I fynde no peace and all my warre is done,
I feare and hope, I bourne and freese lyke yse;
I flye above the wynde, yet cannot ryse;
And nought I have, yet all the worlde I season,
That looseth, nor lacketh, holdes me in pryson,
And holdes me not, yet can I escape no wyse.
Nor lets me leeve, nor die at my devyce,
And yet of death it giveth none occasion.
Without eye I see, and without tongue I playne;
I desyre to perishe, yet aske I health;
I love another, and yet I hate my self;
I feede in sorrow and laughe in all my payne,
Lykewyse pleaseth me both death and lyf,
And my delight is cawser of my greif.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Wyatt's version of Pace non trovo l. 1

    Petrarch's 'Pace non trovo, et non ò da far guerra' ('I find no peace, and have no means to make war') is the most famous catalogue of the lover's contradictions. Sir Thomas Wyatt (1503-1542), who with Surrey brought the sonnet into English, translated it in the 1520s or 1530s; it survives in the Egerton manuscript (BL Egerton MS 2711) and was printed in Tottel's Songes and Sonettes (1557). Bohn (1879) footnotes 'Harrington's Nugae Antiquae', the eighteenth-century printing of the Harington family papers, and its text differs from the Egerton text followed by modern editors in several readings (see the notes on 'lacketh', 'none occasion', 'pleaseth' and 'greif'). The archive keeps the old spelling of Bohn's source.

    The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879); R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'

  2. all my warre is done l. 1

    Petrarch's speaker has no peace yet no means of fighting; Wyatt's has no peace although the war is over, or (on another reading of 'done') no peace and his whole life is war being waged. Editors note the ambiguity of 'done': 'finished', or 'being done, carried on'. Either way the line sets up the first of the poem's paradoxes, which are the sonnet's whole structure.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  3. antithesis and paradox l. 2

    Petrarch's 'e temo, et spero; et ardo, et son un ghiaccio' piles up pairs joined by 'and'. The figure is antithesis, and its compressed form, oxymoron, gave English the 'icy fire' and 'freezing fire' of later Elizabethan sonneteers. Wyatt keeps the list form and the insistent conjunctions ('and... yet... nor'), but his lines are rougher than Petrarch's hendecasyllables, with variable stress and syllable count. Whether that roughness is deliberate or the result of an English metre not yet settled is an old debate in Wyatt criticism.

    Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 134 and notes

  4. I bourne and freese lyke yse l. 2

    Fire and ice are the most famous Petrarchan contraries: the lover burns with desire and freezes with fear or despair, often at once. Petrarch says he 'is' ice; Wyatt makes it a simile, 'freeze like ice'. The spellings here (bourne, freese, yse) are sixteenth-century forms and should be read as modern burn, freeze, ice. The paradox has classical roots: Sappho's fragment 31 already joins a thin fire under the skin with cold sweat and trembling.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 134 and notes

  5. above the wind l. 3

    The Italian 'et volo sopra 'l cielo, et giaccio in terra' ('I fly above the sky, and lie upon the ground') sets heaven against earth. Wyatt's 'above the wind' is less cosmic, and he turns the second half from lying on the ground to the inability to rise. Muir and Thomson's text and Rebholz's read 'yet can I not arise'; Bohn's 'yet cannot ryse' is smoother.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  6. season (seize on) l. 4

    Petrarch writes 'et nulla stringo, et tutto 'l mondo abbraccio' ('I clasp nothing, and embrace the whole world'). In the Egerton manuscript Wyatt's word is spelt 'seson', which modern editors print as 'seize on' (Rebholz). The verb is related to the legal term seisin, possession of land; the spelling 'season' in Bohn's source looks like the modern word but is not. The rhyme with 'pryson' and 'occasion' depends on the older pronunciation.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'seize, v.'; Modernised Egerton-based text of Wyatt's 'I find no peace' (LitCharts)

  7. looseth nor lacketh l. 5

    Petrarch's line is 'Tal m'à in pregion, che non m'apre né serra', 'one holds me in prison who neither opens nor locks'. The Egerton text, followed by Rebholz and by Muir and Thomson, reads 'That loseth nor locketh', loosens nor locks, which exactly translates 'apre' and 'serra'. The reading 'lacketh' in Bohn's text (taken from Harington's Nugae Antiquae) makes poor sense and is best explained as a misreading of 'locketh'. The jailer is Love, or the lady.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; Modernised Egerton-based text of Wyatt's 'I find no peace' (LitCharts); Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  8. the prison of love ll. 5-6

    Petrarch's next line, 'né per suo mi riten né scioglie il laccio' ('neither keeps me as its own nor loosens the noose'), completes the paradox, which Wyatt renders 'And holdes me not, yet can I escape no wyse'. The image of love's prison goes back to the Roman de la Rose and beyond. Some readers connect Wyatt's version with his own imprisonments in the Tower of London (1536 and 1541), but the image is Petrarch's, and the dating of the translation is uncertain, so the biographical reading is at best an overtone.

    Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  9. no wyse l. 6

    The phrase 'in no wise' was ordinary in Tudor English (the King James Bible uses it often, as in 'he shall in no wise lose his reward', Matthew 10:42). Wyatt's 'yet can I escape no wise' means 'yet I can in no way escape'. The same noun appears in 'Lykewyse' in line 13. The archive's spelling 'wyse' is a sixteenth-century form, like 'yse' and 'ryse' in the octave, and should be read as modern 'wise'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wise, n.'; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Matthew 10:42

  10. at my device l. 7

    In older English 'device' could mean 'will, desire, pleasure' (OED, device, n.), so 'at my device' means 'as I choose'. Petrarch's lines 7-8 read 'et non m'ancide Amore, et non mi sferra, / né mi vuol vivo, né mi trae d'impaccio' ('Love does not kill me, nor unshackle me; he does not want me alive, nor free me from my plight'). Wyatt compresses the two lines and moves 'Love' out of the sentence: the subject throughout is an unnamed 'that'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'device, n.'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  11. of death it giveth none occasion l. 8

    In Rebholz's Egerton-based text the line is 'And yet of death it giveth me occasion': love gives me cause to die, though it will not let me die. Bohn's reading, 'giveth none occasion', means love gives me no way out through death, which is closer to Petrarch's 'né mi trae d'impaccio' ('nor frees me from my plight'). The variant is a good instance of how much Wyatt's texts depend on which witness is followed. Students quoting the poem should name the edition.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Modernised Egerton-based text of Wyatt's 'I find no peace' (LitCharts); Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text); The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879)

  12. playne (plain) l. 9

    Petrarch: 'I see without eyes, and have no tongue, and cry out'. Wyatt's 'plain' (from Old French plaindre, Latin plangere) means to lament aloud, the root of 'complaint' and 'plaintive' (OED, plain, v.1). The paradox of seeing without eyes may mean seeing the beloved in imagination, and crying without a tongue the poet's written laments. Egerton reads 'Without eyen', with the old plural.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'plain, v.1'; R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  13. love of another, hatred of self l. 11

    Petrarch places self-hatred first. Wyatt puts love first, and in the Egerton text the conjunction 'thus' makes self-hatred the consequence of love; Bohn's 'and yet' keeps it as one more paradox. The theme, love of another as self-loss, is central to Petrarch's penitential frame (compare Rvf 1, where the lover blushes at himself). The figure recalls Catullus 85, 'odi et amo' ('I hate and I love'), the classical model for such self-division.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Modernised Egerton-based text of Wyatt's 'I find no peace' (LitCharts); Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)

  14. I feed in sorrow l. 12

    The lover nourished by pain recurs throughout the Canzoniere (compare the heart fed on sighs in Rvf 1). Wyatt's 'laugh in all my pain' loses Petrarch's tight oxymoron 'piangendo rido' (weeping I laugh), spreading it across a looser phrase. Egerton reads 'I feed me in sorrow', with the reflexive pronoun that matches 'Pascomi'. Wyatt's 'laugh' also loses the grammatical simultaneity of Petrarch's gerund, in which weeping and laughing happen at the same moment.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 134 and notes

  15. pleaseth / displeaseth l. 13

    The Egerton text (Rebholz) reads 'Likewise displeaseth me both life and death', which exactly translates 'spiace'. Bohn's version, from Harington, says that both please him, which still makes a paradox but reverses Petrarch's sense, and it puts death first, as Petrarch does. Whether the Harington reading is a scribal slip or a different stage of the poem cannot be settled from the printed sources; modern editors prefer Egerton.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Modernised Egerton-based text of Wyatt's 'I find no peace' (LitCharts); Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text); The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879)

  16. the closing couplet ll. 13-14

    Petrarch's sonnet closes 'in questo stato son, donna, per voi' ('in this state I am, lady, because of you'), turning at last to the woman. Wyatt replaces the address with an inward conclusion, 'my delight is causer of my grief' (Egerton: 'of this strife'), so that the cause lies in the lover's own pleasure. Formally, his sestet rhymes CDDC EE, ending in the couplet that became the mark of the English sonnet. The octave keeps Petrarch's ABBA ABBA, though with half-rhymes (done, season, pryson, occasion) that may reflect older pronunciation.

    R. A. Rebholz, ed., Sir Thomas Wyatt: The Complete Poems (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1978), text of 'I find no peace' from the Egerton MS, with notes; Kenneth Muir and Patricia Thomson, eds., Collected Poems of Sir Thomas Wyatt (Liverpool: Liverpool University Press, 1969), commentary on 'I find no peace'; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 134 (Italian text)
