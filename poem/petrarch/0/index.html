Sonnet I (In Life): He Confesses the Vanity of His Passion

by Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) 1304–1374

Translated by Lord Charlemont (in Bohn, 1879) · From To Laura in Life

Love

Ye who in rhymes dispersed the echoes hear
Of those sad sighs with which my heart I fed
When early youth my mazy wanderings led,
Fondly diverse from what I now appear,
Fluttering 'twixt frantic hope and frantic fear,
From those by whom my various style is read,
I hope, if e'er their hearts for love have bled,
Not only pardon, but perhaps a tear.
But now I clearly see that of mankind
Long time I was the tale: whence bitter thought
And self-reproach with frequent blushes teem;
While of my frenzy, shame the fruit I find,
And sad repentance, and the proof, dear-bought,
That the world's joy is but a flitting dream.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The proem sonnet and its translator ll. 1-4

    This is Rerum vulgarium fragmenta 1, the sonnet Petrarch set at the head of his Italian lyrics. It was not written first: it is usually dated about 1350, more than twenty years after he first saw Laura in 1327, when he began arranging the poems into an ordered book. It therefore looks back on the whole love story as a past error. The archive's text comes from the Bohn collection of 1879, which prints it as 'Sonnet I (In Life)' and signs it 'Charlemont', that is, James Caulfeild, first Earl of Charlemont (1728-1799), the Irish statesman, who had spent years in Italy. Bohn's headings ('He Confesses the Vanity of His Passion') are the Victorian editors', not Petrarch's. Charlemont keeps the Italian rhyme scheme, ABBAABBA CDECDE, which few English translators of his period attempted.

    The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1

  2. rhymes dispersed l. 1

    Petrarch's 'rime sparse' means poems written at scattered moments and kept as separate pieces. His own Latin title for the book, Rerum vulgarium fragmenta ('fragments of things in the vernacular'), makes the same point. Modern readers often call the collection the Rime sparse after this line (Durling's translation is so titled). The paradox is that the proem gathers the fragments into a single designed book while calling them scattered; the sonnet itself is the act of gathering. Charlemont's 'dispersed' is a close, almost Latinate equivalent of 'sparse', and keeps the sense of things strewn about, though not the hint of the scattered limbs of a broken self that some critics hear in it.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 1 (Italian text)

  3. the echoes hear l. 1

    The Italian addresses those who 'listen in scattered rhymes to the sound' of the sighs. Charlemont turns the sound into 'echoes', which suits the retrospective frame: the reader hears, in written verse, the after-sound of feelings that are now over. The image also anticipates the end of the poem, where the whole experience is dismissed as a dream. The opening address to an audience ('Voi') is itself significant: the poet imagines a public of fellow-lovers who will judge him, and the sestet turns on what that public has said about him.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879)

  4. my heart I fed l. 2

    Petrarch's verb nudrire (to nourish) makes sorrow the food of the heart, an image that runs through the collection, where the lover feeds on pain and tears. It is part of the Petrarchan habit of paradox: what should starve the lover keeps him alive. Charlemont translates the line closely. Compare Rvf 134, where Wyatt's 'I feede in sorrow' renders 'Pascomi di dolor'.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 1 (Italian text)

  5. mazy wanderings l. 3

    Petrarch writes 'in sul mio primo giovenile errore', 'at the time of my first youthful error'. Italian errore, like Latin error (from errare, to wander), means both a straying path and a fault. Charlemont's 'mazy wanderings' brings out the literal, labyrinthine sense, while the poem's later 'repentance' supplies the moral one. Commentators connect the confessional language here with Augustine's Confessions, which Petrarch read closely, and with his Latin dialogue the Secretum, in which 'Augustinus' rebukes 'Franciscus' for his love of Laura.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes

  6. Fondly diverse l. 4

    The Italian line is 'quand'era in parte altr'uom da quel ch'i' sono', 'when I was in part another man from the one I am'. The phrase 'in parte' is carefully modest: Petrarch does not claim to be wholly converted, and many critics stress that the collection never fully abandons Laura. Charlemont drops the qualification and adds 'Fondly', which in eighteenth-century usage still carried its older sense of 'foolishly' (OED, fondly, adv., sense 1) as well as 'affectionately'. 'Diverse' means simply 'different'. The change slightly hardens the speaker's judgement on his younger self.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fondly'; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1

  7. frantic hope and frantic fear l. 5

    Petrarch's line 6 sets 'vain hopes' against 'vain sorrow', repeating the adjective. Charlemont answers with a repeated adjective of his own, but changes its sense: 'vain' (empty, futile) becomes 'frantic' (frenzied), and 'sorrow' becomes 'fear'. The hope-and-fear pairing is one of the stock antitheses of Petrarchan love, given full treatment in Rvf 134 ('I feare and hope'). 'Fluttering' is the translator's addition and suggests a trapped bird or an unstable heart.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 1 (Italian text); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes

  8. my various style l. 6

    Petrarch announces two modes, lament (piangere) and reflection (ragionare), and a 'varied' style: the collection mixes sonnets, canzoni, sestine, ballate and madrigals, and moves between joy, despair, praise and penitence. Variety (varietas) was a positive rhetorical value, and Petrarch's arrangement deliberately alternates forms and moods. Charlemont keeps 'various style' but loses the weeping-and-reasoning pair. In the lines that follow he also shifts the judgement: Petrarch hopes to find pity 'wherever there is anyone who understands love through experience' ('ove sia chi per prova intenda amore'), which Charlemont turns into 'if e'er their hearts for love have bled'.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes

  9. pardon... a tear l. 8

    The Italian 'nonché' has been read in two ways: as 'not only... but also' (pity as well as pardon) or as 'let alone' (pity, to say nothing of pardon). Most modern editors take it in the first, rising sense, which Charlemont also follows: readers who have loved will not just forgive but sympathise. His 'tear' concretises pietà, a key word in Petrarch, meaning both compassion and the piety of fellow-feeling. The octave thus closes on a hope of shared feeling, which the sestet then undercuts with shame.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 1 (Italian text)

  10. the turn (volta) l. 9

    Petrarch's sonnet hinges at line 9, as the Italian form normally does: the octave addresses readers and hopes for sympathy; the sestet turns to what the poet now 'sees', that he has been a public joke, and moves to shame, repentance and a final general truth. The 'now' ('or') sets the whole collection in retrospect. Charlemont keeps the turn at the same point and the same 'now', and keeps the sestet rhyme CDECDE (mankind, thought, teem; find, dear-bought, dream).

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879)

  11. the tale of mankind ll. 9-10

    Petrarch writes that he was for a long time 'the talk of all the people' ('al popol tutto / favola fui gran tempo'). Commentators point to Horace, Epodes 11.7-8, where the lover laments 'fabula quanta fui', ashamed at being gossiped about through Rome. Biblical idiom offers a parallel: the psalmist is made 'a byword among the heathen' (Psalm 44:14). Charlemont's 'of mankind / Long time I was the tale' keeps the double sense of 'tale': a story told and an object of mockery.

    Horace, Epodes 11.7-8 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 44:14

  12. self-reproach... blushes l. 11

    The Italian line repeats the sounds of 'me' (me medesmo meco mi), enacting a self turned in on itself. Shame (vergogna) is felt privately as well as publicly. Charlemont cannot reproduce the sound play and translates it into a visible symptom, 'frequent blushes', paired with 'bitter thought / And self-reproach'. The word vergogna returns in the next line, so shame is both the feeling and the 'fruit' of the past.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1

  13. shame the fruit l. 12

    Vaneggiar means to rave or to pursue vanities; Charlemont's 'frenzy' takes the first sense. The image of shame as the 'fruit' of past conduct recalls Romans 6:21: 'What fruit had ye then in those things whereof ye are now ashamed? for the end of those things is death.' The Pauline background suits the poem's penitential frame, and the word vaneggiar prepares for the Ecclesiastes-like close. Charlemont adds 'sad repentance' and 'the proof, dear-bought', expanding Petrarch's 'e 'l pentersi, e 'l conoscer chiaramente' (repentance, and knowing clearly).

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Romans 6:21; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes

  14. the world's joy a dream l. 14

    The closing sententia states the moral of the whole book, in the spirit of Ecclesiastes ('vanity of vanities; all is vanity', 1:2) and of the vanitas tradition that Petrarch develops again in the Triumphs. Charlemont's 'flitting dream' renders 'breve sogno' well, but his 'the world's joy' narrows 'quanto piace al mondo' ('all that pleases in the world'), which includes Laura herself. Critics have long noted the tension: the proem condemns the love that the rest of the collection celebrates with such care.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Ecclesiastes 1:2; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 1 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 1
