Sonnet LXIX (In Life): He Paints the Beauties of Laura, Protesting His Unalterable Love

by Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) 1304–1374

anonymous (in Bohn, 1879) · From To Laura in Life

Love

Loose to the breeze her golden tresses flow'd
Wildly in thousand mazy ringlets blown,
And from her eyes unconquer'd glances shone,
Those glances now so sparingly bestow'd.
And true or false, meseem'd some signs she show'd
As o'er her cheek soft pity's hue was thrown;
I, whose whole breast with love's soft food was sown,
What wonder if at once my bosom glow'd?
Graceful she moved, with more than mortal mien,
In form an angel: and her accents won
Upon the ear with more than human sound.
A spirit heavenly pure, a living sun,
Was what I saw; and if no more 'twere seen,
T' unbend the bow will never heal the wound.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Erano i capei d'oro: the sonnet and its translator l. 1

    The Italian opens 'Erano i capei d'oro a l'aura sparsi', 'Her golden hair was loosed to the breeze'. Bohn (1879) prints this anonymous version as 'Sonnet LXIX (In Life)' and signs it 'Anon., Ox., 1795'; the descriptive heading about 'Unalterable Love' is the Victorian editors'. The sonnet looks back to the moment of falling in love and sets that past against a present in which Laura's glance is rarer. Its whole grammar is past tense ('Erano', 'ardea', 'mi parea'), which makes the ending, about the wound that remains, all the stronger.

    The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  2. golden tresses (l'aura / Laura) l. 1

    Petrarch constantly plays on Laura's name: l'aura (breeze), lauro (laurel, the poet's crown), l'auro / oro (gold). Here the hair is 'd'oro' and spread 'a l'aura', so the first line hides the beloved's name in both its nouns. The translator's 'Loose to the breeze her golden tresses flow'd' keeps the picture but cannot keep the name. Golden hair was the standard sign of beauty in the lyric tradition, and Petrarch's image became a commonplace of European Petrarchism, as well as a target of parody (compare Shakespeare's Sonnet 130, 'black wires grow on her head').

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 90 (Italian text)

  3. thousand mazy ringlets l. 2

    In the Italian it is the breeze (l'aura, and so Laura) that twists the hair into 'a thousand sweet knots'. Nodi, knots, is also the word for love's bonds, so the image of hair tangled by wind becomes an image of the lover being tied. The translator's 'Wildly' and 'mazy' make the hair labyrinthine and windblown, but the double sense of 'knots' as fetters is weakened. Compare the 'mazy wanderings' of Charlemont's Rvf 1 in the same collection.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  4. unconquer'd glances l. 3

    Medieval love theory held that love entered through the eyes, by beams passing from the lady's eyes to the lover's heart. Petrarch's 'vago lume' (lovely, also restless or wandering, light) 'burned beyond measure'. The translator's 'unconquer'd' is his own word, perhaps meaning irresistible or untamed; it adds a military note that is not in this sonnet, though Petrarch's love poetry is full of the warfare of love.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  5. now so sparingly bestow'd l. 4

    The line opens the sonnet's contrast between then and now. Critics divide over the cause. Some read it as Laura's ageing or illness, so that the poem becomes a declaration of love that survives the fading of beauty; others read it as her withholding of favour, her eyes now rarely turned on the poet; others again keep both. The final line, that the wound is not healed though the bow is unstrung, works with either reading. Bohn's heading, 'Protesting His Unalterable Love', reflects the first.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; The Sonnets, Triumphs, and Other Poems of Petrarch, now first completely translated into English verse by various hands, with a Life by Thomas Campbell (London: George Bell and Sons, 1879)

  6. meseem'd l. 5

    'Meseems' (and past 'meseemed') is an old impersonal construction with the dative pronoun, already archaic by the eighteenth century and used for poetic colour (OED, meseems). The Italian says that her face seemed to take on a colour of pity, 'I do not know whether true or false'. The doubt is characteristic: the lover cannot tell whether Laura's pity is real or his own projection. The translator keeps the doubt ('true or false') and the blush of pity ('soft pity's hue').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'meseems'; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes

  7. love's soft food l. 7

    The Italian lines 7-8 read 'i' che l'esca amorosa al petto avea, / qual meraviglia se di subito arsi?' ('I, who had love's tinder in my breast, what wonder if I suddenly caught fire?'). In Italian esca can mean food or bait, and also tinder; the context of burning ('arsi') shows that tinder is meant. The 1795 translator took it as food, so his breast is 'sown' with 'love's soft food', a mixed image, though he restores the fire in 'my bosom glow'd'. The slip is a useful example of how translation can lose a governing metaphor.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 90 (Italian text)

  8. more than mortal mien l. 9

    In Aeneid 1, Venus meets her son Aeneas disguised as a huntress, her hair given to the winds ('dederatque comam diffundere ventis', 1.319); when she turns away, 'vera incessu patuit dea', 'by her step she was revealed a true goddess' (1.405). Petrarch's whole sestet draws on the episode: the windblown hair, the walk that is not mortal, the voice that is not human. Laura is thus cast as a Venus figure, at once erotic and divine. The translator's 'mien' (bearing) renders 'andar' (walk) more vaguely.

    Virgil, Aeneid 1.314-410 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  9. In form an angel l. 10

    The poets of the dolce stil novo, Guinizzelli, Cavalcanti and Dante, praised the lady as an angel-like being who leads the lover upward (the 'donna angelo'). Petrarch inherits the language but keeps Laura more ambiguous, both a physical woman who will age and a quasi-divine apparition. The translator places 'In form an angel' after a colon, giving it the weight of a conclusion.

    Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90; Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes

  10. more than human sound l. 11

    Aeneas, recognising something divine in the disguised huntress, says 'namque haud tibi vultus / mortalis, nec vox hominem sonat: o dea certe' ('your face is not mortal, nor does your voice sound human: surely a goddess', Aeneid 1.327-8). Petrarch's verb sonavan picks up Virgil's sonat. The translator's 'her accents won / Upon the ear' (gained a hold on it) adds the idea of persuasion.

    Virgil, Aeneid 1.314-410 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes

  11. a living sun l. 12

    The sun is Petrarch's recurrent figure for Laura, as source of light and life, and sometimes as what blinds or burns. 'Vivo' means living, but also vivid. Pairing 'spirit' and 'sun' completes the sestet's progression from bodily walk to angelic form to pure spirit. The translator adds 'heavenly pure', a moralising touch characteristic of eighteenth-century renderings of Petrarch. The sun image also links Laura to Apollo, god of the sun and of poetry, whose laurel shares her name.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  12. if no more 'twere seen l. 13

    Petrarch's 'fu quel ch'i' vidi' ('was what I saw') closes the past-tense description, and 'se non fosse or tale' admits the present. The translator's 'if no more 'twere seen' is less direct, implying that the vision is no longer visible rather than that Laura has changed. Either way the clause sets up the proverb of the final line: a change in the cause does not cure the effect.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90

  13. unbending the bow l. 14

    The arrow has already struck; loosening the bowstring afterwards does nothing to the wound. The bow is Love's (Cupid's), or Laura's, whose eyes shot the first glance. The closing proverb turns the portrait into an argument for constancy: even if her beauty or kindness has diminished, the love it caused remains. The translator's line is close and keeps the epigrammatic form. The sestet in both the Italian and the English rhymes CDE DCE.

    Robert M. Durling, ed. and trans., Petrarch's Lyric Poems: The Rime sparse and Other Lyrics (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1976), Rvf 90 and notes; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Marco Santagata (Milan: Mondadori, I Meridiani, 1996; new ed. 2004), commentary on Rvf 90; Francesco Petrarca, Canzoniere, ed. Gianfranco Contini (Turin: Einaudi, 1964), Rvf 90 (Italian text)
