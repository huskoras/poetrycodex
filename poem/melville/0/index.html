The Portent

by Herman Melville 1819–1891

From Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War

History & Politics

Hanging from the beam,
Slowly swaying (such the law),
Gaunt the shadow on your green,
Shenandoah!
The cut is on the crown
(Lo, John Brown),
And the stabs shall heal no more.

Hidden in the cap
Is the anguish none can draw;
So your future veils its face,
Shenandoah!
But the streaming beard is shown
(Weird John Brown),
The meteor of the the war.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Dated 1859.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Portent: occasion and placement l. 1

    John Brown, the militant abolitionist, led a raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, on 16-18 October 1859, hoping to start a slave rising; he was captured, tried for treason against Virginia, and hanged at Charles Town on 2 December. Melville's poem stands as a prelude to Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War (1866), printed apart from the main sequence and dated 1859, so that the whole book is framed as the fulfilment of an omen. A 'portent' (Latin portentum) is a sign foretelling calamity. Melville makes Brown neither martyr nor criminal but a sign, and the poem's ambivalence has been much discussed: Warren and Garner both stress its refusal to take sides.

    Herman Melville, Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War (Harper & Brothers, 1866), Project Gutenberg text; National Park Service, Harpers Ferry National Historical Park, 'John Brown'; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'; Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  2. such the law l. 2

    The parenthesis '(such the law)' is the first of the poem's laconic asides. Its sense is multiple. Literally, a hanging body sways according to the law of the pendulum. Legally, Brown was hanged under the law of Virginia, which convicted him of treason, murder and conspiracy to incite slave insurrection. Morally, the phrase may glance at a deeper law, the retributive justice that Brown himself invoked when he wrote, on the day of his execution, that the crimes of the land would be purged only with blood. Melville's tone is ambiguous between grim acceptance and irony; the two-beat rhythm imitates the swing of the rope.

    Hennig Cohen, ed., The Battle-Pieces of Herman Melville (Yoseloff, 1963), notes to 'The Portent'; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'; Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  3. Shenandoah ll. 4, 11

    Harpers Ferry lies where the Shenandoah River joins the Potomac, and Charles Town is in the lower Shenandoah Valley. Melville addresses the valley itself in an exclamatory refrain that recurs in both stanzas. The address is prophetic: the 'green' valley would become one of the most fought-over regions of the war, from Stonewall Jackson's campaign of 1862 to Sheridan's systematic burning of its farms in 1864, which Melville treated later in Battle-Pieces. The shadow of the hanged man falls across that future. Tom Nurmi's essay in the Mickle Street Review reads the valley itself, rather than Brown, as the poem's focus, a figure for the fugitives, dissenters and dead soldiers who would haunt it.

    Herman Melville, Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War (Harper & Brothers, 1866), Project Gutenberg text; Tom Nurmi, 'Shadows in the Shenandoah', Mickle Street Review 21 (2016); Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  4. Gaunt the shadow l. 3

    'Gaunt' (lean, haggard, grim) describes the shadow of the tall, thin old man on the gallows, and by extension the haggard future it portends. The 'green' is the pastoral valley, still at peace in 1859. The image is literally a foreshadowing: the shadow falls before the war it predicts. Melville's syntax is compressed and inverted throughout ('Gaunt the shadow'), with verbs omitted, which gives the poem the clipped quality of an inscription or oracle.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gaunt, adj.'; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'

  5. the cut on the crown l. 5

    When US Marines under Robert E. Lee stormed the engine house at Harpers Ferry on 18 October 1859, Lieutenant Israel Greene struck Brown with his sword, cutting him about the head and wounding him further with thrusts; Brown lay wounded at his trial. 'Crown' means the top of the head, but the word also suggests a martyr's crown, or the crown of thorns: Brown's supporters, Emerson and Thoreau among them, were already comparing him to Christ. Melville admits the association without endorsing it.

    National Park Service, Harpers Ferry National Historical Park, 'John Brown'; Hennig Cohen, ed., The Battle-Pieces of Herman Melville (Yoseloff, 1963), notes to 'The Portent'; Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  6. the stabs shall heal no more l. 7

    Literally, the dead man's wounds will never close. The future tense ('shall') carries the prophecy: the wounds stand for the sectional division of the country, which the raid and the hanging made irreparable. The line can be read as Melville's comment that the violence Brown began and the violence done to him would continue in war. The stanza's form, a short refrain-line in parenthesis ('Lo, John Brown') followed by a longer final line, gives each stanza the shape of a ballad or chant.

    Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'; Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  7. the parenthetic refrain ll. 6, 13

    Each stanza names Brown in a parenthetic aside, framed by rhyme (crown/Brown; shown/Brown). 'Lo' is the archaic biblical exclamation 'behold', used for announcing wonders and prophecies. The parentheses withhold the name from the main sentence, as if the speaker is pointing at the dangling body rather than discussing him. The two stanzas are closely parallel: hanging/cap, law/draw, Shenandoah! repeated, crown/beard, Lo/Weird, heal no more/meteor of the war.

    Herman Melville, Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War (Harper & Brothers, 1866), Project Gutenberg text; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'

  8. the cap l. 8

    The 'cap' is the white hood drawn over a condemned man's face before the drop. It hides Brown's expression, so that 'the anguish none can draw' is both invisible and inexpressible. 'Draw' is a pun: no one can depict (draw) the anguish, and no one can draw it out (extract or relieve it). The concealed face is then compared to the valley's future, which 'veils its face' too. The stanza moves from what is hidden to what is shown.

    Hennig Cohen, ed., The Battle-Pieces of Herman Melville (Yoseloff, 1963), notes to 'The Portent'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'draw, v.'

  9. your future veils its face l. 10

    The simile makes the hooded man an emblem of the unknowable future: the valley, like Brown, has a covered face. In 1859 no one could see the war, though many feared it. The figure of the veiled future has a long history (the veiled image of Isis, the prophetic veil), and Melville was fond of veils and masks as figures of hidden truth throughout his fiction. The poem was in fact composed or revised with hindsight: its date of 1859 is a dramatic fiction, and it was printed in 1866 when the future it 'veils' was known.

    Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'; Stanton Garner, The Civil War World of Herman Melville (UP of Kansas, 1993)

  10. the streaming beard l. 12

    Brown grew his long white beard in 1858-59, and it dominates the portraits of him circulated after his death; the hood hides his face, but the beard shows below it. Melville's phrasing echoes two famous images. In Gray's 'The Bard' (1757) the last Welsh bard, prophesying ruin to his conquerors, has beard and 'hoary hair' that 'Stream'd, like a meteor, to the troubled air' (ll. 19-20); and in Paradise Lost 1.537 Satan's standard 'Shone like a meteor streaming to the wind'. The first makes Brown a prophet of doom, the second hints at rebellion. Whether Melville intended both is uncertain, but the Gray echo fits the poem's prophetic frame closely.

    Thomas Gray, 'The Bard: A Pindaric Ode' (1757), ll. 19-20, in Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Gray, Collins and Goldsmith (Longman, 1969); John Milton, Paradise Lost (1674), 1.537; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'

  11. Weird l. 13

    'Weird' originally meant 'having power over fate' (Old English wyrd, fate or destiny), as in the 'weird sisters' of Macbeth, the Fates. By the nineteenth century it had acquired its modern sense of 'unearthly, uncanny'. Both apply: Brown is an uncanny, unnerving figure and a man of fate, an agent and sign of destiny. The adjective sums up Melville's ambivalence: he does not call Brown hero or villain, but something beyond ordinary moral categories.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weird, adj.'; William Shakespeare, Macbeth, 1.3; Robert Penn Warren, ed., Selected Poems of Herman Melville (Random House, 1970), notes to 'The Portent'

  12. The meteor of the war l. 14

    Comets and meteors were ancient omens of war and the fall of rulers (compare Julius Caesar 2.2, 'When beggars die, there are no comets seen'). Brown is the 'meteor of the war': its herald and portent, giving the poem its title. The image also had contemporary force: a spectacular meteor procession crossed the American sky in July 1860, and Whitman's 'Year of Meteors (1859-60)' brings together Brown's hanging and that meteor as signs of the year. Note: the archive text reads 'the the war', a duplicated word; the 1866 text reads 'The meteor of the war'.

    Herman Melville, Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War (Harper & Brothers, 1866), Project Gutenberg text; Walt Whitman, 'Year of Meteors (1859-60)', in Leaves of Grass; William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, 2.2.30-31
