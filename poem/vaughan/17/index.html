The Retreate.

by Henry Vaughan 1621–1695

Religion

HAppy those early dayes! when I
Shin'd in my Angell-infancy.
Before I understood this place
Appointed for my second race,
Or taught my soul to fancy ought
But a white, Celestiall thought,
When yet I had not walkt above
A mile, or two, from my first love,
And looking back (at that short space,)
Could see a glimpse of his bright-face;
When on some gilded Cloud, or flowre
My gazing soul would dwell an houre,
And in those weaker glories spy
Some shadows of eternity;
Before I taught my tongue to wound
My Conscience with a sinfull sound,
Or had the black art to dispence
A sev'rall sinne to ev'ry sence,
But felt through all this fleshly dresse
Bright shootes of everlastingnesse.
O how I long to travell back
And tread again that ancient track!
That I might once more reach that plaine,
Where first I left my glorious traine,
From whence th' Inlightned spirit sees
That shady City of Palme trees;
But (ah!) my soul with too much stay
Is drunk, and staggers in the way.
Some men a forward motion love,
But I by backward steps would move,
And when this dust falls to the urn
In that state I came return.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Retreate (title and occasion) title; l. 1

    'Retreate' means a withdrawal or going back, and the poem turns the word's usual military sense (falling back) into a spiritual ideal: the speaker wants to 'travell back' to his origin. It appeared in the first part of Vaughan's Silex Scintillans (1650), reissued with a second part in 1655; Martin's Oxford edition prints the 1650 text. The opening exclamation recalls the classical beatus ille tradition ('Happy the man who...'), here applied to a lost time instead of a way of life. Martin suggests Vaughan may have known John Earle's 'Character of a Child' (Micro-cosmographie, 1628), which calls a child the best copy of Adam before the Fall and sees him moving further from God as he grows older.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, text pp. 419-20, notes to p. 419 (Notes begin p. 677); L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  2. Angell-infancy l. 2

    The coined compound makes the child almost an angel, radiant with a light the adult has lost. The idea draws on Christ's sayings about children (Matthew 18:3, 'except ye ... become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven'; 18:10, on the children's angels who behold God's face). Earle's 'Character of a Child', which Martin proposes as a source, likewise sees the child as nearer to God than the adult. Light imagery ('Shin'd', 'bright-face', 'gilded', 'Bright shootes') runs through the poem.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 18:3; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, text pp. 419-20, notes to p. 419 (Notes begin p. 677)

  3. second race l. 4

    'Race' means a course to be run (Hebrews 12:1, 'let us run with patience the race that is set before us'). Calling it 'second' implies that the soul had a first life before birth, with God, a doctrine of pre-existence found in Platonic tradition and in the Hermetic writings that interested Vaughan and his twin brother Thomas. Critics differ on how literally to take it: some see a real Platonic belief, others a devotional fiction or a way of describing baptismal innocence. 'Appointed' stresses that the earthly course is assigned by God. 'This place' (l. 3) is the world.

    Bible (KJV), Hebrews 12:1; Plato, Phaedo 72e-77a (the argument from recollection), trans. B. Jowett; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  4. white, Celestiall thought l. 6

    'White' signifies purity, as in the white robes of those washed in the blood of the Lamb (Revelation 7:14) and in Isaiah 1:18 ('though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow'). 'Celestiall' means heavenly. Before learning to 'fancy ought' (imagine anything) else, the child's mind held only such unmixed thoughts. 'Fancy' here suggests the image-making faculty that will later lead the soul astray. Vaughan's verse often uses whiteness for innocence,.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:14; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 1:18; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  5. my first love ll. 7-8

    The soul has walked only a short distance from God, its first love, and can still look back and glimpse his face. The phrase echoes Christ's reproach to the church of Ephesus in Revelation 2:4. Measuring spiritual distance in miles makes the soul's descent into life a literal journey, prepared by 'race' and continued in 'travell back' and 'ancient track'. 'His bright-face' (l. 10) identifies the first love as God or Christ.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 2:4; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  6. some gilded Cloud, or flowre l. 11

    The child's contemplation of natural beauty is a form of prayer: the 'gazing soul' dwells on a cloud gilded by sunlight or on a flower and sees through it to heaven. Vaughan, like Herbert, has a strong sense of the created world as a book of God. The image of the flower anticipates the Romantic attention to nature that later readers linked with Wordsworth.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650); William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807)

  7. weaker glories ... shadows of eternity ll. 13-14

    The language is Platonic: visible things are shadows of invisible realities (as in Plato's cave, or the doctrine of recollection in the Phaedo). It is also Pauline: earthly things are 'a shadow of things to come' (Colossians 2:17; cf. Hebrews 10:1). 'Weaker glories' concedes that the cloud and flower are glorious, but only faintly compared with the heaven they reflect. The child sees through them because his soul still remembers its first home.

    Plato, Phaedo 72e-77a (the argument from recollection), trans. B. Jowett; Bible (KJV), Colossians 2:17; Bible (KJV), Hebrews 10:1

  8. taught my tongue to wound / My Conscience ll. 15-16

    An infant (Latin infans, 'not speaking') has not yet learned to speak, and so cannot sin with the tongue. The epistle of James calls the tongue 'a fire, a world of iniquity' (James 3:6). Vaughan imagines his own speech as a weapon turned inward, wounding his conscience 'with a sinfull sound'. The repeated 'taught' (ll. 5, 15) makes sin something learned, not innate, a view that sits uneasily with the doctrine of original sin and has prompted debate about Vaughan's theology.

    Bible (KJV), James 3:6; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  9. black art ... sev'rall sinne ll. 17-18

    'The black art' was necromancy or witchcraft, here used figuratively for the expertise in sin that experience brings. 'Dispence' means distribute or deal out, and 'sev'rall' means separate or distinct: a different sin for each of the five senses. The phrasing makes sin a learned, almost pharmaceutical craft (one 'dispenses' drugs). Vaughan, a physician who also translated Hermetic medical works, knew the vocabulary of both magic and medicine; this reading of his background is a suggestion, not something the poem states.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650); Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 18:10-12

  10. fleshly dresse l. 19

    The image of the body as the soul's clothing is ancient and biblical: Paul speaks of being 'unclothed' and 'clothed upon' with a heavenly body (2 Corinthians 5:1-4). The 'dresse' is temporary and opaque, yet the child feels light from beyond it shooting through. The metaphor anticipates the end of the poem, where 'this dust falls to the urn' and the soul is freed from its garment.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 5:1-4; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  11. Bright shootes of everlastingnesse l. 20

    'Shootes' are darting rays of light, and also young shoots of a plant; both senses fit a poem of light and growth. Martin points to Owen Felltham's essay 'Of the Soule' in Resolves, where the conscience and the apprehension of eternity prove the soul 'a shoot of everlastingnesse'. Vaughan transforms the borrowed phrase into a felt experience: the child senses eternity physically, 'through all this fleshly dresse'. The line is often regarded as the most memorable in the poem.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, text pp. 419-20, notes to p. 419 (Notes begin p. 677); L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  12. travell back ... that ancient track ll. 21-22

    The second half of the poem turns from memory to desire ('O how I long'). The 'ancient track' is the way the soul travelled from heaven into life, and also the old path of the righteous (Jeremiah 6:16, 'ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein'). Travelling backward reverses the usual Christian image of life as a forward pilgrimage. Critics have compared Wordsworth's Ode, where heaven 'lies about us in our infancy' and the soul's light fades as it journeys farther from the east.

    Bible (KJV), Jeremiah 6:16; William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807)

  13. glorious traine l. 24

    A 'traine' could be a retinue of attendants or the trailing part of a robe. The soul left behind either the angelic company that attended it, or the robe of glory it wore. Wordsworth's Ode famously speaks of 'trailing clouds of glory' at birth, an image readers have often compared, though no direct borrowing is demonstrated; a 2016 article by Takashi Yoshinaka compares the two poems and proposes Coleridge as a possible point of contact between the poets. The 'plaine' where the train was left is the level ground of the soul's first dwelling.

    William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807); Takashi Yoshinaka, 'Poets Gazing at Flowers: Vaughan and Wordsworth' (in Japanese, English abstract), Hiroshima Studies in English Language and Literature 60 (2016); L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650)

  14. City of Palme trees ll. 25-26

    In Deuteronomy 34:1-3 God shows the dying Moses the promised land from Pisgah, including 'the plain of the valley of Jericho, the city of palm trees'; Moses sees it but is not allowed to enter. Vaughan's 'plaine' (l. 23) picks up 'the plain of the valley'. The 'Inlightned spirit', from its first dwelling, can see the heavenly city as Moses saw Canaan. Palms are also emblems of victory and of the blessed in heaven (Revelation 7:9). 'Shady' suggests cool rest in the desert.

    Bible (KJV), Deuteronomy 34:1-4; Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:9

  15. drunk, and staggers ll. 27-28

    'Stay' means a delay, the soul's lingering in earthly life. The image of reeling and staggering like a drunken man is biblical (Psalm 107:27; Isaiah 24:20, where the earth reels like a drunkard). The 'way' is both the road and Christ, 'the way' (John 14:6). Intoxication with the world's pleasures disables the return journey; the parenthetical '(ah!)' gives the line a note of confession.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 107:27; Bible (KJV), Isaiah 24:20

  16. forward motion ... backward steps ll. 29-30

    The couplet sets the poem's ideal of return against a culture of 'forward motion' (ambition, worldly advance, perhaps even the new philosophy of progress). Vaughan's preference recalls the Platonic idea that knowledge is recollection of what the soul once knew, and the Christian idea of conversion as turning back. The retreat of the title is therefore an advance in spiritual terms. Wordsworth's Ode reverses the motion again, accepting forward growth and finding compensation in 'the philosophic mind'.

    Plato, Phaedo 72e-77a (the argument from recollection), trans. B. Jowett; William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807)

  17. this dust falls to the urn ll. 31-32

    The body is 'dust' (Genesis 3:19, 'dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return'); the urn is a burial vessel, a classical image for the grave. The closing lines echo Ecclesiastes 12:7: 'Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.' The final line is short and metrically abrupt ('In that state I came return'), as if the return were sudden. The poem's circular structure enacts the desired return.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 12:7; Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19

  18. couplets ll. 1-32

    The poem is written in iambic tetrameter couplets, the metre of Milton's 'L'Allegro' and 'Il Penseroso' and of much seventeenth-century meditative verse. The couplets give a plain, aphoristic clarity, especially in the paired antitheses of the close ('forward motion' / 'backward steps'). The movement divides into two halves: twenty lines of remembered innocence, then twelve of longing to return, signalled by 'O how I long'.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914), vol. II, 'The Retreate', pp. 419-20 (text of Silex Scintillans, 1650); William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood' (1807)
