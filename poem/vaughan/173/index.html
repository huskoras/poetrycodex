Death & Dying

They are all gone into the world of light!

by Henry Vaughan 1621–1695

From The Oxford Book of English Verse (1900), ed. A. T. Quiller-Couch

Death & Dying

They are all gone into the world of light!
And I alone sit ling'ring here;
Their very memory is fair and bright,
And my sad thoughts doth clear.

It glows and glitters in my cloudy breast,
Like stars upon some gloomy grove,
Or those faint beams in which this hill is drest
After the sun's remove.

I see them walking in an air of glory,
Whose light doth trample on my days:
My days, which are at best but dull and hoary,
Mere glimmering and decays.

O holy Hope! and high Humility,
High as the heavens above!
These are your walks, and you have show'd them me,
To kindle my cold love.

Dear, beauteous Death! the jewel of the Just,
Shining nowhere, but in the dark;
What mysteries do lie beyond thy dust,
Could man outlook that mark!

He that hath found some fledg'd bird's nest may know,
At first sight, if the bird be flown;
But what fair well or grove he sings in now,
That is to him unknown.

And yet as Angels in some brighter dreams
Call to the soul, when man doth sleep:
So some strange thoughts transcend our wonted themes,
And into glory peep.

If a star were confin'd into a tomb,
Her captive flames must needs burn there;
But when the hand that lock'd her up gives room,
She'll shine through all the sphere.

O Father of eternal life, and all
Created glories under Thee!
Resume Thy spirit from this world of thrall
Into true liberty.

Either disperse these mists, which blot and fill
My perspective still as they pass:
Or else remove me hence unto that hill,
Where I shall need no glass.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, publication and occasion l. 1

    The poem appeared in the second part of Silex Scintillans ('The Flashing Flint'), added to the 1655 edition; like many of Vaughan's poems it has no title, so editors use the first line. The archive text has modernised spelling. 'They' are not named. Biographers connect the poem with the deaths of Vaughan's younger brother William (1648) and possibly his first wife Catherine, and with the wider losses of the Civil Wars; but the generality is deliberate, so the elegy can speak for any mourner. The opening exclamation sets the governing contrast between 'them' in light and the speaker alone in shadow.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  2. Stanza and metre st. 1

    Each stanza pairs a pentameter with a tetrameter and ends on a three-stress line, indented to show its shortness. The pattern lets each quatrain open expansively and then close quietly, enacting the speaker's subsiding into loneliness or into a single sharp insight ('And into glory peep'). The opening stanza sets the tone with its long first line of exclamation and its contracted 'And my sad thoughts doth clear'. Vaughan learned much of his stanza-making from George Herbert, whose influence he acknowledged in the 1655 preface.

    L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  3. doth clear l. 4

    As the RPO edition glosses, 'clear' means 'brighten', as weather clears. The singular verb 'doth' after the plural 'thoughts' is not a slip: the subject of the clause is 'Their very memory' (l. 3), which both is bright and clears the speaker's thoughts. The idea that memory of the dead gives light, not darkness, inverts conventional elegy and governs the whole poem's imagery.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; OED, 'clear, v.'; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655)

  4. stars ... gloomy grove; the sun's remove ll. 5-8

    Two similes for the memory ('It', l. 5): stars seen above a dark grove, and the faint light that lingers on a hill after the sun has set ('remove' = departure). Both are images of light that persists in, and is made visible by, darkness. The hill and grove are often linked to Vaughan's native Usk valley in Breconshire, whose landscape fills Silex Scintillans. The afterglow figure fits the elegiac situation: the dead are 'set', but their light remains.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  5. trample on my days l. 10

    A startling verb. The dead walk in 'an air of glory', and their light tramples on the speaker's days, as if glory had weight. The effect is to make earthly life feel not merely dim but oppressed by comparison. 'Walking' suggests the blessed of Revelation who 'walk with me in white' (Revelation 3:4), and the verb picks up the idea that the saints tread down what is lower.

    Revelation 3:4 (KJV); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655)

  6. hoary l. 11

    'Hoary' means grey or white with age, as of hair; but its root 'hoar' was also used of things grey with mould, musty (OED, hoar adj.; hoary adj.). Both senses suit the speaker's 'dull' days, set against the 'glory' of the dead. 'Mere glimmering and decays' completes the picture: life as a fading light, a process of decline, the opposite of the glowing memory of stanza 2.

    OED, 'hoary, adj.' and 'hoar, adj.'; Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes

  7. holy Hope and high Humility ll. 13-14

    The apostrophe names the two virtues that led the dead to heaven: 'These are your walks', the paths of Hope and Humility. Humility is called 'high' and 'High as the heavens above' in a deliberate paradox drawn from the gospel teaching that 'he that humbleth himself shall be exalted' (Luke 14:11). The virtues have shown the speaker these walks 'To kindle my cold love', so that the poem turns from mourning to the speaker's own spiritual need.

    Luke 14:11 (KJV); L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655); Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  8. Dear, beauteous Death! the jewel of the Just ll. 17-18

    Vaughan addresses death with startling affection. It is 'the jewel of the Just' because it brings them to God: 'Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints' (Psalm 116:15). A jewel 'Shining nowhere, but in the dark' recalls the carbuncle, a gem believed in medieval and Renaissance lore to give off its own light in darkness. The paradox continues the poem's pattern of light seen only against shadow: death's worth is visible only from within mortal darkness.

    Psalm 116:15 (KJV); OED, 'carbuncle, n.'; Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  9. outlook that mark l. 20

    Here 'outlook' is a verb meaning to look beyond or out-see (OED, outlook v.), not the modern noun. 'Mark' is a limit or boundary, as in a target or a land-mark: death's dust is the line beyond which mortal sight cannot pass. The conditional 'Could man' admits the limit even as the poem goes on, in the next stanzas, to imagine ways round it.

    OED, 'outlook, v.' and 'mark, n.'; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes

  10. the fledged bird's nest ll. 21-4

    A 'fledged' bird has its flight feathers, and so has left the nest. The empty nest is the body or grave, from which we know the soul is gone; but where it 'sings' now, by what 'fair well' (spring, pool) or grove, we cannot know. The bird as soul is an ancient emblem, and Vaughan's countryman's precision about nests gives it fresh life. The stanza's tone is gentle and wondering rather than grieving.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  11. Angels in some brighter dreams ll. 25-8

    The most famous biblical dream of angels is Jacob's ladder, 'the angels of God ascending and descending on it' (Genesis 28:12), and Scripture often has angels speak in dreams (Matthew 1:20). The stanza claims that waking thought, at moments, does the same: 'strange thoughts transcend our wonted themes' (usual subjects) 'And into glory peep'. The homely verb 'peep' keeps the claim modest: a glimpse, not a vision.

    Genesis 28:12; Matthew 1:20 (KJV); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655)

  12. a star confined into a tomb ll. 29-32

    The image draws on the idea of the soul as a spark or star of divine fire imprisoned in the body, familiar from Platonic and Hermetic thought. Vaughan's twin brother Thomas was a Hermetic philosopher and alchemist, and critics have traced such ideas through Silex Scintillans, whose title emblem shows a flinty heart struck to give sparks. 'The hand that lock'd her up' is God's; when he 'gives room' the star will fill 'all the sphere', the whole heavens. Critics differ on how much weight to give the Hermetic background as against more orthodox Christian sources.

    Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982); L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655)

  13. Resume l. 35

    From Latin resumere, 'to take up again', 'resume' here means to take back what was given, as RPO glosses it. The prayer echoes Ecclesiastes 12:7: 'the spirit shall return unto God who gave it'. Some readers take 'Thy spirit' as the speaker's own soul, which is God's because given by him; others as God's spirit dwelling in the speaker. Either way the stanza turns elegy into a longing for release.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; Ecclesiastes 12:7 (KJV); OED, 'resume, v.'

  14. world of thrall ... true liberty ll. 35-6

    'Thrall' is servitude or captivity (OED, thrall n.). The contrast of this 'world of thrall' and 'true liberty' answers the opening 'world of light' and recalls Romans 8:21, where creation 'shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God'. Readers attentive to Vaughan's Royalist and Anglican loyalties also hear a political note: written after the defeat of the King and the suppression of the Church of England, the poem's longing for 'true liberty' has an earthly as well as a heavenly edge.

    Romans 8:21 (KJV); OED, 'thrall, n.'; Jonathan F. S. Post, Henry Vaughan: The Unfolding Vision (Princeton, 1982)

  15. perspective l. 38

    Stressed on the first syllable, 'perspective' here means an optical instrument, a telescope (RPO; OED, perspective n.). Mists 'blot and fill' the lens as they drift past. The image is up to date (the telescope was a recent instrument) and spiritually resonant, recalling Paul's 'For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face' (1 Corinthians 13:12).

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'They are all gone into the world of light!', ed. N. J. Endicott, with glosses; OED, 'perspective, n.'; 1 Corinthians 13:12 (KJV)

  16. that hill, Where I shall need no glass ll. 39-40

    The final alternative: either clear my sight, or remove me 'unto that hill' where sight needs no help. The hill is Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem (Hebrews 12:22; Psalm 15:1, 'who shall dwell in thy holy hill?'), and it answers the earthly hill dressed in fading light in stanza 2. 'Need no glass' completes the Pauline allusion of 1 Corinthians 13:12: in heaven we see 'face to face'. The poem thus closes on direct vision, the opposite of the 'cloudy breast' of its opening.

    Hebrews 12:22; Psalm 15:1; 1 Corinthians 13:12 (KJV); Alan Rudrum, ed., Henry Vaughan: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1976; rev. 1983), notes; L. C. Martin, ed., The Works of Henry Vaughan, 2nd ed. (Oxford, 1957), text and commentary on Silex Scintillans (1655)
