Paul Revere's Ride

by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow 1807–1882

Nature

Listen, my children, and you shall hear
Of the midnight ride of Paul Revere,
On the eighteenth of April, in Seventy-five;
Hardly a man is now alive
Who remembers that famous day and year.

He said to his friend, “If the British march
By land or sea from the town to-night,
Hang a lantern aloft in the belfry arch
Of the North Church tower as a signal light,—
One, if by land, and two, if by sea;
And I on the opposite shore will be,
Ready to ride and spread the alarm
Through every Middlesex village and farm
For the country folk to be up and to arm,”

Then he said, “Good night!” and with muffled oar
Silently rowed to the Charlestown shore,
Just as the moon rose over the bay,
Where swinging wide at her moorings lay
The Somerset, British man-of-war;
A phantom ship, with each mast and spar
Across the moon like a prison bar,
And a huge black hulk, that was magnified
By its own reflection in the tide.

Meanwhile, his friend, through alley and street,
Wanders and watches with eager ears,
Till in the silence around him he hears
The muster of men at the barrack door,
The sound of arms, and the tramp of feet,
And the measured tread of the grenadiers,
Marching down to their boats on the shore.

Then he climbed the tower of the Old North Church,
By the wooden stairs, with stealthy tread,
To the belfry-chamber overhead,
And startled the pigeons from their perch
On the sombre rafters, that round him made
Masses and moving shapes of shade,—
By the trembling ladder, steep and tall
To the highest window in the wall,
Where he paused to listen and look down
A moment on the roofs of the town,
And the moonlight flowing over all.

Beneath, in the churchyard, lay the dead,
In their night-encampment on the hill,
Wrapped in silence so deep and still
That he could hear, like a sentinel’s tread,
The watchful night-wind, as it went
Creeping along from tent to tent
And seeming to whisper, “All is well!”
A moment only he feels the spell
Of the place and the hour, and the secret dread
Of the lonely belfry and the dead;
For suddenly all his thoughts are bent
On a shadowy something far away,
Where the river widens to meet the bay,—
A line of black that bends and floats
On the rising tide, like a bridge of boats.

Meanwhile, impatient to mount and ride,
Booted and spurred, with a heavy stride
On the opposite shore walked Paul Revere.
Now he patted his horse’s side,
Now gazed at the landscape far and near,
Then, impetuous, stamped the earth,
And turned and tightened his saddle-girth;
But mostly he watched with eager search
The belfry-tower of the Old North Church,
As it rose above the graves on the hill,
Lonely and spectral and sombre and still.
And lo! as he looks, on the belfry’s height
A glimmer, and then a gleam of light!
He springs to the saddle, the bridle he turns,
But lingers and gazes, till full on his sight
A second lamp in the belfry burns!

A hurry of hoofs in a village street,
A shape in the moonlight, a bulk in the dark,
And beneath, from the pebbles, in passing, a spark
Struck out by a steed flying fearless and fleet:
That was all! And yet, through the gloom and the light,
The fate of a nation was riding that night;
And the spark struck out by that steed, in his flight,
Kindled the land into flame with its heat.
He has left the village and mounted the steep,
And beneath him, tranquil and broad and deep,
Is the Mystic, meeting the ocean tides;
And under the alders, that skirt its edge,
Now soft on the sand, now loud on the ledge,
Is heard the tramp of his steed as he rides.

It was twelve by the village clock
When he crossed the bridge into Medford town.
He heard the crowing of the cock,
And the barking of the farmer’s dog,
And felt the damp of the river fog,
That rises after the sun goes down.

It was one by the village clock,
When he galloped into Lexington.
He saw the gilded weathercock
Swim in the moonlight as he passed,
And the meeting-house windows, blank and bare,
Gaze at him with a spectral glare,
As if they already stood aghast
At the bloody work they would look upon.

It was two by the village clock,
When he came to the bridge in Concord town.
He heard the bleating of the flock,
And the twitter of birds among the trees,
And felt the breath of the morning breeze
Blowing over the meadows brown.
And one was safe and asleep in his bed
Who at the bridge would be first to fall,
Who that day would be lying dead,
Pierced by a British musket-ball.

You know the rest. In the books you have read,
How the British Regulars fired and fled,—
How the farmers gave them ball for ball,
From behind each fence and farm-yard wall,
Chasing the red-coats down the lane,
Then crossing the fields to emerge again
Under the trees at the turn of the road,
And only pausing to fire and load.

So through the night rode Paul Revere;
And so through the night went his cry of alarm
To every Middlesex village and farm,—
A cry of defiance and not of fear,
A voice in the darkness, a knock at the door,
And a word that shall echo forevermore!
For, borne on the night-wind of the Past,
Through all our history, to the last,
In the hour of darkness and peril and need,
The people will waken and listen to hear
The hurrying hoof-beats of that steed,
And the midnight message of Paul Revere.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication l. 1

    Longfellow visited the Old North Church on 5 April 1860 and began the poem the next day. It appeared in the January 1861 Atlantic Monthly (issued 20 December 1860) and was reprinted in the Boston Evening Transcript; in Tales of a Wayside Inn (1863) it became 'The Landlord's Tale', the first story told by guests at the Wayside Inn in Sudbury. The storyteller's opening address to 'my children' frames the poem as oral tradition passed down the generations, which is exactly the national memory it set out to create. The poem made Revere a national hero: his 1818 obituary had not even mentioned the ride.

    'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  2. 'Hardly a man is now alive' l. 4

    The claim was nearly literal: Jonathan Harrington, the fifer at Lexington, the last survivor of the battle on the green, died in 1854. With living memory ending, the speaker takes on the task of transmission. The line also concedes, quietly, that what follows is not eyewitness testimony but a retelling, a point relevant to the poem's famous historical liberties. Longfellow knew he was making legend, and historians such as Fischer have shown how much of Revere's popular image derives from this poem rather than from the documents.

    'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  3. 18 April 1775 l. 3

    General Thomas Gage sent some 700 regulars under Lieutenant Colonel Francis Smith to destroy arms and powder stored at Concord, and Boston's patriots, warned of the plan, sent riders to alert the countryside and the leaders Samuel Adams and John Hancock at Lexington. The fighting at Lexington and Concord on 19 April began the American War of Independence. 'Seventy-five' is the colloquial way of naming the year, as of a date everyone knows.

    David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994); Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House

  4. 'One, if by land, and two, if by sea' l. 10

    In fact Revere, already knowing the route, asked that lanterns be shown in the steeple to warn patriots across the river in Charlestown in case he could not get out of Boston; two lanterns meant that the troops would cross the Charles by boat ('by sea') rather than march out over Boston Neck ('by land'). The 'friend' who hung them was probably Robert Newman, sexton of Christ Church. Longfellow made Revere the receiver of the signal so that the climactic moment could be his. Revere's own account to Jeremy Belknap describes the arrangement.

    Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House; Paul Revere, letter to Jeremy Belknap, c. 1798, Massachusetts Historical Society; 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  5. muffled oar l. 15

    To 'muffle' oars is to wrap them where they rest in the rowlocks so that they make no noise (OED). Revere did not row himself: two companions rowed him from the North End across the Charles to Charlestown, past a British warship. Longfellow's single hero, rowing silently, is again a concentration of a collective effort into one figure. Muffling itself is true to the night: secrecy and silence were essential, since the boat had to pass close to a British warship.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'muffle'; Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House; 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia)

  6. the Somerset ll. 19-23

    The Somerset was a large Royal Navy warship (a 'man-of-war') stationed between Boston and Charlestown. Longfellow turns her into a Gothic image: a 'phantom ship', her masts 'like a prison bar' across the moon, a black hulk magnified by its reflection. The prison image makes British power a jailer of the colony. Historically the moon had risen and the boat passed close to the ship without being challenged.

    'HMS Somerset (1748)', Wikipedia; Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House

  7. grenadiers l. 29

    By 1775 grenadiers were the tall picked men of each British regiment, chosen for strength rather than for throwing grenades. Gage's expedition was made up of the grenadier and light infantry companies drawn from several regiments, which were assembled on Boston Common and taken across the Back Bay in boats. The poem's 'muster', 'sound of arms' and 'measured tread' build suspense by sound.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grenadier'; David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  8. the Old North Church l. 31

    Christ Church, now called Old North, was an Anglican church whose steeple was visible across the harbour to Charlestown, which is why it was chosen for the signal. The poem made the church a shrine: and a granite tablet commemorating the signal lanterns was added to its facade in 1878. Longfellow's visit to the tower in April 1860 is the likely source of the climb, with its startled pigeons, sombre rafters and 'trembling ladder'.

    Old North Church and Historic Site, Boston (Christ Church in the City of Boston); 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia)

  9. the dead in their night-encampment ll. 42-48

    Below the church lies Copp's Hill Burying Ground, where early Bostonians are buried. Longfellow extends a military conceit: the dead are encamped on the hill, the graves are tents, and the night-wind 'creeping along from tent to tent' plays sentinel, whispering the watchman's call 'All is well!'. The passage pauses the action for a moment of eerie stillness and links the coming fight to the ancestral dead, a typical Longfellow move from narrative into meditation.

    David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994); 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia)

  10. a bridge of boats l. 56

    A bridge of boats is a pontoon bridge, a military bridge of boats lashed side by side; the dark line on the tide is the flotilla carrying the troops across the Back Bay toward Cambridge. The simile is the moment of recognition: the troops are going 'by sea', so two lanterns must be hung. The image also continues the poem's pattern of seeing danger as shapes in the dark, a 'shadowy something far away', before it is identified.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bridge of boats'; David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  11. Suspense and tense l. 72

    Longfellow alternates the narrative past ('Then he climbed') with the dramatic present ('he looks', 'He springs'), so the climax happens before the reader's eyes. The stanza delays: Revere 'lingers and gazes' after the first glimmer until the second lamp appears. Critics note the poem's cinematic intercutting between the friend in the tower and Revere on the shore. The repeated 'Meanwhile' marks the cross-cutting. Revere's impatience (he pats his horse, stamps, tightens the girth) is rendered in a quick list of actions while he waits.

    'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia)

  12. The galloping metre l. 73

    The metre is basically a four-stress line in which two unstressed syllables often fall between stresses ('a HURry of HOOFS in a VILLage STREET'), so the rhythm runs like hoofbeats. Rhymes come in couplets and clusters rather than fixed stanzas, and the stanzas vary in length with the action. Readers have long heard the gallop in the cadence, and the strong rhythm made the poem a classroom recitation piece for over a century.

  13. The spark and the nation ll. 75-80

    The image is double: a literal spark from iron on stone, and the 'spark' of rebellion that sets the country ablaze. 'The fate of a nation' shows Longfellow's purpose, to make a founding myth. Writing in 1860, as the Union was splitting over slavery, Longfellow could expect readers to hear another nation's fate in the balance. The repetition of 'spark struck out by that steed' within four lines insists on the image, and the alliteration of 'flying fearless and fleet' speeds the line.

    'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); Jill Lepore, 'Paul Revere's Ride Against Slavery', New York Times, December 2010, and her 2012 essay 'Longfellow's Ride' (as cited in the Wikipedia article)

  14. the Mystic ... Medford town ll. 81-86

    After a British patrol blocked the direct road, Revere rode by a northern route through Medford, crossing the Mystic River, and alarmed the captain of the Medford minutemen before turning west to Lexington. The name, from an Algonquian word for a large tidal river, is a gift to the poem's atmosphere. The stanza slows: 'tranquil and broad and deep', with hooves 'now soft on the sand, now loud on the ledge', a contrast of silence and noise that runs through the whole poem.

    David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994); Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House

  15. The clock refrain ll. 87, 93, 101

    The refrain gives the ride a ritual structure (Medford at twelve, Lexington at one, Concord at two), each with its own village sounds. In fact Revere reached Lexington around midnight, and he never reached Concord: after leaving Lexington with William Dawes and Samuel Prescott he was captured by a British patrol; only Prescott got through to Concord. Modern critics regularly point to these liberties, and Fischer's history documents the real ride in detail.

    Paul Revere Memorial Association, 'The Real Story of Paul Revere's Ride', Paul Revere House; 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  16. the meeting-house windows ll. 97-100

    A New England 'meeting-house' was the Congregational church, used also for town meetings. The personification ('Gaze at him with a spectral glare') anticipates the morning, when eight militiamen were killed on Lexington Green, in front of the meeting-house. Prolepsis, the foreshadowing of future events in the narrative present, is the stanza's device. The weathercock that 'swims' in the moonlight is a precise detail of the gilded vane, and the windows are 'blank and bare' like the eyes of the dead.

    David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'meeting-house'

  17. the bridge in Concord ll. 102-110

    At the North Bridge on the morning of 19 April the British fired on the advancing militia, killing Captain Isaac Davis of the Acton minutemen and Abner Hosmer, and the militia's return fire was the 'shot heard round the world' of Emerson's 'Concord Hymn' (1837). Longfellow's sleeper who will be 'first to fall' is generic, but the details fit Davis. The tender image of the man 'safe and asleep in his bed' heightens the pathos of the morning.

    'Isaac Davis (soldier)', Wikipedia; David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994); R. W. Emerson, 'Concord Hymn' (1837)

  18. 'You know the rest' ll. 111-118

    The phrase assumes a shared national story: the British 'fired and fled', and the militia harried them all the way back to Charlestown, firing 'ball for ball' from behind fences and stone walls. The retreat cost the British about 270 casualties. Longfellow's picture of farmers fighting from cover became the standard image of the minuteman. The brisk summary, after the long build-up, shows where Longfellow's interest lay: in the warning and the waking of the people rather than in the fighting. 'The books you have read' assigns the battle to history, the ride to poetry.

    David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)

  19. The poem's purpose in 1860 ll. 125-130

    Longfellow, an opponent of slavery since Poems on Slavery (1842), published the poem weeks after Lincoln's election and as South Carolina was voting to secede (20 December 1860). 'The hour of darkness and peril and need' points beyond 1775 to the crisis of the Union. Jill Lepore has argued that the poem is in an important sense about slavery and John Brown, whose raid and execution (1859) Longfellow noted in his journal; others read it as a call to unity rather than to arms. The 'night-wind of the Past' makes history a living voice that 'shall echo forevermore'.

    Jill Lepore, 'Paul Revere's Ride Against Slavery', New York Times, December 2010, and her 2012 essay 'Longfellow's Ride' (as cited in the Wikipedia article); 'Paul Revere's Ride', Wikipedia (composition, publication and reception, with references to Fischer, Lepore and Gioia); David Hackett Fischer, Paul Revere's Ride (Oxford UP, 1994)
