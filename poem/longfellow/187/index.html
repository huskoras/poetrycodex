The Tide Rises, The Tide Falls

by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow 1807–1882

Nature

The tide rises, the tide falls,
The twilight darkens, the curlew calls;
Along the sea-sands damp and brown
The traveller hastens toward the town,
And the tide rises, the tide falls.
Darkness settles on roofs and walls,
But the sea in the darkness calls and calls;
The little waves, with their soft, white hands,
Efface the footprints in the sands,
And the tide rises, the tide falls.
The morning breaks; the steeds in their stalls
Stamp and neigh, as the hostler calls;
The day returns, but nevermore
Returns the traveller to the shore,
And the tide rises, the tide falls.
SONNETS

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date and collection l. 1

    Longfellow wrote the poem in 1879, in his early seventies and in failing health, and published it in Ultima Thule (1880). The title of that collection is Latin for the farthest land, the most remote northern island of classical geography, and so already points to the end of life and the edge of the known world. The poem is a short lyric of mortality: a traveller passes along the shore at dusk and does not return, while the tide continues as before. Longfellow died in March 1882.

    Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, Ultima Thule (Boston: Houghton, Mifflin, 1880); Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'; Charles C. Calhoun, Longfellow: A Rediscovered Life (Beacon Press, 2004)

  2. Refrain and rhyme l. 5

    The three stanzas rhyme AABBA. The A-rhyme on '-alls' (falls, calls, walls, stalls) is the same in every stanza, and the refrain opens the poem and closes each stanza. This economy of two rhymes per stanza, with a returning refrain, resembles the French rondeau, though Longfellow does not follow that form strictly. The effect is incantatory: the refrain's repeated rhythm imitates the regular wash of the tide, and its return after each human event (dusk, night, morning) asserts nature's indifferent continuity.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'; Lewis Turco, The Book of Forms (UP of New England, 3rd ed. 2000), s.v. 'rondeau'

  3. curlew l. 2

    The curlew is a long-legged shorebird of mudflats and sea-sands, named, it is often said, from its cry. Its plaintive whistle is a stock image of loneliness in poetry, and in British folklore curlews and related birds (the 'seven whistlers') were sometimes thought to foretell death. Here its call at twilight adds sound and foreboding to the darkening scene, and it anticipates the sea that 'calls and calls' in the next stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'curlew'; Edward A. Armstrong, The Folklore of Birds (Collins, 1958)

  4. The traveller l. 4

    The traveller is never named or described, so he becomes representative, like the pilgrim of allegory, any person crossing the shore of life. His haste toward the town suggests the urge to reach shelter before dark. The poem never says what happens to him; the last stanza simply records that he does not return. The sea-sands 'damp and brown' are the margin between land and sea, a traditional threshold between life and death.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'; Charles C. Calhoun, Longfellow: A Rediscovered Life (2004)

  5. the sea ... calls and calls l. 7

    The doubled verb makes the call insistent and unending. The sea, traditionally a figure of eternity and death, summons through the darkness. Tennyson's 'Crossing the Bar' (1889), written ten years later, uses a similar image of death as 'one clear call' at evening, with the tide and the sea close by, a useful comparison for students. Longfellow's sea is less consoling: it calls, but nothing is said about what lies beyond.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'; Alfred Tennyson, 'Crossing the Bar' (1889)

  6. soft, white hands l. 8

    The personification makes the little waves gentle, almost maternal or ghostly (white hands), and the gentleness makes the erasure more poignant. The action they perform is complete obliteration of the traveller's trace. Death and forgetting arrive not violently but as a soft, routine smoothing of the sand. The word 'little' adds to the effect: it takes no great power to erase a human life's traces.

    Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'

  7. Efface the footprints l. 9

    In 'A Psalm of Life' (1838), one of the most quoted poems of the century, the young Longfellow wrote that great lives leave 'Footprints on the sands of time' for others to follow. In this late poem the footprints in the sands are wiped out by the sea. Whether or not the echo is deliberate, the contrast between the two poems shows a shift from the earlier poem's confident moral exhortation to an old man's recognition of oblivion. 'Efface' (literally, to remove the face or surface) is exact for smoothing sand.

    Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 'A Psalm of Life' (1838; collected in Voices of the Night, 1839); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'efface'

  8. steeds ... hostler l. 12

    A hostler, or ostler, is a stableman at an inn (the word derives from 'hosteler', innkeeper). The traveller was perhaps expected at the inn, and his horse waits in the stall, impatient to go. The ordinary morning, with steeds stamping and neighing and the groom calling, shows the world carrying on without him. The third 'calls' of the poem (after the curlew and the sea) is now human and routine.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ostler'; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'

  9. nevermore l. 13

    'Nevermore' is the refrain of Edgar Allan Poe's 'The Raven' (1845), and any nineteenth-century reader would have heard the echo. There is an irony in the borrowing, since Poe had publicly accused Longfellow of plagiarism in the 'Longfellow war' of 1845. The enjambment ('nevermore / Returns the traveller') holds back the verb and makes the absence felt. The day returns but the man does not: cyclical nature against linear human life.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Raven' (1845); Charles C. Calhoun, Longfellow: A Rediscovered Life (2004), on the 'Longfellow war'

  10. Cycle and mortality l. 13

    The structure of the poem sets repeated natural cycles against one unrepeatable human life. The thought is ancient: 'One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever ... All the rivers run into the sea' (Ecclesiastes 1:4-7). Longfellow, who knew the Bible intimately, offers no Christian consolation in the poem: no heaven is mentioned, only the tide. Readers have found this restraint unusually bleak for him, and especially moving in a poem of old age.

    The Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 1:4-7; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto), 'The Tide Rises, the Tide Falls'
