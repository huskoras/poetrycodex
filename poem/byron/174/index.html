Sonnet on Chillon

by Lord Byron 1788–1824

History & Politics

Eternal Spirit of the chainless Mind!
Brightest in dungeons, Liberty! thou art:
For there thy habitation is the heart--
The heart which love of thee alone can bind;
And when thy sons to fetters are consigned--
To fetters, and the damp vault's dayless gloom,
Their country conquers with their martyrdom,
And Freedom's fame finds wings on every wind.
Chillon! thy prison is a holy place,
And thy sad floor an altar--for 'twas trod,
Until his very steps have left a trace
Worn, as if thy cold pavement were a sod,
By Bonnivard!--May none those marks efface!
For they appeal from tyranny to God.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnet on Chillon: date and occasion l. 1

    On their boat tour round Lake Geneva, Byron and Shelley visited Chillon, probably on 26 June 1816, and were held by bad weather at Ouchy near Lausanne, where Byron wrote The Prisoner of Chillon (E. H. Coleridge). The sonnet was prefixed to the poem. Byron confessed in an 'Advertisement' that when he wrote the tale he knew too little of Bonivard's history to celebrate his courage and virtues, and he appended a French account of his life. The sonnet therefore makes Bonivard a public martyr of liberty, while the narrative poem dwells on the psychological ruin of an imprisoned man. The first draft opened 'Belovéd Goddess of the chainless mind!'

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1986), commentary on the Sonnet on Chillon

  2. the chainless Mind l. 1

    The idea is a Stoic and Christian commonplace, given its best-known English form in Richard Lovelace's 'To Althea, from Prison' (published 1649): 'Stone walls do not a prison make, / Nor iron bars a cage.' Byron's opening apostrophe addresses Liberty as an 'Eternal Spirit', giving the political ideal a quasi-divine status. The sonnet's argument is that imprisonment makes freedom shine brighter, a paradox the narrative poem it introduces largely contradicts.

    Richard Lovelace, 'To Althea, from Prison' (1649); E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet

  3. 'Brightest in dungeons' l. 2

    The paradox of light in darkness runs through the octave, set against 'the damp vault's dayless gloom' (l. 6). The inversion ('Brightest in dungeons, Liberty! thou art') delays the subject, so that the paradox comes first. Liberty's 'habitation' is the heart, a word with biblical overtones of God's dwelling-place (e.g. Psalm 26:8). Liberty is thus imagined as a deity whose temple is the heart, preparing for the sestet's sacred imagery of altar and holy place.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 26:8

  4. 'The heart which love of thee alone can bind' l. 4

    Byron turns the vocabulary of imprisonment (bind, fetters) against itself: the heart is bound only by love of freedom. The repetition 'the heart-- / The heart' and 'to fetters... To fetters' gives the octave a rhetorical, oratorical quality, like a speech in a liberal cause.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet

  5. the damp vault's dayless gloom l. 6

    Byron's own note describes the castle's range of dungeons beneath the lake level, the pillars with rings for fetters, and a beam on which prisoners were executed. 'Dayless' is a strong compound meaning without daylight. The Prisoner of Chillon itself describes the light that enters through a crevice and the sound of the lake beating against the walls.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dayless, adj.'

  6. martyrdom l. 7

    A martyr (Greek martys, witness) testifies to a faith by suffering or death. Byron transfers the Christian idea to political liberty: the imprisoned patriot's endurance spreads 'Freedom's fame' on every wind. The language anticipates the sestet's religious imagery, where the prison becomes a holy place and its floor an altar. Bonivard did not die at Chillon; he was released in 1536 and lived on until 1570.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'martyr, n.'; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet

  7. Freedom's winged fame l. 8

    Virgil's Fama (Aeneid IV.173-188) is a swift winged monster who spreads report through cities. Byron's alliteration ('Freedom's fame finds') gives the line the ring of a rallying cry. The octave ends here; the poem turns with the direct address to Chillon. The image fits Byron's own situation in 1816, a celebrity exile whose fame travelled across Europe, though the sonnet keeps the focus on the prisoner's cause.

    Virgil, Aeneid IV.173-188; E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet

  8. the sonnet's form l. 9

    The octave rhymes Mind/bind/consigned/wind against art/heart and gloom/martyrdom, varying the usual abbaabba; the sestet alternates place/trace/efface with trod/sod/God. The volta at line 9 moves from the general to the particular, from Liberty to Chillon and its prisoner. The sestet runs on by enjambment across 'trod, / Until' and 'a sod, / By Bonnivard!', so that the name falls with emphasis at the start of line 13.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1986), commentary on the Sonnet on Chillon

  9. a holy place, an altar ll. 9-10

    God tells Moses at the burning bush, 'the place whereon thou standest is holy ground' (Exodus 3:5). The altar recalls also the martyrs of Revelation 6:9-10, the souls 'under the altar' crying for justice against their oppressors, which anticipates the appeal to God in the final line. Byron's description makes Chillon a shrine of the liberal cause; the castle did become a destination for Romantic tourists, partly because of Byron.

    The Bible (KJV), Exodus 3:5; The Bible (KJV), Revelation 6:9-10

  10. the worn pavement ll. 10-12

    Byron's note records that 'in the pavement the steps of Bonnivard have left their traces'. The simile 'as if thy cold pavement were a sod' (turf) compares the trodden stone to a worn footpath in grass.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet

  11. Bonnivard l. 13

    Bonivard supported Genevan independence against the Dukes of Savoy. He was seized in 1530 and confined in Chillon until the castle was taken by the Bernese in March 1536. 'Bonnivard' is Byron's spelling. After his release he became a citizen of Geneva, a chronicler and a member of its councils. Byron's tale invents brothers who die beside him, and the French account Byron printed as an appendix is itself partly unhistorical.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 4 (Oxford, 1986), commentary on the Sonnet on Chillon

  12. 'appeal from tyranny to God' ll. 13-14

    To appeal is to take a case to a higher court. Byron's prayer that none should efface the marks makes them permanent evidence against the oppressor. The final line joins political and religious language: liberty's martyrs are vindicated not by men but by God, echoing the martyrs under the altar in Revelation who ask how long their blood will go unjudged.

    E. H. Coleridge, ed., The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. 4 (1901), introduction to The Prisoner of Chillon, Advertisement and notes to the sonnet; The Bible (KJV), Revelation 6:10
