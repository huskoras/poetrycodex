Time & Brevity

So, We’ll Go No More a Roving

by Lord Byron 1788–1824

Time & BrevityLiving

So, we’ll go no more a roving
So late into the night,
Though the heart be still as loving,
And the moon be still as bright.

For the sword outwears its sheath,
And the soul wears out the breast,
And the heart must pause to breathe,
And love itself have rest.

Though the night was made for loving,
And the day returns too soon,
Yet we’ll go no more a roving
By the light of the moon.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Weariness in the voice of the age’s great adventurer. The heart must pause, the sword outwears its sheath — Byron admits that even desire has a curfew.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The occasion: Venice, end of Carnival 1817 l. 1

    Byron told Moore that the latter part of the Carnival, and 'sitting up late o' nights', had 'knocked me up a little', and that now it was Lent, 'with all its abstinence and sacred music'. He added that he found 'the sword wearing out the scabbard' though he had 'but just turned the corner of twenty-nine', and copied out the lyric. Moore printed it in his Letters and Journals of Lord Byron (1830), so it was posthumous. The tone is half-mocking (an invalid regimen after dissipation) and half-elegiac (the end of youthful roving). McGann's Oxford edition is the standard text.

    Thomas Moore, Letters and Journals of Lord Byron, with Notices of his Life (1830), letter to Moore, Venice, 28 February 1817 (Life of Lord Byron, vol. 3); Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986); Wikipedia, 'So, we'll go no more a roving'

  2. a roving l. 1

    'A-' is the old prefix meaning 'on' or 'engaged in' (as in 'a-hunting'; OED, 'a-' prep.). 'Rove' means to wander without fixed aim, and from the seventeenth century also to range amorously (OED, 'rove'). The phrasing echoes the refrain of 'The Jolly Beggar' (Child 279), printed in David Herd's Scottish Songs (1776), in which a beggar who has seduced a farmer's daughter vows to go no more a-roving by night. Byron's poem keeps the song's simplicity and its night setting but turns the vow from bravado into weariness.

    Wikipedia, 'The Jolly Beggar' (Child 279; in David Herd's Scottish Songs, 1776); Oxford English Dictionary, 'rove'; Wikipedia, 'So, we'll go no more a roving'

  3. late into the night l. 2

    In the letter Byron blames the late nights of Carnival's last weeks. Venice's Carnival ran until Shrove Tuesday, after which Lent imposed abstinence; the lyric's renunciation thus has a calendar as well as a physical cause. The repetition of 'So' links this line to the opening. The phrase also hints at lateness in life: at twenty-nine Byron presents himself, half ironically, as a man whose time for revelry is over.

    Thomas Moore, Letters and Journals of Lord Byron, with Notices of his Life (1830), letter to Moore, Venice, 28 February 1817 (Life of Lord Byron, vol. 3)

  4. still as loving l. 3

    The concessive 'Though' introduces the poem's central opposition: the heart's capacity for love and the moon's brightness are undiminished, yet the roving must stop. 'Still' (OED, 'still', adv., 'even now, as before') recurs in line 4, and the slight sense of stillness or quiet is apt to the mood. The feminine rhymes (roving/loving) give the lines a falling, song-like cadence.

    Oxford English Dictionary, 'still'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986)

  5. the moon l. 4

    The moon is unaltered by human fatigue: nature keeps its brightness while the reveller tires. It returns in the final 'By the light of the moon', giving the lyric a circular shape. Byron's moon here is the lovers' moon of song and serenade rather than the moon of Romantic contemplation. Poets often contrast the constancy of nature with human change; here the contrast is lightly made, without lament, and the moon remains an invitation the speaker declines.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986); Wikipedia, 'So, we'll go no more a roving'

  6. the sword outwears its sheath l. 5

    In the letter Byron quotes the saying as 'the sword wearing out the scabbard'. The sheath is the body and the sword the soul or passion within it; the image also carries an obvious sexual undertone suited to the theme of roving. The second stanza is built of four parallel clauses (sword/sheath, soul/breast, heart/breathe, love/rest), each a version of the same idea of energy exhausting its vessel. 'Sheath' and 'breathe' make a near rhyme: the vowels match, but the final th is voiceless in one and voiced in the other.

    Thomas Moore, Letters and Journals of Lord Byron, with Notices of his Life (1830), letter to Moore, Venice, 28 February 1817 (Life of Lord Byron, vol. 3); Oxford English Dictionary, 'sheath'

  7. the soul wears out the breast l. 6

    The parallel with line 5 makes the soul a blade and the breast its scabbard. The thought belongs to a long tradition of the soul as imprisoned in or wearing out the body, and it gives Byron's personal weariness a philosophical generality. Byron was twenty-nine; the poem's prematurely aged tone is part of his self-dramatization. 'Breast' as the seat of feeling also prepares 'heart' in the next line, so the stanza moves inward, from sword to soul to heart to love.

    Thomas Moore, Letters and Journals of Lord Byron, with Notices of his Life (1830), letter to Moore, Venice, 28 February 1817 (Life of Lord Byron, vol. 3); Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986)

  8. love itself have rest ll. 7-8

    'Must' governs both lines: 'the heart must pause to breathe, / And love itself [must] have rest'. Personified Love resting suggests the end of passion, the Lenten fast after Carnival, and perhaps death. The understatement is typical of the lyric's restraint; there is no named beloved and no narrative. Moore's text of the letter capitalizes 'Love', which makes the personification of Love as a god (Amor or Cupid) more explicit than in this text.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986); Thomas Moore, Letters and Journals of Lord Byron, with Notices of his Life (1830), letter to Moore, Venice, 28 February 1817 (Life of Lord Byron, vol. 3)

  9. the night was made for loving l. 9

    The line sounds like an invitation in a song of seduction; the next line laments, in the manner of an aubade, that day returns too soon. Lovers' complaints against the dawn go back to Ovid's Amores 1.13 and appear in Romeo and Juliet 3.5 ('It was the nightingale, and not the lark'). Byron cites the tradition only to step back from it with 'Yet'.

    Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 3.5 (MIT Complete Works); Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986)

  10. the closing line l. 12

    The lyric's three quatrains are in a loose trimeter with alternating feminine and masculine rhymes (roving/loving, night/bright). The final line (de-de-DUM-de-de-DUM) has the lilt of a folk refrain. Its simplicity has made the poem a favourite for musical setting, from nineteenth-century songs to Joan Baez and Leonard Cohen ('Go No More A-Roving', 2004). The last stanza echoes the first, repeating the renunciation as a refrain ('Yet we'll go no more a roving'), so the lyric ends where it began.

    Wikipedia, 'So, we'll go no more a roving'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. IV (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1986)
