When We Two Parted

by Lord Byron 1788–1824

LoveRelationships

When we two parted
In silence and tears,
Half broken-hearted
To sever for years,
Pale grew thy cheek and cold,
Colder thy kiss;
Truly that hour foretold
Sorrow to this.

The dew of the morning
Sunk chill on my brow—
It felt like the warning
Of what I feel now.
Thy vows are all broken,
And light is thy fame:
I hear thy name spoken,
And share in its shame.

They name thee before me,
A knell to mine ear;
A shudder comes o’er me—
Why wert thou so dear?
They know not I knew thee,
Who knew thee too well:—
Long, long shall I rue thee,
Too deeply to tell.

In secret we met—
In silence I grieve,
That thy heart could forget,
Thy spirit deceive.
If I should meet thee
After long years,
How should I greet thee?—
With silence and tears.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A poem of secret grief. Because the love was hidden, so must the mourning be; Byron greets a lost lover’s name years later ‘with silence and tears.’

Study notes by the editors

  1. Date, dedicatee and the false date l. 1

    The lyric first appeared in Poems (London: John Murray, 1816), dated '1808' at its foot. In a letter to his cousin Lady Hardy (1823) Byron identified the subject as Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, with whom he had a passionate but probably unconsummated flirtation in autumn 1813, recorded in his letters and journal. By 1815-16 her name was being linked in the press with the Duke of Wellington, which explains 'light is thy fame' and 'share in its shame'. McGann argues that the poem was probably written in 1815-16, and that the false date shielded the lady and blurred Byron's own situation in the year of his separation scandal. Some critics, however, accept an earlier composition.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'; Byron to Lady Hardy, 17 May 1823, in Leslie A. Marchand, ed., Byron's Letters and Journals, vol. 10 (1980); Leslie A. Marchand, Byron: A Biography (1957), on Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, 1813-16

  2. Metre and stanza st. 1

    The poem uses a song-like accentual metre: each line has two strong stresses with a varying number of light syllables, often dactylic or anapaestic in movement ('When we two parted / In silence and tears'). Rhymes alternate between feminine ('parted / broken-hearted') and masculine ('tears / years'), producing a falling then rising cadence close to Byron's songs in Hebrew Melodies (1815). The brevity of the lines gives the effect of suppressed speech, fitting a poem about silence. The phrase 'In silence and tears' returns at the end, framing the poem.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'

  3. Sever l. 4

    'Sever' (from Old French sevrer, Latin separare) is a stronger word than 'part', suggesting cutting. 'Half broken-hearted' is ambiguous: their hearts were half broken at the parting, or the lovers were only half heartbroken then, compared with the full grief the speaker now feels. The second reading fits the poem's argument that the parting merely 'foretold' the later sorrow. The verb thus prepares the poem's central contrast between the past moment of parting and the present grief.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sever'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'

  4. The parting as omen ll. 7-8

    The first stanza moves from a remembered scene to a present judgement. The cheek grew pale and the kiss cold, signs of fading love, and the speaker now reads these as an omen of the grief that followed. 'Sorrow to this' is compressed: the hour foretold sorrow for this present time. The idea of the omen returns in stanza 2 with the chill dew.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'

  5. Morning dew as warning ll. 9-12

    Cold dew falling on the brow is an image of foreboding and perhaps of bodily chill, as in a fever or a deathly sweat. 'Sunk' (for 'sank') was an acceptable past tense in Byron's day. The stanza's first half places the parting at dawn, a traditional hour for lovers' farewells (the aubade), which deepens the sense of a secret affair ended in the early morning.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sink'

  6. 'light is thy fame' l. 14

    'Fame' here means public reputation, not celebrity. 'Light' combines the senses 'of little worth' and 'frivolous, unchaste' (as in 'light woman'). The phrase points to gossip about the lady's later conduct; in Lady Frances Webster's case, newspaper reports in 1815-16 connecting her with Wellington. The speaker's broken vows and his 'share in its shame' suggest that he too is implicated, either because their secret affair would also be scandalous or because his love makes her disgrace his own.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'light'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fame'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'; Leslie A. Marchand, Byron: A Biography (1957), on Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, 1813-16

  7. Knell l. 18

    A knell (Old English cnyll) is the slow stroke of a bell rung at a death or funeral. The image treats the love as dead and the name, spoken casually by others, as its tolling. 'Mine ear' is a deliberately old-fashioned form before a vowel, appropriate to the song's elevated register. The stanza's question 'Why wert thou so dear?' then turns grief into self-reproach: the speaker cannot explain why the name still hurts so much.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'knell'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'

  8. 'They know not I knew thee, / Who knew thee too well' ll. 21-22

    The repetition of 'know' and 'knew' turns on secrecy. Those who speak her name do not know of the speaker's connection with her. 'Who knew thee too well' may mean that he understood her character all too well, foreseeing her infidelity; it may also play on the biblical sense of 'know' as sexual intimacy. Critics differ on how far the poem implies a physical affair, and the biographical evidence for Lady Frances suggests the relationship was not consummated. The ambiguity is part of the poem's guardedness.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'; The Bible (Authorized King James Version), Genesis 4:1 ('Adam knew Eve his wife'); Leslie A. Marchand, Byron: A Biography (1957), on Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, 1813-16

  9. Rue l. 23

    'Rue' (Old English hreowan) means to feel sorrow or regret, often penitential. To 'rue thee' compresses 'regret having loved thee' and 'mourn for thee'. 'Too deeply to tell' returns to the theme of silence: the grief exceeds speech, which is why the poem itself is so restrained. The rhyme 'rue thee / knew thee' links regret directly to the intimacy that caused it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rue'; Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'

  10. Secrecy and silence ll. 25-26

    The final stanza states the logic of the poem. A clandestine love affords no public mourning, so the speaker 'In silence' grieves. The lines draw a symmetry between the lovers' secret meetings and his concealed sorrow, and between her forgetting heart and deceiving spirit. The poem's reticence, naming no one and explaining nothing, is part of its design. The line 'Thy spirit deceive' completes the charge against the beloved, which the speaker states once and does not elaborate.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'; Byron to Lady Hardy, 17 May 1823, in Leslie A. Marchand, ed., Byron's Letters and Journals, vol. 10 (1980)

  11. The closing refrain l. 32

    The circular structure makes the end repeat the beginning, with the single change from 'In' to 'With'. 'In silence and tears' described the circumstances of the parting; 'With silence and tears' describes the speaker's chosen manner of greeting. The question-and-answer form ('How should I greet thee?') turns the refrain into a resolution: the relationship can be acknowledged only by not speaking. The device resembles the refrains of songs, and the poem has often been set to music.

    Jerome J. McGann, ed., Lord Byron: The Complete Poetical Works, vol. 3 (Oxford, 1981), text and commentary to 'When we two parted'
