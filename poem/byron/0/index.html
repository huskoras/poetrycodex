She Walks in Beauty

by Lord Byron 1788–1824

LoveThe Body

She walks in beauty, like the night
Of cloudless climes and starry skies;
And all that’s best of dark and bright
Meet in her aspect and her eyes;
Thus mellowed to that tender light
Which heaven to gaudy day denies.

One shade the more, one ray the less,
Had half impaired the nameless grace
Which waves in every raven tress,
Or softly lightens o’er her face;
Where thoughts serenely sweet express,
How pure, how dear their dwelling-place.

And on that cheek, and o’er that brow,
So soft, so calm, yet eloquent,
The smiles that win, the tints that glow,
But tell of days in goodness spent,
A mind at peace with all below,
A heart whose love is innocent!

Codex Note

Written after Byron saw his cousin in mourning dress, the lyric balances light and dark in every line, finding a beauty ‘of cloudless climes and starry skies’ that is as much moral serenity as physical grace.

Study notes by the editors

  1. occasion: Mrs Wilmot l. 1

    James Wedderburn Webster recalled taking Byron to Lady Sitwell's party on 11 June 1814, where he first saw 'the beautiful Mrs. Wilmot', in mourning with spangles on her dress; Byron drank a tumbler of brandy to her health and wrote the poem the next day. E. H. Coleridge identifies her as Anne Beatrix Horton, wife of Byron's second cousin Robert John Wilmot. The black dress with points of light may underlie the poem's opening simile of a starry night. 'Walks in beauty' suggests that beauty is an atmosphere she moves through, not only a quality she has.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'

  2. Hebrew Melodies l. 5

    Through Douglas Kinnaird, Byron met the young composer Isaac Nathan and gave him lyrics to set to synagogue melodies; Nathan's folio, with music by Nathan and John Braham, appeared in April 1815, and Murray published the poems separately. Most are biblical, but E. H. Coleridge notes that 'She walks in Beauty' is one of three love poems in a collection whose title is 'somewhat misleading'. Nathan recalled that Byron often asked for this song. The poem was written before the project but placed first.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'

  3. like the night l. 1

    Conventional love poetry compares the beloved to the sun, dawn or the rose. Byron chooses night, which allows the poem to mix dark and bright. The enjambment ('like the night / Of cloudless climes') delays the qualification: it is not any night, but a southern, cloudless one full of stars. The simile matches the occasion of a lady in black spangled mourning.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'

  4. climes l. 2

    'Clime' was a poetic word for region or zone (from Greek klima, a zone of latitude). Byron, who had travelled in Greece and the Levant (1809-11), uses it often in his Eastern poems. The alliteration of 'cloudless climes' and 'starry skies' gives the second line its balanced music. The geographical word quietly brings into a London drawing-room the exotic settings of Byron's verse tales.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clime'

  5. dark and bright l. 3

    The line announces the structure of the whole lyric: the contrast of dark and light (night/stars, raven hair/lightened face) and their reconciliation. 'Aspect' means her look or appearance, both face and bearing. The balance is aesthetic and moral: the final stanza claims that this outward harmony expresses inward goodness. Some critics see a neoclassical ideal of balance in a poet usually associated with Romantic extremes.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'aspect'

  6. gaudy day l. 6

    'Gaudy' means showy, tastelessly bright. In 2 Henry VI 4.1 the scene opens with 'The gaudy, blabbing, and remorseful day / Is crept into the bosom of the sea'. Byron's 'tender light' is the light of evening or night that heaven 'denies' to day: day is too bright to have it. The phrase reverses the usual preference for daylight. The contrast is of tone rather than of value.

    Shakespeare, 2 Henry VI 4.1.1-2, Folger Shakespeare; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gaudy'

  7. One shade the more ... l. 7

    The line is a perfect example of Byron's antithetical style, with the two halves mirroring each other. It suggests beauty as precise proportion, a classical idea. 'Had half impaired' means 'would have half spoiled': the conditional is in older syntax (pluperfect subjunctive). The idea of beauty as a perfect mean, where any addition or subtraction would spoil the whole, goes back to classical aesthetics, and the line performs that balance in its own symmetry.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'

  8. nameless grace l. 8

    The idea of a grace beyond rules or description goes back to classical rhetoric and was a commonplace of eighteenth-century criticism. Pope's Essay on Criticism (1711) says that in music and poetry there are 'nameless graces which no methods teach, / And which a master hand alone can reach'. Byron admired Pope greatly. The grace 'waves' in her hair and 'lightens' over her face: it is a movement, not a fixed feature.

    Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism (1711), Part I ('nameless graces which no methods teach')

  9. raven tress l. 9

    The black of the hair supplies the 'dark' element of the balance, and the lightening of the face the 'bright'. 'Tress' is a poetic word for a lock of hair. 'Raven' as an adjective of colour is conventional in romance. The verbs 'waves' and 'lightens' give the grace motion, so that her beauty is not a still portrait but something alive; Byron preferred dark beauties throughout his verse.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tress'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'raven'

  10. dwelling-place l. 12

    The second stanza shifts from looks to mind. The syntax is compressed: her thoughts, 'serenely sweet', express how pure and dear their dwelling-place (her mind, or her face as the house of the mind) is. The image of the body as the mind's house is old and biblical (2 Corinthians 5:1, 'our earthly house of this tabernacle'). The poem thus moves outward to inward across its three stanzas.

    Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 5:1

  11. eloquent l. 14

    The beauty is quiet, but it communicates. The third stanza is built on triple repetitions ('So soft, so calm', 'The smiles that win, the tints that glow'), and the face 'tells' of her life. 'Tints' are colours of the complexion, the 'bright' counterpart to the 'raven' hair. The word comes from Latin eloqui, 'to speak out': the quiet face 'speaks out' her character, so the poem's last lines can read virtue in her complexion and smile.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'eloquent'

  12. outward beauty, inward goodness l. 17

    The poem ends not on physical beauty but on moral character, a Platonic idea (beauty as the outward sign of goodness) common in the Renaissance. 'All below' means everything on earth. The final exclamation, 'A heart whose love is innocent!', has struck some readers as ironic given Byron's reputation in 1814, others as sincere idealisation of a woman he barely knew. The form is three six-line stanzas in iambic tetrameter, rhymed ababab.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. III, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1900), Introduction to Hebrew Melodies and note to 'She Walks in Beauty'
