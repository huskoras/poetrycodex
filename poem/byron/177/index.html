Death & Dying

Darkness

by Lord Byron 1788–1824

From Poems of July–September, 1816

Death & Dying

I had a dream, which was not all a dream.
The bright sun was extinguished, and the stars
Did wander darkling in the eternal space,
Rayless, and pathless, and the icy Earth
Swung blind and blackening in the moonless air;
Morn came and went--and came, and brought no day,
And men forgot their passions in the dread
Of this their desolation; and all hearts
Were chilled into a selfish prayer for light:
And they did live by watchfires--and the thrones,
The palaces of crownéd kings--the huts,
The habitations of all things which dwell,
Were burnt for beacons; cities were consumed,
And men were gathered round their blazing homes
To look once more into each other's face;
Happy were those who dwelt within the eye
Of the volcanos, and their mountain-torch:
A fearful hope was all the World contained;
Forests were set on fire--but hour by hour
They fell and faded--and the crackling trunks
Extinguished with a crash--and all was black.
The brows of men by the despairing light
Wore an unearthly aspect, as by fits
The flashes fell upon them; some lay down
And hid their eyes and wept; and some did rest
Their chins upon their clenchéd hands, and smiled;
And others hurried to and fro, and fed
Their funeral piles with fuel, and looked up
With mad disquietude on the dull sky,
The pall of a past World; and then again
With curses cast them down upon the dust,
And gnashed their teeth and howled: the wild birds shrieked,
And, terrified, did flutter on the ground,
And flap their useless wings; the wildest brutes
Came tame and tremulous; and vipers crawled
And twined themselves among the multitude,
Hissing, but stingless--they were slain for food:
And War, which for a moment was no more,
Did glut himself again:--a meal was bought
With blood, and each sate sullenly apart
Gorging himself in gloom: no Love was left;
All earth was but one thought--and that was Death,
Immediate and inglorious; and the pang
Of famine fed upon all entrails--men
Died, and their bones were tombless as their flesh;
The meagre by the meagre were devoured,
Even dogs assailed their masters, all save one,
And he was faithful to a corse, and kept
The birds and beasts and famished men at bay,
Till hunger clung them, or the dropping dead
Lured their lank jaws; himself sought out no food,
But with a piteous and perpetual moan,
And a quick desolate cry, licking the hand
Which answered not with a caress--he died.
The crowd was famished by degrees; but two
Of an enormous city did survive,
And they were enemies: they met beside
The dying embers of an altar-place
Where had been heaped a mass of holy things
For an unholy usage; they raked up,
And shivering scraped with their cold skeleton hands
The feeble ashes, and their feeble breath
Blew for a little life, and made a flame
Which was a mockery; then they lifted up
Their eyes as it grew lighter, and beheld
Each other's aspects--saw, and shrieked, and died--
Even of their mutual hideousness they died,
Unknowing who he was upon whose brow
Famine had written Fiend. The World was void,
The populous and the powerful was a lump,
Seasonless, herbless, treeless, manless, lifeless--
A lump of death--a chaos of hard clay.
The rivers, lakes, and ocean all stood still,
And nothing stirred within their silent depths;
Ships sailorless lay rotting on the sea,
And their masts fell down piecemeal: as they dropped
They slept on the abyss without a surge--
The waves were dead; the tides were in their grave,
The Moon, their mistress, had expired before;
The winds were withered in the stagnant air,
And the clouds perished; Darkness had no need
Of aid from them--She was the Universe.

Codex Note

Diodati, July, 1816. First published, Prisoner of Chillon, etc., 1816.

Study notes by the editors

  1. composition: Diodati, July 1816 l. 1

    Byron spent the summer of 1816 at Diodati near the Shelleys; it was the same season in which Mary Shelley began Frankenstein. Volcanic ash from Tambora (Indonesia, April 1815) had darkened skies and ruined harvests across Europe, though no one then knew the cause; the period is remembered as the 'Year Without a Summer'. An astronomer in Bologna had predicted that the sun would go out on 18 July, and the painter William Edward West reported in 1826 that Byron linked the poem to a 'dark day' when fowls went to roost at noon, although the date of composition is uncertain. The poem was published with The Prisoner of Chillon in December 1816.

    Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016); 'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer'); The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  2. not all a dream l. 1

    Coleridge's edition notes that the manuscript was titled 'A Dream'. The line has been read in several ways: as a claim that the vision reflects real conditions (the darkened summer); as a prophetic claim, like the dreams of biblical seers; or as a statement about the imagination, whose dreams carry truth. Byron wrote a companion poem, 'The Dream', the same summer. The form throughout is blank verse.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'; Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016)

  3. darkling l. 3

    'Darkling' is an archaic adverb meaning in darkness. Milton uses it of the nightingale (and of himself, blind) in the invocation to light in Paradise Lost 3. Byron's stars 'wander darkling' as if blind: the cosmos has lost its order. 'Rayless, and pathless' describes the stars without light and without courses. The Greek word for planet (planetes) means 'wanderer'. The word thus joins Byron's vision to Milton's.

    Milton, Paradise Lost 3.38-40 ('Sings darkling'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'darkling'

  4. blind and blackening l. 5

    The alliteration of 'blind and blackening' and the verb 'swung' make the earth an inert object. The idea of the sun's death was discussed in contemporary science (and in the Bologna prophecy of 1816). 'Moonless air' anticipates the end, where the moon has 'expired before'. The adjectives pile up negatives ('Rayless, and pathless', 'moonless'), a stylistic habit of the poem that culminates in the list of 'less' words near the end.

    Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016); 'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer')

  5. selfish prayer l. 9

    The poem's moral argument begins here: catastrophe does not ennoble but degrades. Men 'forgot their passions' (love, ambition) in their dread; all that remains is the wish to survive. This bleak view of human nature distinguishes Byron's poem from apocalypses that offer redemption. The 'prayer for light' gets no answer; no God appears in the poem. Byron's scepticism shows here.

    'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer'); Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016)

  6. watchfires and beacons l. 10

    Watchfires are the fires of soldiers or watchmen at night. The list 'thrones ... palaces ... huts' levels all ranks: the signs of power become fuel. 'Beacons' are signal fires. Men gather round their burning homes 'to look once more into each other's face', a brief moment of human community that the rest of the poem undoes. The burning of the world's goods anticipates the dead world.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'watchfire'

  7. volcanos and their mountain-torch l. 17

    The irony is unknown to the poem: the real darkness of 1816 was caused by Tambora's eruption, though no one at the time linked the two. The 'eye' of a volcano is its crater. 'A fearful hope' is an oxymoron that sums up the state of mankind. The phrase 'mountain-torch' makes the volcano a natural lamp; the poem's survivors must burn the world to see it, a pattern repeated with the forests that follow.

    'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer'); Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016)

  8. pall l. 30

    'Pall' (Latin pallium, cloak) is the funeral cloth spread over a coffin. The phrase 'a past World' anticipates the end: the world is already past while its people still live. The men look up with 'mad disquietude' and then curse, gnash their teeth and howl. The same despair takes contrasting forms in the lines around it: some weep, some smile with clenched hands, some rush about feeding their pyres.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pall'

  9. gnashing of teeth l. 32

    The phrase recurs in Matthew's Gospel for those cast into 'outer darkness'. Byron's earth has become that outer darkness. A contemporary critic in the Imperial Magazine (1828), as reported by E. H. Coleridge, accused Byron of borrowing from the prophets Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Joel. The biblical language makes the secular apocalypse feel like a judgment, but without a judge. Byron's borrowings are open.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 8:12; The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  10. stingless vipers l. 35

    The scene parodies the prophecy of the peaceable kingdom in Isaiah 11:6-8, where the wolf dwells with the lamb and a child plays on the asp's hole. Here the animals' tameness comes from terror and weakness, not peace, and the humans eat them. The inversion of the natural order continues the poem's systematic undoing of Creation. The reptiles 'Hissing, but stingless' are a powerless remnant.

    Bible (KJV), Isaiah 11:6-8; 'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer')

  11. War glutting himself l. 38

    'Glut' means to feed to excess. The brief truce of fear gives way to violence over food. 'Sate' is an archaic past tense of 'sit'. The sequence (truce, war, isolation) gives a political edge: Europe had just emerged from more than twenty years of war in 1815, and the famine of 1816 caused riots and unrest. 'Bought / With blood' makes food a war prize.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'glut'; Patrick Vincent, 'On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron's Darkness', BARS Blog (2016)

  12. no Love was left l. 41

    'All earth was but one thought—and that was Death, / Immediate and inglorious': the death is not heroic but merely physical. Love, personified with a capital as War is, is absent. The poem is often contrasted with Christian apocalypses ending in a new heaven and earth; here nothing follows. The only exception to lovelessness is the faithful dog, which makes the absence among humans sharper.

    'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer')

  13. the faithful dog l. 47

    The episode has an ancient analogue in Argos, Odysseus's dog, who recognises his master and dies (Odyssey 17), and in many tales of faithful dogs. Byron was devoted to his dog Boatswain, for whom he wrote an epitaph (1808) praising canine fidelity above human. 'Corse' is an archaic form of 'corpse'. The dog's love 'answered not with a caress' makes the dead hand's silence terrible.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'corse'

  14. clung l. 50

    E. H. Coleridge notes the source in Macbeth 5.5, where Macbeth threatens the messenger: 'Upon the next tree shalt thou hang alive, / Till famine cling thee'. He explains that fruit is said to be 'clung' when the skin shrivels, and a corpse when the face is wasted and gaunt. 'The dropping dead / Lured their lank jaws': the starving creatures are drawn to eat the dead.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'; Shakespeare, Macbeth 5.5 ('Till famine cling thee'), Folger Shakespeare

  15. the last two men l. 57

    This episode, the poem's climax, is a bitter parody of the 'last man' and 'last couple' stories of the period, and of the biblical idea of reconciliation at the altar. E. H. Coleridge compares the moment of mutual recognition with Vathek and Nouronihar in the Hall of Eblis, in William Beckford's Vathek (1786), waiting to become 'objects of terror' to each other. The men cannot recognise each other because famine has obliterated identity.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  16. the altar l. 60

    The scene recalls the sacred fire on the altar that must never go out (Leviticus 6:13), and the profanation of the temple's holy things. The last flame is 'a mockery', giving only enough light to show the men's horror. The 'cold skeleton hands' and 'feeble breath' are those of men already almost dead. The episode turns religion into fuel, and the act of worship into a scramble for warmth.

    Bible (KJV), Leviticus 6:13; The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  17. Famine had written Fiend l. 69

    The metaphor of a word written on the brow recalls the mark of Cain (Genesis 4:15) and the name written on the foreheads of the damned in Revelation (13:16-17). Personified Famine is the writer. E. H. Coleridge notes that Scott, reviewing the volume, likened the poem's manner to the wild imagination of S. T. Coleridge; Coleridge the editor suggests Scott may have had in mind the 'red-eyed Fiend' of final destruction in Religious Musings, but judges the resemblance general rather than particular.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 4:15; Revelation 13:16; The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  18. a chaos of hard clay l. 72

    'The World was void' echoes Genesis 1:2. The line 'Seasonless, herbless, treeless, manless, lifeless' (asyndeton, a list without connecting words) strips away the works of the six days of Creation in turn. 'Chaos' is the Greek word for the formless void before creation. 'Clay' recalls the creation of Adam from the dust (Genesis 2:7). Byron's apocalypse is an uncreation. The world is reduced to raw matter.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:2; 2:7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'chaos'

  19. ships rotting on the sea l. 75

    In 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' the sea is motionless and 'The very deep did rot'. Byron knew Coleridge's poetry well. His ships, with no one aboard, dropping their masts into a waveless abyss, convey utter stillness. 'Without a surge' means without a wave. The tides are 'in their grave' because the moon that drew them has died. The sea is the last part of nature to die.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'

  20. the Moon, their mistress l. 79

    The tides follow the moon, and poets had long called her the ruler of the sea. With the moon dead, the tides lie still. The image also reverses the creation of 'the lesser light to rule the night' (Genesis 1:16). The order of deaths in this section (sea, waves, tides, moon, winds, clouds) is a systematic shutting down of nature.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:16

  21. Darkness ... She was the Universe l. 82

    The poem ends without consolation or survivor. Darkness, capitalised and feminine, replaces God as the all-in-all (compare 1 Corinthians 15:28, 'that God may be all in all'). Sir Walter Scott, reviewing the volume in the Quarterly Review (October 1816), disliked the poem, finding such phantasms a dangerous employment for Byron's imagination. Later readers have seen it as an early vision of ecological catastrophe.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 15:28; 'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer')

  22. sources and analogues l. 2

    E. H. Coleridge reports that an 1825 article traced the poem to the novel Omegarus and Syderia, an English version of Jean-Baptiste Cousin de Grainville's Le Dernier Homme (1805), and quotes passages: the sun pale with frigid beams, men looking on each other with enmity. He finds it hard to believe Byron had not read it, though he dismisses any charge of plagiarism. Mary Shelley's novel The Last Man (1826) and Thomas Campbell's poem 'The Last Man' (1823) belong to the same tradition.

    The Works of Lord Byron: Poetry, vol. IV, ed. E. H. Coleridge (London: Murray, 1901), notes to 'Darkness'; 'Darkness (poem)', Wikipedia (Tambora and the 'Year Without a Summer')
