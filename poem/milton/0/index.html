Sonnet 19: When I Consider How My Light Is Spent

by John Milton 1608–1674

ReligionLivingTime & Brevity

When I consider how my light is spent,
Ere half my days, in this dark world and wide,
And that one Talent which is death to hide
Lodged with me useless, though my Soul more bent
To serve therewith my Maker, and present
My true account, lest he returning chide;
“Doth God exact day-labour, light denied?”
I fondly ask. But patience, to prevent
That murmur, soon replies, “God doth not need
Either man’s work or his own gifts; who best
Bear his mild yoke, they serve him best. His state
Is Kingly. Thousands at his bidding speed
And post o’er Land and Ocean without rest:
They also serve who only stand and wait.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Written after Milton went blind, this sonnet turns a private terror — uselessness before God — into a theology of patience, closing on its famous redefinition of service as endurance: “They also serve who only stand and wait.”

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date, form l. 1

    Milton was totally blind by early 1652, at forty-three. The sonnet was first printed, without title, in Poems (1673); the familiar title 'On His Blindness' was supplied in the eighteenth century by Thomas Newton. Its date is disputed: its place in the sequence suggests 1655, while others argue for 1652 or earlier. 'Light' means eyesight, and the poem plays on light and dark ('this dark world and wide') and on spending, as of money, which introduces the economic language of the parable that follows. The form is Italian: an octave rhymed abbaabba and a sestet cdecde, but the turn arrives early, in the middle of l. 8.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XIX', notes (text of Poems, 2nd edn, 1673); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; John Milton, Poems, &c. upon Several Occasions (1673)

  2. Ere half my days l. 2

    The phrase measures life by the biblical 'threescore years and ten' (Psalm 90:10). Since Milton was in his forties when he went blind, the reckoning is approximate; some, like the RPO editors, suggest he counts from his dedication of himself to God's service in the early 1630s (Sonnet 7, 'How soon hath time'). Either way, the feeling is of a career cut off before its harvest. Compare Psalm 102:24, 'take me not away in the midst of my days'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XIX', notes (text of Poems, 2nd edn, 1673); KJV, Psalm 90:10; Psalm 102:24; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  3. Talent ll. 3-6

    In Matthew 25:14-30 a master entrusts talents (large sums of silver; Greek talanton, a weight) to three servants; the one who hides his single talent is condemned and thrown 'into outer darkness'. Milton's 'one Talent' is his gift for poetry, and the modern sense of 'talent' derives from this parable. 'Death to hide' takes the punishment literally, with a pun: blindness has already cast him into darkness. 'Lodged with me useless' plays on usury, since the master wanted his money put out to interest. As early as his 'Letter to a Friend' (c. 1633) Milton had worried about the servant 'that hid the talent', so the parable was an old anxiety for him.

    KJV, Matthew 25:14-30; Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XIX', notes (text of Poems, 2nd edn, 1673); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  4. true account ll. 5-6

    In the parable 'after a long time the lord of those servants cometh, and reckoneth with them' (Matthew 25:19). Milton wants to 'present / My true account' and fears that the master 'returning' will 'chide'. The financial and judicial senses merge: account as a statement of credit and debit, and as an answer given at judgement (Romans 14:12, 'every one of us shall give account of himself to God'). 'Bent' (l. 4) means inclined, resolved, as a bow is bent.

    KJV, Matthew 25:19; Romans 14:12; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  5. day-labour, light denied l. 7

    The question is whether God demands a day-labourer's work from a man denied daylight. It echoes Christ's words before healing a blind man: 'I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work' (John 9:4). It also recalls the labourers in the vineyard (Matthew 20:1-16), paid the same whether hired early or at the eleventh hour, which anticipates the poem's answer that God does not measure service by quantity. 'Exact' carries the sense of demanding payment, continuing the economic metaphor.

    KJV, John 9:4; Matthew 20:1-16; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  6. fondly l. 8

    'Fond' originally meant foolish (Middle English fonned, past participle of fonnen, to be foolish), a sense still current in Milton's day; compare Lycidas l. 56, 'Ay me, I fondly dream!'. The speaker judges his own question as soon as he has asked it. The modern sense of affectionate may faintly colour the word, suggesting a doting attachment to his own work.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XIX', notes (text of Poems, 2nd edn, 1673); Oxford English Dictionary, 'fondly', adv.; 'fond', adj.

  7. The volta and 'prevent' ll. 8-9

    In a strict Petrarchan sonnet the turn falls at l. 9. Milton anticipates it in mid-line 8, so that Patience literally 'prevents' (comes before, forestalls) the murmur, and her reply runs over the octave-sestet division to the end. The syntax enacts the meaning: the complaint is cut short before it can become rebellion. 'Murmur' recalls the Israelites who murmured against God in the wilderness (Exodus 16; compare Philippians 2:14). Personified Patience gives the poem a debate structure like a medieval dialogue of virtues.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; Oxford English Dictionary, 'prevent', v. (to anticipate, forestall); KJV, Exodus 16:2-8; Philippians 2:14

  8. his mild yoke ll. 10-11

    The idea that God does not need human works is a Protestant commonplace (compare Acts 17:25, 'neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing'). 'Bear his mild yoke' alludes to Matthew 11:29-30, 'Take my yoke upon you... For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light'. Service is redefined from productive labour to obedient acceptance. The phrase 'his own gifts' returns the talent to its giver: what Milton feared to waste was never his to begin with.

    KJV, Matthew 11:29-30; Acts 17:25; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  9. His state / Is Kingly ll. 11-13

    'State' means royal dignity and the ceremonial of a court (OED, state n., senses of magnificence and a chair of state). The image is of a king served by two kinds of servant: the 'Thousands' of angelic messengers who 'speed / And post' (travel fast, as by post-horses) over land and sea, and those who attend in the presence chamber. Daniel 7:10 supplies the multitude: 'thousand thousands ministered unto him'. The enjambment 'His state / Is Kingly' places emphasis on the sovereignty that makes any individual's labour unnecessary. In a republican writer of the 1650s the royal image is striking, though it is reserved for God alone.

    KJV, Daniel 7:10; Hebrews 1:14; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; Oxford English Dictionary, 'state', n.; 'post', v.

  10. stand and wait l. 14

    The line has become proverbial as a counsel of patient endurance, but its immediate sense is courtly: those who 'stand and wait' are attendants in readiness, like the angels who 'stand in the presence of God' (Luke 1:19). 'Wait' means both 'attend upon' and 'wait for' (Psalm 27:14, 'wait on the Lord'), so the line looks forward in hope. Critics divide on how much resignation it expresses: some read final acceptance, others a waiting for further work, which Milton did eventually perform in Paradise Lost. The placing of Patience's reply entirely in direct speech leaves open whether the speaker fully assents.

    KJV, Luke 1:19; Psalm 27:14; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XIX', notes (text of Poems, 2nd edn, 1673)
