The Mind

Il Penseroso

by John Milton 1608–1674

From Poems (1645; enlarged 1673)

The Mind

Hence vain deluding joyes,
The brood of folly without father bred,
How little you bested,
Or fill the fixed mind with all your toyes;
Dwell in som idle brain
And fancies fond with gaudy shapes possess,
As thick and numberless
As the gay motes that people the Sun Beams,
Or likest hovering dreams
The fickle Pensioners of Morpheus train.
But hail thou Goddess, sage and holy,
Hail divinest Melancholy
Whose Saintly visage is too bright
To hit the Sense of human sight;
And therefore to our weaker view,
Ore laid with black staid Wisdoms hue.
Black, but such as in esteem,
Prince Memnons sister might beseem,
Or that Starr'd Ethiope Queen that strove
To set her beauties praise above
The Sea Nymphs, and their powers offended.
Yet thou art higher far descended,
Thee bright-hair'd Vesta long of yore,
To solitary Saturn bore;
His daughter she (in Saturns raign,
Such mixture was not held a stain)
Oft in glimmering Bowres, and glades
He met her, and in secret shades
Of woody Ida's inmost grove,
While yet there was no fear of Jove.
Com pensive Nun, devout and pure,
Sober, stedfast, and demure,
All in a robe of darkest grain,
Flowing with majestick train,
And sable stole of Cipres Lawn,
Over thy decent shoulders drawn.
Com, but keep thy wonted state,
With eev'n step, and musing gate,
And looks commercing with the skies,
Thy rapt soul sitting in thine eyes:
There held in holy passion still,
Forget thy self to Marble, till
With a sad Leaden downward cast,
Thou fix them on the earth as fast.
And joyn with thee calm Peace, and Quiet,
Spare Fast, that oft with gods doth diet,
And hears the Muses in a ring,
Ay round about Joves Altar sing.
And adde to these retired Leasure,
That in trim Gardens takes his pleasure;
But first, and chiefest, with thee bring,
Him that yon soars on golden wing,
Guiding the fiery-wheeled throne,
The Cherub Contemplation,
And the mute Silence hist along,
'Less Philomel will daign a Song,
In her sweetest, saddest plight,
Smoothing the rugged brow of night,
While Cynthia checks her Dragon yoke,
Gently o're th'accustom'd Oke;
Sweet Bird that shunn'st the noise of folly
Most musical!, most melancholy!
Thee Chauntress oft the Woods among
I woo to hear thy eeven-Song;
And missing thee, I walk unseen
On the dry smooth-shaven Green,
To behold the wandring Moon,
Riding neer her highest noon,
Like one that had bin led astray
Through the Heav'ns wide pathles way;
And oft, as if her head she bow'd,
Stooping through a fleecy cloud.
Oft on a Plat of rising ground,
I hear the far-off Curfeu sound,
Over som wide-water'd shoar,
Swinging slow with sullen roar;
Or if the Ayr will not permit,
Som still removed place will fit,
Where glowing Embers through the room
Teach light to counterfeit a gloom
Far from all resort of mirth,
Save the Cricket on the hearth,
Or the Belmans drowsie charm,
To bless the dores from nightly harm:
Or let my Lamp at midnight hour,
Be seen in som high lonely Towr,
Where I may oft out-watch the Bear,
With thrice great Hermes, or unsphear
The spirit of Plato to unfold
What Worlds, or what vast Regions hold
The immortal mind that hath forsook
Her mansion in this fleshly nook:
And of those Daemons that are found
In fire, air, flood, or under ground,
Whose power hath a true consent
With planet or with Element.
Som time let Gorgeous Tragedy
In Scepter'd Pall com sweeping by,
Presenting Thebs, or Pelops line,
Or the tale of Troy divine.
Or what (though rare) of later age,
Ennobled hath the Buskind stage.
But, O sad Virgin, that thy power
Might raise Musaeus from his bower,
Or bid the soul of Orpheus sing
Such notes as warbled to the string,
Drew Iron tears down Pluto's cheek,
And made Hell grant what Love did seek.
Or call up him that left half told
The story of Cambuscan bold,
Of Camball, and of Algarsife,
And who had Canace to wife,
That own'd the vertuous Ring and Glass,
And of the wondrous Hors of Brass,
On which the Tartar King did ride;
And if ought els, great Bards beside,
In sage and solemn tunes have sung,
Of Turneys and of Trophies hung;
Of Forests, and inchantments drear,
Where more is meant then meets the ear.
Thus night oft see me in thy pale career,
Till civil-suited Morn appeer,
Not trickt and frounc't as she was wont,
With the Attick Boy to hunt,
But Cherchef't in a comly Cloud,
While rocking Winds are Piping loud,
Or usher'd with a shower still,
When the gust hath blown his fill,
Ending on the russling Leaves,
With minute drops from off the Eaves.
And when the Sun begins to fling
His flaring beams, me Goddes bring
To arched walks of twilight groves,
And shadows brown that Sylvan loves
Of Pine, or monumental Oake,
Where the rude Ax with heaved stroke,
Was never heard the Nymphs to daunt,
Or fright them from their hallow'd haunt.
There in close covert by som Brook,
Where no profaner eye may look,
Hide me from Day's garish eie,
While the Bee with Honied thie,
That at her flowry work doth sing,
And the Waters murmuring
With such consort as they keep,
Entice the dewy-feather'd Sleep;
And let som strange mysterious dream,
Wave at his Wings in Airy stream,
Of lively portrature display'd,
Softly on my eye-lids laid.
And as I wake, sweet musick breath
Above, about, or underneath,
Sent by som spirit to mortals good,
Or th'unseen Genius of the Wood.
But let my due feet never fail,
To walk the studious Cloysters pale,
And love the high embowed Roof
With antick Pillars massy proof,
And storied Windows richly dight,
Casting a dimm religious light.
There let the pealing Organ blow,
To the full voic'd Quire below,
In Service high, and Anthems cleer,
As may with sweetnes, through mine ear,
Dissolve me into extasies,
And bring all Heav'n before mine eyes.
And may at last my weary age
Find out the peacefull hermitage,
The Hairy Gown and Mossy Cell,
Where I may sit and rightly spell
Of every Star that Heav'n doth shew,
And every Herb that sips the dew;
Till old experience do attain
To somthing like prophetic strain.
These pleasures Melancholy give,
And I with thee will choose to live.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Il Penseroso: title and design ll. 1-10

    Milton's 'Penseroso' is his own form of Italian pensieroso, 'thoughtful, pensive'. The poem mirrors L'Allegro point for point: an exorcism in alternating six- and ten-syllable lines (rhymed abbacddeec), an invocation with a genealogy, a train of attendant personifications, a sequence of pleasures through a night and the following day, and a conditional close. Here the 'joyes' of folly are banished as L'Allegro banished Melancholy, but the melancholy now welcomed is not the disease of black bile; it is the Renaissance melancholy of scholars and seers, which Ficino, drawing on the pseudo-Aristotelian Problemata 30.1, linked with genius and Saturn. The poem is also four and twenty lines longer than its companion, and many readers think it the culmination of the pair.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964); A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Samuel Johnson, 'Milton', Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779-81)

  2. divinest Melancholy l. 12

    The ancient question why outstanding men in philosophy, politics and poetry are melancholic (pseudo-Aristotle, Problemata 30.1) was revived by Marsilio Ficino's De vita (1489), which linked the melancholy temperament and the planet Saturn with contemplation and inspired insight. Klibansky, Panofsky and Saxl trace this 'noble' melancholy through Dürer's Melencolia I. Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) prefaced its later editions with a poem alternating the pains and joys of melancholy, often compared with Milton's pair. Milton's goddess is 'sage and holy', a figure of religious contemplation rather than mere sadness.

    Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964); Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621; 'The Author's Abstract of Melancholy' added 1628); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  3. too bright ... Black ll. 13-16

    The paradox is Neoplatonic and biblical: the divine is too dazzling to be seen directly and appears to human sight as darkness (compare Exodus 33:20 and Paradise Lost 3.380, 'Dark with excessive bright'). 'Ore laid' means overlaid, and 'staid' (l. 16) means sober, steady. Black becomes the colour of wisdom, not of gloom, and the following lines justify it with examples of black beauty.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Exodus 33:20 (KJV)

  4. Prince Memnon's sister l. 18

    Memnon fought for Troy and was killed by Akhilleus; Odysseus says he saw no handsomer man than Eurypylus except Memnon himself (Odyssey 11.522). Classical literature hardly mentions a sister: Osgood notes that commentators found only a Himera (or Hemera) in the late Latin Troy history ascribed to Dictys Cretensis, which does not mention her beauty. Milton infers her looks from her brother's. As an Ethiopian she is a type of dark beauty, justifying the goddess's black visage.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Homer, Odyssey 11.522; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  5. That Starr'd Ethiope Queen ll. 19-21

    In Apollodorus (2.4.3) Cassiopeia boasted that her daughter Andromeda was fairer than the Nereids, and was punished by a sea monster and later placed among the stars; other classical accounts make the boast about her own beauty. Milton follows the second version, which keeps attention on dark female beauty. 'Starr'd' means turned into a constellation, and 'their powers offended' means she angered the sea deities' divine power.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Apollodorus, Library 2.4.3; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  6. Vesta and Saturn as parents ll. 22-30

    No ancient source gives Melancholy this descent. Saturn (Cronos) was the planet of the melancholic temperament and of solitary study, and ruled the golden age before Jove deposed him. Vesta (Hestia), virgin goddess of the hearth, was his daughter (Hesiod, Theogony 453-54). Milton admits the incest and excuses it as belonging to an age before such 'mixture' was 'held a stain' (l. 26). Critics read the union as joining solitude, fixity and contemplation (Saturn) with purity and steady inner fire (Vesta). 'Ida' (l. 29) is the Cretan mountain where the infant Jove was hidden, so the lovers meet before 'fear of Jove'.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964); Hesiod, Theogony 453-506

  7. pensive Nun ... darkest grain ... Cipres Lawn ll. 31-36

    The nun is a figure of devotion, not a Catholic polemic point. 'Grain' meant a fast dye (originally scarlet kermes, 'grain'), so 'darkest grain' is the deepest dyed colour, perhaps dark purple. 'Cipres Lawn' (cypress or cyprus) was a fine transparent black crape, used for mourning. 'Decent' keeps its Latin sense, decens, 'comely, becoming'. 'Wonted state' (l. 37) is her customary stately bearing, and 'musing gate' her contemplative gait.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'grain'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cypress'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  8. Forget thy self to Marble ll. 39-44

    'Commercing with the skies' means communing, holding converse with heaven. Ecstatic contemplation suspends bodily life so that the soul seems to sit in the eyes. Milton had used a similar petrifaction in 'On Shakespear' (1630), where readers are made 'Marble with too much conceaving'. Lead is the metal of Saturn, so the 'Leaden downward cast' is astrologically exact; 'sad' still carries its older sense of grave, steadfast.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'commerce'

  9. Spare Fast and the Muses' ring ll. 46-48

    Milton's Elegy 6 (1629) tells Diodati that the poet who sings of gods and heroes must live sparingly, like Pythagoras, on simple food and water. Here Fast 'diets' with the gods, and hears the Muses who dance round the altar of Zeus at the opening of Hesiod's Theogony (ll. 1-10). The asceticism links poetic and religious vocation, a theme of Milton's early career.

    Hesiod, Theogony 1-10; Milton, Elegia sexta (1629), ll. 55-78; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  10. The Cherub Contemplation ll. 51-54

    Ezekiel 1 and 10 describe cherubim moving with wheels full of fire beneath the throne of God, and Milton draws on this chariot again for the Son's 'Chariot of Paternal Deitie' in Paradise Lost 6.749-59. In the angelic hierarchy of Pseudo-Dionysius the cherubim were the order distinguished by knowledge and contemplation, so the identification is precise. Contemplation 'soars on golden wing', the highest member of Melancholy's train. 'Hist along' (l. 55) means bring along with a whispered 'hist!', in silence.

    Ezekiel 1:15-21; 10:1-22 (KJV); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.

  11. Philomel, the nightingale ll. 56-64

    In Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.424-674, Philomela is raped and mutilated by her sister's husband Tereus; in the end the sisters become birds, and the tradition made Philomela the nightingale. Her song is 'sweetest, saddest', the melancholy music of the night, matching the lark of L'Allegro. 'Plight' means condition. 'Most musical, most melancholy' (l. 62) was quoted and criticised by Coleridge in 'The Nightingale' (1798), who objected that nothing in nature is melancholy, though he conceded Milton's line was spoken in character.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.424-674; S. T. Coleridge, 'The Nightingale: A Conversation Poem' (Lyrical Ballads, 1798), ll. 13-22 and note

  12. Cynthia's Dragon yoke ll. 59-60

    Cynthia is Diana as moon goddess (from Mount Cynthus on Delos). In classical poetry dragons draw the chariots of Ceres and Medea (Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.642, 7.220-37), not the moon's, which usually has horses or oxen; Osgood collects the parallels. The 'accustom'd Oke' is the oak tree over which she habitually passes, perhaps with a glance at the oak sacred to Jove. 'Checks' means reins in.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.220-37; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  13. Curfeu ll. 73-76

    The curfew bell, rung at dusk, originally signalled that hearth fires were to be covered for the night (Old French cuevrefeu). A 'Plat' is a plot of ground. Milton renders the sound by onomatopoeia and slow monosyllables: 'Swinging slow with sullen roar'. Gray's Elegy opens with the same bell, 'The Curfew tolls the knell of parting day', and Gray's poem is steeped in Il Penseroso.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'curfew'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Roger Lonsdale, ed., The Poems of Gray, Collins and Goldsmith (Longman, 1969), note on Gray's Elegy l. 1

  14. the Lamp in the high lonely Tower ll. 85-88

    The Great Bear never sets in northern latitudes, so to 'out-watch the Bear' is to stay awake reading until dawn hides it. The lighted tower window became an emblem of visionary scholarship: Yeats, in 'The Phases of the Moon' (1919), recalls the tower where 'Milton's Platonist / Sat late'.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; W. B. Yeats, 'The Phases of the Moon', in The Wild Swans at Coole (1919)

  15. thrice great Hermes l. 88

    The Corpus Hermeticum, translated into Latin by Marsilio Ficino (completed 1463, printed 1471), was believed to preserve the wisdom of an Egyptian priest-philosopher older than Moses, whose name means 'thrice-greatest Hermes'. Isaac Casaubon showed in 1614 that the texts were late antique, but Hermetic thought remained influential among Platonists and occultists. Pairing Hermes with Plato places the scholar's reading in the Neoplatonic tradition of the soul's ascent.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964); Frances A. Yates, Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition (1964)

  16. unsphear the spirit of Plato ll. 88-92

    To 'unsphear' (Milton's coinage, OED) is to draw a spirit out of its celestial sphere. In the Timaeus (41d-42b) each soul is assigned a star to which the virtuous return; the Phaedo argues the soul's immortality. The questions asked, 'What Worlds, or what vast Regions hold / The immortal mind' when it has left 'this fleshly nook', are those of the Phaedo and the myth of Er (Republic 10). 'Mansion' means dwelling-place.

    Plato, Timaeus 41d-42b; Phaedo; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unsphere'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  17. Daemons of the four elements ll. 93-96

    Late Platonists (Proclus, Psellus) and Renaissance occult philosophers such as Ficino and Cornelius Agrippa (De occulta philosophia, 1533) classified daemons, intermediate spirits between gods and men, by the element they inhabit. 'Consent' means sympathy or harmony (Latin consentire): the spirits' powers correspond with the planets and elements. The word 'daemon' carries the Greek sense, a spirit not necessarily evil, unlike the Christian 'demon'. Such lore was learned rather than popular, the scholar's equivalent of the fairy tales told by the fire in L'Allegro 100-114.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Raymond Klibansky, Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, Saturn and Melancholy (1964)

  18. Gorgeous Tragedy: Thebes, Pelops' line, Troy ll. 97-102

    Tragedy enters in a royal pall (robe) with a sceptre. Thebes means the story of Oedipus and his children (Sophocles, Aeschylus' Seven against Thebes); 'Pelops line' is the house of Atreus, Agamemnon and Orestes (Aeschylus' Oresteia); 'the tale of Troy divine' covers plays like Euripides' Trojan Women. The buskin (cothurnus), the high boot of the tragic actor, stands for tragedy as the sock stood for comedy in L'Allegro 132. 'What (though rare) of later age' grudgingly admits a few modern tragedies; editors suppose Shakespeare's among them.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900)

  19. Musaeus and Orpheus ll. 103-108

    Musaeus was a legendary pre-Homeric poet, often called the pupil or son of Orpheus; Virgil places him at the centre of the blessed throng in Elysium (Aeneid 6.667). His 'bower' is that Elysian dwelling. 'Iron tears' are the tears of the iron-hearted god of the dead, an oxymoron. Where L'Allegro imagined music that would have freed Eurydice completely, the pensive man recalls only that Hell granted 'what Love did seek', leaving the failure unspoken.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.667-68; Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.40-48; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  20. Cambuscan and Chaucer's Squire's Tale ll. 109-115

    Chaucer's Squire's Tale breaks off unfinished. It tells of Cambyuskan, king of Tartary, his sons Algarsyf and Cambalo, and daughter Canacee, and of the gifts brought to his court: a horse of brass that flies anywhere, a mirror revealing treachery, a ring by which Canacee understands birds, and a healing sword. Milton's 'who had Canace to wife' refers to Chaucer's promise to tell of a suitor who must fight for her. Spenser completed the story in Faerie Queene IV.ii-iii (Cambell and Canacee). The choice shows Milton's reverence for Chaucer as a serious, half-allegorical poet.

    Geoffrey Chaucer, The Squire's Tale, ed. L. D. Benson, The Riverside Chaucer (1987); Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene IV.ii-iii; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  21. more is meant then meets the ear ll. 116-120

    'Great Bards beside' are the romance poets, above all Spenser, and also Tasso and Ariosto, whose tourneys, trophies, forests and enchantments carry hidden meanings. Spenser's Letter to Raleigh calls The Faerie Queene a 'continued Allegory, or darke conceit'. The line has become proverbial. 'Then' is the old spelling of 'than'. Milton's later remark in Areopagitica (1644) calling Spenser a better teacher than Scotus or Aquinas shows the same view of allegory as moral instruction.

    Edmund Spenser, 'A Letter of the Authors' to Raleigh, prefixed to The Faerie Queene (1590); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.

  22. civil-suited Morn and the Attick Boy ll. 121-130

    'Civil-suited' means plainly dressed like a townsman, as opposed to a courtier. 'Trickt and frounc't' means adorned and with frizzled, curled hair. The 'Attick Boy' is Cephalus of Athens, the hunter loved and abducted by Aurora (Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.700-13). 'Cherchef't' means wearing a kerchief, here a cloud. 'Minute drops' (l. 130) may mean tiny drops or drops falling at intervals of a minute; editors are divided, and both suit the after-storm stillness.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.700-13; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'frounce'

  23. Cloisters, storied Windows, dimm religious light ll. 155-166

    'Pale' means enclosure. 'Embowed' means vaulted; 'antick' means ancient (and perhaps carved with grotesque ornament); 'massy proof' means massive and strong. 'Storied Windows' tell Bible stories in stained glass. The passage, celebrating organ, choir and anthems, has struck readers as surprising from the later Puritan pamphleteer, and some critics relate it to the Laudian 'beauty of holiness' of the 1630s; others note that Milton's father was a composer of church music and that Milton loved the organ. 'Dissolve me into extasies' describes rapture in which the soul leaves the body.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'storied'

  24. the peaceful hermitage ll. 167-174

    The hermit in a hair-shirt ('Hairy Gown') and moss-covered cell is a romance figure, like Spenser's hermit Contemplation in Faerie Queene I.x. 'Spell' means to study, decipher letter by letter. Studying 'every Star' and 'every Herb' is natural philosophy and medicine, and in Renaissance thought such knowledge could rise to prophecy. 'Old experience' attaining 'something like prophetic strain' (song, utterance) suggests the poet-prophet Milton aspired to become.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spell'; Edmund Spenser, The Faerie Queene I.x.46-63

  25. The conditional close ll. 175-176

    The final couplet repeats the structure of L'Allegro 151-52, so the two poems end as alternative answers to the same invitation. Here the verb is 'choose', which some critics take as a sign that Il Penseroso represents the deliberate, higher choice; others note that both endings are conditional and that the poems are balanced rather than ranked. Handel's oratorio L'Allegro, il Penseroso ed il Moderato (1740), with a third part added by Charles Jennens, shows the eighteenth century's wish to reconcile them.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Christopher Marlowe, 'The Passionate Shepherd to His Love' (pub. 1599)
