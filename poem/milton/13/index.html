Living

L'Allegro

by John Milton 1608–1674

From Poems (1645; enlarged 1673)

Living

HENCE loathed Melancholy
Of Cerberus, and blackest midnight born,
In Stygian Cave forlorn
'Mongst horrid shapes, and shreiks, and sights unholy,
Find out som uncouth cell,
Where brooding darknes spreads his jealous wings,
And the night-Raven sings;
There under Ebon shades and low-brow'd Rocks,
As ragged as thy Locks,
In dark Cimmerian desert ever dwell.
But com thou Goddes fair and free,
In Heav'n ycleap'd Euphrosyne,
And by men, heart-easing Mirth,
Whom lovely Venus at a birth
With two sister Graces more
To Ivy-crowned Bacchus bore;
Or whether (as som Sager sing)
The frolick Wind that breathes the Spring,
Zephir with Aurora playing,
As he met her once a Maying,
There on Beds of Violets blew,
And fresh-blown Roses washt in dew,
Fill'd her with thee a daughter fair,
So bucksom, blith, and debonair.
Haste thee nymph, and bring with thee
Jest and youthful Jollity,
Quips and Cranks, and wanton Wiles,
Nods, and Becks, and Wreathed Smiles,
Such as hang on Hebe's cheek,
And love to live in dimple sleek;
Sport that wrincled Care derides,
And Laughter holding both his sides.
Com, and trip it as ye go
On the light fantastick toe,
And in thy right hand lead with thee,
The Mountain Nymph, sweet Liberty;
And if I give thee honour due,
Mirth, admit me of thy crue
To live with her, and live with thee,
In unreproved pleasures free;
To hear the Lark begin his flight,
And singing startle the dull night,
From his watch-towre in the skies,
Till the dappled dawn doth rise;
Then to com in spight of sorrow,
And at my window bid good morrow,
Through the Sweet-Briar, or the Vine,
Or the twisted Eglantine.
While the Cock with lively din,
Scatters the rear of darknes thin,
And to the stack, or the Barn dore,
Stoutly struts his Dames before,
Oft list'ning how the Hounds and horn
Chearly rouse the slumbring morn,
From the side of som Hoar Hill,
Through the high wood echoing shrill.
Som time walking not unseen
By Hedge-row Elms, on Hillocks green,
Right against the Eastern gate,
Wher the great Sun begins his state,
Rob'd in flames, and Amber light,
The clouds in thousand Liveries dight.
While the Plowman neer at hand,
Whistles ore the Furrow'd Land,
And the Milkmaid singeth blithe,
And the Mower whets his sithe,
And every Shepherd tells his tale
Under the Hawthorn in the dale.
Streit mine eye hath caught new pleasures
Whilst the Lantskip round it measures,
Russet Lawns, and Fallows Gray,
Where the nibling flocks do stray,
Mountains on whose barren brest
The labouring clouds do often rest:
Meadows trim with Daisies pide,
Shallow Brooks, and Rivers wide.
Towers, and Battlements it sees
Boosom'd high in tufted Trees,
Wher perhaps som beauty lies,
The Cynosure of neighbouring eyes.
Hard by, a Cottage chimney smokes,
From betwixt two aged Okes,
Where Corydon and Thyrsis met,
Are at their savory dinner set
Of Hearbs, and other Country Messes,
Which the neat-handed Phillis dresses;
And then in haste her Bowre she leaves,
With Thestylis to bind the Sheaves;
Or if the earlier season lead
To the tann'd Haycock in the Mead,
Som times with secure delight
The up-land Hamlets will invite,
When the merry Bells ring round,
And the jocond rebecks sound
To many a youth, and many a maid,
Dancing in the Chequer'd shade;
And young and old com forth to play
On a Sunshine Holyday,
Till the live-long day-light fail,
Then to the Spicy Nut-brown Ale,
With stories told of many a feat,
How Faery Mab the junkets eat,
She was pincht, and pull'd she sed,
And he by Friars Lanthorn led
Tells how the drudging Goblin swet,
To ern his Cream-bowle duly set,
When in one night, ere glimps of morn,
His shadowy Flale hath thresh'd the Corn
That ten day-labourers could not end,
Then lies him down the Lubbar Fend.
And stretch'd out all the Chimney's length,
Basks at the fire his hairy strength;
And Crop-full out of dores he flings,
Ere the first Cock his Mattin rings.
Thus don the Tales, to bed they creep,
By whispering Windes soon lull'd asleep.
Towred Cities please us then,
And the busie humm of men,
Where throngs of Knights and Barons bold,
In weeds of Peace high triumphs hold,
With store of Ladies, whose bright eies
Rain influence, and judge the prise
Of Wit, or Arms, while both contend
To win her Grace, whom all commend.
There let Hymen oft appear
In Saffron robe, with Taper clear,
And pomp, and feast, and revelry,
With mask, and antique Pageantry,
Such sights as youthfull Poets dream
On Summer eeves by haunted stream.
Then to the well-trod stage anon,
If Jonsons learned Sock be on,
Or sweetest Shakespear fancies childe,
Warble his native Wood-notes wilde,
And ever against eating Cares,
Lap me in soft Lydian Aires,
Married to immortal verse
Such as the meeting soul may pierce
In notes, with many a winding bout
Of lincked sweetnes long drawn out,
With wanton heed, and giddy cunning,
The melting voice through mazes running;
Untwisting all the chains that ty
The hidden soul of harmony.
That Orpheus self may heave his head
From golden slumber on a bed
Of heapt Elysian flowres, and hear
Such streins as would have won the ear
Of Pluto, to have quite set free
His half regain'd Eurydice.
These delights, if thou canst give,
Mirth with thee, I mean to live.

Study notes by the editors

  1. L'Allegro: title, date and companion poem ll. 1-10

    The Italian title names a temperament rather than a person: 'the cheerful, merry man'. The poem was printed with its companion Il Penseroso ('the pensive man') in Poems of Mr. John Milton (dated 1645, issued early 1646). The date of composition is uncertain; neither poem is in the Trinity Manuscript, and most editors place them in the early 1630s, around or just after Milton left Cambridge (1629-32). E. M. W. Tillyard linked the pair to Milton's academic exercise Prolusion I, on whether day or night is more excellent, and the poems do follow one ideal day and one ideal night. Each opens by banishing the other's presiding spirit and closes by choosing to live with its own goddess. Samuel Johnson thought every reader reads them with pleasure and stressed that each mind selects the objects that suit it.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Samuel Johnson, 'Milton', Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779-81); E. M. W. Tillyard, 'Milton: L'Allegro and Il Penseroso', in The Miltonic Setting (1938)

  2. The exorcism prelude and its metre ll. 1-10

    The opening exorcism is formally set off from the rest. Its ten lines alternate iambic trimeter and pentameter on an interlaced rhyme scheme (abbacddeec), giving a heavy, deliberately over-emphatic effect suited to a mock-solemn banishing. At l. 11 ('But com thou Goddes fair and free') the poem shifts to the light four-stress couplets, often with the first syllable dropped (seven syllables), that carry the rest of it. Il Penseroso begins with exactly the same ten-line pattern, so the two openings mirror each other. Editors note the parody of the classical hymn of banishment (the formula 'procul este, profani'), and the genealogy Milton gives Melancholy here, from Cerberus and Night, is his own invention, a grotesque counterpart to the noble parentage she receives in Il Penseroso.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Stella P. Revard, Milton and the Tangles of Neaera's Hair: The Making of the 1645 Poems (1997), ch. 3

  3. Cerberus l. 2

    Cerberus guards the gate of Hades (Hesiod, Theogony 310-12, makes him a child of Typhon and Echidna; Virgil, Aeneid 6.417-25). No classical source makes him the father of Melancholy: Milton coins the pedigree to make the gloomy, bile-driven melancholy banished here as monstrous as possible. Pairing him with 'blackest midnight' recalls Hesiod's Night, who bears a brood of evils (Theogony 211-25). The point is that this is the diseased melancholy of the physicians, black bile, not the contemplative melancholy that Il Penseroso will honour with a nobler descent from Saturn and Vesta.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Hesiod, Theogony 211-25, 310-12; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  4. Cimmerian desert l. 10

    In Odyssey 11.14-19 the Cimmerians live at the edge of Ocean, shrouded in mist and cloud, where the sun never shines. 'Cimmerian' became a byword for total darkness. Milton sends Melancholy to the bleakest place classical geography could supply; 'desert' means a deserted, uninhabited region rather than a sandy waste. Ebon shades and low-browed rocks (l. 8) complete a scene that is half underworld, half Gothic picture.

    Homer, Odyssey 11.14-19; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  5. ycleap'd Euphrosyne l. 12

    'Ycleap'd' is the old past participle of 'clepe', to call or name, with the Middle English y- prefix; Milton borrows it from the Spenserian archaic vocabulary. Euphrosyne (Greek euphrosyne, 'merriment, good cheer') is one of the three Graces with Aglaia and Thalia; Hesiod makes them daughters of Zeus and Eurynome (Theogony 907-9). The contrast 'In Heav'n ... And by men' imitates the Homeric habit of giving a thing its name among the gods and its name among mortals.

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); Hesiod, Theogony 907-9; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clepe'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  6. Venus and Bacchus as parents ll. 14-16

    The Graces are usually daughters of Zeus; the version making them children of Venus and Bacchus (love and wine) survives in Servius's commentary on the Aeneid (on 1.720), as Osgood noted. Ivy was sacred to Bacchus. Milton then sets this parentage aside in favour of what 'Sager' poets sing (l. 17), perhaps because love and wine suggest a grosser pleasure than the 'unreproved' mirth the poem wants (l. 40).

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  7. Zephyr and Aurora ll. 17-24

    No classical source makes Zephyr (the mild west wind of spring) the lover of Aurora (the dawn); in Hesiod the dawn is the mother of the winds, and her consort is Tithonus. Osgood therefore treats the pairing as Milton's departure from tradition. Its fitness is clear: the cheerful man's day begins at dawn, in fresh spring air, and the scene of 'a Maying', violets and dew-washed roses gives Mirth an innocent, outdoor, English birth instead of a Bacchic one. 'Bucksom' (l. 24) here means lively and yielding, from Middle English buhsum, 'obliging, pliant'; 'debonair' means gracious and courteous (Old French de bon aire).

    Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'buxom'

  8. the light fantastick toe ll. 33-34

    'Trip it' means dance with quick light steps; 'fantastick' means fanciful, capricious, following the play of fancy. The phrase became proverbial in the eighteenth century, when the two poems were endlessly imitated, and it is now chiefly used humorously. Its rhythm enacts the dance: the line's quick unstressed syllables carry the feet forward. The address 'ye' (l. 33) includes Mirth's whole train, not only the goddess, so the poem pictures a group dance led by Mirth with Liberty at her right hand.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fantastic'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Raymond D. Havens, The Influence of Milton on English Poetry (1922)

  9. The Mountain Nymph, sweet Liberty l. 36

    Mountain peoples were traditionally thought hardy and free, and editors compare the mountain nymphs (oreads) of classical pastoral. The line has often been read with hindsight in the light of Milton's later republican writings, but in the poem liberty chiefly means freedom from care and constraint, the condition of 'unreproved pleasures free' (l. 40). 'Unreproved' means blameless, beyond reproach: Milton stresses that this mirth is innocent.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.

  10. Who comes to the window? ll. 41-48

    Grammatically, 'Then to com' (l. 45) may continue 'To hear the Lark' (l. 41), so that the cheerful man himself rises and comes to his window to say good morning through the sweet-briar and vine. Alternatively the lark is the subject, coming to the window to bid the sleeper good morrow, which is ornithologically odd since larks do not visit windows. Some readers have even made the dawn the subject. Editors have not agreed, and the Variorum sets out the long history of the debate. The ambiguity keeps the waking man and the waking world blended. 'Eglantine' is itself another name for the sweet-briar, so some editors have wondered whether Milton meant honeysuckle by 'twisted Eglantine'.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Thomas Warton, ed., Poems upon Several Occasions ... by John Milton, 2nd edn (1791), note ad loc.

  11. the Cock and his Dames ll. 49-52

    'Scatters the rear of darknes thin' is a military metaphor: the cock's crowing chases off the rear-guard of night's retreating forces. 'His Dames' are his hens, mock-chivalric. The rustic scene is generalised rather than particular, and Johnson's summary of the poem singles it out: the cheerful man 'sees the cock strut'. At l. 114 the cock reappears ringing his 'Mattin' at the end of the night.

    Samuel Johnson, 'Milton', Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779-81); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  12. the Sun's state ll. 59-62

    'State' means royal pomp or a stately progress (OED). The 'Eastern gate' is the classical gate of the dawn. The clouds wear 'thousand Liveries', the uniforms of a great lord's retinue, and are 'dight' (dressed, adorned, an archaic past participle). 'Not unseen' (l. 57) is pointed: the cheerful man walks in company and in daylight, whereas the pensive man walks 'unseen' (Il Penseroso 65).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'state'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dight'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Samuel Johnson, 'Milton', Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779-81)

  13. every Shepherd tells his tale l. 67

    'Tell' can mean 'count' and 'tale' can mean 'number, tally' (compare 'teller' of votes and the phrase 'the tale of bricks' in Exodus 5:8, KJV). Warton argued that the shepherd counts his sheep at dawn as they leave the fold, a practical rural task in a list of workers (ploughman, milkmaid, mower). Others read a story or love tale told under the hawthorn, a pastoral commonplace. Both senses were current, and the line may hold both.

    Thomas Warton, ed., Poems upon Several Occasions ... by John Milton, 2nd edn (1791), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tale'; Exodus 5:8 (KJV)

  14. Lantskip l. 70

    The word entered English around 1600 from Dutch painting vocabulary, so 'Lantskip' keeps its sense of a composed scene; the eye 'measures' it as a painter would. The following list ('Russet Lawns, and Fallows Gray', mountains, meadows, brooks, towers) is arranged like a picture with foreground and distance. 'Lawns' are open grassy spaces, 'fallows' ploughed land left unsown, and 'pide' (l. 75) means variegated.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'landscape'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  15. Cynosure l. 80

    Greek kynosoura, 'dog's tail', named the constellation Ursa Minor, whose tail holds the Pole Star; mariners looked to it to steer, so it became a figure for whatever draws all eyes. Milton's line is an early figurative use in English, of the kind that lies behind the modern sense of 'centre of attention'. The beauty 'bosom'd high in tufted Trees' is the lady of a castle seen from afar, a romance motif. Compare Comus 341-42, where Milton again uses the 'Cynosure' as a guiding star.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cynosure'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900)

  16. Corydon, Thyrsis, Phillis, Thestylis ll. 83-88

    Corydon and Thyrsis compete in song in Virgil's Eclogue 7; Phyllis appears in Eclogues 3 and 7; Thestylis prepares a meal of herbs for the reapers in Eclogue 2.10-11. Milton transfers the classical names to an English cottage with its 'savory dinner' of herbs and 'Country Messes' (dishes). The effect is gently literary: the real countryside is seen through Virgilian pastoral. 'Neat-handed' means deft.

    Virgil, Eclogues 2, 3, 7; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  17. rebecks; Sunshine Holyday ll. 91-98

    The rebec, a bowed instrument of two or three strings, was by Milton's time a rustic fiddle. 'Up-land Hamlets' are hill villages; 'secure' (l. 91) means carefree (Latin securus, 'without care'), not 'safe'. 'Chequer'd shade' is the dappled shadow of leaves. Village wakes, maypoles and Sunday sports were politically sensitive in the 1630s: Charles I's reissued Book of Sports (1633) defended them against Puritan objections, and some critics read the passage against that controversy, though Milton presents it without polemic.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rebec'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'secure'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.

  18. Faery Mab and the junkets ll. 102-104

    Queen Mab, the fairies' midwife, is described by Mercutio in Romeo and Juliet 1.4, and Jonson brought her on stage in his Entertainment at Althorp (1603). 'Junkets' are sweet dishes of cream or curds. 'She was pincht, and pull'd' refers to the fairies' punishment of slovenly maids. 'Friars Lanthorn' is the ignis fatuus or will-o'-the-wisp, the marsh light that led night travellers astray (also 'Friar Rush's lantern'). The storytellers alternate (she ... he), a realistic touch of village gossip.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'junket'; Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet 1.4.53-95

  19. The drudging Goblin / Lubbar Fend ll. 105-114

    Folklore told of a hairy household spirit who did farm work overnight in return for a bowl of cream set out for him; Reginald Scot's Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584) mentions the bowl of milk left for Robin Goodfellow, and Puck boasts of the same character in A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1. A 'lubber fiend' is a clumsy, loutish demon (lubber = lout). 'Shadowy Flale' is the ghostly flail; 'Crop-full' means with full belly; 'Mattin' is matins, the first canonical hour, here the cock's crow before dawn.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lubber'; Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1.32-57

  20. Ladies whose bright eies Rain influence ll. 117-124

    The scene shifts to 'Towred Cities' and courtly romance: knights in 'weeds of Peace' (court dress, not armour) hold 'triumphs' (festivities, pageants, tournaments). 'Influence' is the astrological fluid believed to stream from the stars and shape human affairs (Latin influere, 'to flow in'); the ladies' eyes are stars that judge the prize of wit or arms. The image is Petrarchan and Spenserian, and fits the court masques and tilts of Jacobean and Caroline England.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'influence'; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  21. Hymen in saffron robe ll. 125-130

    Ovid opens Metamorphoses 10 with Hymen arriving 'croceo velatus amictu', veiled in a saffron cloak, for the ill-fated wedding of Orpheus and Eurydice, so his appearance here quietly anticipates the Orpheus ending of the poem. Hymen with his taper (wedding torch) was a stock figure of Jonson's wedding masques such as Hymenaei (1606). 'Mask' is masque, and 'antique Pageantry' may mean both 'ancient' and 'antic' (grotesque) display, the spelling not distinguishing them.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-7; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  22. Jonson's learned sock and Shakespeare's wood-notes ll. 131-134

    The soccus was the low slipper worn by actors in Roman comedy, as the cothurnus or buskin was the tragic boot (compare Il Penseroso 102). Jonson's comedies are 'learned' because they follow classical models; Shakespeare is 'fancies childe' warbling 'native Wood-notes wilde', like a songbird untaught by art. This contrast of art and nature became a critical commonplace, already present in Jonson's own folio poem (1623) with its 'small Latine, and lesse Greeke'. Milton's sonnet 'On Shakespear' (printed 1632) praises him for 'easie numbers' that flow without labour. Many later readers have found the 'wood-notes' line condescending.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.; Ben Jonson, 'To the memory of my beloved, the Author Mr. William Shakespeare' (First Folio, 1623)

  23. soft Lydian Aires ll. 135-144

    Greek theory linked each musical mode to an emotional effect. In Republic 3.398e-399a Plato's Socrates excludes the Lydian modes from his ideal city as slack and suited to drinking. Milton's cheerful man asks for exactly that softness 'against eating Cares', but makes it respectable by marrying the music 'to immortal verse', a union Milton returns to in 'At a Solemn Music'. The long sentence that follows, with its 'winding bout' (turn, circuit) and 'lincked sweetnes long drawn out', enacts the melisma it describes. 'Wanton heed, and giddy cunning' are deliberate oxymorons: a careful carelessness, a whirling skill.

    Plato, Republic 3.398e-399a; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, eds, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2: The Minor English Poems (1972), commentary ad loc.

  24. Orpheus and Eurydice ll. 145-150

    Orpheus charmed Pluto with his lyre and was allowed to lead his dead wife Eurydice out of Hades on condition he did not look back; he looked, and lost her (Virgil, Georgics 4.453-527; Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-85). Milton imagines Orpheus lifting his head from the flowers of Elysium to hear music that would have won her outright. Orpheus recurs in Il Penseroso 105-8 and, more darkly, in 'Lycidas' 58-63, where he is torn apart.

    Virgil, Georgics 4.453-527; Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-85; Charles G. Osgood, The Classical Mythology of Milton's English Poems (1900); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.

  25. Closing couplet and Marlowe ll. 151-152

    Marlowe's lyric ends: 'If these delights thy mind may move, / Then live with me, and be my love.' Milton turns the shepherd's invitation around: the poet accepts the goddess's offer conditionally. The conditional 'if' leaves the choice open, and Il Penseroso ends with the same formula addressed to Melancholy ('And I with thee will choose to live'). Critics have long debated whether the pair presents a progression from mirth to the higher contemplative life, or two equally valid modes; others see two equally valid modes of life.

    Christopher Marlowe, 'The Passionate Shepherd to His Love' (pub. 1599); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), note ad loc.
