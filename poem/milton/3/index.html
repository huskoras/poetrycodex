On the Late Massacre in Piedmont

by John Milton 1608–1674

History & PoliticsReligionSocial Commentaries

Avenge, O Lord, thy slaughtered saints, whose bones
Lie scattered on the Alpine mountains cold;
Ev’n them who kept thy truth so pure of old,
When all our fathers worshiped stocks and stones,
Forget not: in thy book record their groans
Who were thy sheep and in their ancient fold
Slain by the bloody Piedmontese that rolled
Mother with infant down the rocks. Their moans
The vales redoubled to the hills, and they
To Heav’n. Their martyred blood and ashes sow
O’er all th’ Italian fields where still doth sway
The triple Tyrant; that from these may grow
A hundredfold, who having learnt thy way
Early may fly the Babylonian woe.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Milton’s furious sonnet on the 1655 slaughter of the Waldensians is public poetry as both prayer and protest. The martyrs’ scattered ashes become seed: persecution, he insists, will multiply a hundredfold the faith it meant to destroy.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: the Easter 1655 massacre l. 1

    The Waldensians (Vaudois), followers of the twelfth-century preacher Valdes, lived in Alpine valleys of Piedmont under the Catholic Duke of Savoy. In April 1655 troops quartered on them at Easter massacred many, reportedly some 1,700 to 2,000 people. Protestant Europe was outraged; Cromwell protested to the Duke and other rulers in Latin letters drafted by Milton as Secretary for Foreign Tongues, sent Samuel Morland as envoy, and raised a public collection. The sonnet probably dates from mid-1655 and was first printed in 1673. Its opening imperative echoes the martyrs under the altar in Revelation 6:9-10 ('How long, O Lord... dost thou not judge and avenge our blood') and Psalm 79:10.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); KJV, Revelation 6:9-10; Psalm 79:1-3,10; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  2. Form: enjambment and the 'o' rhymes ll. 1-14

    The octave rhymes abbaabba on '-ones' and '-old' (bones, cold, old, stones, groans, fold, rolled, moans), and the sestet rhymes cdcdcd on '-ay' and '-o' (they, sway, way; sow, grow, wo). Critics hear in this a prolonged lament or echoing groan, which the poem itself names when the vales 'redoubled' the moans. Milton overrides the traditional divisions: the sentence beginning in l. 1 does not reach its verb 'Forget not' until l. 5, and the volta falls mid-line 10 ('To Heav'n. Their martyr'd blood...') rather than at the octave's end. This 'Miltonic' handling of the sonnet, with its paragraph-like momentum, was imitated by Wordsworth.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); John S. Smart, ed., The Sonnets of Milton (Glasgow, 1921), commentary

  3. the Alpine mountains l. 2

    By earlier agreements the Waldensians were confined to certain valleys on the Italian side of the western Alps. When ordered in January 1655 to withdraw from settlements in the plain, many retreated to the mountains, where the soldiers pursued them. The unburied bones 'scattered' on cold mountains recall Psalm 141:7 ('Our bones are scattered at the grave's mouth') and Psalm 79:2-3 on the unburied servants of God. The coldness of the mountains contrasts with the warmth of the 'blood and ashes' that will be sown in l. 10.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); KJV, Psalm 79:2-3; Psalm 141:7; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  4. kept thy truth so pure of old ll. 3-4

    Reformation historians, including Foxe and later Morland, presented the Waldensians as a church that had never submitted to Rome and so linked the Reformation to the apostles. Milton accepted this view (he cites them as witnesses to pure doctrine in his prose, for instance in The Likeliest Means to Remove Hirelings, 1659). 'When all our fathers worshiped stocks and stones' sets their purity against the medieval English, imagined as idolaters of wooden images ('stocks') and stone statues. The phrase comes from biblical denunciations of idolatry: Jeremiah 2:27, 'Saying to a stock, Thou art my father; and to a stone, Thou hast brought me forth'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); KJV, Jeremiah 2:27; 3:9; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  5. thy book l. 5

    'Forget not' and 'record' appeal to God's memory: 'thou tellest my wanderings: put thou my tears into thy bottle: are they not in thy book?' (Psalm 56:8). At the Last Judgement 'the books were opened' and the dead were judged by what was written in them (Revelation 20:12). The prayer asks that the martyrs' sufferings be kept on record for the day of reckoning, a more patient demand than the vengeance of l. 1.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); KJV, Psalm 56:8; Revelation 20:12; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  6. thy sheep ... ancient fold l. 6

    The pastoral image of the Church as a flock is biblical (Psalm 79:13, 'we thy people and sheep of thy pasture'; John 10). 'Ancient fold' refers both to their home valleys and to the ancient purity of their church. The image reverses its use in Lycidas, where the wolf threatens the sheep: here the 'bloody Piedmontese' play the wolf. Compare Romans 8:36, 'we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter'.

    KJV, Psalm 79:13; Romans 8:36; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  7. Mother with infant ll. 7-8

    Contemporary pamphlets reported that soldiers threw women and children from precipices. Samuel Morland, Cromwell's envoy to Savoy, published The History of the Evangelical Churches of the Valleys of Piemont (1658) with engraved plates of such atrocities, and the RPO editors suggest he may have supplied Milton with details. The single concrete image of mother and infant rolled down the rocks makes the horror particular, and the enjambment from 'rolled' to 'Mother' enacts the fall.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets; Wikipedia, 'On the Late Massacre in Piedmont'

  8. redoubled ll. 8-10

    'Redoubled' means re-echoed, repeated with increased force. The sound rises in stages: from the victims, to the valleys, to the hills, and finally 'To Heav'n', placed emphatically at the start of l. 10. The movement upward turns lament into appeal; it recalls Abel's blood crying from the ground (Genesis 4:10). The echoing structure is matched by the poem's insistent 'o' rhymes.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); KJV, Genesis 4:10; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  9. blood ... sow ... hundredfold ll. 10-13

    Tertullian wrote that 'the blood of Christians is seed' (semen est sanguis Christianorum, Apologeticum 50), proverbially 'the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church'. In the parable of the sower some seed falls on good ground and brings forth 'an hundredfold' (Matthew 13:8). Milton asks that the martyrs' blood and ashes be scattered over Italy so that converts spring up a hundredfold. Some have also heard a pun on the Cadmus myth, in which sown dragon's teeth grew into armed men, suggesting militant Protestants; this is a minority reading.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); Tertullian, Apologeticum 50 (Latin Library); KJV, Matthew 13:3-8

  10. the triple Tyrant l. 12

    The papal tiara, with its three crowns, was interpreted by Protestant polemic as a sign of usurped power over heaven, earth and hell. Calling the Pope a tyrant places him among illegitimate rulers, an important term in Milton's political prose (The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates, 1649). The Duke of Savoy carried out the massacre, but the poem traces responsibility to Rome.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets

  11. Babylonian woe l. 14

    Revelation 17-18 describes Babylon the great, the mother of harlots seated on seven hills, whose fall is lamented with the cry 'Alas, alas' ('woe, woe'). Protestant exegesis identified Babylon with Rome. Those who 'learnt thy way' may flee before the destruction, obeying Revelation 18:4: 'Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins'. 'Early' may mean soon, before the judgement, or early in life. Dante and Petrarch had also called the corrupt papal court Babylon, so the epithet had a long history.

    KJV, Revelation 18:2-4,10; Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnet XVIII: On the Late Massacre in Piemont', notes (text of Poems, 1673); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman, 1997), notes to the sonnets
