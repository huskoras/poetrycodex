On Shakespeare. 1630

by John Milton 1608–1674

Arts & SciencesTime & BrevityLiving

What needs my Shakespeare for his honoured bones,
The labour of an age in pilèd stones,
Or that his hallowed relics should be hid
Under a star-ypointing pyramid?
Dear son of memory, great heir of fame,
What need’st thou such weak witness of thy name?
Thou in our wonder and astonishment
Hast built thyself a live-long monument.
For whilst to th’ shame of slow-endeavouring art,
Thy easy numbers flow, and that each heart
Hath from the leaves of thy unvalued book
Those Delphic lines with deep impression took,
Then thou, our fancy of itself bereaving,
Dost make us marble with too much conceiving;
And so sepúlchred in such pomp dost lie,
That kings for such a tomb would wish to die.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Milton’s first published poem argues that Shakespeare needs no marble tomb: his readers, struck ‘marble’ with wonder, become his living monument. The conceit fuses elegy with poetry’s oldest boast — that verse outlasts stone.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its first printing ll. 1-16

    The poem first appeared, unsigned, in the preliminary pages of the Second Folio of Shakespeare's plays (1632), headed 'An Epitaph on the admirable Dramaticke Poet, W. Shakespeare'. It was Milton's first English poem in print. When he collected it in his Poems (1645) he gave it the title 'On Shakespeare' and the date 1630, when he was about twenty-one and still at Cambridge. The rhetorical question that opens it ('What needs...?') sets up the whole argument: an epitaph usually stands on a tomb, but this one denies that Shakespeare needs a tomb at all. 'My Shakespeare' is affectionate and possessive, the young reader claiming the poet as his own. It is also a direct echo of Ben Jonson's 'My Shakespeare, rise' in the First Folio verses of 1623.

    University of Kansas Libraries exhibit: Shakespeare's Second Folio, 1632; Luminarium: Milton, 'On Shakespeare' (text and notes); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Ben Jonson, 'To the memory of my beloved, the Author Mr. William Shakespeare', prefatory verse in the First Folio (1623); Bodleian First Folio

  2. honoured bones l. 1

    Shakespeare (d. 1616) was buried in the chancel of Holy Trinity, Stratford-upon-Avon. By 1623 a wall monument with a bust stood there, and the gravestone bears the verse curse on anyone who 'moves my bones'. Milton's 'honoured bones' and 'hallowed relics' (l. 3) treat the remains with a reverence close to that given to a saint's relics. The poem's point is that this physical memorial, however honoured, is beside the point: the true shrine is elsewhere. The religious vocabulary ('hallowed', 'relics') is used half-playfully by a Protestant poet, turning Catholic veneration of relics into a figure for literary devotion.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; S. Schoenbaum, William Shakespeare: A Compact Documentary Life (Oxford, 1977), on the grave and monument; Luminarium: Milton, 'On Shakespeare' (text and notes)

  3. The labour of an age in pilèd stones l. 2

    'The labour of an age' means work lasting a lifetime or a generation, the kind of effort that raised the pyramids of l. 4. The grave accent on 'pilèd' tells the reader to sound the -ed as a separate syllable, keeping the pentameter regular. The image looks back to the classical boast that poetry outlasts stone: Horace's monument 'more lasting than bronze' and higher than the 'royal site of the pyramids' (Odes 3.30.1-2). Milton turns the comparison around: piling stones is slow and heavy, while Shakespeare's art is 'easy' (l. 10).

    Horace, Odes 3.30 (Latin text, The Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  4. hallowed relics l. 3

    A relic is the bodily remains, or an object associated with, a saint, venerated in shrines. 'Hallowed' (made holy) sharpens the sense. The pairing with 'honoured bones' prepares for the poem's claim that Shakespeare has been canonised by his readers' devotion rather than by any church or monument. Note the parallel structure of ll. 1-4: two questions, each asking what need there is for an outward memorial ('pilèd stones', a 'pyramid').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  5. star-ypointing pyramid l. 4

    The prefix y- (from Old English ge-) survived in Middle English and in Spenser's deliberately antique diction, almost always on past participles (yclad, ycleped). Attaching it to a present participle, 'ypointing', is irregular, and editors have long noted the oddity; the Second Folio prints 'Starre-ypointing'. The effect is a Spenserian, monumental archaism. The 'pyramid' is likely the tall pointed obelisk often set on Jacobean tombs, as well as the Egyptian pyramid. A close parallel is the epitaph on the Stanley tomb at Tong, Shropshire, traditionally (though uncertainly) attributed to Shakespeare, which says that 'sky-aspiring pyramids' do not preserve our name. Milton may be answering Shakespeare in Shakespeare's own epitaphic idiom.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; University of Kansas Libraries exhibit: Shakespeare's Second Folio, 1632; Sylvia Morris, 'An obscure grave: did Shakespeare write the memorial verses at Tong?', The Shakespeare Blog (2013); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  6. Dear son of memory l. 5

    In Hesiod's Theogony the nine Muses are born to Zeus and Mnemosyne, Memory. To call Shakespeare 'son of memory' is to make him a brother of the Muses, a poet by birth rather than by training. The phrase also has a plainer sense: he lives on in what we remember, and so belongs to fame, whose 'heir' he is. The two titles in the line, 'son' and 'heir', set up the poem's legal and dynastic language: Shakespeare inherits fame as a son inherits an estate, and needs no 'weak witness' (l. 6), no document in stone, to prove his claim.

    Hesiod, Theogony 53-62, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (1914), Theoi Classical Texts; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  7. weak witness l. 6

    A witness, in law, is one who testifies, and a monument is literally a 'reminder' (Latin monere, to remind). Milton makes the tomb a weak witness because its testimony is unnecessary and inferior to the works themselves. The w-sounds of 'weak witness' give the line a scornful edge. The idea that a great man's real monument is his achievement is old (compare Pericles' Funeral Oration in Thucydides, where the whole earth is the tomb of famous men), but Milton applies it specifically to the book: the plays are Shakespeare's monument.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.43, trans. Richard Crawley (1874), Perseus

  8. a live-long monument ll. 7-8

    'Live-long' means lasting a whole life or for ever, and also suggests 'living'. The monument is built 'in our wonder and astonishment', i.e. in the reader's response. Two models stand behind the lines. Horace boasts that his odes are a monument more lasting than bronze (Odes 3.30). Ben Jonson, in the First Folio verses of 1623, had told Shakespeare 'Thou art a monument without a tomb, / And art alive still while thy book doth live'. Milton, writing for the Second Folio, builds on Jonson's lines. His twist is that the readers are the material of the monument: Shakespeare 'hast built thyself' out of us. 'Astonishment' prepares for the pun on turning to stone in l. 14 (Latin ex + tonare, to be thunderstruck; compare 'astony', stunned).

    Horace, Odes 3.30 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Ben Jonson, 'To the memory of my beloved, the Author Mr. William Shakespeare', prefatory verse in the First Folio (1623); Bodleian First Folio; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  9. slow-endeavouring art l. 9

    The contrast is between art won by slow effort and Shakespeare's 'easy numbers'. It draws on the early legend of Shakespeare as a natural genius: Heminges and Condell, in the First Folio address to readers, said they had scarcely received from him a blot in his papers, and Jonson replied in his Discoveries that he wished Shakespeare had blotted a thousand. Some critics take 'slow-endeavouring art' as a sideways reference to Jonson's own laborious method; others read it more generally as the art of all lesser writers, or even of the stone-masons of ll. 1-4. Milton later repeats the contrast in 'L'Allegro', where Shakespeare is 'fancy's child' warbling 'native wood-notes wild'.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Ben Jonson, 'To the memory of my beloved, the Author Mr. William Shakespeare', prefatory verse in the First Folio (1623); Bodleian First Folio; The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth College)

  10. easy numbers l. 10

    In Renaissance usage 'numbers' are measured syllables, hence verse (from Latin numeri, rhythms). 'Easy' does not mean simple, but done without visible strain. 'Flow' suggests a spring or stream, a traditional figure of poetic inspiration that leads into 'Delphic lines' two lines later, since Apollo's Delphi had its sacred Castalian spring. The syntax of ll. 9-14 is a long 'whilst ... that ... Then' period: while (and because) his verse flows and every heart takes its impression, he turns us to marble.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  11. unvalued l. 11

    In early modern English 'unvalued' can mean 'inestimable', beyond valuation. Shakespeare uses it in this sense in Clarence's dream of 'unvalued jewels' on the sea floor (Richard III 1.4). Milton means that the Folio is priceless. A modern reader is tempted to hear 'undervalued', and some critics have suggested a faint irony that the plays were not yet given the esteem they deserved, but the main sense is clear. 'Leaves' are the pages of the book, perhaps with a glance at the leaves of the Sibyl, whose prophecies were written on leaves.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Shakespeare, Richard III 1.4 (MIT Complete Works); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  12. Delphic lines l. 12

    Delphi was the seat of Apollo's oracle and the centre of his cult as god of poetry. 'Delphic lines' are therefore divinely inspired, oracular, possibly obscure and deep. 'With deep impression took' joins two senses of 'impression': the mark made by a seal pressed into wax (the heart as the wax that receives Shakespeare's stamp) and the printing of a book (an 'impression' is an edition, here the Folio itself). The lines thus enact what they say: printed impressions become impressions on readers.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Luminarium: Milton, 'On Shakespeare' (text and notes)

  13. our fancy of itself bereaving l. 13

    'Fancy' is imagination; to 'bereave' is to rob or deprive. Shakespeare's imagination so possesses ours that we are deprived of our own powers and are left in a trance. This rapt, stunned state is what turns readers to 'marble' in the next line. The idea of the reader possessed and transported by poetry belongs to the Platonic tradition of inspired frenzy, and the Muses' power to make one forget oneself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  14. make us marble with too much conceiving l. 14

    Wonder freezes the readers into marble, the very material of the 'pilèd stones' rejected in l. 2. So the tomb is built after all, but it is made of living readers. 'Conceiving' means imagining, forming conceptions, but also carries the sense of pregnancy: our minds are filled to excess. Milton uses a similar image in 'Il Penseroso', where Melancholy is to 'forget thyself to marble' in contemplation (l. 42). Behind it lie Ovidian petrifactions (e.g. Niobe turned to weeping stone in Metamorphoses 6). Critics have asked whether the image is wholly flattering: to be made marble is also to lose one's own creative motion, a worry that some read as the young Milton's ambivalence about Shakespeare's overpowering influence.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth College); Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.301-312 (Niobe), Latin text, The Latin Library

  15. sepúlchred l. 15

    To 'sepulchre' is to place in a tomb. The accent mark in this text shows the early modern stress (se-PUL-chred), required by the metre; the same stress is common in Shakespeare's verse. 'Pomp' is splendid display, the grandeur of royal funerals. The paradox is that a tomb made of the readers' petrified wonder is grander than any made of stone.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  16. kings for such a tomb would wish to die l. 16

    The epigrammatic last line caps the poem with a hyperbole. Kings build pyramids and mausolea for themselves, yet none can match a monument made of living readers. It returns to the 'pyramid' of l. 4, the Egyptian royal tomb, and completes the inversion: what kings strive for in stone, the poet receives freely in human hearts. Compare again the Tong epitaph associated with Shakespeare, which ends by claiming that the memory of the dead man will outlive marble. Such pointed closing couplets were typical of the seventeenth-century epitaph as a genre.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Sylvia Morris, 'An obscure grave: did Shakespeare write the memorial verses at Tong?', The Shakespeare Blog (2013); Luminarium: Milton, 'On Shakespeare' (text and notes)

  17. Form: heroic couplets ll. 1-16

    The poem is in eight pentameter couplets, close in form to the commendatory verses by Jonson and others printed in the Folios. The couplets fall into a clear rhetorical design: two questions (ll. 1-6), an answer (ll. 7-8), and an extended explanation ending in a pointed conclusion (ll. 9-16). The contracted forms ('need'st', 'th' shame') and the marked syllables ('pilèd', 'sepúlchred') show Milton's care for regular metre. Even here, at twenty-one, he writes in long periodic sentences that run across couplets, a sign of the style of his later blank verse.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; University of Kansas Libraries exhibit: Shakespeare's Second Folio, 1632
