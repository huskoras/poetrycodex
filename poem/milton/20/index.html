Death & Dying

Lycidas

by John Milton 1608–1674

From Poems (1645; enlarged 1673)

Death & Dying

YET once more, O ye Laurels, and once more
Ye Myrtles brown, with Ivy never-sear,
I com to pluck your Berries harsh and crude,
And with forc'd fingers rude,
Shatter your leaves before the mellowing year.
Bitter constraint, and sad occasion dear,
Compels me to disturb your season due:
For Lycidas is dead, dead ere his prime
Young Lycidas, and hath not left his peer:
Who would not sing for Lycidas? he knew
Himself to sing, and build the lofty rhyme.
He must not flote upon his watry bear
Unwept, and welter to the parching wind,
Without the meed of som melodious tear.

Begin then, Sisters of the sacred well,
That from beneath the seat of Jove doth spring,
Begin, and somwhat loudly sweep the string.
Hence with denial vain, and coy excuse,
So may som gentle Muse
With lucky words favour my destin'd Urn,
And as he passes turn,
And bid fair peace be to my sable shrowd.
For we were nurst upon the self-same hill,
Fed the same flock by fountain, shade, and rill.

Together both, ere the high Lawns appear'd
Under the opening eye-lids of the morn,
We drove a field and both together heard
What time the Gray-fly winds her sultry horn,
Batt'ning our flocks with the fresh dews of night,
Oft till the Star that rose, at Ev'ning, bright
Toward Heav'ns descent had slop'd his westering wheel.
Mean while the Rural ditties were not mute,
Temper'd to th'Oaten Flute;
Rough Satyrs danc'd, and Fauns with clov'n heel,
From the glad sound would not be absent long,
And old Damoetas lov'd to hear our song.

But O the heavy change, now thou art gon,
Now thou art gon, and never must return!
Thee Shepherd, thee the Woods, and desert Caves,
With wilde Thyme and the gadding Vine o'regrown,
And all their echoes mourn.
The Willows, and the Hazle Copses green,
Shall now no more be seen,
Fanning their joyous Leaves to thy soft layes.
As killing as the Canker to the Rose,
Or Taint-worm to the weanling Herds that graze,
Or Frost to Flowers, that their gay wardrop wear,
When first the White thorn blows;
Such, Lycidas, thy loss to Shepherds ear.

Where were ye Nymphs when the remorseless deep
Clos'd o're the head of your lov'd Lycidas?
For neither were ye playing on the steep,
Where your old Bards, the famous Druids ly,
Nor on the shaggy top of Mona high,
Nor yet where Deva spreads her wisard stream:
Ay me, I fondly dream!
Had ye bin there--for what could that have don?
What could the Muse her self that Orpheus bore,
The Muse her self, for her inchanting son
Whom Universal nature did lament,
When by the rout that made the hideous roar,
His goary visage down the stream was sent,
Down the swift Hebrus to the Lesbian shore.

Alas! What boots it with uncessant care
To tend the homely slighted Shepherds trade,
And strictly meditate the thankles Muse,
Were it not better don as others use,
To sport with Amaryllis in the shade,
Or with the tangles of Neaera's hair?
Fame is the spur that the clear spirit doth raise
(That last infirmity of Noble mind)
To scorn delights, and live laborious dayes:
But the fair Guerdon when we hope to find,
And think to burst out into sudden blaze.
Comes the blind Fury with th'abhorred shears,
And slits the thin spun life. But not the praise,
Phoebus repli'd, and touch'd my trembling ears;
Fame is no plant that grows on mortal soil,
Nor in the glistering foil
Set off to th'world, nor in broad rumour lies,
But lives and spreds aloft by those pure eyes,
And perfet witnes of all judging Jove;
As he pronounces lastly on each deed,
Of so much fame in Heav'n expect thy meed.

O Fountain Arethuse, and thou honour'd floud,
Smooth-sliding Mincius, crown'd with vocall reeds,
That strain I heard was of a higher mood:
But now my Oate proceeds,
And listens to the Herald of the Sea
That came in Neptune's plea,
He ask'd the Waves, and ask'd the Fellon winds,
What hard mishap hath doom'd this gentle swain?
And question'd every gust of rugged wings
That blows from off each beaked Promontory,
They knew not of his story,
And sage Hippotades their answer brings,
That not a blast was from his dungeon stray'd,
The Ayr was calm, and on the level brine,
Sleek Panope with all her sisters play'd.
It was that fatall and perfidious Bark
Built in th'eclipse, and rigg'd with curses dark,
That sunk so low that sacred head of thine.

Next Camus, reverend Sire, went footing slow,
His Mantle hairy, and his Bonnet sedge,
Inwrought with figures dim, and on the edge
Like to that sanguine flower inscrib'd with woe.
Ah; Who hath reft (quoth he) my dearest pledge?
Last came, and last did go,
The Pilot of the Galilean lake,
Two massy Keyes he bore of metals twain,
(The Golden opes, the Iron shuts amain)
He shook his Miter'd locks, and stern bespake,
How well could I have spar'd for thee, young swain,
Anow of such as for their bellies sake,
Creep and intrude, and climb into the fold?
Of other care they little reck'ning make,
Then how to scramble at the shearers feast,
And shove away the worthy bidden guest.
Blind mouthes! that scarce themselves know how to hold
A Sheep-hook, or have learn'd ought els the least
That to the faithfull Herdmans art belongs!
What recks it them? What need they? They are sped;
And when they list, their lean and flashy songs
Grate on their scrannel Pipes of wretched straw,
The hungry Sheep look up, and are not fed,
But swoln with wind, and the rank mist they draw,
Rot inwardly, and foul contagion spread:
Besides what the grim Woolf with privy paw
Daily devours apace, and nothing sed,
But that two-handed engine at the door,
Stands ready to smite once, and smite no more.

Return Alpheus, the dread voice is past,
That shrunk thy streams; Return Sicilian Muse,
And call the Vales, and bid them hither cast
Their Bels, and Flourets of a thousand hues.
Ye valleys low where the milde whispers use,
Of shades and wanton winds, and gushing brooks,
On whose fresh lap the swart Star sparely looks,
Throw hither all your quaint enameld eyes,
That on the green terf suck the honied showres,
And purple all the ground with vernal flowres.
Bring the rathe Primrose that forsaken dies.
The tufted Crow-toe, and pale Gessamine,
The white Pink, and the Pansie freakt with jeat,
The glowing Violet.
The Musk-rose, and the well attir'd Woodbine.
With Cowslips wan that hang the pensive hed,
And every flower that sad embroidery wears:
Bid Amaranthus all his beauty shed,
And daffadillies fill their cups with tears,
And strew the Laureat Herse where Lycid lies.
For so to interpose a little ease,
Let our frail thoughts dally with false surmise.
Ah me! Whilst thee the shores, and sounding Seas
Wash far away, where ere thy bones are hurl'd
Whether beyond the stormy Hebrides.
Where thou perhaps under the whelming tide
Visit'st the bottom of the monstrous world;
Or whether thou to our moist vows deny'd,
Sleep'st by the fable of Bellerus old,
Where the great vision of the guarded Mount
Looks toward Namancos and Bayona's hold;
Look homeward Angel now, and melt with ruth.
And, O ye Dolphins, waft the haples youth.

Weep no more, woful Shepherds weep no more,
For Lycidas your sorrow is not dead,
Sunk though he be beneath the watry floar,
So sinks the day-star in the Ocean bed,
And yet anon repairs his drooping head,
And tricks his beams, and with new spangled Ore,
Flames in the forehead of the morning sky:
So Lycidas sunk low, but mounted high,
Through the dear might of him that walk'd the waves
Where other groves, and other streams along,
With Nectar pure his oozy Lock's he laves,
And hears the unexpressive nuptiall Song,
In the blest Kingdoms meek of joy and love.
There entertain him all the Saints above,
In solemn troops, and sweet Societies
That sing, and singing in their glory move,
And wipe the tears for ever from his eyes.
Now Lycidas the Shepherds weep no more;
Hence forth thou art the Genius of the shore,
In thy large recompense and shalt be good
To all that wander in that perilous flood.

Thus sang the uncouth Swain to th'Okes and rills,
While the still morn went out with Sandals gray,
He touch'd the tender stops of various Quills,
With eager thought warbling his Dorick lay:
And now the Sun had stretch'd out all the hills,
And now was dropt into the Western bay;
At last he rose, and twitch'd his Mantle blew:
To morrow to fresh Woods, and Pastures new.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and opening ll. 1-5

    Edward King, a Fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, drowned when his ship sank off the Welsh coast on 10 August 1637, bound for Ireland. Milton's poem, dated November 1637 in his manuscript, closed the English section of the memorial volume Justa Edouardo King naufrago (1638), signed only 'J.M.'; in Poems (1645) he added a headnote saying it also foretold the ruin of the corrupt clergy. 'Yet once more' recalls Hebrews 12:26-27, where God shakes earth and heaven 'yet once more', so that apocalyptic overtones sound from the first words. It also marks Milton's reluctant return to verse after Comus (1634): he plucks the evergreens before the 'mellowing year', i.e. before he feels his talent ripe. The name Lycidas is a stock pastoral one (Theocritus, Idyll 7; Virgil, Eclogue 9).

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; KJV, Hebrews 12:26-27; Thomas H. Luxon, ed., The Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth), 'Lycidas'

  2. Laurels, Myrtles, Ivy ll. 1-2

    The three plants were the conventional crowns of poets. Laurel belonged to Apollo, god of poetry (and stands for the epic and lyric ambition Milton hoped to fulfil); myrtle was sacred to Venus and suggests love poetry; ivy, Bacchus' plant, was the garland of the learned poet (Horace, Odes 1.1.29, 'doctarum hederae praemia frontium'). 'Brown' means dark, as in Italian bruno; 'never-sear' means never withering. The berries are 'harsh and crude' (unripe, Latin crudus), and he shatters (scatters) the leaves with 'forc'd fingers rude': the young poet confesses that circumstance compels him to write before he is ready. The evergreens also anticipate the poem's ending, where fame proves to be no plant that grows on mortal soil.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Horace, Odes 1.1.29-30 (Latin Library)

  3. dear l. 6

    In early modern English 'dear' could mean dire or grievous (OED, dear adj.2), as in Hamlet's 'my dearest foe in heaven'. 'Sad occasion dear' therefore means a grievously painful occasion; yet the modern sense, 'beloved', is not excluded, since the occasion touches someone dear to the speaker. Milton's syntax ('Bitter constraint, and sad occasion dear, / Compels') makes two subjects take a singular verb, as though necessity and grief were one force. The phrase introduces the tension of the poem between obligation and reluctance. 'Season due' (l. 7) continues the horticultural metaphor: the poet disturbs the plants before their proper time, as death has cut off King before his.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Oxford English Dictionary, 'dear', adj.2 (grievous, dire); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  4. meed ... melodious tear l. 14

    'Meed' (OE med) means a reward or honour due. A 'melodious tear' is an elegiac poem: Spenser had called his complaint The Teares of the Muses (1591), and memorial volumes often presented their poems as tears. Lines 12-13 imagine King's unburied body tossed ('welter', roll about) on its 'watry bear' (bier), the sea itself. Burial was believed necessary for the soul's rest in classical thought (compare Palinurus in Aeneid 6), so the poem offers itself as a substitute rite. The word 'meed' returns at l. 84 ('Of so much fame in Heav'n expect thy meed'), where the earthly tribute is replaced by heavenly reward.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Oxford English Dictionary, 'meed', n.

  5. Sisters of the sacred well ll. 15-16

    The Muses are invoked in the traditional way at the opening of the song proper. Their 'sacred well' is the spring Aganippe or Hippocrene on Mount Helicon; Hesiod's Theogony opens with the Muses dancing round the altar of the son of Cronos on Helicon, which explains 'the seat of Jove'. Milton invokes the same haunts in Paradise Lost 1.10-12. 'Somwhat loudly sweep the string' asks for a fuller strain than ordinary pastoral, already announcing the poem's rise above the low style. In l. 19 the 'gentle Muse' is a future poet who will in turn write an elegy for the speaker, so that the elegist imagines his own death and urn.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Hesiod, Theogony 1-8 (Perseus Digital Library)

  6. the self-same hill ll. 23-36

    Following Virgil and Spenser, the poet allegorizes university life as shepherding. The two 'fed the same flock', heard the gray-fly's 'sultry horn' at noon, and 'batten'd' (fattened) their flocks into the evening, until the evening star (Hesperus) had sloped toward the west. 'Rural ditties' on the 'Oaten Flute' are their college verses. The 'Rough Satyrs' and 'Fauns' have been read as fellow students, and 'old Damoetas' as a senior member of the college (the RPO note says only 'some fellow of the college'); no identification is certain. Some readers note the idealization, since Milton and King had different college careers and King had been preferred to a fellowship by royal mandate.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2 (1972), commentary on 'Lycidas'

  7. Damoetas l. 36

    Damoetas is a herdsman in Theocritus, Idyll 6, and in Virgil, Eclogues 2 and 3. Milton's 'old Damoetas', who loved to hear the young shepherds' songs, has been taken to stand for some fellow or tutor of Christ's College; the RPO editors say only 'presumably' a fellow, and particular names (Joseph Mede, William Chappell) are conjecture. Caution is wise, since pastoral convention requires an elder who appreciates the young singers. The line closes the idyllic memory before the 'heavy change' of l. 37.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Virgil, Eclogue 3 (Latin Library)

  8. Canker, Taint-worm, Frost ll. 45-49

    The canker is the worm or grub that destroys rosebuds; the taint-worm was believed to infect young calves and lambs ('weanling Herds'); frost kills flowers when the hawthorn ('White thorn') first blossoms ('blows') in May. Each simile insists on untimeliness: the destruction falls on the young at the moment of flowering, returning to the poem's theme of death before the 'prime' (l. 8). The passage is the climax of the pathetic fallacy of lines 37-49, in which woods, caves and willows mourn and echo. The 'gay wardrop' (wardrobe) of the flowers anticipates the later flower catalogue.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Oxford English Dictionary, 'canker', n.; 'taint-worm', n.

  9. Where were ye Nymphs ll. 50-55

    The reproach to the nymphs imitates Theocritus, Idyll 1.66-69, and Virgil, Eclogue 10.9-12. Milton transposes it to British geography near the wreck: the steep of the Druid burial places in Wales; Mona (Anglesey), a Druid stronghold according to Tacitus; and Deva, the Dee, which flows past Chester, the port King sailed from. The Dee is 'wisard' because shifts in its course were said to foretell fortunes for England or Wales (the RPO note says it was supposed to possess magic powers, foretelling the fortunes of hostile nations; Drayton tells the legend in Poly-Olbion). The speaker at once rebukes his own question, 'I fondly (foolishly) dream', since even the Muse could not save her son Orpheus.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Virgil, Eclogue 10.9-12 (Latin Library)

  10. Orpheus ll. 58-63

    Ovid (Metamorphoses 11.1-55) and Virgil (Georgics 4.517-527) tell how the Thracian women in Bacchic frenzy ('the rout that made the hideous roar') dismembered the poet Orpheus; his head and lyre drifted down the river Hebrus and out to Lesbos, still singing. The allusion is double: it shows that poetry and divine parentage cannot protect the poet, and it shadows King's death in water. For Milton the figure was personally charged; in Paradise Lost 7.32-38 he again fears the fate of Orpheus at the hands of a barbarous rout. Some critics also read the floating head as an image of poetic survival after death, preparing for the consolations to come.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.1-55 (Latin Library); Virgil, Georgics 4.517-527 (Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  11. Amaryllis, Neaera ll. 64-69

    'What boots it' means 'what use is it'. To 'meditate the thankles Muse' translates Virgil, Eclogue 1.2 (silvestrem tenui Musam meditaris avena), where meditari means to practise or compose. Amaryllis appears in Virgil, Eclogue 1, and Neaera in Eclogue 3 and in Tibullus; the 'tangles' of her hair recall Renaissance love lyric. The temptation is to write for pleasure, as 'others use' (are accustomed); the RPO editors suggest a glance at the Caroline court poets. The passage raises the central doubt of the poem: if a young poet like King can die before fulfilling his promise, disciplined preparation seems wasted.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Virgil, Eclogue 1.1-5 (Latin Library)

  12. Fame is the spur ll. 70-84

    'Clear' means noble, illustrious (Latin clarus). The parenthesis 'That last infirmity of Noble mind' renders Tacitus, Histories 4.6, on Helvidius Priscus: even in the wise, the desire for glory is the last thing to be shed. The 'Guerdon' (reward) is snatched away by 'the blind Fury', Milton's deliberate substitution of a Fury for Atropos, the Fate who cuts the thread of life. Phoebus Apollo then 'touch'd my trembling ears', a gesture from Virgil, Eclogue 6.3-4, where Apollo plucks the poet's ear to recall him to humbler song. His answer moves the argument from earthly reputation ('broad rumour', the glittering 'foil' or metal backing of a gem) to the judgement of 'all judging Jove', the Christian God under a classical name.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Tacitus, Histories 4.6 (Latin Library); Virgil, Eclogue 6.3-5 (Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  13. Arethuse and Mincius ll. 85-88

    Arethusa, a nymph pursued by the river-god Alpheus, became a spring at Syracuse in Sicily, home of Theocritus; the Mincius flows past Mantua, Virgil's birthplace, and is described in Eclogue 7.12-13 as fringed with tender reeds. Invoking both, the speaker apologizes for the elevated strain of Phoebus ('a higher mood', a musical term for mode) and returns to his 'Oate', the oaten pipe of pastoral. This structure of digression and recall recurs after St Peter's speech (ll. 132-133, 'Return Alpheus... Return Sicilian Muse'). The pattern makes the poem's form a series of interruptions of pastoral by more powerful voices.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Virgil, Eclogue 7.12-13 (Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  14. Triton, Hippotades, Panope ll. 89-99

    The 'Herald of the Sea' is Triton, who comes 'in Neptune's plea', that is, to defend the sea-god from blame. Hippotades, 'son of Hippotes', is Aeolus, keeper of the winds, so called in Odyssey 10.2 and Ovid. His evidence that no wind strayed from its 'dungeon' and the sea was level clears nature of guilt. Panope is one of the Nereids, sea-nymphs associated with calm, named in Virgil, Aeneid 5.240. The quasi-legal hearing reflects the fact that King's ship went down in calm weather, as the memorial volume reported, and points the blame elsewhere: to the ship itself.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Homer, Odyssey 10.1-2 (Perseus); Virgil, Aeneid 5.239-240 (Latin Library)

  15. fatall and perfidious Bark ll. 100-102

    An eclipse was proverbially a time of evil influence; compare the witches' 'slips of yew / Sliver'd in the moon's eclipse' in Macbeth 4.1. 'Perfidious' (faithless) is a word applied to the sea and to ships in classical poetry. The line may also hint at a human failing, since the ship seems to have been unseaworthy. The sound pattern of 'sunk so low that sacred head' slows the line on the moment of loss; the phrase 'sacred head' anticipates the rising day-star of l. 169, which 'repairs his drooping head'.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  16. Camus ll. 103-107

    Camus walks slowly, in a hairy mantle of river weed and a bonnet of sedge; the sedge bears dim figures like 'that sanguine flower inscrib'd with woe', the hyacinth. In Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.162-219, Apollo accidentally kills the youth Hyacinthus and turns his blood into a flower marked AI AI, the Greek cry of grief. The detail links another beloved youth dead before his time to Apollo, the god of poetry, who has just spoken. 'My dearest pledge' renders Latin pignus, a child as a pledge of love: King is the university's son.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.162-219 (Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  17. St Peter ll. 108-112

    Peter fished on the Sea of Galilee and was made 'pilot' of the Church. Christ promised him 'the keys of the kingdom of heaven' (Matthew 16:19; compare Isaiah 22:22): the golden key opens, the iron shuts 'amain' (with force). His 'Miter'd locks' make him a bishop, the only true one in the poem; Milton, at this date still within the Church of England but hostile to Laud's policies, has Peter himself denounce unworthy pastors. Since King had been destined for the ministry, his death becomes the occasion of the poem's 1645 headnote foretelling 'the ruin of our corrupted Clergy'. The intrusion of an apostle into a classical pastoral was criticized by Dr Johnson as an irreverent mixture of trifling fictions with sacred truths.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); KJV, Matthew 16:19; Isaiah 22:22; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Samuel Johnson, 'Milton', Lives of the Poets (1779-81), on Lycidas; summarized in Wikipedia, 'Lycidas'

  18. Blind mouthes l. 119

    The clergy who 'creep and intrude, and climb into the fold' are the hirelings and thieves of John 10:1. In Sesame and Lilies (1865), Ruskin explained the compressed metaphor: 'bishop' means one who oversees (Greek episkopos) and 'pastor' one who feeds; the worst bishop therefore lacks sight and the worst pastor wants to be fed rather than to feed, hence 'blind mouths'. Ruskin also dwelt on 'scrannel' (thin, harsh-sounding; possibly Milton's coinage, echoing Virgil, Eclogue 3.27) for their 'lean and flashy' (insipid) songs. 'What recks it them?' asks what it matters to them; 'They are sped' means they are provided for. The hungry sheep, swollen with wind, rot inwardly: the image of false doctrine spreading like disease.

    John Ruskin, Sesame and Lilies (1865), Lecture 1, 'Of Kings' Treasuries'; Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); KJV, John 10:1-13; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  19. the grim Woolf ll. 128-129

    The wolf that ravages the fold is a standard image of false teachers (Matthew 7:15; John 10:12) and appears in Spenser's ecclesiastical eclogues (Shepheardes Calender, May and September). Most editors take it to mean Roman Catholic proselytizing, which Puritans believed was tolerated at the court of Henrietta Maria and under Archbishop Laud; some add the Jesuits specifically. 'Nothing sed' (said) refers to the silence of the authorities. The reading 'nothing sed' is that of 1645; an earlier reading with 'little' is reported by some editors (consult an apparatus for which witness has which), and the sharpening has been cited as evidence of Milton's hardening position.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; KJV, Matthew 7:15; John 10:12

  20. that two-handed engine ll. 130-131

    The line promises that retribution waits 'at the door', ready to strike once and for all. Candidates proposed include the axe laid to the root of the trees (Matthew 3:10), the two-edged sword of the Word (Hebrews 4:12; Revelation 1:16), St Michael's sword, Peter's keys, Time's scythe, the two Houses of Parliament, and England and Scotland together. Donald Dorian's 1930 survey and W. Arthur Turner's of 1950 (which collected ten readings and favoured the lock or power of Peter's keys) are classic summaries, and later counts reach dozens of proposals. The RPO editors settle for a heavy weapon kept at the fold against the wolf. Many critics now argue that the vagueness is purposeful: the menace is felt as certain while its instrument remains unnamed.

    Donald C. Dorian, 'Milton's "Two-Handed Engine"', PMLA 45 (1930), 204-215; Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2 (1972), commentary on 'Lycidas'; KJV, Matthew 3:10; Hebrews 4:12; W. Arthur Turner, 'Milton's Two-Handed Engine', Journal of English and Germanic Philology 49 (1950), 562-565

  21. Alpheus l. 132

    Alpheus, a river of the Peloponnese, was said to flow under the sea to rise in the spring of Arethusa at Syracuse, uniting with the nymph he loved (Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.572-641). He 'shrunk' at the 'dread voice' of St Peter, as pastoral shrinks before prophecy. 'Return Sicilian Muse' invokes the Muse of Theocritus. The subterranean river that passes through the sea untainted has also been read as a figure for Lycidas's passage through the waters to new life, anticipating the resurrection imagery of the close.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); Ovid, Metamorphoses 5.572-641 (Latin Library); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  22. The flower catalogue ll. 133-151

    The 'swart Star' is Sirius, the dog-star, whose late-summer rising was thought to scorch vegetation; it looks 'sparely' on shaded valleys. 'Rathe' means early (OE hrathe), 'freakt with jeat' streaked with jet-black, 'Crow-toe' perhaps the wild hyacinth. Amaranthus is the 'unfading' flower (Greek amarantos), here asked to shed its beauty in grief; in Paradise Lost 3.353-357 it is transplanted to heaven. The Trinity College manuscript shows that Milton drafted this passage separately and revised it heavily. The whole catalogue is then called a 'false surmise': there is no hearse, since the body lies somewhere under the sea.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2 (1972), commentary on 'Lycidas'; Oxford English Dictionary, 'rathe', adj.

  23. Bellerus, the guarded Mount, Namancos ll. 156-163

    The speaker imagines the corpse carried either north past the 'stormy Hebrides' or south to Cornwall. Bellerus is a giant apparently invented from Bellerium, the Roman name for Land's End. The 'great vision of the guarded Mount' is the Archangel Michael, who according to legend appeared on St Michael's Mount; he looks across the sea toward Namancos and Bayona's stronghold in north-west Spain (both on Mercator's maps), as guardian against the Catholic power. 'Look homeward Angel now' asks him to turn his gaze from Spain to England and the drowned youth, and to 'melt with ruth' (pity). The dolphins evoke Arion, carried to safety by a dolphin, and Melicertes-Palaemon, made a sea-god.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; A. S. P. Woodhouse and Douglas Bush, A Variorum Commentary on the Poems of John Milton, vol. 2 (1972), commentary on 'Lycidas'

  24. the day-star ll. 165-171

    'Weep no more' announces the consolation. The day-star is primarily the sun, which seemingly sinks into the ocean and rises refreshed, 'tricks' (adorns) his beams, and flames with 'new spangled Ore' (gold, Latin aurum). The word also carries the scriptural sense of 2 Peter 1:19 ('until the day dawn, and the day star arise in your hearts') and Revelation 22:16, where Christ is the bright and morning star. The resurrection is effected 'Through the dear might of him that walk'd the waves', Christ walking on the sea (Matthew 14:25), the decisive answer to drowning. Note the repetition of 'sunk' from l. 102.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); KJV, 2 Peter 1:19; Matthew 14:25; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  25. unexpressive nuptiall Song ll. 175-181

    'Unexpressive' means inexpressible (compare 'the fair, the chaste and unexpressive she' in As You Like It 3.2). The nuptial song is the hymn at the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:6-9) and the new song which only the 144,000 virgins could learn (Revelation 14:3-4); critics note that King died unmarried, so his virginity qualifies him for that choir. 'Laves' means washes, in nectar, the drink of the gods; the saints 'entertain' (receive) him in 'sweet Societies'. 'Wipe the tears for ever from his eyes' echoes Revelation 7:17 and 21:4 and Isaiah 25:8, closing the poem's long sequence of tears.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); KJV, Revelation 14:3-4; 19:6-9; 21:4; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'

  26. Genius of the shore ll. 183-185

    In Roman religion a genius loci is the tutelary spirit of a place. Lycidas becomes the guardian of the very waters that drowned him, 'good / To all that wander in that perilous flood', in the manner of Melicertes-Palaemon, who after drowning was deified as a harbour god; Virgil's Daphnis (Eclogue 5.64-65) is similarly made a beneficent deity. The pagan and Christian consolations sit side by side: Lycidas is both among the saints and a classical sea-spirit. 'Large recompense' recalls the 'meed' of l. 84, now granted.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'; Virgil, Eclogue 5.56-80 (Latin Library)

  27. The coda: uncouth Swain, Dorick lay ll. 186-193

    Up to l. 185 the speaker sings in his own voice; the last eight lines step outside and describe him. 'Uncouth' means unknown, unrefined (OE uncuth, unknown); the 'Quills' are reed pipes; 'Dorick' is the Doric dialect of Theocritus, standing for pastoral song. The coda is a regular ottava rima (abababcc), the only regular stanza in a poem otherwise built from irregular rhymed verse paragraphs modelled on the Italian canzone, with ten lines left unrhymed (F. T. Prince, The Italian Element in Milton's Verse, 1954). The sun sets, the swain rises and twitches his blue mantle (the colour of hope, or simply a shepherd's cloak), and the last line, 'fresh Woods, and Pastures new', looks forward to new poetic work.

    F. T. Prince, The Italian Element in Milton's Verse (Oxford, 1954), ch. on 'Lycidas'; Representative Poetry Online, 'Lycidas', notes by A. S. P. Woodhouse and Hugh MacCallum (text of Poems, 1645); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, 2nd edn (Longman Annotated English Poets, 1997), notes to 'Lycidas'
