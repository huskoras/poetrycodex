Time & Brevity

Sonnet 7: How Soon Hath Time

by John Milton 1608–1674

Time & BrevityLivingReligion

How soon hath Time, the subtle thief of youth,
Stol’n on his wing my three-and-twentieth year!
My hasting days fly on with full career,
But my late spring no bud or blossom shew’th.
Perhaps my semblance might deceive the truth
That I to manhood am arriv’d so near,
And inward ripeness doth much less appear,
That some more timely-happy spirits endu’th.
Yet be it less or more, or soon or slow,
It shall be still in strictest measure ev’n
To that same lot, however mean or high,
Toward which Time leads me, and the will of Heav’n:
All is, if I have grace to use it so
As ever in my great Task-Master’s eye.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

At twenty-three, Milton frets that he has produced nothing equal to his ambition. The sonnet resolves that anxiety into submission: whatever his pace, his life unfolds ‘in strictest measure’ under ‘my great Task-Master’s eye.’

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and date ll. 1-14

    The sonnet survives in the Trinity College (Cambridge) manuscript, copied into the draft of a letter to an unnamed friend who seems to have reproached Milton for idling in study instead of entering the ministry. Milton sends the sonnet as proof of his self-examination. It was printed in Poems (1645) and numbered VII in the 1673 edition. The date depends on how one reads 'three-and-twentieth year' (l. 2): the traditional dating is 9 December 1631, Milton's twenty-third birthday, but some editors prefer December 1632. Milton was then living at home after Cambridge, preparing through private study for a vocation as a poet.

    Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; Luminarium: Milton, Sonnet VII (text and notes); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  2. the subtle thief of youth l. 1

    'Subtle' means crafty, stealthy. Time's theft of youth is a commonplace of Renaissance poetry, descending from Ovid's 'tempus edax rerum', time the devourer of things (Metamorphoses 15.234). Shakespeare's Sonnet 77 speaks of 'Time's thievish progress to eternity'. The pun in 'Stol'n' (l. 2) works both ways: time has stolen the year, and has stolen past him, quietly. The octave thus opens with Time as an enemy, a figure the sestet will reverse when Time 'leads' him (l. 12).

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.234, Latin text, The Latin Library; Shakespeare, Sonnet 77 (MIT Complete Works); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  3. on his wing l. 2

    Winged Time (often with scythe and hourglass) was a standard figure in Renaissance emblem books, and the proverb 'time flies' goes back to Virgil, 'fugit inreparabile tempus' (Georgics 3.284). Time carries off the year on its wing as a bird of prey carries its catch. Compare Job 9:25-26, where the speaker's days 'flee away' 'as the eagle that hasteth to the prey', which also anticipates 'hasting days' in l. 3.

    Virgil, Georgics 3.284, Latin text, The Latin Library; Bible (KJV), Job 9:25-26; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  4. my three-and-twentieth year l. 2

    If the 'three-and-twentieth year' is the year that has just been stolen, Milton has completed it and is now twenty-three, which gives 9 December 1631, the traditional date (Luminarium and many chronologies). But the phrase can also be counted as the year he is living in, a twenty-third year that closes only on his twenty-fourth birthday, in December 1632, and some editors prefer that date. The evidence of the sonnet alone does not settle it. Either way it was written while Milton was living at home after Cambridge, and it goes with the letter to a friend preserved in the Trinity manuscript.

    Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; Luminarium: Milton, Sonnet VII (text and notes); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  5. full career l. 3

    The noun 'career' first meant a racecourse or the short gallop at full speed made in a tournament or the manege, hence 'in full career', headlong. The modern sense of a course of professional life had not yet developed, though for modern readers the word nicely anticipates the poem's concern with vocation. The days are 'hasting', hurrying, like the eagle in Job 9:26.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Bible (KJV), Job 9:25-26

  6. my late spring no bud or blossom l. 4

    'Late' means both recent (his spring has just passed) and belated (a slow spring). The plant image of bud and blossom, continued in 'ripeness' (l. 7), measures Milton's development as a poet in natural terms. He had already written the Nativity Ode, 'L'Allegro' and 'Il Penseroso', but by his own high standard none of this counted as the great work he intended. 'Shew'th' (shows) is contracted to one syllable so as to rhyme with 'youth' (l. 1), as 'endu'th' (l. 8) rhymes with 'truth' (l. 5).

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse

  7. semblance l. 5

    'Semblance' is outward appearance. Milton looked young for his age: at Cambridge he was nicknamed 'the Lady of Christ's', partly for his delicate looks, and he alludes to the nickname in his sixth Prolusion. Lines 5-8 are hard to parse: perhaps my looks belie the fact that I am so near manhood, and inner maturity shows itself even less in me than it does in some more fortunate spirits who mature on time.

    Milton, Prolusion VI, in Complete Prose Works, vol. 1, ed. Don M. Wolfe (Yale, 1953); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  8. inward ripeness l. 7

    Ripeness carries on the vegetable image of l. 4: the bud and blossom should lead to ripe fruit. The concept is central in Milton's thinking about readiness for vocation: in the letter that contains the sonnet he defends his delay as a deliberate preparation, not idleness. Compare Edgar's 'Ripeness is all' in King Lear 5.2, where ripeness also means a readiness that cannot be hurried and is in the hands of providence.

    Shakespeare, King Lear 5.2 (MIT Complete Works); Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  9. some more timely-happy spirits l. 8

    'Timely-happy' means fortunate in timeliness, blessed with an early ripening. The phrase need not refer to anyone in particular, but it implies comparison with precocious contemporaries who had already made names for themselves. Editors have speculated about particular writers, but the poem gives no clue, and the general sense of envy and self-measurement is what matters. The hyphenated compound epithet is typical of Milton's early style.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Luminarium: Milton, Sonnet VII (text and notes)

  10. endu'th l. 8

    To 'endue' (or 'endow') is to furnish with a quality or gift; it overlaps with 'indue', to put on like a garment. The verb implies that ripeness is given, not earned, preparing for the sestet's appeal to 'grace' (l. 13). The rhyme with 'truth' (l. 5) completes the octave's two rhymes in the Italian manner (abba abba; 'shew'th' rhymes with 'youth'). The archaic -th ending, already old-fashioned by 1632, lends the line a grave, scriptural tone.

    Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.

  11. The volta and Italian form l. 9

    The sonnet follows the Italian (Petrarchan) pattern Milton preferred: an octave abbaabba and a sestet cdedce, with no closing couplet. The volta at 'Yet' is clean: the octave laments outward and inner slowness, the sestet resolves it by submitting the question to Providence. The balanced pairs 'less or more, or soon or slow' and 'mean or high' sweep away the anxious comparisons of the octave. Milton had studied Italian sonneteers (Petrarch, Della Casa, Tasso) and had written Italian sonnets himself.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth), Sonnet 7

  12. in strictest measure even l. 10

    'Still' means always; 'ev'n' (one syllable) means level, exactly corresponding. Whatever his gifts ('it', the inward ripeness), they will always be exactly proportioned to the lot to which Time and God are leading him. 'Measure' may also suggest the measure of verse, a fitting pun for a poet, and the biblical idea that God measures each person's portion (compare 'with what measure ye mete', Matthew 7:2).

    Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v.; Bible (KJV), Matthew 7:2

  13. that same lot, however mean or high l. 11

    A 'lot' is the share allotted, originally by the casting of lots, and in the Bible the inheritance God assigns (Psalm 16:5-6, 'thou maintainest my lot. The lines are fallen unto me in pleasant places'). 'However mean or high' accepts in advance either a humble or a glorious calling. The modern reader knows that it would be high, but in 1632 Milton could not, and the poem's force lies in that uncertainty.

    Bible (KJV), Psalm 16:5-6; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  14. Time ... and the will of Heaven l. 12

    The sestet transforms the figure of Time from l. 1. There it was a thief; here it 'leads', in partnership with 'the will of Heav'n'. Time becomes an instrument of Providence rather than its enemy. The idea that each event comes at its due season echoes Ecclesiastes 3:1, 'To every thing there is a season'. The submission anticipates the patient waiting of Sonnet 19's last line.

    Bible (KJV), Ecclesiastes 3:1; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line

  15. All is, if I have grace to use it so ll. 13-14

    The syntax is elliptical and has been much debated. One reading: 'All that matters is whether I have grace to use my lot as one ever in God's sight'. Another: 'All is (as it has always been) in God's eye, provided I have grace to use it'. A third takes 'All is ... as ever' to mean that everything, including his slow growth, is eternally seen and ordered by God. On every reading, success depends on grace, God's unmerited gift, rather than on Milton's own effort or timing; the poem's anxiety about achievement is answered by the theology of grace.

    Representative Poetry Online, Milton, Sonnet VII, notes by Hugh MacCallum and A. S. P. Woodhouse; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems, Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn (1997), note on the line; Luminarium: Milton, Sonnet VII (text and notes)

  16. my great Task-Master's eye l. 14

    The image recalls the Gospel parables of service: the master who gives talents to his servants and on return demands an account (Matthew 25:14-30), and the householder who hires labourers at different hours yet pays them alike (Matthew 20:1-16), which suits a man worried about being late. In Exodus 'taskmasters' are the Egyptian overseers (Exodus 1:11), but Milton's 'great' Task-Master is just and watchful, not cruel. The thought returns, darker, in Sonnet 19 ('When I consider how my light is spent'), with its 'one talent which is death to hide' and its question whether God exacts 'day-labour, light denied'.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 25:14-30; Bible (KJV), Matthew 20:1-16; Bible (KJV), Exodus 1:11; The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth College)
