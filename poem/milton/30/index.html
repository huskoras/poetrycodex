Death & Dying

Sonnet 23: Methought I Saw My Late Espoused Saint

by John Milton 1608–1674

From Poems (1645; enlarged 1673)

Death & Dying

Methought I saw my late espoused Saint
Brought to me like Alcestis from the grave,
Whom Joves great Son to her glad Husband gave,
Rescu'd from death by force though pale and faint.
Mine as whom washt from spot of child-bed taint,
Purification in the old Law did save,
And such, as yet once more I trust to have
Full sight of her in Heaven without restraint,
Came vested all in white, pure as her mind:
Her face was vail'd, yet to my fancied sight,
Love, sweetness, goodness, in her person shin'd
So clear, as in no face with more delight.
But O as to embrace me she enclin'd
I wak'd, she fled, and day brought back my night.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The sonnet: date and publication l. 1

    Milton was totally blind from about 1652. He married Katherine Woodcock in November 1656; she bore a daughter in October 1657 and died in February 1658, the child dying soon after. Samuel Johnson and most later editors connect the sonnet with her and date it to 1658, though the identification is disputed (see the note on 'late espoused'). It was printed in the enlarged second edition of the Poems (1673) and is conventionally numbered Sonnet 23. The poem is a dream vision: the dead wife appears, veiled, and vanishes as he wakes. It is one of the few poems in which Milton writes directly about his marriage and his blindness.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); Thomas H. Luxon, ed., The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth), Sonnet 23 (in the 1673 Poems section); "Sonnet XXIII: Methought I saw my late espoused saint", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  2. Methought l. 1

    'Methought' is an impersonal verb ('it seemed to me', Old English me thuhte), used to introduce dreams in medieval and Renaissance poetry. It prepares for the waking of the last line. Milton places the vision on the boundary between imagination and revelation, a question that matters for a blind man who 'sees' only in dreams. Ancient tradition held that some dreams were true and others false, and the poem leaves its status uncertain.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "methinks, v. (methought)"; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  3. late espoused: which wife? l. 1

    Both senses of 'late' operate: she was espoused (married) only lately, and she is the late (deceased) wife. The identification is a long-standing crux. Most commentators, Samuel Johnson among them, favour Katherine Woodcock, married only fifteen months, whom the blind poet had never seen, which gives special point to 'vail'd' and 'fancied sight'. A minority view, associated especially with W. R. Parker, argues for Mary Powell, Milton's first wife, who died in 1652 a few days after childbirth and whom he had seen; others take the figure as an idealised wife rather than a single woman. The purification lines (ll. 5-6) have been used on both sides of the argument, and the question cannot be settled from the text alone.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); William Riley Parker, Milton: A Biography, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968)

  4. Saint l. 1

    In Protestant usage 'saint' could mean any of the elect, especially one who has died and is received into heaven, rather than a person formally canonised. Milton applies the word to his dead wife without any Catholic overtone; Henry King's 'Shrine of my dead Saint' in 'The Exequy' uses the same idiom. The word prepares for the white robes of line 9, the garment of the saints in Revelation, and for the hope of seeing her 'in Heaven'. It also makes the whole vision devotional in tone: a dead wife appears as one of the blessed, and the poem asks what can be known of such a person in this life.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); William Riley Parker, Milton: A Biography, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1968)

  5. Alcestis l. 2

    Alcestis agreed to die instead of her husband Admetus, king of Pherae. Herakles, Admetus's guest, fought Death at her tomb and restored her, bringing her to Admetus veiled and presenting her as a woman won in a contest; only when the veil was lifted did he recognise her. In Euripides she cannot speak until three days have passed and she is purified from the powers below. Milton, who knew Greek tragedy well, uses the myth as the first of three stages: pagan myth, Jewish law, and Christian hope. The veil and silence of the play prepare for the veiled, silent figure of the dream.

    Euripides, Alcestis, ll. 1006-1158 (veiled woman; three days' silence), trans. David Kovacs (Loeb, 1994), Perseus Digital Library; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  6. Jove's great son l. 3

    Herakles was the son of Zeus by the mortal Alcmena; Milton uses the Latin name Jove for Zeus. 'Rescu'd from death by force' refers to the wrestling match with Thanatos (Death) at the tomb. Renaissance writers often read Herakles as a type (foreshadowing) of Christ, who also overcame death, and that reading would strengthen the sonnet's movement toward Christian resurrection. 'Pale and faint' describes Alcestis as she is restored, still weak from the grave.

    Euripides, Alcestis, ll. 1006-1158 (veiled woman; three days' silence), trans. David Kovacs (Loeb, 1994), Perseus Digital Library; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  7. Purification in the old Law ll. 5-6

    Under the Mosaic law ('the old Law'), a woman was ritually unclean after childbirth for forty days after a son and eighty after a daughter, after which she offered a sacrifice and was declared clean (Leviticus 12). 'Mine as whom' means 'mine, like one whom': she appears like a woman who has completed that purification. The lines point to death in or after childbirth, which fits both of Milton's first two wives. Critics have argued over whether the purification had been completed (it would have been, by the dates, for Katherine Woodcock) or was never completed (as with Mary Powell), and whether the image is literal or merely typological.

    Bible (KJV), Leviticus 12:1-8; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977)

  8. Full sight ... without restraint l. 8

    For the blind poet, 'full sight' is a double hope: restored sight in the resurrection and an unobstructed vision of his wife. The line recalls 1 Corinthians 13:12. 'Without restraint' implies that in the dream the sight is restrained, both by the veil and by his blindness. The sentence that begins in line 5 runs over the octave's end into line 9, a deliberate blurring of the Petrarchan division that Milton favours in his later sonnets.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:12; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  9. vested all in white l. 9

    'Vested' means clothed (Latin vestis, garment), with a priestly or liturgical overtone. White robes are worn by the saints in heaven in Revelation (7:9, 13-14; 19:8), 'washed' white in the blood of the Lamb, which takes up the 'washt from spot' of line 5. Women also wore white for churching, the Christian ceremony after childbirth that replaced the Levitical purification. The clause 'pure as her mind' moves from outward dress to inward virtue.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:13-14; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  10. Her face was veiled l. 10

    'Vail'd' is the period spelling of 'veiled'. The veil recalls Alcestis' return and the veiled bride. If the wife is Katherine Woodcock, whom Milton married after he was blind, he could not have known her face: hence 'to my fancied sight' (seen in imagination) her goodness 'shin'd' through the veil. Some readers find a biblical echo as well, of Moses' veiled face (Exodus 34:33-35) and Paul's contrast of veiled and unveiled sight (2 Corinthians 3:18). The veil is therefore both a dramatic detail and an emblem of the limits of vision in this life.

    Euripides, Alcestis, ll. 1006-1158 (veiled woman; three days' silence), trans. David Kovacs (Loeb, 1994), Perseus Digital Library; John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; Bible (KJV), 2 Corinthians 3:18

  11. Love, sweetness, goodness l. 11

    The asyndetic list (no 'and') presents her qualities as radiance: they 'shin'd' in her person 'so clear' that no face ever gave more delight. The paradox is that the clearest vision in the poem is of something invisible. 'Person' here means her bodily presence. Line 12 is elliptical: '[they shone] so clear as [they shine] in no face with more delight'.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Sonnet XXIII: Methought I saw my late espoused saint", Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto)

  12. inclined l. 13

    'Enclin'd' (inclined) means leaned or bent toward. The movement of embrace recalls classical scenes in which the living try to embrace the dead and grasp only air, as Aeneas tries three times to embrace the shade of Creusa (Aeneid 2.792-794) and of Anchises (6.700-702), and Akhilleus that of Patroklos (Iliad 23). Milton compresses the motif into a half-line, and the vision fails exactly when touch seems possible.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; Virgil, Aeneid 2.792-794, Perseus Digital Library

  13. day brought back my night l. 14

    For the sighted, waking brings light; for Milton, waking brings back the darkness of blindness, while the dream had given him sight. The 'night' is also bereavement: she 'fled', and the day returns him to loss. The line is made of monosyllables, three short clauses ('I wak'd, she fled, and day brought back my night') that enact the abruptness of waking. Critics have read the ending either as despair, or as pointing beyond itself, since the 'full sight' of line 8 is still promised in heaven.

    John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes; "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); Thomas H. Luxon, ed., The John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth), Sonnet 23 (in the 1673 Poems section)

  14. Myth, law, grace whole poem

    On this influential reading the three comparisons are progressive. Alcestis is restored by force but remains 'pale and faint'; the Levitical mother is purified by ritual; the Christian saint is promised 'full sight' in heaven. Each stage brings the wife nearer, and each is truer. John Spencer Hill (1977) found the same threefold pattern elsewhere in Milton. Other readers stress that the poem undercuts any such ascent with its ending, where the dream simply breaks.

    "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes

  15. Form whole poem

    The rhymes are Italian, but the syntax does not respect the usual pause after line 8: the long sentence begun at line 5 continues to 'Came vested all in white' (l. 9). Milton learned from Italian models such as Giovanni della Casa a sonnet that is weighty and enjambed rather than neatly divided. The turn comes instead at 'But O' (l. 13), very late, so that the waking falls in the last line and a half.

    "Methought I Saw my Late Espoused Saint", Wikipedia (Johnson's identification; Spitzer 1951; J. S. Hill 1977); John Carey, ed., Milton: Complete Shorter Poems (Longman Annotated English Poets, 2nd edn 1997), Sonnet 23, headnote and notes
