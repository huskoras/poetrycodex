The Charcoal-Seller

by Po Chü-i 772–846

Translated by Arthur Waley (1918) · From A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (1918)

Social Commentaries

An old charcoal-seller
Cutting wood and burning charcoal in the forests of the Southern
Mountain.
His face, stained with dust and ashes, has turned to the colour of
smoke.
The hair on his temples is streaked with gray: his ten fingers are
black.
The money he gets by selling charcoal, how far does it go?
It is just enough to clothe his limbs and put food in his mouth.
Although, alas, the coat on his back is a coat without lining.
He hopes for the coming of cold weather, to send up the price of
coal!
Last night, outside the city,--a whole foot of snow;
At dawn he drives the charcoal wagon along the frozen ruts.
Oxen,--weary; man,--hungry: the sun, already high;
Outside the Gate, to the south of the Market, at last they stop in
the mud.
Suddenly, a pair of prancing horsemen. Who can it be coming?
A public official in a yellow coat and a boy in a white shirt.
In their hands they hold a written warrant: on their tongues--the
words of an order;
They turn back the wagon and curse the oxen, leading them off to the
north.
A whole wagon of charcoal,
More than a thousand pieces!
If officials choose to take it away, the woodman may not complain.
Half a piece of red silk and a single yard of damask,
The Courtiers have tied to the oxen’s collar, as the price
of a wagon of coal!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Bai Juyi's 'Old Charcoal-Seller' (Mai tan weng), New Yuefu no. 32 title, l. 1

    賣炭翁 Mai tan weng, 'The Old Charcoal-Seller', is no. 32 of the fifty 新樂府 Xin yuefu ('New Music Bureau Ballads') that Bai Juyi (772-846) wrote around 809, when he was a censor at court. Each poem has a subtitle stating its target; this one is 苦宮市也, 'suffering under the palace market'. In his preface Bai said the series was written for the ruler, ministers, people, things and events, 'not for literature', and he wanted the meaning plain. Waley printed it in A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (1918) with the subtitle 'A Satire against Kommandatur', a pointed allusion to wartime German military requisitioning that the archive's text omits. Waley's introduction calls these poems 'moral tales in verse'.

    Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949); 'Bai Juyi', Wikipedia

  2. the Southern Mountain (Nanshan): the Zhongnan range l. 2

    伐薪燒炭南山中, 'cutting wood and burning charcoal in the Southern Mountains'. 南山 Nanshan is the Zhongnan range, some tens of kilometres south of the Tang capital Chang'an (modern Xi'an), a region of forests, Daoist and Buddhist retreats and estates such as Wang Wei's Wangchuan. Charcoal was the main fuel for heating in the capital's winters, and woodcutters from the hills carried it in by cart. The poem's realism lies in such specific detail of labour and place.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  3. the charcoal-burner's face ll. 3-4

    滿面塵灰煙火色，兩鬢蒼蒼十指黑: 'face full of dust and ash, the colour of smoke and fire; temples grizzled grey, ten fingers black'. Bai Juyi was famous for writing so plainly that, according to a later anecdote, he read his poems to an old woman and revised anything she could not understand. The two parallel lines move from face to hair to hands. Waley keeps the parallelism closely, including 'his ten fingers are black', a literal rendering of 十指黑.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  4. the poor man's paradox ll. 7-8

    可憐身上衣正單，心憂炭賤願天寒, 'pitiable: the clothes on his body are just a single layer, yet, worrying that charcoal will be cheap, he wishes the weather cold'. 可憐 kelian, 'pitiable, alas', is the poet's comment, which Waley translates 'alas'. The couplet is often singled out by Chinese critics as the heart of the poem: poverty forces the man to wish for his own suffering. Waley's 'a coat without lining' is a precise rendering of 單 dan, 'single-layered'. Note that Waley here writes 'coal' for charcoal, as also in the last line.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  5. the snowfall and frozen ruts ll. 9-10

    夜來城外一尺雪，曉駕炭車輾冰轍, 'overnight a foot of snow outside the city; at dawn he drives the charcoal cart over frozen ruts'. A Chinese foot (chi) was about 30 cm in the Tang. The snow seems to answer his hope, setting up the reversal that follows. Waley's punctuation with dashes (here and in 'Oxen,--weary; man,--hungry') imitates the clipped, list-like syntax of the Chinese seven-character lines.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  6. the market gate of Chang'an l. 12

    市南門外泥中歇, 'outside the market's south gate he rests in the mud'. Tang Chang'an was laid out on a grid with two official markets, the East Market and the West Market, each walled with gates that opened and closed at set hours. The detail of mud (snow melted by the high sun) brings out the man's exhaustion, while the market itself is the place where he hopes for a fair sale. Waley's capitalised 'Gate' and 'Market' mark them as named places.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  7. the 'yellow-robed envoy' and the boy in white l. 14

    翩翩兩騎來是誰？黃衣使者白衫兒: 'Prancing, two riders: who comes? An envoy in yellow robes, a lad in a white shirt'. The 'yellow-robed envoys' (黃衣使者) were the palace eunuchs who bought goods for the court; the 'white shirts' were their hangers-on, recruited from the city's idle youth. Waley's 'A public official' is accurate in function but loses the specific reference to eunuchs, whose growing power was a major political issue in Bai Juyi's day. The rhetorical question ('Who can it be coming?') mimics the ballad style of folk songs.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949); Han Yu, Shunzong shilu (順宗實錄, Veritable Records of Emperor Shunzong), juan 2, on the palace-market (gongshi) abuses; Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  8. warrant and edict (chi 敕) l. 15

    手把文書口稱敕, 'in hand they hold documents, in their mouths they call it an edict'. 敕 chi is an imperial order. The line implies a gap between the paper they wave and the authority they claim: the eunuchs invoke the emperor's name for what is in effect robbery. Waley's 'on their tongues--the words of an order' keeps the contrast of hand and mouth.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  9. 'to the north': towards the palace l. 16

    迴車叱牛牽向北, 'they turn the cart, curse the ox, and lead it north'. Chang'an's palace city and imperial city occupied the north centre of the grid, with the markets to the south-west and south-east, so 'north' needs no further explanation for a Tang reader: the charcoal goes to the palace. Waley's plural 'oxen' differs from the singular ox usually understood (the Chinese does not mark number).

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  10. 'a thousand jin': weight, not pieces ll. 17-18

    一車炭，重千餘斤, 'one cart of charcoal, weighing over a thousand jin'. 斤 jin, the catty, was a unit of weight (about 600 g in the Tang, so the load was over half a tonne). Waley's 'More than a thousand pieces' treats jin as a counter for pieces. The sense, the size of the load and the value of the whole winter's labour, comes through, but readers should note the change. The short exclamatory lines (three characters each in some texts) break the seven-character rhythm for emphasis.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290

  11. the palace market (gongshi 宮市) l. 19

    宮使驅將惜不得, 'the palace envoys drive it away, and he cannot grudge it'. The 宮市 gongshi system, in which eunuch commissioners bought supplies for the palace in the capital's markets, degenerated in the late eighth century into open extortion. Han Yu's Veritable Records of Emperor Shunzong records the abuses, including a story of a peasant whose firewood was taken in a similar way, and the system was suspended in the reforms of 805, though some editions read the subtitle 官市 'official market' and the abuses the poem describes evidently remained a live issue. Bai Juyi's poem, written a few years later, attacks the practice as an example of the court's indifference to ordinary people. Waley's 'If officials choose' generalises the line into a statement about power.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Han Yu, Shunzong shilu (順宗實錄, Veritable Records of Emperor Shunzong), juan 2, on the palace-market (gongshi) abuses; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)

  12. payment in silk ll. 20-22

    半匹紅綃（紗）一丈綾，繫向牛頭充炭直, 'half a bolt of red gauze and a zhang of damask, tied to the ox's head as the price of the charcoal'. Silk cloth was a form of currency in Tang China, and taxes and salaries were often paid in it, so payment in silk was not itself unusual; but half a bolt and a zhang (about three metres, Waley's 'a single yard') were worth much less than a cartload of charcoal, and useless to a man who needed food and warm clothes. 'Tied to the ox's head' (Waley: 'the oxen's collar') shows contempt: the payment is attached to the animal, not given to the man. The poem ends without comment, letting the fact speak, as Bai's satirical programme required.

    Bai Juyi, 'Mai tan weng' (賣炭翁), Xin yuefu, Chinese Wikisource (with the subtitle 苦宮/官市也); Arthur Waley, trans., A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems (London: Constable, 1918), Project Gutenberg ebook 42290; Arthur Waley, The Life and Times of Po Chu-i, 772-846 A.D. (London: Allen & Unwin, 1949)
