The Mind

I Am

by John Clare 1793–1864

From Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (1920, ed. Blunden & Porter)

The MindLiving

I AM: yet what I am none cares or knows,
My friends forsake me like a memory lost;
I am the self-consumer of my woes,
They rise and vanish in oblivious host,
Like shades in love and death's oblivion lost;
And yet I am, and live with shadows tost

Into the nothingness of scorn and noise,
Into the living sea of waking dreams,
Where there is neither sense of life nor joys,
But the vast shipwreck of my life's esteems;
And een the dearest--that I loved the best--
Are strange--nay, rather stranger than the rest.

I long for scenes where man has never trod;
A place where woman never smiled or wept;
There to abide with my Creator, GOD,
And sleep as I in childhood sweetly slept:
Untroubling and untroubled where I lie;
The grass below--above the vaulted sky.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Written in the Northampton asylum, this may be Clare's most quoted poem — a plain, devastating statement of a self stripped down to bare existence.

Study notes by the editors

  1. I Am: context and text l. 1

    Clare was confined from 1837 in Dr Matthew Allen's asylum at High Beach in Epping Forest, walked home from it in July 1841, and in December 1841 was committed to the Northampton General Lunatic Asylum, where he lived until his death in 1864. The poem survives in a transcript by W. F. Knight, the asylum's house steward from 1845 to 1850, who copied many of Clare's later poems. It is usually said to have first appeared in the Bedford Times on 1 January 1848, and Frederick Martin's Life (1865) printed it with variants. The standard text is in Robinson and Powell's Later Poems (1984); Blunden and Porter's 1920 selection, the source here, brought much of Clare's manuscript verse to twentieth-century readers.

    'I Am (poem)', Wikipedia (composition c. 1844-45; Knight's transcript; Bedford Times, 1 Jan. 1848); Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., with Margaret Grainger, The Later Poems of John Clare 1837-1864, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984); Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; Frederick Martin, The Life of John Clare (London, 1865) [prints the poem with slight variants]; Edmund Blunden and Alan Porter, eds., John Clare: Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (London, 1920) [the text printed here]

  2. 'I AM' l. 1

    At the burning bush God tells Moses, 'I AM THAT I AM ... say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you' (Exodus 3:14). Clare's capitals give the bare fact of his existence an almost sacred weight, and the next words, 'yet what I am none cares or knows', shrink it to a lonely fact without recognition. The poem asserts existence, not identity, and it ends by imagining the self returned to its 'Creator'. Readers have also compared Descartes' cogito, though the poem argues nothing philosophically. Bate took the phrase as the title of his 2003 selection.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; René Descartes, Discourse on Method (1637), Part IV; Jonathan Bate, ed., 'I Am': The Selected Poetry of John Clare (New York, 2003)

  3. 'My friends forsake me' l. 2

    Job complains, 'My kinsfolk have failed, and my familiar friends have forgotten me' (Job 19:14), and the Psalmist, 'I am forgotten as a dead man out of mind' (Psalm 31:12). Clare had been celebrated in 1820 as the Northamptonshire peasant poet and was now largely forgotten, with few visitors. The simile turns round: it is not that he forgets them, but that they let him go, as one loses a memory.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum

  4. 'self-consumer' l. 3

    The compound means that his woes have no outlet but himself: he digests his own grief. 'Consume' (Latin consumere, to use up, devour) also carried the sense of wasting away, as in 'consumption', so the phrase hints that grief eats him as he eats it. The coinage has the compressed force of Clare's late style. It also reverses the usual relation of poet and reader: the poet who once had a public now has no audience for his sorrows but himself.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes

  5. 'oblivious host' l. 4

    A 'host' is a multitude, originally an army (from Latin hostis). 'Oblivious' could mean forgetful or, in an older sense, bringing forgetfulness. The woes 'rise and vanish' without being remembered, a crowd that leaves no trace. The word prepares for 'death's oblivion' in the next line and suggests the shades who drink from Lethe in Virgil's underworld. Like the 'memory lost' of l. 2, the woes vanish into forgetting, so that even suffering fails to give the self a lasting shape.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Virgil, Aeneid 6 (the shades at the Styx, ll. 295-330, and at Lethe, ll. 703-51)

  6. 'Like shades' l. 5

    'Shades' are the spirits of the dead in classical epic, and Clare's woes, like the crowding shades by the Styx in Aeneid 6, are restless and unremembered. The line pairs love and death as twin forms of oblivion, perhaps thinking of Mary Joyce, his early love, who had died in 1838 without his knowing it. The repetition of 'lost' at the ends of ll. 2 and 5 is an identical rhyme that presses on the idea of loss.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6 (the shades at the Styx, ll. 295-330, and at Lethe, ll. 703-51); Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum

  7. 'shadows tost' l. 6

    'Tost' is the older spelling of 'tossed' and suggests being thrown about by waves. 'And yet I am' reasserts existence against everything in the stanza. The sentence then runs on across the stanza break ('tost / Into the nothingness'), an unusual enjambment that enacts the tossing it describes. The word belongs to the idiom of hymns and of the Psalms, where the soul is tossed on troubled waters, and Clare's late verse often stays close to hymn language.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes

  8. 'scorn and noise' l. 7

    A public asylum with many patients was a noisy place, and Clare often wrote of being mocked or ignored. 'Nothingness' is a strong abstract word, a void filled only with scorn and noise. Bate's biography describes his life at Northampton, including the relative freedom of his early years there, when he could walk into the town, and his deep sense of being cut off.

    Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; The Victorian Web: John Clare, biography

  9. 'living sea of waking dreams' l. 8

    'Waking dreams' suggests both the delusions recorded in Clare's asylum years (he sometimes claimed to be Byron, and believed he had two wives) and a general sense of living in unreality. The 'sea' prepares for the 'shipwreck' of l. 10. 'Living' is paradoxical: this sea is alive, yet it holds 'neither sense of life nor joys'. The two halves of the phrase pull against each other: 'living' and 'waking' promise reality, while 'sea' and 'dreams' dissolve it.

    Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; 'I Am (poem)', Wikipedia (composition c. 1844-45; Knight's transcript; Bedford Times, 1 Jan. 1848)

  10. 'shipwreck of my life's esteems' l. 10

    The plural 'esteems' is unusual. It can mean the things he held dear, his values and hopes, and also the regard of others, his reputation. His fame of the 1820s, his family life and his home village were all lost. Shipwreck is a common figure in poems of despair, and a comparable case is Cowper's 'The Castaway', written in Cowper's last depression, though no direct borrowing is proven. Clare admired Cowper and echoes him elsewhere, as Vincent Newey has shown.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; William Cowper, 'The Castaway' (written 1799; publ. 1803); Vincent Newey, 'Existing at the Margins: A Double Echo of Cowper in Clare', Notes and Queries 54.2 (2007) [on Clare's debt to Cowper]; Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum

  11. 'een the dearest' l. 11

    Clare married Martha ('Patty') Turner in 1820 and they had seven children; she is not known to have visited him at Northampton. Yet in his asylum delusions he thought of Mary Joyce, his childhood love, as his first wife, and wrote poems and letters to her. Bate stresses how both women haunt the late poems. The poem need not settle the question: the dearest have become 'strange', 'nay, rather stranger than the rest', because illness and confinement have estranged him from everyone.

    Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; The Victorian Web: John Clare, biography; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes

  12. 'where man has never trod' l. 13

    After the stanza of loss, the last stanza turns to longing for a place without men or women, alone with God. It recalls Romantic longing for wild solitude, such as Byron's 'There is a pleasure in the pathless woods' (Childe Harold IV, st. 178). Clare identified strongly with Byron, and in 1841 wrote his own 'Child Harold' and 'Don Juan'. The untrodden place can also be read as Eden before the Fall, or as the grave.

    Lord Byron, Childe Harold's Pilgrimage, Canto IV (1818), st. 178; Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum

  13. 'woman never smiled or wept' l. 14

    Paired with 'man' in l. 13, 'woman' completes the rejection of human company. The line suggests the pain that love and family had brought him, with Mary Joyce and Patty in the background. It may also recall Eden before Eve, where Adam was alone with his maker (Genesis 2:18-22). 'Smiled or wept' covers the whole range of feeling, joy and sorrow alike, from which he now wishes to be free; the place he imagines is one without the claims of human affection.

    Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611

  14. 'my Creator, GOD' l. 15

    The capitals pair the poem's opening and close, so that the 'I AM' whom nobody knows longs to 'abide' with the original I AM. 'Abide' has a biblical ring ('Abide in me, and I in you', John 15:4). The religious hope is simple and childlike, and leads straight into the memory of childhood sleep. The comma before 'GOD' makes the name a solemn apposition, spoken with emphasis.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes

  15. 'as I in childhood sweetly slept' l. 16

    Clare's childhood in the village of Helpston, Northamptonshire, fed much of his poetry, and the enclosure of its open fields was one of his great losses. The wish to sleep as he slept then is a wish for death, softened into rest. The alliteration of 'sleep', 'sweetly' and 'slept' gives the line a lulling sound. Childhood is the last place in the poem where the 'I' felt whole.

    Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum; The Victorian Web: John Clare, biography

  16. 'Untroubling and untroubled' l. 17

    The figure is polyptoton, the same word in different grammatical forms. It implies that he knows he has troubled others and wishes to be free both of causing and of suffering harm. 'Where I lie' gently names the grave. The paired words are slow and evenly weighted, and the repeated prefix 'un-' strips them down, as the whole last stanza strips life to a minimum of grass and sky. It is the poem's quietest line, with the rhythm of a lullaby, matching the childhood sleep of the line before.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes; Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (London, 2003), chapters on High Beach and the Northampton asylum

  17. 'the vaulted sky' l. 18

    'Vaulted' means arched, like the stone vault of a church, so the sky becomes the roof of nature's cathedral. The line is balanced around its dash, 'below' and 'above' turning on each other almost as a chiasmus. The poem resolves into peace found in nature and with God, not in human society. Grass over the dead is a biblical commonplace ('All flesh is grass', Isaiah 40:6), and it suits a poet of fields and hedgerows.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. the headword; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes; 'I Am (poem)', Wikipedia (composition c. 1844-45; Knight's transcript; Bedford Times, 1 Jan. 1848)

  18. Form whole poem

    Each sestet has a quatrain of alternate rhyme followed by a couplet, the form Shakespeare used in Venus and Adonis. Stanza 1 is irregular: its last two lines rhyme with lines 2 and 4 ('lost', 'host', 'lost', 'tost'), so the sound of loss dominates. The steady pentameter and regular stanzas give the poem a composed, almost classical calm that contrasts with its subject.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare (The Oxford Authors, 1984), text and notes; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., with Margaret Grainger, The Later Poems of John Clare 1837-1864, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984); William Shakespeare, Venus and Adonis (1593) [the ababcc sestet]
