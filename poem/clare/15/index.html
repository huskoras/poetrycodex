Nature

Badger

by John Clare 1793–1864

From Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (1920, ed. Blunden & Porter)

Nature

When midnight comes a host of dogs and men
Go out and track the badger to his den,
And put a sack within the hole, and lie
Till the old grunting badger passes bye.
He comes and hears--they let the strongest loose.
The old fox hears the noise and drops the goose.
The poacher shoots and hurries from the cry,
And the old hare half wounded buzzes bye.
They get a forked stick to bear him down
And clap the dogs and take him to the town,
And bait him all the day with many dogs,
And laugh and shout and fright the scampering hogs.
He runs along and bites at all he meets:
They shout and hollo down the noisy streets.

He turns about to face the loud uproar
And drives the rebels to their very door.
The frequent stone is hurled where eer they go;
When badgers fight, then every one's a foe.
The dogs are clapt and urged to join the fray;
The badger turns and drives them all away.
Though scarcely half as big, demure and small,
He fights with dogs for bones and beats them all.
The heavy mastiff, savage in the fray,
Lies down and licks his feet and turns away.
The bulldog knows his match and waxes cold,
The badger grins and never leaves his hold.
He drives the crowd and follows at their heels
And bites them through--the drunkard swears and reels.

The frighted women take the boys away,
The blackguard laughs and hurries on the fray.
He tries to reach the woods, an awkward race,
But sticks and cudgels quickly stop the chace.
He turns agen and drives the noisy crowd
And beats the many dogs in noises loud.
He drives away and beats them every one,
And then they loose them all and set them on.
He falls as dead and kicked by boys and men,
Then starts and grins and drives the crowd agen;
Till kicked and torn and beaten out he lies
And leaves his hold and cackles, groans, and dies.

Codex Note

One of several unsparing asylum-era poems that watch an animal baited to death without looking away.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Text, date and title l. 1

    Clare left this poem untitled, so editors call it 'Badger' or '[The Badger]'. It was first published by Edmund Blunden and Alan Porter in Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (1920). The fuller manuscript version is a group of sonnet-length blocks in couplets (sixty-eight lines in five blocks in Brewer's description) that begins with the badger's habits and ends with a tame badger; anthologies, like this archive, often print only the central stanzas of the hunt and baiting. Modern editors such as Robinson and Powell generally place it among the Northborough poems of the mid-1830s, before Clare's admission to an asylum in 1837, so the archive's description of it as an asylum poem should be treated with caution. David Perkins discusses the group as 'badger sonnets'.

    Edmund Blunden and Alan Porter, eds., John Clare: Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (London, 1920); Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022); David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003)

  2. Catching the badger ll. 2-4

    Badgers forage by night, so the men wait for the animal's return to its 'den' (its sett), having placed a sack in the entrance to trap it. 'Grunting' is accurate natural observation of the badger's noises. 'Bye' is Clare's spelling of 'by', and his unconventional spelling and punctuation are retained in many editions. The detail is plainly from a countryman's experience rather than literary convention.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  3. Fox, poacher and hare ll. 6-8

    These lines widen the scene into a nocturnal panorama of rural life, and each figure is itself a predator or a victim: the fox with its goose, the poacher with his gun, the hare 'half wounded'. Clare's world is not idyllic; violence runs through all its levels. 'Buzzes bye' is an odd, vivid verb for the hare's rapid passage, suggesting a whirring sound or blur.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; Jonathan Bate, John Clare: A Biography (2003), on the Northborough years, 1832-37

  4. Forked stick l. 9

    The forked stick pins the animal to the ground without the hunter coming within reach of its strong bite. The men then 'clap the dogs', setting them on or encouraging them, and 'take him to the town', where the baiting becomes a public spectacle. The move from field to town shifts the poem from hunting to entertainment. The phrase 'bear him down' implies the animal's resistance even at capture.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  5. Clap l. 10; l. 19

    'Clap' recurs in 'The dogs are clapt and urged to join the fray' (l. 19), where it is paired with 'urged', which suggests the sense of encouraging or setting on dogs with claps and shouts. Clare's vocabulary mixes standard English with East Midlands dialect, and editors such as Robinson and Powell gloss such words in their glossaries. The exact nuance is uncertain, but the sense of goading is clear from context.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'clap'

  6. Badger-baiting l. 11

    Baiting (from Old Norse beita, 'to cause to bite') means setting dogs on a captive bull, bear or badger. It was a popular rural and urban entertainment, often accompanied by betting and drinking. The Cruelty to Animals Act of 1835 banned the baiting of animals in England and Wales, but enforcement was weak and badger-baiting continued illegally. Perkins places Clare's poem in this social history, noting that the 'savage amusements of the poor' were attacked by reformers while being part of village culture. Clare neither preaches nor approves; the moral judgement is carried by description.

    David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'bait'; William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  7. The noisy streets ll. 12-14

    Pigs often roamed freely in town streets in the period, and the scattering hogs add a note of farce to the scene. The crowd's laughter and shouting contrast with the badger's single-minded fighting. The first stanza ends with the badger loose in the 'noisy streets', biting 'at all he meets', a reversal in which the hunted becomes the attacker. The 'noisy streets' mark the public, holiday character of the baiting.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  8. Rebels l. 16

    Calling the townspeople 'rebels' inverts the moral order. The badger is treated as the rightful party, the crowd as insurgents whom he drives back 'to their very door'. The phrase belongs to a pattern of epic and military diction in the poem ('fray', 'foe', 'uproar') that gives the small animal heroic stature. Critics have read this as Clare's sympathy with the beleaguered creature, and sometimes, given Clare's own position, with the rural poor and outsiders.

    David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003); William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022)

  9. 'The frequent stone' l. 17

    The singular with 'frequent' (in the Latin sense of 'crowded, repeated') is an eighteenth-century poetic idiom, an instance of Clare's mixing of literary diction with plain speech. The next line, 'When badgers fight, then every one's a foe', is proverbial in shape: the badger is everyone's enemy, and everyone his. The stoning, too, shows the crowd's readiness to attack from a safe distance, where the dogs must fight at close quarters.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'frequent'; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes

  10. The badger's courage ll. 21-26

    These lines are the poem's celebration of the badger's courage. 'Demure' (quiet, modest) is an unexpected word for a fighter, stressing that the animal is not naturally aggressive. The 'heavy mastiff' gives up and licks its feet; the bulldog, bred for baiting and famous for never letting go, 'knows his match and waxes cold' (grows faint-hearted). 'The badger grins and never leaves his hold' transfers to the badger the bulldog's own proverbial tenacity. 'Grins' means bares its teeth. Brewer stresses the contrast between the badger's bravery and the crowd's cruelty and cowardice.

    William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022); David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wax'

  11. The crowd: drunkard, women, blackguard ll. 28-30

    Clare's crowd is drawn in brief types. The drunkard, bitten, swears and staggers; the 'frighted women' remove the boys; the 'blackguard' (a scoundrel, pronounced 'blaggard') laughs and pushes the fight on. Such details show the baiting as communal entertainment across the town while hinting at its degradation. 'Hurries on the fray' means urges the fight forward. None of these figures is named or individualised, in contrast with the closely watched badger.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blackguard'; Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes

  12. Spelling: 'chace', 'agen', 'bye' ll. 31-32

    Clare's manuscripts are irregular in spelling and almost unpunctuated; editors from the 1820s onward 'corrected' them, and modern editors (Robinson, Powell) have argued for preserving his forms. 'Chace' was an acceptable older spelling of 'chase', 'agen' records his pronunciation of 'again', and 'where eer' is 'wherever'. The badger's attempt to reach the woods is 'an awkward race' because badgers are built for digging, not running.

    Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes; Edmund Blunden and Alan Porter, eds., John Clare: Poems Chiefly from Manuscript (London, 1920)

  13. The death of the badger ll. 39-40

    The final couplet lists three sounds ('cackles, groans') and then death, the bare 'dies' as the last word. 'Leaves his hold' marks the first time the badger lets go, the defeat of its tenacity. 'Starts and grins and drives the crowd agen' in line 38 shows that even apparent death is not final until the last line. Readers debate the effect of Clare's refusal to moralise: for some it intensifies indignation, for others it shows an unsentimental countryman's witness. In the manuscript sequence a further block on a tame badger follows, softening the ending; anthologies like this one end with the death.

    David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003); William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022); Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes

  14. Form: couplets in sonnet-length blocks st. 1-3

    The poem is written in heroic couplets, but Clare groups them in fourteen-line units, so that each block has the length of a sonnet without the sonnet's rhyme scheme or turn; Perkins accordingly speaks of 'badger sonnets'. Most lines are end-stopped and many begin with 'And' or 'He', a paratactic style that moves forward event by event. Verbs pile up ('drives' is especially frequent), giving the narrative its breathless, relentless pace. The final block in the archive is twelve lines long.

    William D. Brewer, 'Badger by John Clare', EBSCO Research Starters (2022); David Perkins, 'Savage amusements of the poor: John Clare's badger sonnets', in Romanticism and Animal Rights (Cambridge, 2003); Eric Robinson and David Powell, eds., John Clare: Major Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1984; rev. 2004), 'Badger' and notes
