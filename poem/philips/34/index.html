To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship.

by Katherine Philips 1632–1664

Relationships

I Did not live untill this time
Crown'd my felicity,
When I could say without a crime,
I am not thine, but Thee.

This Carcass breath'd, and walkt, and slept,
So that the World believ'd
There was a Soul the Motions kept;
But they were all deceiv'd.

For as a Watch by art is wound
To motion, such was mine:
But never had Orinda found
A Soul till she found thine;

Which now inspires, cures and supplies,
And guides my darkned Breast:
For thou art all that I can prize,
My Joy, my Life, my Rest.

No Bridegrooms not Crown-conquerors mirth
To mine compar'd can be:
They have but pieces of this Earth,
I've all the World in thee.

Then let our Flame still light and shine,
And no false fear controul,
As innocent as our Design,
Immortal as our Soul.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Lucasia, Orinda, and the poem title; l. 1

    Katherine Philips (1632-1664), known as 'the Matchless Orinda', wrote a series of poems to women friends under pastoral names. Anne Owen of Orielton received the name Lucasia in 1651 and was the centre of Philips's 'Society of Friendship', a circle of friends with coterie names. Philips's poems circulated in manuscript; an unauthorised edition appeared in 1664 and an authorised one, by Sir Charles Cotterell, in 1667. The opening echoes Donne's 'The Good-Morrow' ('I wonder by my troth, what thou, and I / Did, till we lov'd?'), declaring that true life began only with this friendship.

    Orlando: Women's Writing in the British Isles (Cambridge), "Society of Friendship"; Peter Beal, "Katherine Philips (Orinda)", Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts 1450-1700 (Folger), introduction; Jack Lynch, ed., "To My Excellent Lucasia, on Our Friendship", text of Philips's Poems (1667), with gloss; Patrick Thomas, ed., The Collected Works of Katherine Philips, the Matchless Orinda, vol. I: The Poems (Stump Cross Books, 1990)

  2. felicity l. 2

    'Felicity' (Latin felicitas) means happiness, and in religious writing often the bliss of heaven. The syntax is compressed: 'I did not live until this time [which] crowned my felicity'. The crown image, completion and reward, returns in the 'Crown-conquerors' of stanza 5. 'Untill this time' marks the moment of Lucasia's friendship as the true beginning of the speaker's life, a secular version of spiritual rebirth.

    Jack Lynch, ed., "To My Excellent Lucasia, on Our Friendship", text of Philips's Poems (1667), with gloss; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "felicity, n."

  3. without a crime l. 3

    'Crime' in the period could mean a sin or a charge of wrongdoing. The phrase anticipates the last stanza's 'innocent' design and 'false fear'. Philips's friendship poems repeatedly insist on the purity of the bond, partly against contemporary suspicion and satire of intense friendships between women. The phrase can also mean she now has a lawful title to what she claims.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "crime, n."; Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide)

  4. not thine, but thee l. 4

    The distinction between possession ('thine') and identity ('Thee') is the poem's central claim. It draws on the classical doctrine that a friend is 'another self' (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.4) and on Montaigne's famous essay on friendship, in which two souls mingle so completely that the seam is lost. It also adapts Donne's language of lovers' souls merging ('The Ecstasy'). Whether this is spiritual friendship or a love that later readers would call lesbian is the main critical debate about the poem.

    Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.4, 1166a (the friend as "another self"), Perseus Digital Library; Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide); Jack Lynch, ed., "To My Excellent Lucasia, on Our Friendship", text of Philips's Poems (1667), with gloss

  5. This carcass ll. 5-8

    'Carcass' (body, with the suggestion of a dead body) is deliberately harsh. The world believed a soul kept its 'motions' going, but they were deceived. The idea that the soul is the principle of motion in the body is Aristotelian; here the true soul is the friend, so before meeting Lucasia the speaker was animated only mechanically, which leads to the watch image of the next stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "carcass, n."; Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide)

  6. The watch ll. 9-10

    Pocket watches were still fairly new luxury objects in the mid-seventeenth century, and writers used them as figures for mechanism without life. The conceit is in the Metaphysical manner, drawing an image from contemporary technology. It also touches on the new mechanical philosophy (associated with Descartes) that explained animal bodies as machines; the poem answers it by insisting that a human life without a soul, that is without love, is merely mechanical. 'Art' means skill, artifice, as opposed to nature.

    Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "watch, n."

  7. Orinda l. 11

    'Orinda' was Philips's coterie name, by which she was known in print ('the Matchless Orinda'). The third person turns personal confession into something like a formal declaration. 'Found / A Soul till she found thine' means both that she had no soul until finding Lucasia's and that she never met a soul worth the name. The poem thus moves from the private 'I' of the first stanza to a publicly named persona.

    Peter Beal, "Katherine Philips (Orinda)", Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts 1450-1700 (Folger), introduction; Jack Lynch, ed., "To My Excellent Lucasia, on Our Friendship", text of Philips's Poems (1667), with gloss

  8. inspires, cures and supplies l. 13

    'Inspires' keeps its root sense of breathing in (Latin inspirare), recalling God breathing life into Adam (Genesis 2:7). The three verbs give the friend a power that is creative, medical and providential. 'Darkned Breast' implies the friend is also light, preparing for the 'Flame' of the last stanza. 'My Joy, my Life, my Rest' uses the language normally addressed to a lover or to God.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 2:7; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "inspire, v."

  9. Bridegrooms ... Crown-conquerors l. 17

    The archive's 'not' is probably a slip: the 1667 Poems reads 'No Bridegrooms nor Crown-conquerors mirth'. The two figures are the most fortunate men imaginable: the bridegroom (perhaps also the bridegroom of Psalm 19:5) and the conqueror who wins a kingdom. Writing in the 1650s, after the Civil War and the execution of Charles I, Philips may glance at those who had seized a crown; she was royalist in sympathy, though married to a Parliamentarian. That topical reading is plausible but not certain.

    Jack Lynch, ed., "To My Excellent Lucasia, on Our Friendship", text of Philips's Poems (1667), with gloss; Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:5

  10. all the world in thee l. 20

    Donne's 'The Good-Morrow' makes one little room 'an everywhere', and 'The Sun Rising' declares 'She is all states, and all princes I'. Philips applies the hyperbole to friendship: conquerors hold only fragments of the earth, but she possesses everything in Lucasia. Commentators note that her language closely follows Donne's in proclaiming the completeness of the lovers' world. The borrowing is significant, since it transfers to two women the self-sufficient world of heterosexual lovers.

    John Donne, "The Good-Morrow", in The Poems of John Donne, ed. H. J. C. Grierson (Oxford, 1912), vol. I; text at Luminarium; Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide)

  11. our Flame l. 21

    Fire is the conventional figure of love, but Philips stresses its light rather than its heat: it 'lights and shines' and illuminates her 'darkned Breast'. 'No false fear controul' means let no groundless fear restrain or overrule it; 'control' then had the stronger sense of check or overpower. The fear may be of the world's suspicion or of losing the friend.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "control, v."; Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide)

  12. innocent ... immortal ll. 23-24

    The final couplet balances 'innocent' with 'Immortal' and 'Design' with 'Soul': because the bond is spiritual it can last for ever. Note 'our Soul', singular: they have one soul between them, completing the claim of stanza 1. The closing assertion of innocence answers 'without a crime' (l. 3) and frames the whole poem as a defence of declared friendship, a theme Philips addresses directly in another poem, 'To my Lucasia, in defence of declared Friendship'.

    Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide); Patrick Thomas, ed., The Collected Works of Katherine Philips, the Matchless Orinda, vol. I: The Poems (Stump Cross Books, 1990)

  13. Friendship or love? whole poem

    Older readings place the poem in the mid-century cult of platonic love and friendship, in which intense, even erotic language was a conventional way of describing spiritual union. Since the 1980s many feminist and queer critics (for example Harriette Andreadis and Arlene Stiebel) have read Philips's Lucasia poems as poems of love between women, the language of friendship giving cover to desire. Some study guides warn that a sexual reading ignores the historical context; others argue that the boundary between friendship and love was drawn differently then. The poem itself never resolves the question, which is part of its interest.

    Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide); "Let Us Speak Our Love": Romance and Eroticism in the Lyric Friendship Poetry of Katherine Philips, Washington College Review (2018); Orlando: Women's Writing in the British Isles (Cambridge), "Society of Friendship"

  14. Stanza form whole poem

    Common metre (8.6.8.6 syllables) was familiar from metrical psalms and hymns, and its simplicity suits a poem that wants to sound sincere and devotional rather than ornate. Philips combines that plain form with Metaphysical conceits (the watch, the soul as the principle of motion), producing a lighter, more lucid version of Donne's manner. Several of her poems were set to music by Henry Lawes.

    Crossref-it, "To my Excellent Lucasia, on our Friendship" (Metaphysical Poets study guide); Patrick Thomas, ed., The Collected Works of Katherine Philips, the Matchless Orinda, vol. I: The Poems (Stump Cross Books, 1990)
