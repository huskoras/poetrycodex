A Musical Instrument

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From The Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912)

Mythology & Folklore

What was he doing, the great god Pan,
Down in the reeds by the river?
Spreading ruin and scattering ban,
Splashing and paddling with hoofs of a goat,
And breaking the golden lilies afloat
With the dragon-fly on the river.

He tore out a reed, the great god Pan,
From the deep cool bed of the river;
The limpid water turbidly ran,
And the broken lilies a-dying lay,
And the dragon-fly had fled away,
Ere he brought it out of the river.

High on the shore sat the great god Pan,
While turbidly flow'd the river;
And hack'd and hew'd as a great god can
With his hard bleak steel at the patient reed,
Till there was not a sign of the leaf indeed
To prove it fresh from the river.

He cut it short, did the great god Pan
(How tall it stood in the river!),
Then drew the pith, like the heart of a man,
Steadily from the outside ring,
And notch'd the poor dry empty thing
In holes, as he sat by the river.

'This is the way,' laugh'd the great god Pan
(Laugh'd while he sat by the river),
'The only way, since gods began
To make sweet music, they could succeed.'
Then dropping his mouth to a hole in the reed,
He blew in power by the river.

Sweet, sweet, sweet, O Pan!
Piercing sweet by the river!
Blinding sweet, O great god Pan!
The sun on the hill forgot to die,
And the lilies revived, and the dragon-fly
Came back to dream on the river.

Yet half a beast is the great god Pan,
To laugh as he sits by the river,
Making a poet out of a man:
The true gods sigh for the cost and pain —
For the reed which grows nevermore again
As a reed with the reeds of the river.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication st. 1

    The poem appeared in Thackeray's new Cornhill Magazine in July 1860 with an engraving after a design by Frederic Leighton. It was among the last poems Barrett Browning wrote, and Robert Browning included it in the posthumous Last Poems (1862). The archive text comes from the Oxford Book of Victorian Verse (1912). The opening question, 'What was he doing...?', has the simple directness of a ballad or nursery rhyme. The light, singing manner contrasts sharply with the violence the poem describes and the bitter moral it draws.

    'A Musical Instrument', The Cornhill Magazine 2 (July 1860), with engraving after Frederic Leighton (OUR Heritage, University of Otago); Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Last Poems, ed. Robert Browning (Chapman and Hall, 1862); Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'

  2. The great god Pan l. 1

    Pan (Greek Pan) was the Arcadian god of flocks, shepherds and wild nature, imagined with a goat's legs, horns and hoofs. He was associated with panic terror and with the shepherd's pipe. The phrase 'great god Pan', repeated through the poem like a refrain, recalls Plutarch's anecdote (On the Obsolescence of Oracles 17) of a voice proclaiming 'Great Pan is dead'. Christian writers later linked that cry to the coming of Christ. Barrett Browning had already treated the story in 'The Dead Pan' (1844). Here Pan is very much alive, and the final stanza sets him against 'the true gods'.

    Plutarch, De defectu oraculorum 17 (Moralia), Loeb translation at LacusCurtius; Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Pan, n.'

  3. Stanza and refrain st. 1–7

    Each stanza's first line ends on 'Pan' (or a rhyme for it: began / can / man) and its second and last lines end on 'river'. The pattern is abaccb, with a couplet in the middle. The lines mix dactyls and anapaests, so they skip like a ballad or a nursery song. This lilting music is the poem's irony. A poem about the cruel making of music is itself sweetly musical, so its form shows the effect it describes. Repeating 'river' keeps the reed's lost home in view to the very end ('the reeds of the river').

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Last Poems, ed. Robert Browning (Chapman and Hall, 1862)

  4. ban l. 3

    In older English a 'ban' is a solemn curse or anathema, and the verb 'to ban' meant to curse. Pan 'spreading ruin and scattering ban' is therefore a destructive, cursing presence, not the genial pastoral deity of Arcadian convention. The phrase prepares for the stanza's damage to lilies and dragon-fly, and for the poem's view of the artist-god as both creative and harmful.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ban, n.1'; Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'

  5. Hoofs of a goat l. 4

    The detail 'Splashing and paddling with hoofs of a goat' is clumsy and physical, with no grace in it. The goat body was traditional in images of Pan, and later Christian imagery gave the devil the same features. The poem keeps this bestial side in view, and the last stanza says it outright: 'Yet half a beast is the great god Pan'. The hoofs tie the god's crudeness to the act of making art.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; 'Syrinx', Wikipedia

  6. Lilies and dragon-fly ll. 5–6, 10–11, 35–36

    Pan's first entry breaks 'the golden lilies afloat' and disturbs 'the dragon-fly on the river'. In stanza 2 the lilies lie 'a-dying' and the dragon-fly has 'fled away'. In stanza 6, when he plays, 'the lilies revived, and the dragon-fly / Came back to dream on the river'. The images thus frame the poem's ethical problem. The art that came out of destruction repairs the damage in the natural world, but not for the reed itself. The cycle makes the music seem a kind of restoration while the poem insists that its cost remains.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'

  7. Pan and Syrinx st. 2

    Ovid tells how Pan pursued the nymph Syrinx to the river Ladon, where her sisters turned her into marsh reeds. Hearing the wind sigh through them, Pan bound reeds of unequal length together with wax and named the instrument syrinx (the panpipe) after her (Metamorphoses 1.689–712). Barrett Browning's poem does not name Syrinx and has him make a single reed pipe. But readers who know the myth will hear the nymph's story behind the 'patient reed'. Critics have stressed that the source myth is one of pursuit and attempted rape, and this underlies the poem's violent verbs (tore, hack'd, hew'd, drew).

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.689–712 (Pan and Syrinx), Latin text, Perseus Digital Library; 'Syrinx', Wikipedia; Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'

  8. limpid / turbidly l. 9

    'Limpid' (clear, transparent, from Latin limpidus) and 'turbidly' (muddily, from Latin turbidus, disturbed) are set against each other in the same line, so purity is muddied in the space of a few syllables. The phrase recurs as 'turbidly flow'd the river' (l. 14), keeping the disturbance going while Pan works at the reed. The Latinate diction stands out among the poem's plain words.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'limpid, adj.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'turbid, adj.'

  9. Hack'd and hew'd ll. 15–16

    The reed is called 'patient' in the old sense of enduring, suffering. The god cuts it with 'his hard bleak steel', and the alliterative 'hack'd and hew'd' makes the act sound brutal. 'As a great god can' is ironic: divine power shows itself here as the capacity to mutilate. He goes on until 'there was not a sign of the leaf' to show it came from the river. The making of art requires the reed to lose its natural identity.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'patient, adj.'

  10. Pith like a heart ll. 21–24

    Pith is the soft spongy tissue inside a plant stem. Pan draws it out 'steadily', then notches the 'poor dry empty thing / In holes'. The simile 'like the heart of a man' anticipates the last stanza's 'Making a poet out of a man'. To become an instrument the reed must be hollowed and pierced. For the poet that means having his heart removed and being emptied, wounded and opened so that the god's breath can sound through him. Readers have connected this with Romantic ideas of the poet as an instrument played by inspiration (the Aeolian harp), here made violent.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pith, n.'

  11. Pan's laugh st. 5

    The god's laughter is the poem's most disturbing touch. He presents suffering as the necessary condition of beautiful art, 'The only way, since gods began / To make sweet music, they could succeed', and treats it as a matter of craft. 'He blew in power' gives the moment of inspiration (literally a breathing-in) a forceful, even violating, sense. The stanza puts into a single figure a widely held Victorian and Romantic view that poetry is born of pain.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Last Poems, ed. Robert Browning (Chapman and Hall, 1862)

  12. Sweet, sweet, sweet st. 6

    Stanza 6 changes from narrative to exclamation ('Sweet, sweet, sweet, O Pan!'). The adjectives 'Piercing' and 'Blinding' suggest that the sweetness itself is a kind of wound, echoing the notched holes of the reed. Nature responds. 'The sun on the hill forgot to die' (time halted, the sunset postponed), and the broken lilies revive. Barrett Browning grants art's power in full before the final stanza counts its cost.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'

  13. Half a beast l. 37

    'Yet' turns the poem from celebration to moral judgement. The god who makes such music is still 'half a beast', because he can 'laugh as he sits by the river' while the reed suffers. The goat-hoofs of stanza 1 now become a moral fact. Readers have connected the stanza with Barrett Browning's view of poetry as a costly vocation, set out in Aurora Leigh (1856) and elsewhere. Some critics also see a gendered dimension, with the male god forcibly 'making' an artist from a passive material.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Last Poems, ed. Robert Browning (Chapman and Hall, 1862)

  14. Making a poet out of a man ll. 39–42

    The last lines spell out the allegory. To make 'a poet out of a man' is to make the reed into a pipe, so that it 'grows nevermore again / As a reed with the reeds of the river'. The poet can never rejoin ordinary life. 'The true gods sigh for the cost and pain' sets Pan against higher deities, and these are often read as the Christian God, or as a truer, compassionate divinity. On this reading pagan aesthetics is set beside Christian sympathy. Critics differ on whether the poem condemns the art that comes of suffering or accepts it sadly as the price of song. Many find an autobiographical note in a poet who wrote out of illness and grief.

    Sandra Donaldson et al. (eds), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes on 'A Musical Instrument'; Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Last Poems, ed. Robert Browning (Chapman and Hall, 1862); 'A Musical Instrument', The Cornhill Magazine 2 (July 1860), with engraving after Frederic Leighton (OUR Heritage, University of Otago)
