Love

XIV

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From Sonnets from the Portuguese

Love

If thou must love me, let it be for nought
Except for love’s sake only. Do not say
“I love her for her smile—her look—her way
Of speaking gently,—for a trick of thought
That falls in well with mine, and certes brought
A sense of pleasant ease on such a day”—
For these things in themselves, Belovëd, may
Be changed, or change for thee,—and love, so wrought,
May be unwrought so. Neither love me for
Thine own dear pity’s wiping my cheeks dry,—
A creature might forget to weep, who bore
Thy comfort long, and lose thy love thereby!
But love me for love’s sake, that evermore
Thou may’st love on, through love’s eternity.

Study notes by the editors

  1. 'If thou must love me' ll. 1-2

    The conditional 'If thou must' carries the reluctance of the early sonnets, in which the speaker doubts she is worthy of Browning's love. 'For nought / Except for love's sake only' means for no cause outside love. The argument is a familiar one in love poetry (love that depends on qualities ends when the qualities change), but Barrett's turn is to forbid even the most flattering reasons. The sonnet is one of the best known of the sequence, often anthologised and set to music.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  2. the rejected reasons ll. 3-6

    Barrett ventriloquises a lover listing the beloved's charms, a gentle refusal of the blazon, the catalogue of the beloved's features in Petrarchan poetry. But the charms here are of manner rather than body: smile, look, 'way / Of speaking gently', 'a trick of thought / That falls in well with mine'. 'Trick' here means habit or characteristic turn, not deceit. The passage pointedly sets aside the conventions of love poetry by having the woman refuse them.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Oxford English Dictionary

  3. certes l. 5

    Barrett's diction mixes everyday speech with archaisms like 'certes' (from Old French), used by Chaucer and Spenser. The effect is half-playful, giving the imagined lover's praise ('certes brought / A sense of pleasant ease on such a day') a mock-courtly flavour, as if he were remembering a particular afternoon visit. The Brownings' courtship was conducted largely through letters and brief visits to her room in Wimpole Street, where such moments of 'pleasant ease' mattered.

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Brownings' Correspondence (Baylor University / Wedgestone Press), online edition

  4. 'Be changed, or change for thee' ll. 7-9

    The alternative 'Be changed, or change for thee' distinguishes two dangers: the beloved's qualities may actually alter, or the lover's perception of them may. Love 'so wrought, / May be unwrought so', made by reasons and unmade by them. 'Wrought' (made, worked) and 'unwrought' are a craftsman's terms. The argument recalls Shakespeare's Sonnet 116, 'Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds', which Barrett's lines seem to answer from the beloved's side.

    Shakespeare, Sonnet 116, ll. 2-3 ('Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds'); Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  5. pity wiping my cheeks dry ll. 9-12

    Barrett had been an invalid for years, and much of the sequence fears that Browning's love is compassion for a sick, grieving woman. Here she argues that a woman long comforted might 'forget to weep', and if pity were the cause of love, the love would then cease. The phrase 'A creature' modestly describes herself as any living being. The logic is witty and anxious at once: happiness itself could cost her his love.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  6. love's eternity ll. 13-14

    The final couplet-like sentence returns to the opening phrase 'love's sake' and adds 'love on, through love's eternity'. The word 'love' appears six times in the last six lines, a deliberate saturation. The religious idea that love is eternal (cf. 1 Corinthians 13:8, 'Charity never faileth') underlies the close. Some critics find the ending abstract; others note how the speaker, after refusing all particular reasons, finally claims for herself the most generous reason of all.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:8; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  7. Form: Petrarchan rhyme, broken lines ll. 9-14

    The octave rhymes abbaabba on 'nought/thought/brought/wrought' and 'say/way/day/may'; the sestet rhymes 'for/bore/evermore' with 'dry/thereby/eternity', a cdcdcd pattern. The turn comes mid-line 9 with a full stop and a new sentence ending on the weak word 'for', a strong enjambment. Barrett's sonnets were sometimes criticised for such roughness; recent critics read it as a conscious effect, giving speech-like urgency to a strict form.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese
