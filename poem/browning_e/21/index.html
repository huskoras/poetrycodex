Arts & Sciences

XXII

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From Sonnets from the Portuguese

Arts & Sciences

When our two souls stand up erect and strong,
Face to face, silent, drawing nigh and nigher,
Until the lengthening wings break into fire
At either curvëd point,—what bitter wrong
Can the earth do to us, that we should not long
Be here contented? Think! In mounting higher,
The angels would press on us and aspire
To drop some golden orb of perfect song
Into our deep, dear silence. Let us stay
Rather on earth, Belovëd,—where the unfit
Contrarious moods of men recoil away
And isolate pure spirits, and permit
A place to stand and love in for a day,
With darkness and the death-hour rounding it.

Study notes by the editors

  1. two souls standing face to face ll. 1-2

    The image is of two angelic figures, souls 'erect and strong', silent and approaching. Readers have compared Donne's 'The Ecstasy', where lovers' souls leave their bodies and negotiate between them, and Plato's Phaedrus, where the soul is winged. Barrett's emphasis on equality ('erect and strong', 'Face to face') matters in a sequence that often stresses the speaker's weakness: here she stands level with her lover. 'Face to face' also echoes 1 Corinthians 13:12 ('then face to face'), the vision of heavenly knowledge.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:12; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  2. wings breaking into fire ll. 3-4

    The wings 'lengthen' as the souls draw nearer until they touch 'At either curvëd point' and burst into fire, an image of passionate union that is still chaste and spiritual. It recalls the cherubim whose wings touched one another over the ark in Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:27) and Ezekiel's living creatures whose wings were joined (Ezekiel 1:9). The diaeresis in 'curvëd' marks a separate syllable for the metre (cur-ved). The fire is both purifying and erotic.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Kings 6:27; Bible (KJV), Ezekiel 1:9; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  3. 'what bitter wrong can the earth do' ll. 4-6

    The rhetorical question asks what injury the world could do that would make them unwilling to remain on earth. The syntax is compressed: 'that we should not long / Be here contented?' (where 'long' means 'for a long time'). There is an echo of the Brownings' circumstances: her father's opposition to any marriage, her illness, and the world's disapproval. The answer implied is none.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; The Brownings' Correspondence (Baylor University / Wedgestone Press), online edition

  4. the angels and the 'golden orb of perfect song' ll. 6-9

    'Mounting higher' would bring them among angels who would 'press on us' (crowd round) and 'aspire / To drop some golden orb of perfect song' into their 'deep, dear silence'. The 'golden orb' suggests the music of the spheres, the old idea that the heavenly bodies make perfect harmony; it is also simply a golden sphere let fall into their silence. The speaker prefers the lovers' silence to the angels' song, an audacious preference of human love over heaven.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  5. 'Let us stay rather on earth' ll. 9-10

    The turn comes, as often in the sequence, in the middle of line 9 with an imperative: 'Let us stay / Rather on earth, Belovëd'. This reverses the Neoplatonic movement of much love poetry, in which human love is a ladder to divine love. Barrett, a devout Christian, does not deny heaven, but asks for a place 'to stand and love in for a day'. The choice of earth, with its imperfections, is the sonnet's argument.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  6. Contrarious l. 11

    The 'unfit / Contrarious moods of men' are the hostile or unsuitable tempers of other people, which 'recoil away' from pure spirits and so 'isolate' them. The paradox is that the world's hostility protects the lovers by leaving them alone. 'Contrarious' had become rare by the nineteenth century, and Barrett's choice of it gives the line an older, more formal texture.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  7. darkness and the death-hour rounding it l. 14

    The final line limits the lovers' earthly happiness: a single 'day' bounded by darkness and 'the death-hour'. 'Rounding' means encircling, completing. Prospero's speech in The Tempest (4.1.156-158), 'We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep', is a likely echo. The ending is not tragic but realistic; the love chosen is mortal, and the awareness of death intensifies it.

    Shakespeare, The Tempest 4.1.156-158; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes
