Death & Dying

I

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From Sonnets from the Portuguese

Death & Dying

I thought once how Theocritus had sung
Of the sweet years, the dear and wished-for years,
Who each one in a gracious hand appears
To bear a gift for mortals, old or young:
And, as I mused it in his antique tongue,
I saw, in gradual vision through my tears,
The sweet, sad years, the melancholy years,
Those of my own life, who by turns had flung
A shadow across me. Straightway I was ’ware,
So weeping, how a mystic Shape did move
Behind me, and drew me backward by the hair;
And a voice said in mastery, while I strove,—
“Guess now who holds thee!”—“Death,” I said, But, there,
The silver answer rang, “Not Death, but Love.”

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnets from the Portuguese: the sequence l. 1

    Elizabeth Barrett wrote the sequence privately during her courtship by Robert Browning, from their first meeting in May 1845 until their secret marriage in September 1846, and showed it to him only in 1849. The title was meant to disguise the poems' personal nature by pretending they were translations; 'Portuguese' alluded to her earlier poem 'Catarina to Camoens' and to Browning's pet name for her. The sequence appeared in the second edition of her Poems (1850). Sonnet I sets the tone: a woman who expected only death, after years of illness and grief, is seized by Love.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Sonnets from the Portuguese, Project Gutenberg text

  2. Theocritus l. 1

    Theocritus of Syracuse, working in Alexandria and Cos in the third century BC, wrote the Idylls, short poems that include the first pastoral poems in European literature. Barrett was an accomplished Greek scholar who had translated Aeschylus' Prometheus Bound, so 'his antique tongue' means she read him in the original. The reference sets her sequence in a learned tradition at its very first line, a bold claim for a woman poet entering a form (the love sonnet sequence) dominated by men.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Sonnets from the Portuguese: I', ed. Marc R. Plamondon; Theocritus, Idyll 15.100-105 (the Hours in the Adonis song), trans. J. M. Edmonds, Theoi Classical Texts; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  3. the 'wished-for years' (the Hours) ll. 2-4

    In Idyll 15 a singer at the festival of Adonis addresses the Hours (Greek Horai, seasons), 'slowest of the blessed ones' yet 'longed-for', who always come bringing some gift to mortals. Barrett renders them as 'the sweet years, the dear and wished-for years', each appearing 'in a gracious hand' with a gift. Reading this, she sees instead 'the sweet, sad years, the melancholy years', her own, which cast 'A shadow across me'. The Greek ideal of time as benefactor is reversed by her experience of illness and bereavement (notably the drowning of her brother Edward in 1840).

    Theocritus, Idyll 15.100-105 (the Hours in the Adonis song), trans. J. M. Edmonds, Theoi Classical Texts; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  4. flung a shadow ll. 8-9

    The octave ends on a run-on into the sestet ('who by turns had flung / A shadow across me'), so the transition from reading to vision happens mid-line. The shadow prepares for the 'mystic Shape' that moves behind her: the dark years seem to become a single figure. The tears through which she sees the vision ('in gradual vision through my tears') blur reading into prophecy.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  5. ’ware l. 9

    The clipped form ''ware' (aware) and 'Straightway' (immediately) give the sudden turn a biblical or old-fashioned solemnity. 'Straightway' is frequent in the KJV Gospels for sudden events. The volta, or turn, in this Petrarchan sonnet comes in the middle of line 9, not at its start, a characteristic Barrett irregularity: the shift from memory to presence breaks into the verse.

    Oxford English Dictionary

  6. drawn back by the hair ll. 10-11

    In the first book of the Iliad, as Akhilleus draws his sword on Agamemnon, Athena comes up behind him, visible to him alone, and seizes him by his golden hair (Iliad 1.197). Barrett, who knew Homer intimately, adapts this divine intervention. The 'mystic Shape' behind her is likewise a supernatural power checking a human impulse; she 'strove' against it as one restrains a struggling person. The allusion turns a love poem into an epiphany and gives Love an epic, almost violent authority.

    Homer, Iliad 1.188-200 (Athena seizes Akhilleus by the hair), Perseus Digital Library; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes

  7. 'Death' / 'Not Death, but Love' ll. 13-14

    The voice demands 'Guess now who holds thee!', and the speaker, long an invalid expecting to die, answers 'Death'. The 'silver answer' corrects her: 'Not Death, but Love.' The shape of the scene is an old one (a guessing-game, a lover's hands over the eyes), made solemn. The last line's 'silver' sound contrasts with the 'shadow' and tears. Critics note the ambiguity: love arrives with the force of death, overpowering and possessing her ('in mastery'), and the sequence will often explore love's mixture of rescue and threat to selfhood.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  8. Form: Petrarchan sonnet ll. 1-14

    The rhyme scheme is Italian: the octave rhymes on '-ung' and '-ears' (sung, years, appears, young...), the sestet alternates '-are' and '-ove' (ware, move, hair, strove, there, Love). The sense, however, runs across the octave-sestet division and the volta falls mid-line 9. The dramatic dialogue packed into the last two lines, with dashes and quotation marks, is unusual for the form. Barrett chose the Petrarchan sonnet, the form of Petrarch's Laura poems, to speak as a woman lover rather than a beloved, reversing the tradition's roles.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), Sonnets from the Portuguese, notes; Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese
