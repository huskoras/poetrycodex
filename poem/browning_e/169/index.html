The Cry of the Children

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From The Poetical Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Volume 2

Religion

~"Pheu, pheu, ti prosderkesthe m' ommasin, tekna?"~

--Medea.

I.

Do ye hear the children weeping, O my brothers,
Ere the sorrow comes with years?
They are leaning their young heads against their mothers.
And that cannot stop their tears.
The young lambs are bleating in the meadows,
The young birds are chirping in the nest,
The young fawns are playing with the shadows,
The young flowers are blowing toward the west--
But the young, young children, O my brothers,
They are weeping bitterly!
They are weeping in the playtime of the others,
In the country of the free.

II.

Do you question the young children in the sorrow
Why their tears are falling so?
The old man may weep for his to-morrow
Which is lost in Long Ago;
The old tree is leafless in the forest,
The old year is ending in the frost,
The old wound, if stricken, is the sorest,
The old hope is hardest to be lost:
But the young, young children, O my brothers,
Do you ask them why they stand
Weeping sore before the bosoms of their mothers,
In our happy Fatherland?

III.

They look up with their pale and sunken faces,
And their looks are sad to see,
For the man's hoary anguish draws and presses
Down the cheeks of infancy;
"Your old earth," they say, "is very dreary,
Our young feet," they say, "are very weak;
Few paces have we taken, yet are weary--
Our grave-rest is very far to seek:
Ask the aged why they weep, and not the children,
For the outside earth is cold,
And we young ones stand without, in our bewildering,
And the graves are for the old."

IV.

"True," say the children, "it may happen
That we die before our time:
Little Alice died last year, her grave is shapen
Like a snowball, in the rime.
We looked into the pit prepared to take her:
Was no room for any work in the close clay!
From the sleep wherein she lieth none will wake her,
Crying, 'Get up, little Alice! it is day.'
If you listen by that grave, in sun and shower,
With your ear down, little Alice never cries;
Could we see her face, be sure we should not know her,
For the smile has time for growing in her eyes:
And merry go her moments, lulled and stilled in
The shroud by the kirk-chime.
It is good when it happens," say the children,
"That we die before our time."

V.

Alas, alas, the children! they are seeking
Death in life, as best to have:
They are binding up their hearts away from breaking,
With a cerement from the grave.
Go out, children, from the mine and from the city,
Sing out, children, as the little thrushes do;
Pluck your handfuls of the meadow-cowslips pretty,
Laugh aloud, to feel your fingers let them through!
But they answer, "Are your cowslips of the meadows
Like our weeds anear the mine?
Leave us quiet in the dark of the coal-shadows,
From your pleasures fair and fine!

VI.

"For oh," say the children, "we are weary,
And we cannot run or leap;
If we cared for any meadows, it were merely
To drop down in them and sleep.
Our knees tremble sorely in the stooping,
We fall upon our faces, trying to go;
And, underneath our heavy eyelids drooping,
The reddest flower would look as pale as snow.
For, all day, we drag our burden tiring
Through the coal-dark, underground;
Or, all day, we drive the wheels of iron
In the factories, round and round.

VII.

"For all day the wheels are droning, turning;
Their wind comes in our faces,
Till our hearts turn, our heads with pulses burning,
And the walls turn in their places:
Turns the sky in the high window, blank and reeling,
Turns the long light that drops adown the wall,
Turn the black flies that crawl along the ceiling:
All are turning, all the day, and we with all.
And all day the iron wheels are droning,
And sometimes we could pray,
'O ye wheels' (breaking out in a mad moaning),
'Stop! be silent for to-day!'"

VIII.

Ay, be silent! Let them hear each other breathing
For a moment, mouth to mouth!
Let them touch each other's hands, in a fresh wreathing
Of their tender human youth!
Let them feel that this cold metallic motion
Is not all the life God fashions or reveals:
Let them prove their living souls against the notion
That they live in you, or under you, O wheels!
Still, all day, the iron wheels go onward,
Grinding life down from its mark;
And the children's souls, which God is calling sunward,
Spin on blindly in the dark.

IX.

Now tell the poor young children, O my brothers,
To look up to Him and pray;
So the blessed One who blesseth all the others,
Will bless them another day.
They answer, "Who is God that He should hear us,
While the rushing of the iron wheels is stirred?
When we sob aloud, the human creatures near us
Pass by, hearing not, or answer not a word.
And we hear not (for the wheels in their resounding)
Strangers speaking at the door:
Is it likely God, with angels singing round Him,
Hears our weeping any more?

X.

"Two words, indeed, of praying we remember,
And at midnight's hour of harm,
'Our Father,' looking upward in the chamber,
We say softly for a charm.
We know no other words except 'Our Father,'
And we think that, in some pause of angels' song,
God may pluck them with the silence sweet to gather,
And hold both within His right hand which is strong.
'Our Father!' If He heard us, He would surely
(For they call Him good and mild)
Answer, smiling down the steep world very purely,
'Come and rest with me, my child.'

XI.

"But, no!" say the children, weeping faster,
"He is speechless as a stone:
And they tell us, of His image is the master
Who commands us to work on.
Go to!" say the children,--"up in Heaven,
Dark, wheel-like, turning clouds are all we find.
Do not mock us; grief has made us unbelieving:
We look up for God, but tears have made us blind."
Do you hear the children weeping and disproving,
O my brothers, what ye preach?
For God's possible is taught by His world's loving,
And the children doubt of each.

XII.

And well may the children weep before you!
They are weary ere they run;
They have never seen the sunshine, nor the glory
Which is brighter than the sun.
They know the grief of man, without its wisdom;
They sink in man's despair, without its calm;
Are slaves, without the liberty in Christdom,
Are martyrs, by the pang without the palm:
Are worn as if with age, yet unretrievingly
The harvest of its memories cannot reap,--
Are orphans of the earthly love and heavenly.
Let them weep! let them weep!

XIII.

They look up with their pale and sunken faces,
And their look is dread to see,
For they mind you of their angels in high places,
With eyes turned on Deity.
"How long," they say, "how long, O cruel nation,
Will you stand, to move the world, on a child's heart,--
Stifle down with a mailed heel its palpitation,
And tread onward to your throne amid the mart?
Our blood splashes upward, O gold-heaper,
And your purple shows your path!
But the child's sob in the silence curses deeper
Than the strong man in his wrath."

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion: the child labour reports st. 1

    The Royal Commission on Children's Employment published a report on mines in 1842, with evidence of very young children working underground, and a second report on trades and manufactures in 1843. Barrett's friend the poet R. H. Horne was one of the sub-commissioners, and the evidence he gathered is said to have prompted her poem. The poem appeared in Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine in August 1843 and was reprinted, revised, in Poems (1844). It became one of the best-known texts of the factory reform movement associated with Lord Ashley (later Shaftesbury).

    Wikipedia, 'The Cry of the Children (poem)'; Baylor University, 19th-Century Research Seminar blog: bound copy of Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine (Aug. 1843) with the first printing; Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  2. The epigraph from Medea epigraph

    The epigraph, added in 1844, quotes Euripides' Medea (l. 1040) in transliterated Greek. Medea, about to murder her sons to punish Jason, falters at their gaze. Barrett's implication is fierce: England is a mother destroying its own children, and the children's gaze, like that of Medea's sons, accuses. The theme of the children looking up 'with their pale and sunken faces' (st. 3, 13) answers the epigraph. The use of Greek also announces the learned, prophetic authority of a woman poet addressing public questions.

    Euripides, Medea 1040-1041, trans. David Kovacs, Perseus Digital Library; Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  3. 'O my brothers': address and metre st. 1

    The repeated apostrophe 'O my brothers' casts the speaker as a prophet or preacher addressing the men who make laws and own mills. The long lines are predominantly trochaic (stress-unstress: 'DO ye HEAR the CHILdren WEEPing'), alternating with shorter lines, with a heavy, chant-like rhythm. The stanzas are mostly of twelve lines (the fourth runs to sixteen) rhyming in alternate lines. Barrett used many inexact rhymes ('children / bewildering', 'stilled in / children'), which drew criticism as careless; she treated such rhyme as a legitimate experiment.

    Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'; Wikipedia, 'The Cry of the Children (poem)'

  4. 'the country of the free'; 'our happy Fatherland' sts. 1-2

    The British Slavery Abolition Act of 1833 was a source of national pride, and anti-slavery language was often turned against industrial conditions at home, with factory children called 'white slaves'. Barrett herself came from a family whose wealth derived from Jamaican plantations. 'The country of the free' and 'our happy Fatherland' are ironic, and stanza 12 makes the comparison explicit: the children 'Are slaves, without the liberty in Christdom'. The opening contrast with lambs, birds, fawns and flowers places the children outside the natural order of youth.

    Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  5. the old faces of children st. 3

    'Hoary' (grey-haired, aged) anguish 'draws and presses / Down the cheeks of infancy'. The paradox of children who are old before their time runs through the poem: they are 'weary' after 'few paces', and their 'grave-rest is very far to seek'. The line returns almost unchanged in the last stanza ('They look up with their pale and sunken faces'), where 'looks are sad' becomes 'look is dread', a turn from pity to accusation. The image answers the epigraph's children whose gaze Medea cannot bear.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'; Oxford English Dictionary

  6. Little Alice st. 4

    Stanza 4 is spoken by the children, who report that 'It is good when it happens ... / That we die before our time.' Alice's grave is a small mound in the 'rime' (hoarfrost). There 'Was no room for any work in the close clay', a grim joke: the grave is the one place without labour. No one will wake her with 'Get up, little Alice! it is day', recalling the early-morning summons to work. Child mortality in the mines and mills was a central concern of the reports.

    Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Oxford English Dictionary

  7. kirk-chime st. 4

    The northern word 'kirk' fits the industrial north and Scotland, where many mills and mines were. Alice's moments, 'lulled and stilled in / The shroud by the kirk-chime', are 'merry' because still. This is one of the poem's controversial rhymes ('stilled in' / 'children'), which links the dead girl to the living. The sound of church bells, a promise of rest, is set against the noise of the iron wheels later.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  8. cerement st. 5

    From Latin cera, wax, the cerement or cerecloth was used to wrap the dead (Hamlet speaks of his father's 'cerements', 1.4.48). The image is of children who, seeking 'Death in life', numb their feelings in advance. When the speaker urges them to go out into the meadows and pick cowslips, they answer that meadow flowers are nothing to them, 'Like our weeds anear the mine'; they want only to be left 'in the dark of the coal-shadows'.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.4.48

  9. the mines and factories st. 6

    The 1842 report described children, some as young as five or six, working as 'trappers' (opening ventilation doors) and 'hurriers' (dragging coal tubs, sometimes harnessed by chains) in narrow seams, 'Our knees tremble sorely in the stooping'. The Mines Act of 1842 banned underground work for women and for boys under ten. The 'wheels of iron / In the factories, round and round' refer to the textile mills, where long hours were regulated by the Factory Act of 1833, with further reform in 1844 and the Ten Hours Act of 1847.

    Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Wikipedia, 'The Cry of the Children (poem)'

  10. the turning wheels st. 7

    Stanza 7 is the poem's most striking rendering of industrial experience. The repetition of 'turn' (seven times) and the inverted syntax ('Turns the sky ... / Turns the long light ... / Turn the black flies') reproduce the dizziness of children who stand for hours beside revolving machinery. The 'wind' of the wheels blows in their faces. The children's only prayer is to the wheels: 'Stop! be silent for to-day!' In stanza 8 the speaker takes up their cry ('Ay, be silent!'), insisting that the 'cold metallic motion / Is not all the life God fashions or reveals'.

    Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  11. souls called 'sunward' but spinning in the dark st. 8

    The souls are imagined as being turned by the wheels, 'Grinding life down from its mark' (from its aim or its natural measure). 'Sunward' recalls the earlier contrast with flowers that 'blow toward the west' and anticipates stanza 12: 'They have never seen the sunshine, nor the glory / Which is brighter than the sun' (God's glory, Revelation 21:23, 'the glory of God did lighten it'). Spinning is both factory work and blind, purposeless motion.

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 21:23; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  12. 'Our Father' st. 10

    The 1842-43 reports recorded the shocking ignorance of many working children, who could not name Christ or say a prayer; the children here know 'no other words except "Our Father"' (Matthew 6:9). They hope God might 'pluck them' out of the angels' song and hold them in his right hand. The pathos lies in the gap between the universal fatherhood of God invoked in the prayer and the absence of any father, earthly or heavenly, who seems to hear.

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 6:9; Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes

  13. 'He is speechless as a stone' st. 11

    The children's despair is theological: 'they tell us, of His image is the master / Who commands us to work on' (man made in God's image, Genesis 1:27). If the master is God's image, God must be like the master. 'Go to!' is an archaic exclamation of impatience or disbelief (as in Genesis 11:3-4). The speaker then generalises: 'For God's possible is taught by His world's loving', that is, people learn what God might be from the love they meet in the world. The stanza was bold for 1843, charging social injustice with producing atheism.

    Bible (KJV), Genesis 1:27; Oxford English Dictionary; Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'

  14. Christdom; martyrs without the palm st. 12

    'Christdom', a coinage or rare variant for Christendom, means the realm of Christ, where (St Paul says) servants are 'the Lord's freeman' (1 Corinthians 7:22). The 'palm' is the symbol of the martyr's victory (Revelation 7:9, the multitude 'with palms in their hands'). The children have the pain of martyrdom ('the pang') without its reward, the grief of age without its wisdom, and they 'Are orphans of the earthly love and heavenly'. The stanza ends with the speaker's exhausted 'Let them weep! let them weep!'

    Bible (KJV), Revelation 7:9; Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 7:22; Oxford English Dictionary

  15. 'their angels in high places' st. 13

    Matthew 18:10: 'Take heed that ye despise not one of these little ones; for I say unto you, That in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father.' The children's look reminds the 'brothers' of these angels 'With eyes turned on Deity'. The cry 'How long ... how long, O cruel nation' echoes the martyrs under the altar in Revelation 6:10 ('How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood'), and the Psalms (13:1).

    Bible (KJV), Matthew 18:10; Bible (KJV), Revelation 6:10

  16. the mailed heel, the mart, the gold-heaper st. 13

    The image of standing on something 'to move the world' recalls Archimedes' boast that with a place to stand he could move the earth; here the fulcrum of national power is a child's heart. The 'mailed heel' is armoured like a knight's, and the 'throne amid the mart' (market) combines imperial pride with commerce. The 'gold-heaper' is the capitalist whose 'purple' (the royal colour) is stained by the children's 'blood splash[ing] upward'. The final couplet claims that 'the child's sob in the silence curses deeper / Than the strong man in his wrath', a prophetic threat.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'; Victorian Poetry open anthology (Texas A&M), 'The Cry of the Children (1843)', introduction and notes

  17. Barrett's rhymes st. 3

    Barrett's experimental rhymes (also 'Heaven / unbelieving' in st. 11 and 'unretrievingly / heavenly' in st. 12) drew complaints of carelessness from reviewers of the 1844 Poems. Such rhymes belong to her deliberate experiments with rhyme in these years, though reviewers took them for lapses. In this poem the half-rhymes on 'children' are effective: the word that should be the centre of the stanza never finds a true partner, as the children find no place. 'Without' here means outside.

    Sandra Donaldson (gen. ed.), The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to 'The Cry of the Children'; The Brownings' Correspondence (Baylor University / Wedgestone Press), online edition
