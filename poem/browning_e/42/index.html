Love

XLIII

by Elizabeth Barrett Browning 1806–1861

From Sonnets from the Portuguese

Love

How do I love thee? Let me count the ways.
I love thee to the depth and breadth and height
My soul can reach, when feeling out of sight
For the ends of Being and ideal Grace.
I love thee to the level of everyday’s
Most quiet need, by sun and candlelight.
I love thee freely, as men strive for Right;
I love thee purely, as they turn from Praise.
I love thee with the passion put to use
In my old griefs, and with my childhood’s faith.
I love thee with a love I seemed to lose
With my lost saints,—I love thee with the breath,
Smiles, tears, of all my life!—and, if God choose,
I shall but love thee better after death.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Sonnets from the Portuguese l. 1

    Elizabeth Barrett wrote the sonnets privately during the courtship that ended in her secret marriage to Robert Browning in September 1846; she showed them to him only in 1849. They were published in the second edition of her Poems (1850), and the title presented them as if translated, disguising their autobiographical content; it alludes to Robert's pet name for her, linked with her poem 'Catarina to Camoens'. A supposed private edition dated Reading 1847 was a forgery by Thomas J. Wise, exposed by Carter and Pollard in 1934. This is the forty-third of the forty-four sonnets in the sequence.

    The Victorian Web, 'Sonnet 43' (headnote, citing Ousby, Cambridge Guide to Literature in English); John Carter and Graham Pollard, An Enquiry into the Nature of Certain Nineteenth Century Pamphlets (1934); see 'Thomas James Wise', Wikipedia; Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  2. Let me count the ways l. 1

    Counting the ways of love is a rhetorical figure with roots in the catalogue or blazon of Renaissance love poetry, but here the speaker enumerates not the beloved's beauties but the dimensions of her own love. The repeated 'I love thee', in the manner of a liturgical litany, gives the sonnet its incantatory force. Critics have observed that the poem's fame has made it seem conventional, whereas in its context it is unusual: a woman speaking as the active lover in a genre where women were traditionally silent objects of praise (Mermin).

    Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986); The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  3. Petrarchan sonnet ll. 1-14

    The rhymes are ways/height/sight/Grace/everyday's/candlelight/Right/Praise (abbaabba) and use/faith/lose/breath/choose/death (cdcdcd). EBB favoured the stricter Italian form, which she used throughout the sequence, and treated it freely: enjambment carries the octave's syntax across line-ends ('height / My soul can reach'), and the volta at line 9 is softened, as the catalogue continues. The real turn comes in the final line, which moves from life to 'after death'.

    Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986)

  4. depth and breadth and height l. 2

    In Ephesians 3:18-19 Paul prays that the Ephesians 'may be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; And to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge'. The echo frames human love in terms of divine love and its measureless dimensions. The spatial terms also give the love a quasi-physical extent, reaching as far as the soul can stretch 'when feeling out of sight' (groping beyond what is visible) towards the limits of existence.

    Ephesians 3:17-19 (KJV); Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  5. the ends of Being and ideal Grace ll. 3-4

    'Ends' can mean the outer limits of being, which the soul gropes towards 'out of sight', or its final purposes (the philosophical sense of 'end' as telos). 'Ideal Grace' may be divine grace in its perfect form or ideal beauty, so the phrase combines theology and aesthetics. EBB, a learned reader of Greek and of Christian Platonism, compresses metaphysical ambition into an abstract line; some readers find it the poem's most strained moment, others its most daring claim that love reaches toward the absolute.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'end, n.'; Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  6. everyday's / Most quiet need ll. 5-6

    The sonnet moves from the infinite to the domestic: the love meets the plainest needs of every day, 'by sun and candlelight', that is, day and evening. The contrast between the metaphysical and the homely is a feature of the whole sequence, which draws on the circumstances of EBB's invalid life in her Wimpole Street room. Leighton and Mermin note how the sonnets give equal weight to everyday intimacy and spiritual exaltation.

    Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986); Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989)

  7. freely, as men strive for Right l. 7

    The comparison links private love to public virtue. EBB was deeply engaged with political causes (later the Italian Risorgimento and the anti-slavery cause), and 'Right' here suggests justice pursued voluntarily, without reward. 'Freely' also recalls the theology of grace freely given. The paired line (l. 8) gives a matching moral analogy, so that ll. 7-8 form a balanced pair within the octave.

    Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986)

  8. purely, as they turn from Praise l. 8

    To 'turn from Praise' is to refuse flattery or the desire for fame, an act of moral purity. EBB, at the time of the courtship the better-known poet of the two, was acutely conscious of reputation; the line implies a love free of self-regard. The capitalised abstractions (Being, Grace, Right, Praise) give the octave a quasi-allegorical dignity, and the parallel syntax of ll. 7-8 ('freely, as ... purely, as ...') shows the poem's controlled, almost liturgical balance before the more personal sestet.

    Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989)

  9. the passion put to use / In my old griefs ll. 9-10

    'Passion' originally meant suffering (as in Christ's Passion). EBB had endured long illness and grief, including her mother's death in 1828 and the drowning of her favourite brother Edward ('Bro') at Torquay in 1840, which she blamed on herself. The line suggests that emotional energy formerly consumed by sorrow is now 'put to use' in love. The sestet thus turns to her personal history, as the octave dealt in abstractions.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'passion, n.'; Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986)

  10. my childhood's faith l. 10

    The phrase suggests unquestioning trust, as in Christ's saying that the kingdom of heaven belongs to those who become 'as little children' (Matthew 18:3). Coupled with 'old griefs', it spans her whole life, from early belief to later sorrow, and prepares for the next lines on a love she 'seemed to lose' with her 'lost saints'. The suggestion is that the new love recovers a capacity for unreserved belief that grief and illness had eroded, a movement of restoration traced across the whole sequence.

    Matthew 18:3 (KJV); Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986)

  11. my lost saints ll. 11-12

    'Saints' may refer to people she revered who have died, chiefly her mother and her brother Edward, so that the love 'lost' with them is the love she gave her family. Alternatively it may mean the saints and religious ideals of her early faith, a fervour she lost as she grew up. Both readings imply that this new love restores an earlier capacity for devotion. The dash and the sudden exclamation that follow ('I love thee with the breath, / Smiles, tears, of all my life!') break the measured catalogue with overflowing feeling.

    Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); Angela Leighton, Elizabeth Barrett Browning (Harvester, 1986); Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese

  12. if God choose ... after death ll. 13-14

    'If God choose' places the claim under providence, a humility that tempers the boast. 'But' means 'only': death will only increase her love. The idea of love surviving death is an old topos of love poetry, but here it also reflects EBB's earnest Christian hope of reunion. Given her chronic illness and expectation of early death, the line had personal weight. It turns the catalogue of the present into a promise for eternity.

    Sandra Donaldson et al., eds., The Works of Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 5 vols (Pickering & Chatto, 2010), notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese; Dorothy Mermin, Elizabeth Barrett Browning: The Origins of a New Poetry (University of Chicago Press, 1989); The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. E: The Victorian Age, notes to Sonnets from the Portuguese
