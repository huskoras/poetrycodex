Nature

When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Nature

1
When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d,
And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night,
I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring.
Ever-returning spring, trinity sure to me you bring,
Lilac blooming perennial and drooping star in the west,
And thought of him I love.
2
O powerful western fallen star!
O shades of night--O moody, tearful night!
O great star disappear’d--O the black murk that hides the star!
O cruel hands that hold me powerless--O helpless soul of me!
O harsh surrounding cloud that will not free my soul.
3
In the dooryard fronting an old farm-house near the white-wash’d palings,
Stands the lilac-bush tall-growing with heart-shaped leaves of rich green,
With many a pointed blossom rising delicate, with the perfume strong I love,
With every leaf a miracle--and from this bush in the dooryard,
With delicate-color’d blossoms and heart-shaped leaves of rich green,
A sprig with its flower I break.
4
In the swamp in secluded recesses,
A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song.
Solitary the thrush,
The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements,
Sings by himself a song.
Song of the bleeding throat,
Death’s outlet song of life, (for well dear brother I know,
If thou wast not granted to sing thou wouldst surely die.)
5
Over the breast of the spring, the land, amid cities,
Amid lanes and through old woods, where lately the violets peep’d
from the ground, spotting the gray debris,
Amid the grass in the fields each side of the lanes, passing the
endless grass,
Passing the yellow-spear’d wheat, every grain from its shroud in the
dark-brown fields uprisen,
Passing the apple-tree blows of white and pink in the orchards,
Carrying a corpse to where it shall rest in the grave,
Night and day journeys a coffin.
6
Coffin that passes through lanes and streets,
Through day and night with the great cloud darkening the land,
With the pomp of the inloop’d flags with the cities draped in black,
With the show of the States themselves as of crape-veil’d women standing,
With processions long and winding and the flambeaus of the night,
With the countless torches lit, with the silent sea of faces and the
unbared heads,
With the waiting depot, the arriving coffin, and the sombre faces,
With dirges through the night, with the thousand voices rising strong
and solemn,
With all the mournful voices of the dirges pour’d around the coffin,
The dim-lit churches and the shuddering organs--where amid these
you journey,
With the tolling tolling bells’ perpetual clang,
Here, coffin that slowly passes,
I give you my sprig of lilac.
7
(Nor for you, for one alone,
Blossoms and branches green to coffins all I bring,
For fresh as the morning, thus would I chant a song for you O sane
and sacred death.
All over bouquets of roses,
O death, I cover you over with roses and early lilies,
But mostly and now the lilac that blooms the first,
Copious I break, I break the sprigs from the bushes,
With loaded arms I come, pouring for you,
For you and the coffins all of you O death.)
8
O western orb sailing the heaven,
Now I know what you must have meant as a month since I walk’d,
As I walk’d in silence the transparent shadowy night,
As I saw you had something to tell as you bent to me night after night,
As you droop’d from the sky low down as if to my side, (while the
other stars all look’d on,)
As we wander’d together the solemn night, (for something I know not
what kept me from sleep,)
As the night advanced, and I saw on the rim of the west how full you
were of woe,
As I stood on the rising ground in the breeze in the cool transparent night,
As I watch’d where you pass’d and was lost in the netherward black
of the night,
As my soul in its trouble dissatisfied sank, as where you sad orb,
Concluded, dropt in the night, and was gone.
9
Sing on there in the swamp,
O singer bashful and tender, I hear your notes, I hear your call,
I hear, I come presently, I understand you,
But a moment I linger, for the lustrous star has detain’d me,
The star my departing comrade holds and detains me.
10
O how shall I warble myself for the dead one there I loved?
And how shall I deck my song for the large sweet soul that has gone?
And what shall my perfume be for the grave of him I love?
Sea-winds blown from east and west,
Blown from the Eastern sea and blown from the Western sea, till
there on the prairies meeting,
These and with these and the breath of my chant,
I’ll perfume the grave of him I love.
11
O what shall I hang on the chamber walls?
And what shall the pictures be that I hang on the walls,
To adorn the burial-house of him I love?
Pictures of growing spring and farms and homes,
With the Fourth-month eve at sundown, and the gray smoke lucid and bright,
With floods of the yellow gold of the gorgeous, indolent, sinking
sun, burning, expanding the air,
With the fresh sweet herbage under foot, and the pale green leaves
of the trees prolific,
In the distance the flowing glaze, the breast of the river, with a
wind-dapple here and there,
With ranging hills on the banks, with many a line against the sky,
and shadows,
And the city at hand with dwellings so dense, and stacks of chimneys,
And all the scenes of life and the workshops, and the workmen
homeward returning.
12
Lo, body and soul--this land,
My own Manhattan with spires, and the sparkling and hurrying tides,
and the ships,
The varied and ample land, the South and the North in the light,
Ohio’s shores and flashing Missouri,
And ever the far-spreading prairies cover’d with grass and corn.
Lo, the most excellent sun so calm and haughty,
The violet and purple morn with just-felt breezes,
The gentle soft-born measureless light,
The miracle spreading bathing all, the fulfill’d noon,
The coming eve delicious, the welcome night and the stars,
Over my cities shining all, enveloping man and land.
13
Sing on, sing on you gray-brown bird,
Sing from the swamps, the recesses, pour your chant from the bushes,
Limitless out of the dusk, out of the cedars and pines.
Sing on dearest brother, warble your reedy song,
Loud human song, with voice of uttermost woe.
O liquid and free and tender!
O wild and loose to my soul--O wondrous singer!
You only I hear--yet the star holds me, (but will soon depart,)
Yet the lilac with mastering odor holds me.
14
Now while I sat in the day and look’d forth,
In the close of the day with its light and the fields of spring, and
the farmers preparing their crops,
In the large unconscious scenery of my land with its lakes and forests,
In the heavenly aerial beauty, (after the perturb’d winds and the storms,)
Under the arching heavens of the afternoon swift passing, and the
voices of children and women,
The many-moving sea-tides, and I saw the ships how they sail’d,
And the summer approaching with richness, and the fields all busy
with labor,
And the infinite separate houses, how they all went on, each with
its meals and minutia of daily usages,
And the streets how their throbbings throbb’d, and the cities pent--
lo, then and there,
Falling upon them all and among them all, enveloping me with the rest,
Appear’d the cloud, appear’d the long black trail,
And I knew death, its thought, and the sacred knowledge of death.
Then with the knowledge of death as walking one side of me,
And the thought of death close-walking the other side of me,
And I in the middle as with companions, and as holding the hands of
companions,
I fled forth to the hiding receiving night that talks not,
Down to the shores of the water, the path by the swamp in the dimness,
To the solemn shadowy cedars and ghostly pines so still.
And the singer so shy to the rest receiv’d me,
The gray-brown bird I know receiv’d us comrades three,
And he sang the carol of death, and a verse for him I love.
From deep secluded recesses,
From the fragrant cedars and the ghostly pines so still,
Came the carol of the bird.
And the charm of the carol rapt me,
As I held as if by their hands my comrades in the night,
And the voice of my spirit tallied the song of the bird.
Come lovely and soothing death,
Undulate round the world, serenely arriving, arriving,
In the day, in the night, to all, to each,
Sooner or later delicate death.
Prais’d be the fathomless universe,
For life and joy, and for objects and knowledge curious,
And for love, sweet love--but praise! praise! praise!
For the sure-enwinding arms of cool-enfolding death.
Dark mother always gliding near with soft feet,
Have none chanted for thee a chant of fullest welcome?
Then I chant it for thee, I glorify thee above all,
I bring thee a song that when thou must indeed come, come unfalteringly.
Approach strong deliveress,
When it is so, when thou hast taken them I joyously sing the dead,
Lost in the loving floating ocean of thee,
Laved in the flood of thy bliss O death.
From me to thee glad serenades,
Dances for thee I propose saluting thee, adornments and feastings for thee,
And the sights of the open landscape and the high-spread shy are fitting,
And life and the fields, and the huge and thoughtful night.
The night in silence under many a star,
The ocean shore and the husky whispering wave whose voice I know,
And the soul turning to thee O vast and well-veil’d death,
And the body gratefully nestling close to thee.
Over the tree-tops I float thee a song,
Over the rising and sinking waves, over the myriad fields and the
prairies wide,
Over the dense-pack’d cities all and the teeming wharves and ways,
I float this carol with joy, with joy to thee O death.
15
To the tally of my soul,
Loud and strong kept up the gray-brown bird,
With pure deliberate notes spreading filling the night.
Loud in the pines and cedars dim,
Clear in the freshness moist and the swamp-perfume,
And I with my comrades there in the night.
While my sight that was bound in my eyes unclosed,
As to long panoramas of visions.
And I saw askant the armies,
I saw as in noiseless dreams hundreds of battle-flags,
Borne through the smoke of the battles and pierc’d with missiles I saw them,
And carried hither and yon through the smoke, and torn and bloody,
And at last but a few shreds left on the staffs, (and all in silence,)
And the staffs all splinter’d and broken.
I saw battle-corpses, myriads of them,
And the white skeletons of young men, I saw them,
I saw the debris and debris of all the slain soldiers of the war,
But I saw they were not as was thought,
They themselves were fully at rest, they suffer’d not,
The living remain’d and suffer’d, the mother suffer’d,
And the wife and the child and the musing comrade suffer’d,
And the armies that remain’d suffer’d.
16
Passing the visions, passing the night,
Passing, unloosing the hold of my comrades’ hands,
Passing the song of the hermit bird and the tallying song of my soul,
Victorious song, death’s outlet song, yet varying ever-altering song,
As low and wailing, yet clear the notes, rising and falling,
flooding the night,
Sadly sinking and fainting, as warning and warning, and yet again
bursting with joy,
Covering the earth and filling the spread of the heaven,
As that powerful psalm in the night I heard from recesses,
Passing, I leave thee lilac with heart-shaped leaves,
I leave thee there in the door-yard, blooming, returning with spring.
I cease from my song for thee,
From my gaze on thee in the west, fronting the west, communing with thee,
O comrade lustrous with silver face in the night.
Yet each to keep and all, retrievements out of the night,
The song, the wondrous chant of the gray-brown bird,
And the tallying chant, the echo arous’d in my soul,
With the lustrous and drooping star with the countenance full of woe,
With the holders holding my hand nearing the call of the bird,
Comrades mine and I in the midst, and their memory ever to keep, for
the dead I loved so well,
For the sweetest, wisest soul of all my days and lands--and this for
his dear sake,
Lilac and star and bird twined with the chant of my soul,
There in the fragrant pines and the cedars dusk and dim.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion, composition and publication § 1

    Lincoln was shot on 14 April 1865 and died the next morning; his body was carried by funeral train from Washington to Springfield, Illinois. Whitman, in Brooklyn with his mother when the news came, composed the elegy in the following months; it appeared in Sequel to Drum-Taps (fall 1865), was revised for later editions of Leaves of Grass, and took its final place in the cluster 'Memories of President Lincoln' (1881). The archive text is the late version. Like 'O Captain!', it never names Lincoln. It is generally ranked among the great elegies in English, in the line of Milton's 'Lycidas', Shelley's 'Adonais' and Tennyson's In Memoriam, but it reshapes their conventions.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]; Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' (1891-92 text, with notes); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  2. lilacs in the dooryard § 3

    In his lecture 'Death of Abraham Lincoln' (first delivered 1879) Whitman recalled that where he was staying 'there were many lilacs in full bloom', and that their sight and odour always reminded him of the tragedy. The 'dooryard' is the small yard in front of a farmhouse door; section 3 describes a real-seeming bush by 'white-wash' palings, with heart-shaped leaves and pointed blossoms. The lilac becomes the poem's emblem of love, renewal and the returning spring, and the first of its three symbols.

    Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg, 'Death of Abraham Lincoln' (lecture, 1879); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  3. the great star § 1

    The 'great star' that 'droop'd in the western sky' is Venus, the evening star. In Specimen Days, in an entry of March 1865, Whitman wrote that 'The western star, Venus' had never been so large or so clear, and seemed 'as if it told something'. In 1879 he recalled walking by the Potomac in the spring before Lincoln's murder watching Venus, 'moody as myself', and quoted this poem's section 8. The setting star becomes the dead president, 'my departing comrade'.

    Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg, 'The Weather - Does It Sympathize with These Times?' and 'Night of March 18, '79'; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  4. the trinity § 1

    The 'trinity' is lilac, drooping star and 'thought of him I love', which spring will always bring back together. The word quietly recalls the Christian Trinity, and Lincoln's death fell on Good Friday weekend (he died on Saturday 15 April, the day before Easter), though the poem refuses Christian consolation. Later the hermit thrush joins, and the closing line braids 'Lilac and star and bird'. Many critics treat the interplay of these symbols as the poem's structure.

    Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  5. Section 2: conventional lament § 2

    Section 2 is the most conventional part of the poem: five lines of apostrophe ('O ... O ...') lamenting the fallen star, the night, the black murk and the 'cruel hands' that hold the speaker powerless. This is the rhetoric of classical and Renaissance elegy, as in the opening of 'Lycidas'. The 'harsh surrounding cloud' introduces the cloud that reappears in sections 6 and 14 as the darkness of national grief. The following sections turn to concrete things, the lilac bush and the bird, and the despairing cry is gradually transformed.

    John Milton, 'Lycidas' (1638), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  6. breaking the sprig § 3

    Strewing flowers on the hearse is a convention of pastoral elegy: Milton's 'Lycidas' asks the valleys to bring their flowers to the bier. Whitman's speaker breaks one sprig, which he lays on the coffin at the end of section 6 ('I give you my sprig of lilac'). The inversion of the sentence ('A sprig ... I break') makes the act ceremonial. In section 7 the gesture is multiplied for all the dead.

    John Milton, 'Lycidas' (1638), Dartmouth Milton Reading Room; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  7. the hermit thrush § 4

    The bird is the hermit thrush (Catharus guttatus), a reclusive North American thrush that sings at dusk from deep woods. The naturalist John Burroughs, Whitman's friend in Washington from late 1863, heard it in the Catskills in the summer of 1865 and described it to Whitman, giving him, according to the Whitman Archive, the image the elegy needed. The thrush, 'solitary' and 'withdrawn', sings 'by himself', a figure for the poet, and his song will become the 'carol of death' of section 14.

    Edward Renehan, essay for the Vassar College exhibition 'John Burroughs and Walt Whitman'; John Burroughs, Wake-Robin (1871), Project Gutenberg; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  8. song of the bleeding throat § 4

    The 'bleeding throat' recalls the nightingale of classical myth, Philomela, whose tongue was cut out and whose song is grief (Ovid, Metamorphoses 6), and the later fancy of the nightingale singing with its breast against a thorn. Whitman makes the American hermit thrush his singer of sorrow. 'Death's outlet song of life' is a paradox: the song is life's response to death, the release without which the singer 'wouldst surely die'. Addressing the bird as 'dear brother', the speaker identifies with it.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.424-674 (Tereus, Procne and Philomela); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  9. the coffin's journey § 5-6

    Sections 5 and 6 follow the funeral train, which left Washington on 21 April 1865 and reached Springfield, Illinois, on 3 May, travelling some 1,650 miles through cities including Baltimore, Philadelphia, New York, Cleveland, Columbus, Indianapolis and Chicago, with public viewings along the way. Section 5 is one long sentence whose subject and verb ('journeys a coffin') are held back to the end, so the reader travels through the spring landscape before reaching the coffin. Section 6 catalogues the mourning: draped cities, torches, crowds, dirges and tolling bells.

    The Henry Ford, 'Map of the Abraham Lincoln Funeral Train Route, April 21-May 3, 1865'; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  10. grain from its shroud § 5

    The spring wheat has 'uprisen' from 'its shroud in the dark-brown fields', each seed having been buried like a body. The image echoes St Paul's figure for resurrection, the seed that must die to live (1 Corinthians 15:36-37), and Christ's 'Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die' (John 12:24). Whitman places this natural renewal along the coffin's path without stating a doctrine: the land's revival accompanies the dead man's burial.

    The Bible, King James Version, John 12:24; The Bible, King James Version, 1 Corinthians 15:36; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  11. inloop'd flags, crape, flambeaus § 6

    'Inloop'd' flags are flags gathered up into loops or festoons, as they were hung for mourning, rather than flying free. The States appear 'as of crape-veil'd women', crape being the black crinkled fabric of mourning dress. 'Flambeaus' (from French flambeau) are flaming torches, carried in the night processions. RPO glosses 'flambeaus' as torches and 'palings' (section 3) as fence pickets.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' (1891-92 text, with notes); Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'crape' and 'flambeau'; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  12. flowers for all the dead § 7

    Section 7, enclosed in parentheses, generalises the gift: 'Nor for you, for one alone', but for all the dead and for 'sane and sacred death'. The armfuls of roses, 'early lilies' and lilac recall Anchises' lament for the young Marcellus in Aeneid 6, 'manibus date lilia plenis' ('give lilies with full hands'), one of the most quoted passages of classical mourning. The parenthesis marks a turn from grief for one man toward an acceptance of death as such, which the carol of section 14 will complete.

    Virgil, Aeneid 6.883-6 (The Latin Library); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  13. the western orb § 8

    Section 8 returns to the star, Venus, and to the weeks before the assassination when the speaker walked at night and felt the star 'had something to tell'. In retrospect its sinking 'full of woe' foreshadowed the death. Whitman quoted the line 'As we walk'd in silence the transparent shadowy night' (slightly altered) in an 1879 diary entry recalling those walks by the Potomac. The long anaphoric 'As ... As ...' sentence ends with the star 'Concluded, dropt in the night, and was gone'.

    Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg, 'Night of March 18, '79'; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  14. sea-winds § 10

    Section 10 asks how to adorn the grave. The answer is not incense but the winds from the Atlantic and Pacific meeting over the prairies of the Midwest, where Lincoln is buried, mingled with 'the breath of my chant'. The image makes the continent itself the mourner and joins East and West, as the war had preserved North and South. It anticipates the national panoramas of sections 11 and 12.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  15. Fourth-month; the pictures § 11

    Instead of hanging the burial chamber with mourning, the speaker will hang it with 'pictures' of growing spring, farms, rivers, cities and workmen going home. 'Fourth-month' is the Quaker way of naming April by number rather than a pagan name; Whitman had Quaker connections through his family and admired the Quaker preacher Elias Hicks, and he uses such forms elsewhere. The catalogue of sections 11 and 12, with 'My own Manhattan', 'Ohio's shores and flashing Missouri' and the prairies, is Whitman's characteristic method, here offered as a memorial to the man who held the land together.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  16. the cloud and the black trail § 14

    Section 14 opens with a long catalogue of ordinary life, farmers, ships, households 'with its meals and minutia of daily usages', and then 'Appear'd the cloud, appear'd the long black trail' falling 'upon them all'. The cloud recalls section 2's 'harsh surrounding cloud' and section 6's 'great cloud darkening the land': the news of the assassination spreading over the country. From it comes 'the sacred knowledge of death'.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  17. comrades three § 14

    The speaker flees to the swamp with 'the knowledge of death' on one side and 'the thought of death' on the other, holding their hands like comrades. The bird receives 'us comrades three'. The personification is distinctive: death is not an enemy but companionship, and 'comrade' is one of Whitman's key words for male friendship and solidarity (compare the 'Calamus' poems). The three walk together through the remaining sections until he unlooses their hands in section 16.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  18. tally § 14-16

    A tally was a notched stick split in two, each party keeping a half; the halves 'tallied' when they matched (Latin talea, a cutting or stick). Hence to tally is to correspond or agree. Whitman uses the word repeatedly at the poem's climax: 'the voice of my spirit tallied the song of the bird', 'the tally of my soul', 'the tallying song of my soul'. The poet's song answers and matches the thrush's, so that the carol that follows is both the bird's song and the poet's translation of it.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'tally'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'tally', n.1 and v.; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  19. the carol of death § 14

    The 'carol' is the poem's emotional and philosophical centre. It praises 'the fathomless universe' for life and love and then for 'the sure-enwinding arms of cool-enfolding death', which arrives 'to all, to each'. Death is addressed as 'Dark mother', 'strong deliveress', a sea in which the dead are 'Laved in the flood of thy bliss'. The welcoming of death recalls Whitman's 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking', where the sea whispers 'death'. The carol has been set by composers, notably in Paul Hindemith's requiem When Lilacs Last in the Door-yard Bloom'd (1946).

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  20. Dark mother § 14

    Whitman feminises death as a 'Dark mother ... with soft feet', the counterpart to the earth-mother of his other poems, and calls her 'strong deliveress', one who delivers and frees. The maternal figure makes death a return rather than an ending. The speaker claims to offer 'a chant of fullest welcome' that no one has sung before, rejecting the dread of traditional elegy.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]

  21. 'high-spread shy' (textual) § 14

    The archive's text reads 'the high-spread shy are fitting', which makes no sense. The 1891-92 Leaves of Grass, the edition this text follows, reads 'the high-spread sky', as in standard editions. The error is an old transcription slip (h for k). Students quoting the line should use 'sky'. The line belongs to the carol's list of the 'serenades' and 'adornments' offered to death: the open landscape, the sky, the fields and the 'huge and thoughtful night', which the speaker presents as fitting gifts.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' (1891-92 text, with notes); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, Leaves of Grass (1891-92)

  22. the vision of the war dead § 15

    'Askant' means sideways, obliquely (RPO). The speaker's sight, 'unclosed', sees battle-flags shot through and torn, 'battle-corpses, myriads of them', 'white skeletons of young men'. Whitman had seen the war's wounded closely as a volunteer visitor in Washington hospitals from 1862, recorded in Memoranda During the War and Specimen Days. The vision widens the elegy from Lincoln to the war's hundreds of thousands of dead. Its conclusion reverses expectation: the dead 'suffer'd not'; it is the living, mothers, wives, children, comrades, who suffer.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' (1891-92 text, with notes); Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  23. The ending § 16

    Section 16 is a long leave-taking with the participle 'Passing' repeated: the speaker leaves the lilac 'blooming, returning with spring', ceases his song to the star, and lets go his comrades' hands. Yet he will keep everything, 'retrievements out of the night'. The final lines name the dead man's quality most directly, 'the sweetest, wisest soul of all my days and lands', and braid 'Lilac and star and bird' with 'the chant of my soul'. The closing image returns to the swamp's pines and cedars, ending in the bird's dim haunt rather than in heaven.

    Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd' [1865]
