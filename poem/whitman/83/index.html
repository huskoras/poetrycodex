Nature

Crossing Brooklyn Ferry

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Nature

1
Flood-tide below me! I see you face to face!
Clouds of the west--sun there half an hour high--I see you also face
to face.
Crowds of men and women attired in the usual costumes, how curious
you are to me!
On the ferry-boats the hundreds and hundreds that cross, returning
home, are more curious to me than you suppose,
And you that shall cross from shore to shore years hence are more
to me, and more in my meditations, than you might suppose.
2
The impalpable sustenance of me from all things at all hours of the day,
The simple, compact, well-join’d scheme, myself disintegrated, every
one disintegrated yet part of the scheme,
The similitudes of the past and those of the future,
The glories strung like beads on my smallest sights and hearings, on
the walk in the street and the passage over the river,
The current rushing so swiftly and swimming with me far away,
The others that are to follow me, the ties between me and them,
The certainty of others, the life, love, sight, hearing of others.
Others will enter the gates of the ferry and cross from shore to shore,
Others will watch the run of the flood-tide,
Others will see the shipping of Manhattan north and west, and the
heights of Brooklyn to the south and east,
Others will see the islands large and small;
Fifty years hence, others will see them as they cross, the sun half
an hour high,
A hundred years hence, or ever so many hundred years hence, others
will see them,
Will enjoy the sunset, the pouring-in of the flood-tide, the
falling-back to the sea of the ebb-tide.
3
It avails not, time nor place--distance avails not,
I am with you, you men and women of a generation, or ever so many
generations hence,
Just as you feel when you look on the river and sky, so I felt,
Just as any of you is one of a living crowd, I was one of a crowd,
Just as you are refresh’d by the gladness of the river and the
bright flow, I was refresh’d,
Just as you stand and lean on the rail, yet hurry with the swift
current, I stood yet was hurried,
Just as you look on the numberless masts of ships and the
thick-stemm’d pipes of steamboats, I look’d.
I too many and many a time cross’d the river of old,
Watched the Twelfth-month sea-gulls, saw them high in the air
floating with motionless wings, oscillating their bodies,
Saw how the glistening yellow lit up parts of their bodies and left
the rest in strong shadow,
Saw the slow-wheeling circles and the gradual edging toward the south,
Saw the reflection of the summer sky in the water,
Had my eyes dazzled by the shimmering track of beams,
Look’d at the fine centrifugal spokes of light round the shape of my
head in the sunlit water,
Look’d on the haze on the hills southward and south-westward,
Look’d on the vapor as it flew in fleeces tinged with violet,
Look’d toward the lower bay to notice the vessels arriving,
Saw their approach, saw aboard those that were near me,
Saw the white sails of schooners and sloops, saw the ships at anchor,
The sailors at work in the rigging or out astride the spars,
The round masts, the swinging motion of the hulls, the slender
serpentine pennants,
The large and small steamers in motion, the pilots in their pilothouses,
The white wake left by the passage, the quick tremulous whirl of the wheels,
The flags of all nations, the falling of them at sunset,
The scallop-edged waves in the twilight, the ladled cups, the
frolic-some crests and glistening,
The stretch afar growing dimmer and dimmer, the gray walls of the
granite storehouses by the docks,
On the river the shadowy group, the big steam-tug closely flank’d on
each side by the barges, the hay-boat, the belated lighter,
On the neighboring shore the fires from the foundry chimneys burning
high and glaringly into the night,
Casting their flicker of black contrasted with wild red and yellow
light over the tops of houses, and down into the clefts of streets.
4
These and all else were to me the same as they are to you,
I loved well those cities, loved well the stately and rapid river,
The men and women I saw were all near to me,
Others the same--others who look back on me because I look’d forward
to them,
(The time will come, though I stop here to-day and to-night.)
5
What is it then between us?
What is the count of the scores or hundreds of years between us?
Whatever it is, it avails not--distance avails not, and place avails not,
I too lived, Brooklyn of ample hills was mine,
I too walk’d the streets of Manhattan island, and bathed in the
waters around it,
I too felt the curious abrupt questionings stir within me,
In the day among crowds of people sometimes they came upon me,
In my walks home late at night or as I lay in my bed they came upon me,
I too had been struck from the float forever held in solution,
I too had receiv’d identity by my body,
That I was I knew was of my body, and what I should be I knew I
should be of my body.
6
It is not upon you alone the dark patches fall,
The dark threw its patches down upon me also,
The best I had done seem’d to me blank and suspicious,
My great thoughts as I supposed them, were they not in reality meagre?
Nor is it you alone who know what it is to be evil,
I am he who knew what it was to be evil,
I too knitted the old knot of contrariety,
Blabb’d, blush’d, resented, lied, stole, grudg’d,
Had guile, anger, lust, hot wishes I dared not speak,
Was wayward, vain, greedy, shallow, sly, cowardly, malignant,
The wolf, the snake, the hog, not wanting in me.
The cheating look, the frivolous word, the adulterous wish, not wanting,
Refusals, hates, postponements, meanness, laziness, none of these wanting,
Was one with the rest, the days and haps of the rest,
Was call’d by my nighest name by clear loud voices of young men as
they saw me approaching or passing,
Felt their arms on my neck as I stood, or the negligent leaning of
their flesh against me as I sat,
Saw many I loved in the street or ferry-boat or public assembly, yet
never told them a word,
Lived the same life with the rest, the same old laughing, gnawing, sleeping,
Play’d the part that still looks back on the actor or actress,
The same old role, the role that is what we make it, as great as we like,
Or as small as we like, or both great and small.
7
Closer yet I approach you,
What thought you have of me now, I had as much of you--I laid in my
stores in advance,
I consider’d long and seriously of you before you were born.
Who was to know what should come home to me?
Who knows but I am enjoying this?
Who knows, for all the distance, but I am as good as looking at you
now, for all you cannot see me?
8
Ah, what can ever be more stately and admirable to me than
mast-hemm’d Manhattan?
River and sunset and scallop-edg’d waves of flood-tide?
The sea-gulls oscillating their bodies, the hay-boat in the
twilight, and the belated lighter?
What gods can exceed these that clasp me by the hand, and with voices I
love call me promptly and loudly by my nighest name as approach?
What is more subtle than this which ties me to the woman or man that
looks in my face?
Which fuses me into you now, and pours my meaning into you?
We understand then do we not?
What I promis’d without mentioning it, have you not accepted?
What the study could not teach--what the preaching could not
accomplish is accomplish’d, is it not?
9
Flow on, river! flow with the flood-tide, and ebb with the ebb-tide!
Frolic on, crested and scallop-edg’d waves!
Gorgeous clouds of the sunset! drench with your splendor me, or the
men and women generations after me!
Cross from shore to shore, countless crowds of passengers!
Stand up, tall masts of Mannahatta! stand up, beautiful hills of Brooklyn!
Throb, baffled and curious brain! throw out questions and answers!
Suspend here and everywhere, eternal float of solution!
Gaze, loving and thirsting eyes, in the house or street or public assembly!
Sound out, voices of young men! loudly and musically call me by my
nighest name!
Live, old life! play the part that looks back on the actor or actress!
Play the old role, the role that is great or small according as one
makes it!
Consider, you who peruse me, whether I may not in unknown ways be
looking upon you;
Be firm, rail over the river, to support those who lean idly, yet
haste with the hasting current;
Fly on, sea-birds! fly sideways, or wheel in large circles high in the air;
Receive the summer sky, you water, and faithfully hold it till all
downcast eyes have time to take it from you!
Diverge, fine spokes of light, from the shape of my head, or any
one’s head, in the sunlit water!
Come on, ships from the lower bay! pass up or down, white-sail’d
schooners, sloops, lighters!
Flaunt away, flags of all nations! be duly lower’d at sunset!
Burn high your fires, foundry chimneys! cast black shadows at
nightfall! cast red and yellow light over the tops of the houses!
Appearances, now or henceforth, indicate what you are,
You necessary film, continue to envelop the soul,
About my body for me, and your body for you, be hung our divinest aromas,
Thrive, cities--bring your freight, bring your shows, ample and
sufficient rivers,
Expand, being than which none else is perhaps more spiritual,
Keep your places, objects than which none else is more lasting.
You have waited, you always wait, you dumb, beautiful ministers,
We receive you with free sense at last, and are insatiate henceforward,
Not you any more shall be able to foil us, or withhold yourselves from us,
We use you, and do not cast you aside--we plant you permanently within us,
We fathom you not--we love you--there is perfection in you also,
You furnish your parts toward eternity,
Great or small, you furnish your parts toward the soul.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, occasion and history sect. 1

    Before the Brooklyn Bridge opened (1883), commuters crossed the East River by ferry; the Fulton Ferry ran from the foot of Fulton Street in Brooklyn to Fulton Street in Manhattan. Whitman, who lived in Brooklyn and edited the Brooklyn Eagle in the 1840s, rode it constantly and later recalled his fondness for the ferries in Specimen Days. The poem first appeared in the second edition of Leaves of Grass (1856) as 'Sun-Down Poem', was renamed 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry' in 1860, and was revised in later editions until the final text of 1881, the nine-section form printed here. Critics generally rank it among Whitman's greatest shorter poems, alongside 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking' and 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd'.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, versions of "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry" (1856 "Sun-Down Poem" to 1881); J. R. LeMaster and Donald D. Kummings, eds., Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia (1998), "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry"; Walt Whitman, Specimen Days (1882), recollections of New York and its ferries; Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry", Wikipedia (summary of publication history and criticism)

  2. face to face sect. 1

    In 1 Corinthians 13:12 Paul contrasts present knowledge, 'through a glass, darkly', with future vision 'face to face'. Whitman claims that vision now, in the ordinary world: he sees the flood-tide and the sunset clouds 'face to face'. The phrase sets the poem's central wager: that direct, bodily perception of things can join people across time. The poem ends by calling objects 'dumb, beautiful ministers', a religious image that answers the biblical echo here.

    Bible (KJV), 1 Corinthians 13:12; James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  3. flood-tide and ebb-tide sect. 2

    The flood-tide is the rising tide that flows in from the sea; the ebb-tide drains back out. The East River is not a river but a tidal strait, so its current reverses with the tide. Whitman makes this daily rhythm an emblem of continuity: others 'will watch the run of the flood-tide' and the 'falling-back to the sea of the ebb-tide'. In section 9 he commands it: 'flow with the flood-tide, and ebb with the ebb-tide!' Coffman notes how the closing section repeats the earlier catalogue as imperatives, turning observation into blessing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flood-tide, n.'; Stanley K. Coffman, "'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': A Note on the Catalogue Technique in Whitman's Poetry", Modern Philology 51 (1954); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  4. The address to future readers sect. 1

    From the first section Whitman addresses 'you that shall cross ... years hence', and later 'you who peruse me'. The reader is imagined as a future commuter, then as a reader of this very page. The poem's tenses shift: Whitman moves from the present to the past ('I too ... cross'd the river of old') as if he were already dead, speaking back to us from our future, a shift Gilbert examines. The effect is to collapse the time between writer and reader, a theme Whitman returns to in 'Full of Life Now' and 'So Long!'.

    Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987); James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); J. R. LeMaster and Donald D. Kummings, eds., Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia (1998), "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry"

  5. the scheme sect. 2

    Section 2 opens with a paradox: the 'scheme' (plan, design) of existence is simple and well-joined, yet every self in it is 'disintegrated', that is, not whole in itself but distributed through everything. The 'impalpable sustenance' Whitman draws 'from all things' is a spiritual nourishment that cannot be touched. The idea resembles Emerson's view that each person shares in a universal 'Over-Soul', but Whitman grounds it in the senses: 'the walk in the street and the passage over the river'.

    R. W. Emerson, "The Over-Soul", Essays: First Series (1841); Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  6. similitudes sect. 2

    'Similitude' (Latin similitudo, likeness) means resemblance, or an image that shows a likeness. Whitman's argument depends on sameness of experience: the tide, the gulls, the light and the crowds will be much the same for later generations, so their experience will repeat his. Section 3's repeated 'Just as you ... so I' builds this likeness into the syntax. Coffman shows how the catalogues of sections 3 and 9 mirror one another to embody it.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'similitude, n.'; Stanley K. Coffman, "'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': A Note on the Catalogue Technique in Whitman's Poetry", Modern Philology 51 (1954)

  7. It avails not sect. 3

    'Avail' means to be of use or force. Whitman repeats the phrase in sections 3 and 5 ('distance avails not, and place avails not') as a kind of refrain, denying that the 'scores or hundreds of years between us' matter. Critics differ on how far the claim is earned: Gargano describes the poem as moving through a crisis (the 'dark patches' of section 6) to a sense of the 'everlasting moment'; Gilbert reads the poem's shifts of grammar as moving from anxiety to a sense of power over time.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'avail, v.'; James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987)

  8. Twelfth-month sect. 3

    Quakers avoided month-names derived from pagan gods and called the months by number, so December is 'Twelfth-month'. Whitman's family admired the Quaker preacher Elias Hicks, and he heard Hicks speak as a boy, an experience he wrote about in November Boughs; he uses similar forms elsewhere ('the Ninth-month midnight' in 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'). The gulls 'floating with motionless wings, oscillating their bodies' are observed with naturalist precision; the 'gradual edging toward the south' suggests winter movement. The poem mixes seasons (winter gulls, 'summer sky'), compiling many crossings into one.

    Walt Whitman, "Elias Hicks", in November Boughs (1888); Walt Whitman, "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking", Leaves of Grass (1881); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  9. spokes of light round the shape of my head sect. 3

    When one looks at one's shadow on sunlit, rippling water, bright rays seem to stream outward from the head of the shadow (an optical effect sometimes called heiligenschein or 'holy light'). 'Centrifugal' means moving outward from a centre. The image is a democratic halo: in section 9 Whitman tells the spokes of light to diverge 'from the shape of my head, or any one's head', so that every passenger is haloed. Critics read it as a figure for the poem's sanctification of ordinary people.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'centrifugal, adj.'; Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text; J. R. LeMaster and Donald D. Kummings, eds., Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia (1998), "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry"

  10. lighter sect. 3

    A lighter (from Dutch lichter, from lichten, to unload, lighten) is a barge for transferring cargo between ships and wharves. The catalogue of section 3 is a precise picture of the busy port of New York in the 1850s: schooners and sloops, steamers with their 'pilothouses', the steam-tug 'closely flank'd on each side by the barges', the hay-boat, the 'granite storehouses by the docks', and the foundry fires of the industrial shore. Coffman shows how the same items return in section 8 and as commands in section 9.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'lighter, n.1'; Stanley K. Coffman, "'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': A Note on the Catalogue Technique in Whitman's Poetry", Modern Philology 51 (1954)

  11. flags of all nations, lowered at sunset sect. 3

    New York was the nation's greatest port, and ships 'of all nations' lay at anchor. Naval and merchant custom was to strike (lower) the ensign at sunset, which Whitman records as 'the falling of them at sunset' and later commands: 'be duly lower'd at sunset!' The internationalism of the image fits the poem's widening embrace, from the river crowd to future generations and, implicitly, to all peoples.

    Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text; J. R. LeMaster and Donald D. Kummings, eds., Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia (1998), "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry"

  12. struck from the float ... held in solution sect. 5

    In chemistry a substance 'held in solution' is dissolved in a liquid; it can be 'struck' or precipitated out as a solid. Whitman imagines a cosmic 'float' (a floating mass, a fluid) from which each person is struck to receive 'identity by my body'. The image explains how the self is both separate and part of the whole (compare the 'scheme' of section 2). In section 9 he calls on it: 'Suspend here and everywhere, eternal float of solution!' Critics have read the passage as Whitman's version of the soul's pre-existence, physicalised; others stress its materialism, since identity comes only 'of my body'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'solution, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'float, n.'; Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987)

  13. curious abrupt questionings sect. 5

    'Curious' in Whitman often means both 'inquisitive' and 'strange'. The 'abrupt questionings' are unanswerable thoughts about identity and death. By confessing them ('I too felt'), Whitman makes his inner life another shared experience. In section 9 he addresses his own mind: 'Throb, baffled and curious brain! throw out questions and answers!', turning anxiety into energy. Gilbert's reading follows this shift from anxiety to power in the poem's grammar.

    Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  14. the dark patches sect. 6

    The 'dark patches' are moments of depression and moral failure. Whitman lists his vices with deliberate frankness ('Blabb'd, blush'd, resented, lied, stole, grudg'd'), and admits that 'The wolf, the snake, the hog' were 'not wanting' in him. The point is not repentance but fellowship: shared weakness binds as surely as shared joy. Gargano sees this section as the poem's crisis; some critics also read the lines about young men who call him by his 'nighest name' and lean against him, and the many he loved but 'never told', as discreet references to Whitman's homosexual desire, anticipating the 'Calamus' poems of 1860.

    James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); J. R. LeMaster and Donald D. Kummings, eds., Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia (1998), "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry"

  15. knot of contrariety sect. 6

    'Contrariety' (Latin contrarietas) means the state of being contrary: opposition, inconsistency, perverseness. To 'knit the old knot' is to tie oneself again in the familiar tangle of contradiction, a homely image (knitting) for moral confusion. Whitman's acceptance of his own contradictions recalls 'Song of Myself' ('Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself'). The confession of section 6 is thus not a lapse in the poem's optimism but part of its argument: shared imperfection joins people as much as shared joy.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'contrariety, n.'; Walt Whitman, "Song of Myself", sect. 51

  16. nighest name sect. 6

    'Nigh' means near; 'nighest name' is the most intimate name, the given name (Walt) used by friends rather than a formal surname. Whitman, who was known as 'Walt', is recalled as being greeted familiarly by working men, ferry hands and omnibus drivers. The phrase recurs in sections 6, 8 and 9, becoming a refrain of intimacy. In section 8 he even sets these familiar voices above the gods: 'What gods can exceed these that clasp me by the hand'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'nigh, adj.'; Walt Whitman, Specimen Days (1882), recollections of New York and its ferries; Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  17. the part that looks back on the actor sect. 6

    The old idea of the world as a stage (theatrum mundi; 'All the world's a stage', As You Like It 2.7) is turned in an unusual direction: the 'part' 'looks back on the actor or actress', as if the role observes and judges its player, or as if what we perform becomes what we are. 'The role that is what we make it, as great as we like, / Or as small' stresses freedom: the significance of a life depends on how it is played. The line returns in section 9 as a command.

    Shakespeare, As You Like It 2.7.139-66; Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text; Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987)

  18. Closer yet I approach you sect. 7

    Whitman 'laid in my stores in advance', like a provident traveller, by imagining his readers before their birth. 'Who knows but I am enjoying this?' playfully suggests that the dead poet may be present at the reading. This is the climax of the poem's intimacy and has been read both as a confident prophecy of poetic immortality and as a wistful, half-joking hope. The address recalls the close of 'Song of Myself', where he tells the reader to look for him 'under your boot-soles'.

    Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987); James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999)

  19. Manhattan / Mannahatta sect. 8

    'Mast-hemm'd' pictures the island bordered on all sides by the masts of ships at its wharves. Whitman preferred the Lenape-derived form 'Mannahatta' (the name recorded in early Dutch sources), which he praised in a poem of that title (1860) as a word 'liquid, sane, unruly, musical'; he saw in it an authentic American name. The pairing of 'tall masts of Mannahatta' and 'beautiful hills of Brooklyn' sets the two shores the ferry joins.

    Walt Whitman, "Mannahatta", Leaves of Grass (1860; 1881); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  20. What the study could not teach sect. 8

    'The study' is book learning, 'the preaching' organised religion. Whitman's rhetorical question ('is accomplish'd, is it not?') claims that the poem itself has made the connection that education and the pulpit only describe. The confidence, and the coy reference to what he 'promis'd without mentioning it', are typical of his conception of the poem as an act that changes the reader rather than a statement of doctrine. Compare 'Song of Myself' sect. 2: 'You shall no longer take things at second or third hand'.

    Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987); Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); Walt Whitman, "Song of Myself", sect. 2

  21. Section 9: the catalogue of imperatives sect. 9

    The final section repeats the earlier catalogue almost item by item (tide, waves, clouds, crowds, masts, hills, gulls, summer sky in the water, spokes of light, schooners, flags, foundry fires), now in the imperative: 'Flow on', 'Frolic on', 'Burn high'. Coffman shows that this repetition, with its new confident tone, is the poem's main structural device. The commands are not orders but a blessing on the world's continuance, and they enact the poem's claim that what the poet saw will be seen again. The long lines and anaphora are typical of Whitman's free verse, often compared with the parallelism of the English Bible.

    Stanley K. Coffman, "'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': A Note on the Catalogue Technique in Whitman's Poetry", Modern Philology 51 (1954); James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  22. you who peruse me sect. 9

    'Peruse' means to read carefully. By writing 'peruse me' rather than 'peruse this', Whitman makes the book his body, as in 'So Long!' ('Camerado, this is no book, / Who touches this touches a man'). He asks the reader to consider 'whether I may not in unknown ways be looking upon you', the most direct of his claims to be present across time. Gilbert reads the poem's shifts of address and tense as the means by which this presence is made convincing.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'peruse, v.'; Roger Gilbert, "From Anxiety to Power: Grammar and Crisis in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry'", Nineteenth-Century Literature 42 (1987); Walt Whitman, "So Long!", Leaves of Grass (1881)

  23. necessary film sect. 9

    In much Platonic and Christian thought the body and the visible world are veils hiding spiritual reality. Whitman accepts the veil: 'Appearances ... indicate what you are', and the 'film' must 'continue to envelop the soul'. The body is 'hung' with 'our divinest aromas'. Critics read this as Whitman's transcendentalism with a materialist turn: like Emerson, he holds that natural facts are signs of spiritual facts, but unlike Emerson he insists on the value of the sign itself.

    R. W. Emerson, Nature (1836), ch. 4 "Language"; Jerome Loving, Walt Whitman: The Song of Himself (University of California Press, 1999); Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass, ed. Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett (Norton Critical Edition, 1973), annotated text

  24. dumb, beautiful ministers sect. 9

    'Ministers' carries both senses: servants and clergy. Things are 'dumb' because they cannot speak, yet they serve the soul and 'furnish your parts toward eternity'. The closing lines move from 'I' to 'We', a communal voice of humanity that will 'plant you permanently within us'. The poem thus ends where Emerson's Nature begins, with the world as a sacred text, but Whitman's emphasis is on love ('We fathom you not--we love you'). Critics often see this as the poem's resolution: the barrier of time is overcome by shared perception of things.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'minister, n.'; R. W. Emerson, Nature (1836), ch. 4 "Language"; James W. Gargano, "Technique in 'Crossing Brooklyn Ferry': The Everlasting Moment", JEGP 62 (1963)
