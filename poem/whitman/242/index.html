A Noiseless Patient Spider

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Religion

A noiseless patient spider,
I mark’d where on a little promontory it stood isolated,
Mark’d how to explore the vacant vast surrounding,
It launch’d forth filament, filament, filament out of itself,
Ever unreeling them, ever tirelessly speeding them.
And you O my soul where you stand,
Surrounded, detached, in measureless oceans of space,
Ceaselessly musing, venturing, throwing, seeking the spheres to
connect them,
Till the bridge you will need be form’d, till the ductile anchor hold,
Till the gossamer thread you fling catch somewhere, O my soul.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its versions l. 1

    The earliest version is a rough notebook passage (usually dated 1862-63) beginning with the Soul 'reaching, throwing out for love', in which the spider is only a simile and the subject is a lonely search for love that ends in a glimpse of eyes turning wishfully in the street. Whitman then reworked it as the third section of 'Whispers of Heavenly Death' in The Broadway, a London magazine, in October 1868; it gained its own title in Passage to India (1871) and passed, with small changes of wording and punctuation, into the 1881 Leaves of Grass. The revision matters for interpretation: the published poem turns an erotic and social longing into a metaphysical one, the soul seeking 'the spheres' rather than a lover. Paul Diehl traced these stages in detail, and the Classroom Electric edition prints the versions side by side.

    Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared); Paul Diehl, '"A Noiseless Patient Spider": Whitman's Beauty - Blood and Brain', Walt Whitman Quarterly Review 6 (1989); The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price (texts of the 1868, 1871 and 1881 printings)

  2. noiseless patient l. 1

    Spiders have a long double life in poetry and scripture. In Job 8:14 the hypocrite's 'trust shall be a spider's web', an image of fragility, and a long tradition makes the spider a figure for cunning or entrapment. Whitman discards these associations. His spider is 'noiseless' and 'patient', qualities that make it a model of humble, unfinished effort; the web is not a trap but a means of reaching across emptiness. The inversion is typical of Whitman, who habitually finds dignity in small, overlooked creatures (compare the 'leaf of grass' and the 'pismire' of Song of Myself, section 31). The Walt Whitman Archive's encyclopedia entry calls the poem an illustration of Emerson's dictum that nature is a symbol of spirit, and Wilton Eckley has argued for Emerson's influence on Whitman's spider-soul figure.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Job 8:14; The Walt Whitman Archive, 'Noiseless Patient Spider, A' (1868), Walt Whitman: An Encyclopedia; Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  3. mark'd l. 2

    To 'mark' here is to watch with deliberate attention, an older literary sense the OED records alongside the commoner 'to put a mark on'. The first stanza is entirely an act of observation, past-tense and precise ('where', 'how'), while the second stanza moves into the present tense of address ('where you stand'). The apostrophe in 'mark’d', 'launch’d' and 'form’d' shows that the -ed is not to be pronounced as a separate syllable; Whitman used this spelling consistently, although his verse is not metrical, because he cared about the spoken rhythm of each line. The repetition 'I mark’d ... Mark’d' links the two lines and slows the reader to the spider's pace.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mark, v.'; The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price (texts of the 1868, 1871 and 1881 printings)

  4. promontory l. 2

    A promontory is high land projecting into water (Latin promunturium). The word is grand and geographical, and Whitman deliberately sets it beside 'little': the spider's perch is minute, but from the spider's point of view it is a cliff-edge above a void. The image prepares for the second stanza's 'measureless oceans of space', where the soul too stands on an edge. The same word appears in the notebook draft ('from some little promontory'), so it belongs to the poem's oldest layer. The scale-shift between the tiny and the cosmic is the poem's central method: an exact natural observation is enlarged, step by step, into a statement about the soul's place in the universe.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'promontory'; Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared)

  5. isolation l. 2

    'Isolated' derives (through Italian isolato) from Latin insula, 'island', which suits the poem's maritime imagery of promontories, oceans and anchors. Isolation is the condition both halves of the poem share: the spider 'stood isolated', the soul stands 'Surrounded, detached'. Critics have often read the poem biographically, linking it to Whitman's loneliness in Washington during and after the Civil War and to the unanswered longing of the notebook draft, though the printed poem generalises the feeling. Its interest lies in the response to isolation, not in lament: neither spider nor soul waits passively, both send out lines in the hope that one will hold.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'isolated, adj.'; Paul Diehl, '"A Noiseless Patient Spider": Whitman's Beauty - Blood and Brain', Walt Whitman Quarterly Review 6 (1989); Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared)

  6. vacant vast surrounding l. 3

    The phrase is syntactically unusual: 'surrounding' functions as a noun ('that which surrounds'), qualified by two alliterating adjectives. The alliteration on v gives the emptiness a kind of weight, and 'vacant' (Latin vacare, to be empty) anticipates the 'measureless oceans of space' of l. 7. The line also states the spider's purpose: it is not simply spinning but trying 'to explore' the space, a word of voyaging and discovery that links the spider to the explorers celebrated elsewhere in Passage to India, the volume in which the poem was first titled.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'surrounding, n.'; Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  7. filament, filament, filament l. 4

    A filament is a fine thread (late Latin filamentum, from filum). The word repeated three times is the poem's most famous effect: the sound itself imitates the steady, unbroken production of silk, and the line lengthens as it goes, like the thread. Whitman's notebook draft already speaks of 'filament after filament', so the device was refined rather than invented in revision. 'Out of itself' is important: the spider's line is spun from its own body, as the soul's reaching comes from within, and critics have also read it as a figure for the poet's own work, words spun out of the self in hope of reaching a reader.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'filament'; Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared); Paul Diehl, '"A Noiseless Patient Spider": Whitman's Beauty - Blood and Brain', Walt Whitman Quarterly Review 6 (1989)

  8. unreeling ... speeding l. 5

    'Unreeling' borrows from spinning and from fishing or sailing (paying out line from a reel). With 'ever ... ever' and 'tirelessly', the line insists on unbroken continuity, and the participles keep the action open-ended: the stanza ends without any thread catching. That incompleteness is structural. The first stanza shows the effort; only the second stanza, applied to the soul, speaks of the hoped-for result (the bridge, the anchor, the catch), and even there in the future-conditional 'Till ... will need'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unreel, v.'; Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  9. O my soul l. 6

    The second stanza opens with an apostrophe to the soul, a habit of Whitman's from 'I believe in you my soul' (Song of Myself, section 5) onwards. The two five-line stanzas form a strict analogy: spider and soul, promontory and 'where you stand', vacant space and 'measureless oceans', filaments and 'gossamer thread'. The structure is close to the emblem or the epic simile, but Whitman reverses the usual order by leaving the comparison unstated until the turn. The 'O my soul' is repeated at the end of the poem, framing the stanza as a single extended address.

    Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'; The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price (texts of the 1868, 1871 and 1881 printings)

  10. Surrounded, detached l. 7

    The paradox is that the soul is 'surrounded' (hemmed in by immensity) and 'detached' (attached to nothing) at once. According to the comparison of versions in The Classroom Electric, 'detached' first appears in the 1881 text, sharpening the isolation of the earlier printings. The pairing picks up 'stood isolated' and 'vacant vast surrounding' from the first stanza, so that the soul's situation repeats the spider's point for point.

    Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'detached, adj.'

  11. measureless oceans of space l. 7

    The metaphor turns the spider's 'vacant vast' into cosmic space and gives it the character of a sea, unmeasured and uncharted. The marine imagery runs through the stanza (oceans, bridge, anchor) and links the poem with the title-poem of Passage to India (1871), where the soul is urged to 'sail forth' on a voyage of spiritual discovery. The notebook draft spoke of 'fathomless' oceans of love; the printed poem keeps the oceans but redirects them from love to space.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price (texts of the 1868, 1871 and 1881 printings); Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared); Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  12. the spheres ll. 8-9

    In older astronomy the 'spheres' were the concentric crystalline shells carrying the planets and stars; by Whitman's time the word could also mean simply the heavenly bodies, or any sphere of existence. Readers differ on what the soul is reaching for. A religious reading takes the spheres as the divine or the afterlife, which suits the cluster title 'Whispers of Heavenly Death'. A humanist reading takes them as other people, the separate 'spheres' of other selves that the soul longs to join; this has strong support in the notebook draft, where the throwing-out is 'for love' and is and eyes are seen turning wishfully in the street. Some critics see the revision as a sublimation of erotic longing into metaphysical language. The poem allows both, which may be its point.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sphere, n.'; Robert Belasco and Kenneth M. Price, 'Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider"', The Classroom Electric (draft and printed versions compared); Paul Diehl, '"A Noiseless Patient Spider": Whitman's Beauty - Blood and Brain', Walt Whitman Quarterly Review 6 (1989)

  13. Till ... till ... Till ll. 9-10

    The last two lines repeat 'Till' three times, each clause naming a possible success: a bridge formed, an anchor holding, a thread catching. This anaphora is one of Whitman's favourite structures, borrowed in part from the parallelism of the King James Bible. The clauses are subordinate to the participles of l. 8 ('musing, venturing, throwing, seeking'), so the sentence never quite closes: the soul goes on seeking 'till' something holds. The final 'catch somewhere' is strikingly vague, a modest hope that some connection, anywhere, will be made.

    Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'; The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price (texts of the 1868, 1871 and 1881 printings)

  14. ductile anchor l. 9

    Ductile, from Latin ducere, describes metals that can be drawn out into wire, and so fits the spider's silk, stretched thread by thread. The phrase is a bold mixed metaphor: an anchor is usually heavy and rigid, but this one is thin and pliable, a line that holds by its flexibility. 'Anchor' continues the sea imagery of 'oceans', and in Christian tradition the anchor is an emblem of hope (Hebrews 6:19, 'hope ... as an anchor of the soul'), a resonance that fits the poem's spiritual reading.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ductile, adj.'; The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Hebrews 6:19; Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  15. gossamer l. 10

    Gossamer is the filmy cobweb seen drifting on still autumn days or spread over grass; the OED derives the word, probably, from 'goose summer', the mild autumn season when geese were eaten, when such webs are common. Applied to the soul's thread, it stresses fragility: what the soul sends out is as light and breakable as cobweb, yet it is flung across 'measureless' space. The poem ends on 'O my soul', returning to the apostrophe of l. 6.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gossamer, n.'; Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'

  16. Free verse and parallel stanzas ll. 1-10

    The poem has no metre or rhyme, but it is tightly built. Each stanza has five lines (in this printing l. 8 runs over), each moves from position ('where ... it stood' / 'where you stand') to action, and each piles up present participles: 'unreeling', 'speeding' in the first; 'musing, venturing, throwing, seeking' in the second. The participles keep the action in progress, never completed. Whitman's lines are long and cumulative, ruled by breath and syntax rather than by counted stresses, a technique he associated with the rhythms of the English Bible and of oratory.

    Sculley Bradley and Harold W. Blodgett, eds., Leaves of Grass: A Norton Critical Edition (Norton, 1973), notes to 'A Noiseless Patient Spider'; Paul Diehl, '"A Noiseless Patient Spider": Whitman's Beauty - Blood and Brain', Walt Whitman Quarterly Review 6 (1989)
