Death & Dying

O Captain! My Captain!

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Death & Dying

O Captain! my Captain! our fearful trip is done,
The ship has weather’d every rack, the prize we sought is won,
The port is near, the bells I hear, the people all exulting,
While follow eyes the steady keel, the vessel grim and daring;
But O heart! heart! heart!
O the bleeding drops of red,
Where on the deck my Captain lies,
Fallen cold and dead.
O Captain! my Captain! rise up and hear the bells;
Rise up--for you the flag is flung--for you the bugle trills,
For you bouquets and ribbon’d wreaths--for you the shores a-crowding,
For you they call, the swaying mass, their eager faces turning;
Here Captain! dear father!
This arm beneath your head!
It is some dream that on the deck,
You’ve fallen cold and dead.
My Captain does not answer, his lips are pale and still,
My father does not feel my arm, he has no pulse nor will,
The ship is anchor’d safe and sound, its voyage closed and done,
From fearful trip the victor ship comes in with object won;
Exult O shores, and ring O bells!
But I with mournful tread,
Walk the deck my Captain lies,
Fallen cold and dead.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication l. 1

    Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre on the evening of 14 April 1865 and died the next morning, six days after Lee's surrender at Appomattox. Whitman's poem first appeared in the New-York Saturday Press on 4 November 1865 and then in Sequel to Drum-Taps (1865-66); after revision it was placed in 'President Lincoln's Burial Hymn' (1871) and finally in the cluster 'Memories of President Lincoln' in Leaves of Grass (1881). It quickly became his most popular poem and the one most often anthologised in his lifetime, much to his own unease, since its rhyme and regular stanzas are unlike his free verse. The poem never names Lincoln; the 'Captain' is understood by every reader.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Walt Whitman Archive, manuscript description, 'O Captain! My Captain!' (Library of Congress); Library of Congress, 'O Captain! My Captain!' manuscript, item description; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  2. The ship of state ll. 1-2

    The poem develops the ancient image of the state as a ship, found in Alcaeus and in Horace's ode 'O navis' (Odes 1.14), and familiar to Americans from Longfellow's 'The Building of the Ship' (1849), with its apostrophe 'Sail on, O Ship of State!'. The 'fearful trip' is the Civil War (1861-65), now over; the ship has come through every storm and reached port. The conceit lets Whitman fuse public victory with private loss: the voyage succeeds while its captain dies on deck.

    Horace, Odes 1.14; Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 'The Building of the Ship', in The Seaside and the Fireside (1849); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  3. rack l. 2

    In Webster and the OED, rack (from Old Norse) denotes thin, flying clouds driven before the wind, and hence the storm itself; Shakespeare uses it so ('The rack dislimns', Antony and Cleopatra 4.14). The ship 'has weather'd every rack': it has come through every storm. The word may also call up 'wrack', wreck or destruction ('rack and ruin'), and even the instrument of torture, both fitting a nation that has survived four years of war.

    Noah Webster, An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828), s.v. 'rack'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'rack', n. (cloud); Shakespeare, Antony and Cleopatra 4.14 (Folger Shakespeare)

  4. The prize l. 2

    The 'prize' is the victory sought in the war: the restoration of the Union, and by 1865 the abolition of slavery, which Congress had sent to the states as the Thirteenth Amendment in January 1865 (ratified that December). In naval language a 'prize' is also a captured enemy ship, keeping the metaphor consistent. The line's triumph is immediately undercut, as each stanza turns from public rejoicing to the dead captain.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  5. bells and crowds l. 3

    The scene recalls the jubilation in Northern cities when the war ended in April 1865, with bells rung and crowds in the streets. Within days the same bells tolled for Lincoln. The poem holds both meanings: the shore rejoices while the speaker grieves. The 'steady keel' and 'vessel grim and daring' personify the Union as a battle-worn ship watched by the crowds.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  6. Rhyme, metre and refrain st. 1

    Each stanza has four long lines in rough couplets, followed by four short lines that end on a refrain, 'Fallen cold and dead'. The indentation of the short lines, stepping inward on the page, visually narrows the poem towards the dead figure. Rhyme, metre and refrain are rare in Whitman, who usually wrote free verse; he seems to have chosen a popular, song-like form suitable for public mourning. Its popularity, and Whitman's mixed feelings about it, follow from that choice.

    Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Library of Congress, 'O Captain! My Captain!' manuscript, item description; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  7. bleeding drops l. 6

    The exclamation turns from celebration to the body on deck. The 'bleeding drops of red' recall the gunshot wound to Lincoln's head; the image also fuses the president's blood with the blood of the war's dead. The triple 'heart! heart! heart!' before it is a cry of pain that breaks the line, the most emotional moment in the stanza. The colour red is set against the white and blue implied by the flags of the next stanza, and the private wound against the public triumph.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  8. flag, bugle, wreaths ll. 10-11

    The second stanza piles up honours with the repeated 'for you': the flag flung out, the bugle trilling, bouquets, 'ribbon'd wreaths', crowds on the shore. These are signs of a victory welcome, but they are also the trappings of the funeral that followed, when flags and wreaths lined the route of Lincoln's funeral train from Washington to Springfield (21 April-3 May 1865). The speaker begs the captain to 'rise up' and receive them, an appeal that the third stanza answers with silence.

    The Henry Ford, 'Map of the Abraham Lincoln Funeral Train Route, April 21-May 3, 1865'; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)

  9. 'dear father' l. 13

    The speaker shifts from crewman to son, calling the captain 'dear father'. Lincoln was widely known as 'Father Abraham', a name popularised by the 1862 recruiting song 'We Are Coming, Father Abraham' (words by James Sloan Gibbons). The shift makes the national loss a personal bereavement, and the speaker cradles the father's head on his arm. Whitman, who had nursed wounded soldiers in Washington hospitals, often imagined himself as a comrade tending the dying.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg

  10. the arm beneath the head l. 14

    The speaker supports the captain's head as one might a wounded soldier's. Whitman spent much of 1863-65 visiting wounded men in Washington hospitals, and the gesture appears often in his war writing. The image also resembles a pieta, with the dead hero held by a mourner. In the third stanza the father 'does not feel my arm', and the tableau becomes final.

    Walt Whitman, Specimen Days & Collect (1882), Project Gutenberg; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  11. disbelief l. 15

    'It is some dream' records the first stage of grief: refusal to accept what is seen. The speaker has asked the captain to rise and hear the bells; now he tries to treat the death itself as unreal. The third stanza abandons address to the captain and speaks about him in the third person ('My Captain does not answer'), marking the move from denial to acknowledgement.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999); Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865)

  12. public joy, private grief l. 21

    The final stanza separates the two responses the poem has held together. The shores and bells are told to rejoice, since the ship is 'anchor'd safe and sound' with 'object won'; the speaker, 'with mournful tread', keeps walking the deck where the captain lies. The poem thus honours the victory without letting it cancel the loss. The pacing 'tread' also suggests the slow march of a funeral procession.

    Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965); Helen Vendler, 'Poetry and the Mediation of Value: Whitman on Lincoln', Michigan Quarterly Review 39 (2000), 1-18 (Tanner Lecture, 1999)

  13. mournful tread l. 22

    The speaker walks the deck slowly, as a sentry keeps watch or as mourners walk behind a coffin. The image looks to the funeral processions of April and May 1865, in which thousands walked beside Lincoln's coffin in city after city. The poem's last lines, 'Walk the deck my Captain lies, / Fallen cold and dead', return the refrain one final time.

    The Henry Ford, 'Map of the Abraham Lincoln Funeral Train Route, April 21-May 3, 1865'; Walt Whitman Archive, encyclopedia entry 'O Captain! My Captain!' (1865); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York UP, 1965)
