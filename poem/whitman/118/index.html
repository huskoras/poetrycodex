Time & Brevity

When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Time & Brevity

When I heard the learn’d astronomer,
When the proofs, the figures, were ranged in columns before me,
When I was shown the charts and diagrams, to add, divide, and measure them,
When I sitting heard the astronomer where he lectured with much
applause in the lecture-room,
How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,
Till rising and gliding out I wander’d off by myself,
In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,
Look’d up in perfect silence at the stars.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem's publication l. 1

    The poem first appeared in Drum-Taps (1865), Whitman's volume of Civil War poems, though its subject is not the war. From the 1881 edition of Leaves of Grass onward it belongs to the cluster 'By the Roadside', a group of short occasional pieces. It is one of Whitman's shortest and most anthologised poems: a single sentence of eight lines that sets the classroom against the night sky. The text here keeps Whitman's habitual apostrophes (learn'd, wander'd, Look'd), which mark a contracted '-ed'.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price; Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass (1881-82), 'By the Roadside'

  2. learn'd astronomer l. 1

    'Learn'd' (learned, educated) is the conventional epithet for a scholar, and here it carries a mild irony: the man is learned about stars he presents as figures and diagrams. Whitman's apostrophe marks a contracted ending, as in 'wander'd' and 'Look'd' later. The astronomer is not mocked. The poem notes how much applause he receives, but his learning belongs to the lecture-room.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'learned, adj.'

  3. Anaphora and lengthening lines l. 2

    Anaphora, repeating the same word at the start of successive lines, is Whitman's signature device, learned partly from the parallelism of the King James Bible. Lines 1-4 each begin 'When', and each is longer than the last, with the fourth so long it runs over. The syntax holds the main clause back, just as the speaker is held in the lecture. Relief comes only in line 5 ('How soon ...'), and from then on the lines grow shorter and calmer. The form itself contrasts accumulation with release.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price; Gay Wilson Allen, The New Walt Whitman Handbook (New York UP, 1975), on Whitman's parallelism

  4. ranged in columns l. 2

    Proofs and figures 'ranged in columns' are the mathematical working of astronomy laid out on the blackboard or the page. 'Ranged' and 'columns' can also describe troops drawn up in order, a resonance some readers find apt in a poem first printed among Whitman's war poems, though the link is suggestive, not certain. Either way, the image is of disciplined arrangement imposed on the sky.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'range, v.'; The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price

  5. charts and diagrams l. 3

    Charts and diagrams were standard aids in popular scientific lectures. The infinitives 'to add, divide, and measure them' make the listener a calculator, someone handling the stars arithmetically rather than looking at them, and the three verbs give the lecture a dull, mechanical rhythm. The speaker is passive ('I was shown'), receiving demonstrations, in contrast to the active, solitary seeing of the end. The poem never tells us what the diagrams showed.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price

  6. much applause in the lecture-room l. 4

    Public lectures on science, including astronomy, were a popular form of education and entertainment in nineteenth-century America, part of the lyceum movement that also made Emerson's career. The astronomer is a success: the room applauds. The poem does not dispute his competence. Its point is that a social, verbal and quantitative experience of the stars, however admired, is not the same as encountering them.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price; Angela G. Ray, The Lyceum and Public Culture in the Nineteenth-Century United States (Michigan State UP, 2005)

  7. unaccountable l. 5

    Whitman uses the adjective 'unaccountable' where standard usage would want the adverb 'unaccountably', a colloquial freedom typical of his style. The word means 'for no reason that can be explained', and it puns quietly on accounting: after the adding and dividing, the speaker's reaction cannot be reckoned up. 'Tired and sick' is physical: the response comes from the body before any argument.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'unaccountable'; The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price

  8. The turn l. 6

    Although the poem is not a sonnet, it has a pronounced turn, like a volta, as the speaker leaves the hall. The verbs change from passive and static ('heard', 'was shown', 'sitting') to active and fluid ('rising', 'gliding', 'wander'd'). 'By myself' replaces the applauding crowd. The poem's two halves set collective, mediated knowledge against solitary, immediate experience. The moment of leaving is not explained; the body simply rises and goes.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price

  9. mystical moist night-air l. 7

    The alliteration (mystical, moist) and the compound 'night-air' make the outdoors felt, damp and atmospheric, in contrast to the dry figures indoors. 'Mystical' introduces a spiritual register: the night offers something like a religious apprehension that the lecture cannot give. 'From time to time' suggests leisure and intermittent looking, without system, unlike the ordered columns of the lecture. The phrase answers the strict sequence of the opening clauses.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price

  10. Look'd up in perfect silence at the stars l. 8

    After the long, irregular lines of the opening, the last line falls into regular iambic pentameter, the traditional line of English verse. Formal order comes back just when the speaker escapes the lecture's order, so the music of the line enacts the harmony he finds. 'Perfect silence' answers the lecture's speech and applause. The speaker offers no description of the stars and no conclusion: silent looking is the whole response.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, ed. Ed Folsom and Kenneth M. Price; Gay Wilson Allen, The New Walt Whitman Handbook (1975)

  11. Poetry and science l. 5

    The poem belongs to a Romantic tradition of protest against analytical science: Wordsworth's 'We murder to dissect' ('The Tables Turned', 1798), and Keats's complaint in Lamia (1820) that 'cold philosophy' will 'unweave a rainbow'. Yet Whitman was no enemy of science. In 'Song of Myself' he cries 'Hurrah for positive science! long live exact demonstration!' (section 23), and he took a lively interest in astronomy. Many critics therefore read the poem as a criticism of one way of knowing, the second-hand and quantitative, rather than of astronomy itself. The weariness comes from passively receiving figures, not from the stars.

    William Wordsworth, 'The Tables Turned' (Lyrical Ballads, 1798); John Keats, Lamia (1820), II.229-237; Walt Whitman, 'Song of Myself', section 23
