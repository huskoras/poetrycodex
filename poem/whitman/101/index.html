Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking

by Walt Whitman 1819–1892

From Leaves of Grass

Nature

Out of the cradle endlessly rocking,
Out of the mocking-bird’s throat, the musical shuttle,
Out of the Ninth-month midnight,
Over the sterile sands and the fields beyond, where the child
leaving his bed wander’d alone, bareheaded, barefoot,
Down from the shower’d halo,
Up from the mystic play of shadows twining and twisting as if they
were alive,
Out from the patches of briers and blackberries,
From the memories of the bird that chanted to me,
From your memories sad brother, from the fitful risings and fallings I heard,
From under that yellow half-moon late-risen and swollen as if with tears,
From those beginning notes of yearning and love there in the mist,
From the thousand responses of my heart never to cease,
From the myriad thence-arous’d words,
From the word stronger and more delicious than any,
From such as now they start the scene revisiting,
As a flock, twittering, rising, or overhead passing,
Borne hither, ere all eludes me, hurriedly,
A man, yet by these tears a little boy again,
Throwing myself on the sand, confronting the waves,
I, chanter of pains and joys, uniter of here and hereafter,
Taking all hints to use them, but swiftly leaping beyond them,
A reminiscence sing.
Once Paumanok,
When the lilac-scent was in the air and Fifth-month grass was growing,
Up this seashore in some briers,
Two feather’d guests from Alabama, two together,
And their nest, and four light-green eggs spotted with brown,
And every day the he-bird to and fro near at hand,
And every day the she-bird crouch’d on her nest, silent, with bright eyes,
And every day I, a curious boy, never too close, never disturbing
them,
Cautiously peering, absorbing, translating.
Shine! shine! shine!
Pour down your warmth, great sun.’
While we bask, we two together.
Two together!
Winds blow south, or winds blow north,
Day come white, or night come black,
Home, or rivers and mountains from home,
Singing all time, minding no time,
While we two keep together.
Till of a sudden,
May-be kill’d, unknown to her mate,
One forenoon the she-bird crouch’d not on the nest,
Nor return’d that afternoon, nor the next,
Nor ever appear’d again.
And thenceforward all summer in the sound of the sea,
And at night under the full of the moon in calmer weather,
Over the hoarse surging of the sea,
Or flitting from brier to brier by day,
I saw, I heard at intervals the remaining one, the he-bird,
The solitary guest from Alabama.
Blow! blow! blow!
Blow up sea-winds along Paumanok’s shore;
I wait and I wait till you blow my mate to me.
Yes, when the stars glisten’d,
All night long on the prong of a moss-scallop’d stake,
Down almost amid the slapping waves,
Sat the lone singer wonderful causing tears.
He call’d on his mate,
He pour’d forth the meanings which I of all men know.
Yes my brother I know,
The rest might not, but I have treasur’d every note,
For more than once dimly down to the beach gliding,
Silent, avoiding the moonbeams, blending myself with the shadows,
Recalling now the obscure shapes, the echoes, the sounds and sights
after their sorts,
The white arms out in the breakers tirelessly tossing,
I, with bare feet, a child, the wind wafting my hair,
Listen’d long and long.
Listen’d to keep, to sing, now translating the notes,
Following you my brother.
Soothe! soothe! soothe!
Close on its wave soothes the wave behind,
And again another behind embracing and lapping, every one close,
But my love soothes not me, not me.
Low hangs the moon, it rose late,
It is lagging--O I think it is heavy with love, with love.
O madly the sea pushes upon the land,
With love, with love.
O night! do I not see my love fluttering out among the breakers?
What is that little black thing I see there in the white?
Loud! loud! loud!
Loud I call to you, my love!
High and clear I shoot my voice over the waves,
Surely you must know who is here, is here,
You must know who I am, my love.
Low-hanging moon!
What is that dusky spot in your brown yellow?
O it is the shape, the shape of my mate.’
O moon do not keep her from me any longer.
Land! land! O land!
Whichever way I turn, O I think you could give me my mate back again
if you only would,
For I am almost sure I see her dimly whichever way I look.
O rising stars!
Perhaps the one I want so much will rise, will rise with some of you.
O throat! O trembling throat!
Sound clearer through the atmosphere!
Pierce the woods, the earth,
Somewhere listening to catch you must be the one I want.
Shake out carols!
Solitary here, the night’s carols!
Carols of lonesome love! death’s carols!
Carols under that lagging, yellow, waning moon!
O under that moon where she droops almost down into the sea!
O reckless despairing carols.
But soft! sink low!
Soft! let me just murmur,
And do you wait a moment you husky-nois’d sea,
For somewhere I believe I heard my mate responding to me,
So faint, I must be still, be still to listen,
But not altogether still, for then she might not come immediately to me.
Hither my love!
Here I am! here!
With this just-sustain’d note I announce myself to you,
This gentle call is for you my love, for you.
Do not be decoy’d elsewhere,
That is the whistle of the wind, it is not my voice,
That is the fluttering, the fluttering of the spray,
Those are the shadows of leaves.
O darkness! O in vain!
O I am very sick and sorrowful
O brown halo in the sky near the moon, drooping upon the sea!
O troubled reflection in the sea!
O throat! O throbbing heart!
And I singing uselessly, uselessly all the night.
O past! O happy life! O songs of joy!
In the air, in the woods, over fields,
Loved! loved! loved! loved! loved!
But my mate no more, no more with me!
We two together no more.
The aria sinking,
All else continuing, the stars shining,
The winds blowing, the notes of the bird continuous echoing,
With angry moans the fierce old mother incessantly moaning,
On the sands of Paumanok’s shore gray and rustling,
The yellow half-moon enlarged, sagging down, drooping, the face of
the sea almost touching,
The boy ecstatic, with his bare feet the waves, with his hair the
atmosphere dallying,
The love in the heart long pent, now loose, now at last tumultuously
bursting,
The aria’s meaning, the ears, the soul, swiftly depositing,
The strange tears down the cheeks coursing,
The colloquy there, the trio, each uttering,
The undertone, the savage old mother incessantly crying,
To the boy’s soul’s questions sullenly timing, some drown’d secret hissing,
To the outsetting bard.
Demon or bird! (said the boy’s soul,)
Is it indeed toward your mate you sing? or is it really to me?
For I, that was a child, my tongue’s use sleeping, now I have heard you,
Now in a moment I know what I am for, I awake,
And already a thousand singers, a thousand songs, clearer, louder
and more sorrowful than yours,
A thousand warbling echoes have started to life within me, never to die.
O you singer solitary, singing by yourself, projecting me,
O solitary me listening, never more shall I cease perpetuating you,
Never more shall I escape, never more the reverberations,
Never more the cries of unsatisfied love be absent from me,
Never again leave me to be the peaceful child I was before what
there in the night,
By the sea under the yellow and sagging moon,
The messenger there arous’d, the fire, the sweet hell within,
The unknown want, the destiny of me.
O give me the clue! (it lurks in the night here somewhere,)
O if I am to have so much, let me have more!
A word then, (for I will conquer it,)
The word final, superior to all,
Subtle, sent up--what is it?--I listen;
Are you whispering it, and have been all the time, you sea-waves?
Is that it from your liquid rims and wet sands?
Whereto answering, the sea,
Delaying not, hurrying not,
Whisper’d me through the night, and very plainly before daybreak,
Lisp’d to me the low and delicious word death,
And again death, death, death, death
Hissing melodious, neither like the bird nor like my arous’d child’s heart,
But edging near as privately for me rustling at my feet,
Creeping thence steadily up to my ears and laving me softly all over,
Death, death, death, death, death.
Which I do not forget.
But fuse the song of my dusky demon and brother,
That he sang to me in the moonlight on Paumanok’s gray beach,
With the thousand responsive songs at random,
My own songs awaked from that hour,
And with them the key, the word up from the waves,
The word of the sweetest song and all songs,
That strong and delicious word which, creeping to my feet,
(Or like some old crone rocking the cradle, swathed in sweet
garments, bending aside,)
The sea whisper’d me.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, publication and revision l. 1

    The poem first appeared on the front page of the New York Saturday Press on 24 December 1859 as 'A Child's Reminiscence', with an editorial note, probably by Whitman, presenting it as a holiday gift. The Cincinnati Daily Commercial attacked it as drivel, and the Saturday Press replied on 7 January 1860 with 'All About a Mocking-Bird', a defence that Whitman himself may have written. It entered the 1860 Leaves of Grass as 'A Word Out of the Sea', was revised in 1867, took its present title in 1871, and reached its final form in 1881, when it opened the 'Sea-Drift' cluster. The archive text is this late version. The opening line itself sets the frame: the sea is a cradle endlessly rocking, and the cradle returns at the close with the old crone, linking birth and death.

    The Walt Whitman Archive, 'A Child's Reminiscence' (Saturday Press, 24 Dec. 1859) and reviews; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; The Walt Whitman Archive (whitmanarchive.org), texts and publication history of the poem

  2. The overture: one long periodic sentence ll. 1-22

    Whitman opens with a cascade of prepositional phrases ('Out of', 'Over', 'Down from', 'Up from', 'From') that pile up the sources of the poem, the rocking sea, the bird's throat, the September midnight, the memories, before the subject 'I' and the verb 'sing' arrive in l. 22. Leo Spitzer's classic explication analyses this sentence in detail as a deliberate rhetorical period, and the structure has often been compared to an operatic overture that announces the themes developed later. The delay enacts the poem's subject, the slow gathering of memory into song. The anaphora (repeated openings) is typical of Whitman's free verse, which borrows from the parallelism of the King James Bible.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Robert D. Faner, Walt Whitman and Opera (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1951)

  3. the musical shuttle l. 2

    A shuttle carries the weft thread back and forth across a loom. The metaphor captures the rapid, repeated phrases of the mockingbird's song and the bird's darting flight 'to and fro', and it makes song a kind of weaving, in which separate notes are woven into a fabric. Later the boy 'translates' the notes, and the poem itself weaves together bird, sea, boy and man.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Oxford English Dictionary

  4. Ninth-month; Fifth-month ll. 3, 24

    The Society of Friends (Quakers) traditionally avoided the names of months and weekdays derived from Roman and Norse gods, calling them by number: First-day for Sunday, Fifth-month for May, Ninth-month for September. Whitman's mother came from a family with Quaker connections, and he admired the Quaker preacher Elias Hicks. The usage gives the poem a plain, homely, Long Island idiom. The two months mark the story's span: the birds nest in May ('Fifth-month grass'), and the song the boy hears on the beach is remembered from a Ninth-month (September) midnight, as summer ends.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Walt Whitman, 'Elias Hicks', in November Boughs (1888)

  5. sad brother l. 9

    The poet addresses the bird directly as a 'brother', and the word recurs ('Yes my brother I know', 'Following you my brother', 'my dusky demon and brother'). The kinship is double: the boy shares the bird's grief, and he will inherit its vocation as a singer. Romantic poets had made birds into figures of the poet (Shelley's skylark, Keats's nightingale), but Whitman's bird is a fellow mourner rather than an immortal voice above human sorrow.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962)

  6. 'I, chanter of pains and joys' ll. 18-22

    The lines set up the poem's double time: the man ('yet by these tears a little boy again') throws himself on the sand and recalls the childhood scene. He calls himself 'chanter of pains and joys, uniter of here and hereafter', which anticipates the end, where the sea's word death unites life and what follows it. 'Taking all hints to use them, but swiftly leaping beyond them' is a statement of Whitman's poetics, using experience as a starting point for vision. Stephen Whicher argued that the tears belong to the adult Whitman of 1859, in a personal crisis, more than to the child.

    Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  7. Paumanok l. 23

    Whitman was born at West Hills, Long Island, in 1819, and spent much of his childhood and youth on the island. He preferred the Algonquian name 'Paumanok', which he understood to mean something like 'fish-shaped', to the English one, and it appears throughout Leaves of Grass ('Starting from fish-shape Paumanok where I was born', 'Starting from Paumanok'). Here 'Once Paumanok' begins the story itself, as though the island were the poem's 'once upon a time'. The poem's seashore, briers and sand belong to the island's north and south shores, which Whitman knew well.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Walt Whitman, 'Starting from Paumanok', Leaves of Grass (1881)

  8. two feather'd guests from Alabama l. 26

    The northern mockingbird (Mimus polyglottos) was then chiefly a southern bird, and a nesting pair on Long Island would have been a notable sight. Calling them 'guests from Alabama' makes them visitors from far away, and in 1859, on the eve of the Civil War, the southern origin may carry a faint resonance of national division and union ('two together'). The mockingbird is famous for its loud, varied song, often sung at night, which makes it a natural figure for the poet.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962)

  9. 'Cautiously peering, absorbing, translating' l. 31

    The three participles sum up Whitman's account of how a poet is made: by attention, absorption and translation. The word recurs at l. 69 ('now translating the notes'), and the bird's arias that follow are presented as the boy's translation of birdsong into English. This makes the poem about its own origin, the moment when a child became a singer. Critics such as Spitzer read the whole poem as a myth of the poet's vocation.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  10. The first aria: 'Shine! shine! shine!' ll. 32-40

    In Whitman's own editions the birds' songs are printed in italic, like arias set within the recitative of the narrative. The first is a duet of the two birds, happy in the sun, with the refrain 'two together' and short, strongly rhythmic lines; their steady pulse contrasts with the long narrative lines around it. Robert Faner traced the poem's alternation of recitative and aria to Whitman's passion for Italian opera, which he attended frequently in New York in the 1840s and 1850s. The triple exclamation becomes the pattern for every later aria: 'Blow! blow! blow!', 'Soothe! soothe! soothe!', 'Loud! loud! loud!'

    Robert D. Faner, Walt Whitman and Opera (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1951); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  11. 'Blow! blow! blow!' ll. 52-54

    The she-bird disappears without explanation ('May-be kill'd, unknown to her mate'), a death the poem never describes, which is part of its power. The he-bird's three-line song begins the series of laments. It echoes, perhaps deliberately, the refrains of Shakespeare ('Blow, blow, thou winter wind') and of ballads, but its plainness belongs to Whitman. From now on the bird sings by night, 'on the prong of a moss-scallop'd stake', amid the slapping waves.

    Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  12. The great aria (ll. 71-129) ll. 71-129

    This sixty-line aria is the centre of the poem. The bird calls on the waves, the moon, the land, the stars and its own throat; it mistakes a dark spot in the moon and a black thing in the breakers for its mate; it hushes, thinking it hears an answer; and it ends in 'O darkness! O in vain!' and 'We two together no more'. The emotional curve follows the shape of an operatic lament, and the bird's mistakes (wind, spray, shadows of leaves) show love projecting its object onto nature. Note the moon 'heavy with love' and the sea that 'pushes upon the land, / With love': for the grieving bird, all nature is love and loss.

    Robert D. Faner, Walt Whitman and Opera (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1951); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  13. 'The aria sinking': the trio ll. 130-143

    The word 'aria' (Italian, an 'air', a solo song in opera) is Whitman's open acknowledgement of his operatic model. After the song stops, a single sentence of participles ('continuing', 'shining', 'blowing', 'moaning') gathers the whole scene: stars, winds, the moaning sea, the drooping moon, and the boy 'ecstatic', with the love in his heart 'now at last tumultuously bursting'. 'The colloquy there, the trio, each uttering' names the three voices as in an operatic ensemble: the bird's song, the sea's undertone, and the boy's soul. The bird's grief has become the boy's awakening.

    Robert D. Faner, Walt Whitman and Opera (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1951); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  14. the fierce old mother l. 133

    The sea is first a rocking cradle, then 'the fierce old mother incessantly moaning' and 'the savage old mother incessantly crying', and finally the 'old crone'. Whitman's mother-sea draws on a long tradition (life emerging from the sea; the sea as mother in many mythologies) and on his own feeling for his mother. Whicher noted that Whitman's own mother figure and the sea of death are fused here. The sea's 'undertone', its 'drown'd secret hissing', prepares the word it will finally whisper.

    Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  15. the outsetting bard l. 143

    'Outsetting' (setting out, at the start of a journey) is an uncommon form built on 'outset', in keeping with Whitman's fondness for unusual coinages. The boy is now named a 'bard', the old Celtic title of the poet-singer that Whitman often uses for himself. The phrase marks the climax of the poem's myth of vocation: the child has become, in an instant, the poet he will be.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'; Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  16. Demon or bird! l. 144

    The echo of Poe's 'The Raven' ('Prophet!' said I, 'thing of evil! prophet still, if bird or devil!') is often noted, and Whitman may be deliberately answering Poe: his bird is not evil but a daimon in the Greek sense, a guiding spirit or genius, like the daimonion of Socrates. Later the poet calls it 'my dusky demon and brother'. The question 'Is it indeed toward your mate you sing? or is it really to me?' is the turning point, for the boy recognises that the song is meant for him, as a call to his own vocation.

    Edgar Allan Poe, 'The Raven' (1845), st. 15; Plato, Apology 31c-d (Socrates' daimonion); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  17. 'Now in a moment I know what I am for' ll. 147-157

    The boy, 'my tongue's use sleeping', now 'awakes' to his purpose: to sing. The bird's sorrow becomes the source of 'a thousand warbling echoes' within him, songs 'more sorrowful' than the bird's. The lines are both triumphant and fearful: 'never more shall I escape'; never again will he be 'the peaceful child I was'. 'The messenger there arous'd' may echo the Greek angelos (messenger, angel), and 'the fire, the sweet hell within' names a passion at once creative and painful, 'the unknown want'. Whicher read this as Whitman's confession of an adult crisis of 1859, projected back into childhood.

    Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962); Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  18. the fire, the sweet hell within l. 156

    'Sweet hell' combines pleasure and torment in the manner of Petrarchan love poetry, where love is a sweet fire. Here the fire is the poet's vocation as well as unsatisfied love ('the cries of unsatisfied love'). The phrase is one of several in the poem that bind delight and death ('the low and delicious word death'). The oxymoron is the poem's emotional key.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962)

  19. the clue l. 158

    'Clue' (from 'clew', a ball of thread) originally meant the thread by which Theseus found his way out of the Cretan labyrinth, and then any key to a mystery. The boy demands 'the word final, superior to all', and the poem's suspense hangs on what it will be. The sea's answer, death, is the clue: the meaning of the bird's loss and the source of all the boy's future songs.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962)

  20. the word death ll. 165-173

    The sea, 'delaying not, hurrying not', whispers 'death, death, death, death, death', the hissing sound of the surf on the sand. For Whitman the word is 'delicious', 'the word of the sweetest song and all songs'. Leo Spitzer read the ending as a consoling vision in which death is the mother who completes the cycle begun with the cradle, and poetry the reconciliation of love and death. Stephen Whicher saw instead a darker poem of crisis, in which the poet faces loss and the limits of his earlier, buoyant faith. Readers also compare Keats's 'Ode to a Nightingale', where the poet is 'half in love with easeful Death'.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Stephen E. Whicher, 'Whitman's Awakening to Death: Toward a Biographical Reading of "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in R. W. B. Lewis, ed., The Presence of Walt Whitman (New York: Columbia UP, 1962); John Keats, 'Ode to a Nightingale' (1819), st. 6

  21. 'But fuse the song of my dusky demon and brother' ll. 175-183

    The closing sentence says that the poet will 'fuse' the bird's song with 'the thousand responsive songs' that awoke in him that night and with 'the key, the word up from the waves'. It is Whitman's statement of how his poetry is made: from grief (the bird), from the imagination it arouses (his own songs) and from death (the sea). The poem ends quietly, 'The sea whisper'd me', where 'me' is both indirect object (whispered to me) and perhaps direct object, as if the sea whispered the poet into being.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W. Blodgett and Sculley Bradley, eds., Leaves of Grass: Comprehensive Reader's Edition (New York: New York UP, 1965), notes to 'Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking'

  22. some old crone rocking the cradle l. 182

    The parenthesis brings the poem full circle to its first line. The sea that rocked the child's cradle now appears as an 'old crone', swathed in 'sweet garments', bending over a cradle as she whispers death. The figure suggests a grandmother or a Fate, and it makes death a nurse rather than a terror. Spitzer stressed this framing as the formal sign of the poem's reconciliation, in which death is understood as a return to the mother.

    Leo Spitzer, 'Explication de Texte Applied to Walt Whitman's Poem "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking"', in Essays on English and American Literature (Princeton: Princeton UP, 1962); Harold W.
