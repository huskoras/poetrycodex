A Description of a City Shower

by Jonathan Swift 1667–1745

Nature

Careful observers may foretell the hour,
(By sure prognostics,) when to dread a shower.
While rain depends, the pensive cat gives o'er
Her frolics, and pursues her tail no more.
Returning home at night, you'll find the sink
Strike your offended sense with double stink.
If you be wise, then, go not far to dine:
You'll spend in coach-hire more than save in wine.
A coming shower your shooting corns presage,
Old a-ches throb, your hollow tooth will rage;
Sauntering in coffeehouse is Dulman seen;
He damns the climate, and complains of spleen.
Meanwhile the South, rising with dabbled wings,
A sable cloud athwart the welkin flings,
That swill'd more liquor than it could contain,
And, like a drunkard, gives it up again.
Brisk Susan whips her linen from the rope,
While the first drizzling shower is borne aslope;
Such is that sprinkling which some careless quean
Flirts on you from her mop, but not so clean:
You fly, invoke the gods; then, turning, stop
To rail; she singing, still whirls on her mop.
Not yet the dust had shunn'd the unequal strife,
But, aided by the wind, fought still for life,
And wafted with its foe by violent gust,
'Twas doubtful which was rain, and which was dust.
Ah! where must needy poet seek for aid,
When dust and rain at once his coat invade?
Sole coat! where dust, cemented by the rain,
Erects the nap, and leaves a cloudy stain!
Now in contiguous drops the flood comes down,
Threatening with deluge this devoted town.
To shops in crowds the daggled females fly,
Pretend to cheapen goods, but nothing buy.
The Templar spruce, while every spout's abroach,
Stays till 'tis fair, yet seems to call a coach.
The tuck'd-up sempstress walks with hasty strides,
While streams run down her oil'd umbrella's sides.
Here various kinds, by various fortunes led,
Commence acquaintance underneath a shed.
Triumphant Tories, and desponding Whigs,
Forget their feuds, and join to save their wigs.
Box'd in a chair the beau impatient sits,
While spouts run clattering o'er the roof by fits,
And ever and anon with frightful din
The leather sounds; he trembles from within.
So when Troy chairmen bore the wooden steed,
Pregnant with Greeks impatient to be freed,
(Those bully Greeks, who, as the moderns do,
Instead of paying chairmen, ran them through,)
Laocoon struck the outside with his spear,
And each imprison'd hero quaked for fear.
Now from all parts the swelling kennels flow,
And bear their trophies with them as they go:
Filth of all hues and odour, seem to tell
What street they sail'd from, by their sight and smell.
They, as each torrent drives with rapid force,
From Smithfield to St. Pulchre's shape their course,
And in huge confluence join'd at Snowhill ridge,
Fall from the conduit prone to Holborn bridge.
Sweeping from butchers' stalls, dung, guts, and blood,
Drown'd puppies, stinking sprats, all drench'd in mud,
Dead cats, and turnip-tops, come tumbling down the flood.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and publication ll. 1-2

    Swift wrote the poem during his London stay of autumn 1710; his letters to Esther Johnson ('Stella') follow its progress and on 17 October report that the Tatler had come out 'made up wholly of my Shower' and that people said it was the best thing he ever wrote, and he thought so too. On 27 October he reports that Rowe and Prior said there never was such a shower since Danae's. It is a companion to his 'Description of the Morning' (Tatler No. 9, 1709). Both apply the precise, low, urban detail of satire to genres of elevated description, producing a mock-georgic or 'town eclogue'. It was reprinted in Swift's Miscellanies of 1711, and later printings differ in details of wording.

    Jonathan Swift, Journal to Stella, Letter 6 (10-17 Oct. 1710), Letter 7 (27 Oct. 1710) and Letter 8 (31 Oct. 1710); Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; W. E. Browning, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift (1910), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'

  2. sure prognostics l. 2

    Virgil's Georgics 1.351-92 opens a catalogue of weather signs with the claim that we may learn them by sure signs ('certis ... signis'), drawn from Aratus's Phaenomena; signs include animals' behaviour such as cattle sniffing the air and frogs croaking. Swift translates the didactic catalogue to London: the cat, the sink, corns and aching teeth are the city's signs. Brendan O Hehir's influential essay read the whole poem as a sustained parody of Virgil's Georgics, and of Dryden's 1697 translation, with an apocalyptic undertone of the Flood.

    Virgil, Georgics 1.351-92 (weather signs); Brendan O Hehir, 'Meaning of Swift's "Description of a City Shower"', ELH 27 (1960); John Dryden, trans., Virgil's Georgics, Book 1 (The Works of Virgil, 1697); Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'

  3. depends l. 3

    Swift uses the word in its root sense, the rain hanging in the clouds before it falls; the elevated Latinism contrasts comically with the 'pensive cat', whose frolics and tail-chasing stop as a sign of rain. Cats' behaviour was part of popular weather lore, a domestic substitute for Virgil's birds and cattle. Swift's verb 'gives o'er' and the cat's 'frolics' keep a mock-dignified tone, as if the cat were a melancholy philosopher abandoning her pleasures.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'depend'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  4. the sink l. 5

    A sink was a pit or drain for foul water and sewage. Lowering air pressure before rain was observed to release the stink. In a letter to Stella of 31 October 1710 (Letter 8) Swift complained that he was almost stunk out of his lodging by the sink, adding that it helped him to verses in his 'Shower'. 'Double stink' and the following coach-hire couplet (go not far to dine, or you will pay more for a coach home than you save on wine) give the Georgics' practical advice an urban, economical twist.

    Jonathan Swift, Journal to Stella, Letter 6 (10-17 Oct. 1710), Letter 7 (27 Oct. 1710) and Letter 8 (31 Oct. 1710); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sink'; Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'

  5. a-ches l. 10

    The noun 'ache' was formerly pronounced like the letter H, with the plural 'aches' in two syllables, as in Shakespeare's Tempest 1.2 ('Fill all thy bones with aches'). Swift's spelling 'a-ches' marks the pronunciation. Some later printings read 'Old aches will throb', which treats the word as one syllable and pads the line with 'will'. Shooting corns and a hollow tooth are bodily barometers.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ache'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  6. Dulman and the spleen ll. 11-12

    Swift's invented name labels a type, in the manner of the Tatler and Spectator's character sketches. 'Spleen' was the fashionable name for low spirits, hypochondria and ill-humour, a melancholy thought especially English and blamed on the damp climate. Coffee-houses were the centres of London news and idleness. The sauntering Dulman is the poem's first human weather-sign: like the cat and the aching tooth, his complaint signals rain. His damning of the climate is the typical grumble of the idle: talk in place of Virgil's useful labour.

    Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'spleen'; Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'

  7. the South, rising with dabbled wings l. 13

    In Ovid's account of the Flood (Metamorphoses 1.264), Jupiter releases the south wind Notus, who flies out 'madidis ... alis', with soaking wings, and pours down rain. Swift's 'dabbled' (splashed, soiled with wet) brings the classical deity down to street level. The cloud that 'swill'd more liquor than it could contain' and 'gives it up again' like a drunkard turns the sublime storm into a London tavern image of vomiting.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.262-69; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; Brendan O Hehir, 'Meaning of Swift's "Description of a City Shower"', ELH 27 (1960)

  8. athwart the welkin l. 14

    Swift deliberately uses the stock vocabulary of elevated verse, 'sable' for black, 'athwart' for across, 'welkin' for sky, to describe a London rain cloud. The mismatch between diction and subject is the engine of the mock-georgic. Pope ridiculed similar poetic diction in Peri Bathous (1728). 'Flings' gives the cloud a sudden, violent movement, and 'sable' (heraldic black) dignifies what is merely a dark rain cloud over the city.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'welkin'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  9. Brisk Susan ... careless quean ll. 17-22

    'Brisk Susan' is a generic servant name. 'Aslope' means slanting, aslant. The 'quean' (from Old English cwene, woman, which came to mean a low or impudent woman) 'flirts' (flicks) water from her mop, a sprinkling like the first rain 'but not so clean'. The victim flies, 'invokes the gods' in epic fashion, then stops to swear at her while she goes on singing. The mock-epic invocation is reduced to cursing a maid.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'quean'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'flirt'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  10. the battle of dust and rain ll. 23-26

    The dust 'fought still for life' in an 'unequal strife', aided by its ally the wind, epic battle language. The last line echoes a line in Samuel Garth's mock-heroic The Dispensary (1699) about sea and sky, to which a note in the 1735 Works draws attention. Garth's line in turn recalls descriptions of the Flood (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.292: all was sea, and the sea had no shores). Swift's parody of a parody multiplies the literary layers.

    Samuel Garth, The Dispensary (1699); Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  11. needy poet ... Sole coat! ll. 27-30

    'Ah! where must needy poet seek for aid' parodies the epic or tragic apostrophe. The joke is the hack writer's poverty, a constant theme of Augustan satire (compare Swift's own Grub Street pieces and Pope's Dunciad). Rain 'erects the nap' (raises the short fibres of the cloth) and leaves a stain. The 1711 Miscellanies text reads 'His only Coat, where Dust confus'd with Rain'; the sharper 'Sole coat!' and 'cemented' belong to a later revision.

    Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  12. deluge ... this devoted town ll. 31-32

    Latin devotus means vowed to the gods, hence doomed. The 'contiguous drops' (touching, continuous) make a 'flood' that threatens 'deluge', inviting comparison with God's punishment of a corrupt world in Genesis 6-7 and with Ovid's flood (Metamorphoses 1.253-312), and with the destruction of Troy. O Hehir argued that the poem's final flood of filth enacts a mock Last Judgement on the city, while other critics think the apocalyptic reading overstated.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'devoted'; Brendan O Hehir, 'Meaning of Swift's "Description of a City Shower"', ELH 27 (1960); Genesis 6-7 (KJV)

  13. daggled females ... cheapen goods ll. 33-34

    'Daggled' means splashed and soiled with mud, of skirts trailing in wet streets. To 'cheapen' was to ask the price of, bargain for (from Old English ceap, trade). The couplet captures a social comedy: the rain turns shops into shelters and customers into pretenders. Throughout this section Swift catalogues social types (women shoppers, a Templar, a sempstress, party men, a beau), each revealed by how they behave in the rain.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'daggle'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'cheapen'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  14. The Templar spruce ll. 35-36

    Templars were members of the Inns of Court at the Temple, often fashionable young men more interested in town pleasures than law. 'Spruce' means neat, dapper. 'Abroach' means pouring out, as of a cask that has been tapped: every rain-spout is running. He 'seems to call a coach' to save face but will not pay for one, matching the earlier coach-hire economy (l. 8).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'Templar'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'abroach'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  15. oil'd umbrella ll. 37-38

    In the early eighteenth century the umbrella was chiefly a woman's accessory; men who carried one could be mocked as effeminate, and Gay's Trivia (1716) also treats it as a female item. The waterproofing was done with oil. A sempstress, a seamstress, is a working woman who cannot afford to wait or ride; 'tuck'd-up' means her skirts are pinned up out of the wet. Her purposeful stride contrasts with the Templar's affected idleness in the couplet before.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'umbrella'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; John Gay, Trivia; or, The Art of Walking the Streets of London (1716), Book 1

  16. Triumphant Tories, and desponding Whigs ll. 41-42

    Queen Anne dismissed the Whig Godolphin ministry in August 1710, and the Tories won a sweeping victory in the elections of October, just as the poem appeared. Swift, a Whig by origin, was then being courted by Robert Harley and would soon become the chief Tory propagandist, as the Journal to Stella records. The joke is that party feuds yield to the common need to save expensive periwigs from the rain under a shed.

    W. E. Browning, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift (1910), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'; Jonathan Swift, Journal to Stella, Letters 5-8 (Oct. 1710); Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  17. The beau in his chair and the Trojan horse ll. 43-52

    A 'chair' is a sedan chair carried by two chairmen; its leather roof 'sounds' under the rain. In Aeneid 2.50-53 Laocoon hurls his spear into the side of the wooden horse, and the hollow belly echoes; Virgil's Greeks, Swift implies, quaked inside. Swift adds a contemporary joke: 'bully Greeks', like modern rakes, ran their chairmen through instead of paying them. The simile is a full epic simile in form, applied to a fop.

    Virgil, Aeneid 2.40-56; W. E. Browning, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift (1910), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  18. kennels l. 53

    'Kennel' is a variant of 'channel': the open gutter running down the middle or side of a street. The gutters 'bear their trophies' like a victorious army carrying spoils, a mock-heroic image; the filth reveals 'What street they sail'd from, by their sight and smell', so that refuse becomes a guide to London's trades. The verb 'sail'd' continues the mock-heroic voyage; Swift treats the gutters as epic rivers flowing to their confluence.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'kennel'; Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.

  19. Smithfield, St Pulchre's, Snowhill, Holborn bridge ll. 57-60

    Smithfield was London's livestock market, notorious for blood and offal. 'St. Pulchre's' is the colloquial name of St Sepulchre-without-Newgate. Snow Hill ran steeply down to Holborn Bridge, which crossed the Fleet Ditch, an open sewer famous for filth; the 'conduit' was a water conduit on Snow Hill. Pope later set the diving contest of The Dunciad (1728) in the Fleet Ditch. The route traces an actual drainage path, giving the mock-epic river a real geography.

    Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; The Map of Early Modern London (University of Victoria)

  20. The closing triplet and Alexandrine ll. 61-63

    Dryden often closed a verse paragraph with a triplet (three rhymes) ending in an Alexandrine (a twelve-syllable line); in the dedication of his Aeneis (1697) he defended both licences. Editors since the eighteenth century have read Swift's final triplet and Alexandrine as a jibe at this habit. Swift heaps 'dung, guts, and blood, / Drown'd puppies, stinking sprats' and 'Dead cats' into the stately form, so the swollen line tumbles like the flood itself.

    Harold Williams, ed., The Poems of Jonathan Swift, 2nd edn (1958), vol. 1, notes to 'A Description of a City Shower'; John Dryden, Dedication of the Aeneis (1697); Pat Rogers, ed., Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems (Penguin, 1983), notes ad loc.; Brendan O Hehir, 'Meaning of Swift's "Description of a City Shower"', ELH 27 (1960)
