In Flanders Fields

by John McCrae 1872–1918

From In Flanders Fields and Other Poems (1919)

History & PoliticsDeath & Dying

In Flanders fields the poppies blow
Between the crosses, row on row,
That mark our place; and in the sky
The larks, still bravely singing, fly
Scarce heard amid the guns below.

We are the Dead. Short days ago
We lived, felt dawn, saw sunset glow,
Loved and were loved, and now we lie,
In Flanders fields.

Take up our quarrel with the foe:
To you from failing hands we throw
The torch; be yours to hold it high.
If ye break faith with us who die
We shall not sleep, though poppies grow
In Flanders fields.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Written in 1915 after McCrae buried a friend at Ypres and first published in Punch that December. The dead speak to the living from among the poppies of Flanders, and ask that the quarrel be carried on: 'If ye break faith with us who die / We shall not sleep.'

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion l. 1

    John McCrae (1872-1918), a Canadian physician serving as brigade surgeon with the artillery, was at a dressing station near Ypres during the Second Battle of Ypres (April-May 1915). On 2 May his friend Lieutenant Alexis Helmer was killed; in the chaplain's absence McCrae read the burial service, and the poem was begun the following day. It was rejected by The Spectator and printed, unsigned, in Punch on 8 December 1915. It became the most famous English poem of the war, used for recruitment and war bonds, and the source of the remembrance poppy. McCrae died of pneumonia and meningitis at Wimereux on 28 January 1918.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'; John F. Prescott, In Flanders Fields: The Story of John McCrae (Boston Mills Press, 1985); McGill University, Osler Library of the History of Medicine blog, '"In Flanders fields" at the Osler Library'

  2. blow (or 'grow') l. 1

    'Blow' here is the old verb meaning to flower or blossom (the OED records it from Old English; compare 'full-blown'), not 'to be blown by the wind', though that sense is also present. McCrae's handwritten copies vary: a 1916 copy in the Osler Library at McGill reads 'grow', and accounts differ on which word Punch printed and which McCrae preferred; the 1919 book has 'blow'. The variant matters little to meaning but shows that the text was not fixed. 'Grow' recurs at l. 14 ('though poppies grow').

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'blow, v.2'; McGill University, Osler Library of the History of Medicine blog, '"In Flanders fields" at the Osler Library'; The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'

  3. Flanders ll. 1, 9, 15

    Flanders, historically a county of the Low Countries, covers the flat land of western Belgium and the adjoining corner of France. The Ypres salient, held by British and Commonwealth troops from 1914 to 1918, saw four major battles, including the first use of poison gas in April 1915 at the Second Battle of Ypres, where McCrae served. The name became, through the poem, a synonym for the war dead. Helmer was buried at Essex Farm, beside the dressing station where the poem was written, but his grave was later lost; he is commemorated on the Menin Gate, and a memorial at Essex Farm Cemetery marks the poem.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'; Commonwealth War Graves Commission, 'Essex Farm Cemetery'

  4. poppies l. 1

    The common or corn poppy germinates when soil is turned over, so it bloomed abundantly on the churned battlefields and burial grounds of Flanders. McCrae's image fixed it as the emblem of the war dead. Inspired by the poem, the American teacher Moina Michael began in 1918 to wear a red poppy in remembrance, and the American Legion adopted it; Madame Anna Guérin promoted poppy sales in France and then, in 1921, in Britain, where Earl Haig's British Legion took up the Poppy Appeal. The colour also suggests blood.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'; Royal British Legion, 'The story of the poppy'

  5. the crosses, row on row l. 2

    Battlefield graves were marked with temporary wooden crosses, later replaced by the uniform headstones of the Imperial (now Commonwealth) War Graves Commission. 'Row on row' gives both the orderly cemetery and the image of soldiers in formation, the dead still drawn up in ranks. The crosses also carry Christian meaning, the sacrifice of the cross, which the poem's later language of keeping faith develops.

    Commonwealth War Graves Commission, history of war graves; John F. Prescott, In Flanders Fields: The Story of John McCrae (Boston Mills Press, 1985)

  6. the larks ll. 4-5

    The skylark, which sings while climbing high above open fields, was a familiar sound over the trenches, and larks recur in soldiers' writing of the war, often set against the guns. The lark also carries a literary heritage, from Shelley's 'To a Skylark' to Shakespeare's lark at heaven's gate (Sonnet 29), as a figure of joy and aspiration. Here they sing 'bravely', a soldierly word, their song 'Scarce heard' amid gunfire.

    P. B. Shelley, 'To a Skylark' (1820); William Shakespeare, Sonnet 29

  7. the speaking dead l. 6

    The second stanza reveals that the speakers are the dead, using the first-person plural. Speaking epitaphs, in which the dead address the passer-by, go back to Greek inscriptions such as Simonides' epitaph for the Spartans at Thermopylae ('Go tell the Spartans ...'). McCrae makes the convention collective and immediate: they were alive 'Short days ago'. The list of verbs ('lived, felt dawn, saw sunset glow, / Loved and were loved') condenses a whole life into a line and a half.

    Simonides, epitaph for the Spartans at Thermopylae, in Herodotus, Histories 7.228; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975)

  8. Rondeau ll. 1-15

    The rondeau is a French fixed form: thirteen full lines on two rhymes plus a refrain, the 'rentrement', formed from the opening words. McCrae's rhymes are on '-ow' (blow, row, below, ago, glow, foe, throw, grow) and '-y' (sky, fly, lie, high, die), and the refrain 'In Flanders fields' closes the second and third stanzas. The form, more often used for light or courtly verse, imposes a calm, measured music on the subject, and the repeated refrain gives it the quality of an incantation. The iambic tetrameter lines are short and regular.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'; Lewis Turco, The Book of Forms (UP of New England, 3rd edn 2000), s.v. 'rondeau'

  9. Take up our quarrel l. 10

    The third stanza shifts from lament to command. 'Quarrel' is a plain, almost personal word for the war, as if it were a feud to be inherited. This turn has divided readers. Paul Fussell, in The Great War and Modern Memory, treated the poem as an example of conventional pastoral imagery and settled sentiment in the literature of the war, and its last stanza was put to use as propaganda. Others reply that McCrae, a front-line doctor, sincerely believed the dead would be betrayed by a negotiated peace. The stanza was widely used in recruiting and in Canada's Victory Bond campaigns, and in the 1917 election during the conscription crisis.

    Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975); The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'; John F. Prescott, In Flanders Fields: The Story of John McCrae (Boston Mills Press, 1985)

  10. the torch ll. 11-12

    The image draws on the Greek torch-race (lampadedromia), in which runners passed a lighted torch from hand to hand; Lucretius uses it for the succession of generations ('like runners, they hand on the torch of life', De rerum natura 2.79). The 'failing hands' are those of dying soldiers. The lines became a Canadian commonplace: they are painted on the wall of the Montreal Canadiens' dressing room. The run-on from 'throw' to 'The torch' enacts the handing over across the line-break.

    Lucretius, De rerum natura 2.77-79; The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'

  11. break faith l. 13

    To 'break faith' is to violate a pledge; the dead regard their sacrifice as a contract that the living must honour by continuing the war. The archaic 'ye' gives their speech a biblical solemnity. 'Us who die' is in the present tense: the dying is still going on. The conditional threat follows: if the living fail, the dead will find no rest.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'faith, n.'; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (Oxford UP, 1975)

  12. We shall not sleep, though poppies grow l. 14

    Since antiquity the poppy has been associated with sleep and forgetfulness: in Ovid's Metamorphoses poppies grow before the cave of Sleep (11.605-6), and the opium poppy's narcotic effect was well known. McCrae turns this against itself: though the poppies of oblivion grow over them, the dead will not 'sleep' if they are betrayed. The idea draws on the folk belief that the unavenged dead cannot rest. The line thus gives the poppy its double meaning in remembrance: both peace and the refusal to forget.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.605-607; The Canadian Encyclopedia, 'In Flanders Fields'
