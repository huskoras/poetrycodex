The New Colossus

by Emma Lazarus 1849–1887

From Poems, Vol. I

History & Politics

Not like the brazen giant of Greek fame,
With conquering limbs astride from land to land;
Here at our sea-washed, sunset gates shall stand
A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame
Is the imprisoned lightning, and her name
Mother of Exiles. From her beacon-hand
Glows world-wide welcome; her mild eyes command
The air-bridged harbor that twin cities frame.
"Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!" cries she
With silent lips. "Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to be free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!"

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its occasion l. 1

    Bartholdi's statue, 'Liberty Enlightening the World', was a gift from France, but the pedestal had to be paid for by Americans, and in 1883 a fund-raising Art Loan Exhibition was organised in New York. William Maxwell Evarts and Constance Cary Harrison asked Emma Lazarus (1849-1887) for a contribution; she wrote the sonnet on 2 November 1883. It was largely forgotten at the statue's unveiling in 1886. After her death her friend Georgina Schuyler campaigned to commemorate it, and in 1903 a bronze plaque with the poem was fixed inside the pedestal. The poem largely created the statue's later meaning as a welcome to immigrants, rather than the Franco-American celebration of republican liberty its makers intended.

    National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument, 'Emma Lazarus'; Jewish Women's Archive, 'Emma Lazarus writes "The New Colossus"' (This Week in History, 2 Nov. 1883); Museum of the City of New York, 'The New Colossus'; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  2. the brazen giant ll. 1-2

    The Colossus of Rhodes, made by Chares of Lindos to celebrate the city's survival of a siege, stood about 33 metres high and fell in an earthquake some sixty years later; Pliny the Elder describes its fallen fragments (Natural History 34.41). 'Brazen' means made of brass or bronze, and also, by a pun, shameless or impudent. The image of a giant 'astride from land to land', legs planted on either side of the harbour, is a medieval and Renaissance legend, not ancient fact, familiar from Cassius's 'he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus' (Julius Caesar 1.2). Lazarus sets the conquering male colossus against her new female one.

    Pliny the Elder, Natural History 34.41; William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, 1.2.135-36; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'brazen, adj.'

  3. sea-washed, sunset gates l. 3

    The 'gates' are the harbour entrance through which ships reached New York. 'Sunset' marks America as the West, the direction of the setting sun for European voyagers, a long-standing figure in which the New World is the Hesperides or westward land of promise. The gates are 'sea-washed' because they open on the Atlantic. The phrase is answered in the last line by 'the golden door': gates of arrival framing the sonnet.

    Museum of the City of New York, 'The New Colossus'; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  4. a mighty woman with a torch l. 4

    Bartholdi's statue personifies Liberty as a robed classical woman holding a torch aloft, a tablet in her other hand. Lazarus, writing before the statue was erected, stresses gender: the Rhodian Colossus is a 'giant' with 'conquering limbs'; the new colossus is a 'mighty woman', and her power is used to welcome, not to dominate. The torch reappears as the 'lamp' of the final line, moving from a symbol of enlightenment to a domestic lamp held at a door.

    National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument, 'Emma Lazarus'; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  5. imprisoned lightning l. 5

    The phrase is a modern conceit: the statue's torch was to be illuminated by electric light, the new technology of the 1880s (Edison's Pearl Street station opened in New York in 1882). Lightning 'imprisoned' is natural force tamed, an image of American ingenuity. There may also be a glance at Franklin, the American who identified lightning with electricity. Lazarus's 'imprisoned' quietly sets up a paradox in a poem about freedom: the light of liberty is itself captive.

    Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006); Museum of the City of New York, 'The New Colossus'

  6. Mother of Exiles l. 6

    The official name of Bartholdi's statue was 'Liberty Enlightening the World'. Lazarus substitutes a maternal title. The choice reflects her own work from 1881 with Jewish refugees fleeing the Russian pogroms, many of whom were housed on Ward's Island in New York; she wrote and campaigned on their behalf and became an advocate for a Jewish homeland. 'Exiles' recalls the biblical exile of Israel. Esther Schor's biography stresses how the poem joins Lazarus's Jewish advocacy to a vision of America as refuge.

    Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006); National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument, 'Emma Lazarus'; Jewish Women's Archive, 'Emma Lazarus writes "The New Colossus"' (This Week in History, 2 Nov. 1883)

  7. twin cities l. 8

    In 1883 Brooklyn was an independent city, the third largest in the United States; it was consolidated with New York only in 1898. The harbour lies between them. 'Air-bridged' suggests the harbour 'bridged' by the air above it, or more specifically the Brooklyn Bridge, opened in May 1883, six months before the poem was written, the first bridge to link the two cities. Not all commentators accept the bridge reference, but the date makes it likely.

    Museum of the City of New York, 'The New Colossus'; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Brooklyn Bridge'

  8. Petrarchan sonnet and volta ll. 9-14

    The octave describes the statue and rejects the ancient colossus; the sestet gives her silent speech. The rhyme scheme is strict Italian (fame/stand/land/flame ... she/poor/free), and the turn at l. 9 is marked by quotation marks. The paradox 'cries she / With silent lips' acknowledges that the statue cannot literally speak; the poet speaks for her. The choice of the Petrarchan sonnet, an Old World form, for a New World manifesto is itself a kind of transplanting.

    Wikipedia, 'The New Colossus' (form and interpretation); Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  9. storied pomp l. 9

    'Storied' means celebrated in history or legend, and also decorated with scenes from history (as a storied window or urn). Lazarus's statue tells Europe to keep its monuments and aristocratic splendour; America asks instead for the poor. The phrasing recalls Gray's Elegy, where 'the pomp of pow'r' leads but to the grave and no 'storied urn' can recall the dead (ll. 33-41). Gray's poem, which honours the obscure poor against the proud, is an apt subtext for a sonnet that honours the 'wretched refuse'.

    Thomas Gray, 'Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard' (1751), ll. 33-41; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'storied, adj.'

  10. huddled masses l. 11

    'Huddled' gives the image of people pressed together, cold and frightened, the conditions of steerage passengers on emigrant ships. 'Masses' was a political word of the period for the working poor. The line's famous phrase has been quoted and contested in American immigration debates ever since; in 2019 a federal official proposed rewording it, provoking controversy. In the poem, the masses are 'yearning to be free', the one place in the sestet where 'liberty' (the statue's real name) is directly invoked.

    National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument, 'Emma Lazarus'; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  11. wretched refuse l. 12

    'Refuse' (stressed on the first syllable) is waste matter, what has been discarded; the word comes from Old French refus, 'refusal', and so means literally 'the refused'. It is a deliberately harsh word: Europe treats its poor as rubbish, and the statue takes them in. Some readers have found it condescending to the immigrants; others read it as Lazarus's bitter irony against Europe's view of them. 'Wretched' (miserable, also in the old sense 'exiled', Old English wrecca) and 'teeming' (overcrowded) intensify the picture.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'refuse, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'wretched, adj.'; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  12. tempest-tost l. 13

    The emigrant crossing of the Atlantic was dangerous, and the homeless are pictured as storm-tossed sailors arriving in port. The phrase has strong literary ancestry: in Isaiah 54:11 God comforts Zion, 'O thou afflicted, tossed with tempest, and not comforted', promising to rebuild her; and in Macbeth one of the witches curses a sailor whose 'bark cannot be lost, / Yet it shall be tempest-tost' (1.3). The Isaiah passage, addressed to exiled Israel and promising restoration, fits Lazarus's Jewish concerns especially well.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Isaiah 54:11; William Shakespeare, Macbeth, 1.3.24-25

  13. I lift my lamp l. 14

    The torch of l. 4 is now a 'lamp', a homelier word: the statue is less a beacon of enlightenment than a woman at the door of her house, holding up a light for those arriving at night. Lamps in scripture are images of guidance ('Thy word is a lamp unto my feet', Psalm 119:105), and the wise virgins' lamps await the bridegroom (Matthew 25). The alliteration on l binds the closing line.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 119:105; Esther Schor, Emma Lazarus (Schocken/Nextbook, 2006)

  14. the golden door l. 14

    The 'golden door' answers the 'sunset gates' of l. 3: the harbour entrance is now the door of a house, golden with the light of the lamp and of the setting sun, and with the promise of prosperity ('streets paved with gold' was a byword among emigrants). The image completes the poem's transformation of a colossal monument into a hospitable home. Within a decade the federal immigration station on Ellis Island (opened 1892) would become the literal door, and the poem's line is now associated with it.

    National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument, 'Emma Lazarus'; Museum of the City of New York, 'The New Colossus'
