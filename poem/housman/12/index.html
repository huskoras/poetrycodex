Love

XIII

by A. E. Housman 1859–1936

From A Shropshire Lad

Love

When I was one-and-twenty
I heard a wise man say,
"Give crowns and pounds and guineas
But not your heart away;
Give pearls away and rubies
But keep your fancy free."
But I was one-and-twenty,
No use to talk to me.

When I was one-and-twenty
I heard him say again,
"The heart out of the bosom
Was never given in vain;
'Tis paid with sighs a plenty
And sold for endless rue."
And I am two-and-twenty,
And oh, 'tis true, 'tis true.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A Shropshire Lad XIII: context l. 1

    A. E. Housman (1859-1936), a classical scholar then at University College London, published A Shropshire Lad in 1896. Its sixty-three short poems are spoken mostly by young countrymen of Shropshire facing love, loss, soldiering and early death. This lyric, untitled in the book and known by its first line, is one of the lightest in tone, though its theme of experience bought too late runs through the whole collection. Burnett's Clarendon edition gives the standard text and commentary. George Butterworth set it in his Six Songs from A Shropshire Lad (1911), and it remains one of Housman's most anthologised and most sung poems.

    A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary; A. E. Housman, Collected Poems and Selected Prose, ed. Christopher Ricks (Penguin, 1988); A. E. Housman, A Shropshire Lad (1896), Project Gutenberg text

  2. one-and-twenty l. 7

    The old-fashioned inverted form of the number ('one-and-twenty' rather than 'twenty-one') suits the ballad style. Twenty-one was the traditional age of coming of age in English law; the speaker is newly 'of age' and therefore unwilling to listen to advice ('No use to talk to me'). The repetition of the phrase three times in the poem (ll. 1, 7, 9) makes the age itself his excuse, and the joke of the ending is that a single year makes him wise.

    Oxford English Dictionary; A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  3. the wise man's advice ll. 2-6

    The 'wise man' is unnamed and proverbial, the voice of experience in folk songs and moral verse. His advice inverts ordinary prudence: give away money and jewels, but keep your heart. A biblical background is the counsel of Proverbs, 'Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life' (Prov. 4:23), though Housman uses the idea in the secular sense of falling in love. The wise man's two speeches form the centre of each stanza, set off by quotation marks.

    Proverbs 4:23 (KJV); A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  4. crowns, pounds and guineas l. 3

    The list rises in value: the crown (a coin worth five shillings), the pound (twenty shillings) and the guinea (twenty-one shillings). The gold guinea had stopped being minted early in the nineteenth century but survived as a unit for gentlemanly payments such as fees. The wise man treats money as easily given away, and the poem's later language of payment ('paid', 'sold') turns love itself into a bad bargain.

    Oxford English Dictionary; A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  5. pearls and rubies l. 5

    Pearls and rubies are biblical and proverbial types of great value: wisdom is 'more precious than rubies' (Proverbs 3:15), and the virtuous woman's 'price is far above rubies' (Proverbs 31:10). The wise man's point is that even these may be given away, but not the heart. The pairing also has the ring of a folk ballad's stock treasures, and continues the rising list of riches begun in line 3.

    Proverbs 3:15; 31:10 (KJV); A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  6. fancy free l. 6

    In older English 'fancy' often means love or a liking for someone (OED). The phrase recalls Oberon's description of the 'imperial vot'ress', usually taken as a compliment to Queen Elizabeth, who passed on 'In maiden meditation, fancy-free', untouched by Cupid's arrow, in A Midsummer Night's Dream (2.1.164). The wise man urges the same immunity on the young man, who of course does not keep it.

    Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1.164, Folger Shakespeare text; Oxford English Dictionary

  7. 'No use to talk to me' l. 8

    The plain, almost spoken phrasing closes the first stanza with a comic admission. Housman often sets a colloquial line inside a formal ballad style, and here the shift of register gives the speaker's own voice: a young man looking back on his stubbornness from the distance of a single year. The line also prepares the turn of the second stanza, where he will find that the advice he ignored was right.

    A. E. Housman, Collected Poems and Selected Prose, ed. Christopher Ricks (Penguin, 1988); A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  8. 'The heart out of the bosom' l. 11

    The wise man's second speech makes the familiar metaphor of giving one's heart physical: it is taken 'out of the bosom'. Such a gift, he says, is 'never given in vain', but only in the bitter sense that it is always paid for. The line plays on the stock phrase 'in vain' (to no purpose): the heart's gift is never without consequence, and the consequence is sorrow.

    A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary; Oxford English Dictionary

  9. love as a bargain ll. 13-14

    The money of the first stanza returns as metaphor. The heart is not freely given; it is 'paid with sighs' and 'sold for endless rue', a transaction in which the giver always loses. 'A plenty', meaning in abundance, is a colloquial or dialect form that suits the rustic speaker. The commercial language makes the wise man's earlier advice exact: you can give away coins, but the heart is an expense that is never repaid.

    Oxford English Dictionary; A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary

  10. rue l. 14

    'Rue' means grief or regret (OED, rue n.1); it is also the name of a bitter herb (rue n.2), traditionally called 'herb of grace' and associated with repentance, as when Ophelia hands out flowers: 'There's rue for you' (Hamlet 4.5). Housman returns to the word in A Shropshire Lad LIV, 'With rue my heart is laden'. Here 'endless rue' is the price that never stops being paid, and it rhymes with the poem's last word, 'true'.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Shakespeare, Hamlet 4.5 (Ophelia's flowers), Folger Shakespeare text; A. E. Housman, A Shropshire Lad (1896), Project Gutenberg text

  11. two-and-twenty l. 15

    The change of tense from 'When I was' to 'I am' brings the poem into the present. The short time between twenty-one and twenty-two mocks the speaker's new wisdom, but the experience is real: he has given his heart and suffered. The poem can be read as a gentle joke about youthful heartbreak or, in the context of the whole book, as one more case of early disillusion among Housman's young men; much of its appeal lies in holding both at once.

    A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary; A. E. Housman, Collected Poems and Selected Prose, ed. Christopher Ricks (Penguin, 1988)

  12. the closing repetition l. 16

    The repetition of ''tis true' gives the ending the cadence of a folk ballad refrain and the effect of a rueful nod. It also confirms the wise man's speech, turning his warning into the speaker's own experience. The interjection 'oh' before it lightens the tone, so that the poem ends poised between comedy and real grief, much as the whole of A Shropshire Lad moves between song and lament.

    A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary; A. E. Housman, Collected Poems and Selected Prose, ed. Christopher Ricks (Penguin, 1988)

  13. Metre and stanza st. 1-2

    Each line has three stresses; the odd lines end on an unstressed syllable (twenty, guineas, rubies), the even lines on a stress (say, away, free, me). The even lines carry the rhyme in each stanza (say/away, free/me; again/vain, rue/true), while the feminine odd lines are largely unrhymed apart from the refrain-like 'twenty' endings and the late 'plenty'/'two-and-twenty' echo, so the poem closes on a strong chime. The two stanzas mirror each other closely, opening with the same line and ending on the speaker's age, a symmetry that makes the year's difference the poem's whole action.

    A. E. Housman, The Poems of A. E. Housman, ed. Archie Burnett (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), A Shropshire Lad XIII and commentary; A. E. Housman, Collected Poems and Selected Prose, ed. Christopher Ricks (Penguin, 1988)
