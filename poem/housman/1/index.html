Nature

II

by A. E. Housman 1859–1936

From A Shropshire Lad

Nature

Loveliest of trees, the cherry now
Is hung with bloom along the bough,
And stands about the woodland ride
Wearing white for Eastertide.

Now, of my threescore years and ten,
Twenty will not come again,
And take from seventy springs a score,
It only leaves me fifty more.

And since to look at things in bloom
Fifty springs are little room,
About the woodlands I will go
To see the cherry hung with snow.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A Shropshire Lad II: date and occasion l. 1

    Housman's sequence A Shropshire Lad (1896) numbers its poems rather than titling them; this is II, known by its first line. The surviving draft has been dated to April or May 1895 and had only two stanzas; the arithmetic middle stanza was composed later and much revised. Burnett's edition notes that the cherry was personal: there was a locally admired cherry tree at Housman's childhood home, he recorded the cherries' flowering in his diary in the 1890s, and in later life he planted cherry trees at Trinity College, Cambridge. The poem is among the most anthologised of English lyrics.

    Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad; Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013); The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes)

  2. Metre and rhyme ll. 1-2

    The poem is in three quatrains made of rhyming couplets (now/bough, ride/Eastertide), in four-stress iambic lines, many of them headless (seven syllables). The Housman Society notes that the first word, 'Loveliest', disrupts the metre: its three syllables crowd the opening foot, so the poem begins with an overflow of feeling before settling into regularity. The simple metre, close to hymn and ballad, is typical of A Shropshire Lad and belies the arithmetic precision of the argument. 'Is hung with bloom' anticipates the poem's last line, 'hung with snow', binding beginning to end.

    The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes); Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013)

  3. ride l. 3

    In this sense 'ride' is a noun for a path or green road cut through woodland, used especially for riding or hunting; the OED records it from the eighteenth century. The Housman Society glosses it as a path broad enough for a horse. The cherry 'stands about' the ride, lining it, as if the trees were spectators at a procession, and the next line dresses them for a festival.

    The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  4. Wearing white for Eastertide l. 4

    Eastertide is the season of Easter. White is the liturgical colour of the feast, and in the early church the newly baptised wore white robes through Easter week, so the tree is personified as a celebrant in festal dress. The religious allusion is pointed: the season celebrates resurrection and eternal life, while the poem goes on to count a single, limited life in years. Burnett lists among possible analogues Robert Bridges' 'Spring goeth all in white', which shares the image of spring dressed in white. The cherry's whiteness also prepares the final transformation into 'snow'.

    The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad; Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013)

  5. threescore years and ten l. 5

    The phrase comes from Psalm 90:10 in the King James Bible and the Prayer Book: 'The days of our years are threescore years and ten'. The psalm is a meditation on the brevity of life before an eternal God and is read at the Anglican burial service, so it brings a funereal undertone into a spring poem. Housman, an atheist by the time he wrote the poem, borrows the measure without the consolation. The speaker accepts the biblical allotment as a fact and then does sums with it.

    Psalm 90:10 (KJV); Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013); The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes)

  6. Twenty will not come again l. 6

    The speaker has lived twenty years, which 'will not come again', and so has 'fifty more' springs. The arithmetic is deliberately plain, almost comic, in the manner of a country lad. The speaker is a persona: Housman himself was about thirty-six in 1895. Critics have read the poem as a young man's first recognition of his own mortality, prompted by the short-lived cherry blossom (Leggett, Firchow, as summarised in the Wikipedia article). The phrase 'will not come again' looks ahead to 'And cannot come again' at the end of A Shropshire Lad XL, one of the sequence's recurrent laments for lost time.

    Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad

  7. Fifty springs are little room l. 10

    The paradox of the last stanza is that half a century is 'little room' (too little space) for looking at things in bloom. The poem therefore resolves not in pleasure-seeking but in looking: 'I will go / To see'. D. T. Siebert called it 'a little masterpiece of carpe diem' (as quoted in the Wikipedia summary); other critics stress its sense that beauty is precious because it is brief. Peter Firchow, by contrast, judged the middle stanza convoluted, so critical opinion is not unanimous. The phrase 'little room' may recall Marlowe's 'infinite riches in a little room' (The Jew of Malta), one of the analogues Burnett lists.

    Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad

  8. hung with snow l. 12

    The poem ends by transforming the cherry's 'bloom' of l. 2 into 'snow'. On one level the image simply describes masses of white blossom. But snow belongs to winter, and to the old age and death that the arithmetic has just measured, so the last word quietly carries the end of the reckoning into the moment of beauty. It may also hint that the speaker will go on looking even into winter, when the boughs are hung with real snow. The ambiguity between celebration and memento mori is the source of the poem's tension; the plain diction hides it.

    The Housman Society, 'Loveliest of trees' (study notes); Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013)

  9. Reception and musical settings l. 11

    The poem's song-like regularity made it a favourite of English composers. More than sixty settings are known, among them George Butterworth's Six Songs from A Shropshire Lad (1911) and his orchestral rhapsody, Arthur Somervell's song cycle, and settings by Ivor Gurney, E. J. Moeran and C. W. Orr. Many of these composers wrote in the years around the First World War, when the sequence's dead young men took on new meaning; Butterworth himself was killed on the Somme in 1916. In a 1995 BBC poll the poem was among the nation's hundred favourites.

    Wikipedia, 'Loveliest of trees, the cherry now' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Firchow 2002, Siebert 2013)
