Time & Brevity

To an Athlete Dying Young

by A. E. Housman 1859–1936

From A Shropshire Lad

Time & Brevity

The time you won your town the race
We chaired you through the market-place;
Man and boy stood cheering by,
And home we brought you shoulder-high.

To-day, the road all runners come,
Shoulder-high we bring you home,
And set you at your threshold down,
Townsman of a stiller town.

Smart lad, to slip betimes away
From fields where glory does not stay
And early though the laurel grows
It withers quicker than the rose.

Eyes the shady night has shut
Cannot see the record cut,
And silence sounds no worse than cheers
After earth has stopped the ears:

Now you will not swell the rout
Of lads that wore their honours out,
Runners whom renown outran
And the name died before the man.

So set, before its echoes fade,
The fleet foot on the sill of shade,
And hold to the low lintel up
The still-defended challenge-cup.

And round that early-laurelled head
Will flock to gaze the strengthless dead,
And find unwithered on its curls
The garland briefer than a girl's.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title and context title, st. 1

    A Shropshire Lad, Housman's sequence of sixty-three lyrics, was privately financed and published in 1896. Many of the poems concern young men dying early. This one is an apostrophe: the speaker, one of the townsmen, addresses the dead athlete. Its argument, that early death preserves glory, has classical roots in the Greek idea that those loved by the gods die young, and in the tradition of the athletic victory ode; but the tone is ambiguous, praising the athlete in a way that is also bleak. Some biographers relate Housman's preoccupation with young men to his love for his friend Moses Jackson, though this poem is not about any known person.

    A. E. Housman, A Shropshire Lad (1896), no. XIX, Project Gutenberg text; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  2. Metre and couplets st. 1–7

    Each stanza rhymes aabb in four-stress lines; many begin with a stressed syllable (headless iambic or trochaic tetrameter), giving the brisk, song-like movement typical of A Shropshire Lad. The epigrammatic couplets suit the poem's paradoxes ('early though the laurel grows / It withers quicker than the rose'). Critics note the contrast between the jaunty, almost ballad-like music and the grave subject, which creates much of the poem's irony.

    Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  3. chaired l. 2

    The OED gives 'chair' (verb) as to carry someone in a chair or on shoulders in triumph, a custom for winners of elections and contests. The image of being borne 'shoulder-high' through the market-place of a country town is repeated in stanza 2 for the funeral, where the dead runner is carried in his coffin. The parallel between triumph and funeral procession is the poem's central structural irony.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  4. shoulder-high … home l. 6

    Stanza 2 echoes stanza 1 with chiasmus: 'home we brought you shoulder-high' becomes 'Shoulder-high we bring you home'. 'Home' now means the grave, and 'threshold' the entrance to the tomb. 'The road all runners come' is death, the course that every runner eventually completes; the race imagery turns into the common journey to the grave. The shift from past ('brought') to present ('bring') marks the change from memory to the funeral now taking place.

    A. E. Housman, A Shropshire Lad (1896), no. XIX, Project Gutenberg text; Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  5. a stiller town l. 8

    The metaphor of the grave as a town or city of the dead is ancient: the Greek 'necropolis' means city of the dead. Housman's comparative 'stiller' implies the living town is already relatively still, so the line combines rural understatement with finality. The dead become 'townsmen' of a new community, an idea developed in stanza 7, where they 'flock to gaze' at the newcomer.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  6. Smart lad … betimes l. 9

    'Betimes' (archaic) means early or in good season. The colloquial 'Smart lad' praises the athlete for a shrewd move, as if dying young were a tactical decision in a race. The phrase is shockingly matter-of-fact, which is the source of much of the poem's dark irony. Readers debate whether this praise is sincere consolation or an ironic exposure of the speaker's bleak view; Housman's poems characteristically leave both possibilities open.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  7. laurel … rose ll. 11–12

    In ancient Greece victors at the Pythian Games at Delphi were crowned with laurel, sacred to Apollo since the myth of Daphne, transformed into a laurel tree (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1). The rose, by contrast, is the classic emblem of transient beauty and youth. The paradox inverts expectation: the evergreen laurel, image of enduring fame, fades faster than the short-lived rose. Glory 'does not stay' in the fields of competition, so the young athlete who dies at the moment of victory escapes the slow eclipse that awaits champions who live on.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452–567 (Apollo and Daphne; the laurel); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  8. the record cut l. 14

    To 'cut' a record means to lower or better the existing best time, thereby breaking it. Sporting records and times were becoming a matter of public interest in the 1890s with the growth of organised athletics. The dead athlete's eyes, shut by 'the shady night', will not witness his own achievement being superseded. The following couplet ('silence sounds no worse than cheers / After earth has stopped the ears') takes the same consolation from hearing: death makes failure and acclaim equal.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  9. rout l. 17

    'Rout' (from Old French route, a company or band) meant a disorderly crowd or company, a sense now archaic and separate from 'rout' meaning defeat. To 'swell the rout' is to add to the crowd of former champions who 'wore their honours out', who lived to see their fame fade. The word possibly also carries the military sense of a disorderly retreat, which fits the image of champions falling back from glory.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  10. Runners whom renown outran l. 19

    The race metaphor applies to fame itself: the athletes who once outran rivals are now outrun by their renown, which passes on to others. The following line, 'And the name died before the man', states the plain meaning: their fame dies before they do. The neat inversion is typical of Housman's epigrammatic technique and his debt to Latin and Greek elegy.

    Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  11. the sill of shade l. 22

    A 'sill' is the threshold or lower beam of a doorway, and the 'lintel' is the upper beam. 'Shade' carries its classical sense of a ghost or the underworld (Latin umbra, the shades of the dead). The athlete sets his 'fleet foot', the swift foot of the runner, on the threshold of the land of the dead. In the following couplet he holds up the trophy 'to the low lintel' of a low doorway, which suggests the grave or tomb, so the door is both the house of Hades and the cramped entrance of the grave.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  12. challenge-cup l. 24

    A challenge cup is a trophy that remains with the winner only until he is defeated in a later contest. 'Still-defended' means not yet lost, and also, with a pun on 'still', 'defended forever in stillness'. The athlete's death makes his tenure permanent: he will never be challenged again. The image combines the modern sporting world of trophies with the classical image of the dead bringing their attributes into Hades.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'

  13. early-laurelled head l. 25

    The compound adjective recalls 'early though the laurel grows' and makes the crown a sign both of precocious triumph and early death. The image of the shades crowding to look at the newcomer resembles scenes in Homer's and Virgil's underworlds, where the ghosts gather around a living or newly arrived visitor (Odyssey 11; Aeneid 6). The athlete becomes an object of wonder among the dead, as he was among the living townsfolk.

    Homer, Odyssey 10.521, 10.536, 11.29, 11.49 (νεκύων ἀμενηνὰ κάρηνα), Perseus Digital Library; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  14. the strengthless dead l. 26

    Homer repeatedly calls the dead in Hades 'nekuon amenena karena', usually translated 'the strengthless (or powerless) heads of the dead' (Odyssey 10.521, 536; 11.29, 49). Housman, a professional classical scholar, uses the epithet to place his Shropshire athlete in the Homeric underworld. The contrast is pointed: the dead lack strength, while the athlete was strong; yet he is now one of them. The shades 'flock to gaze', like the ghosts that crowd around Odysseus.

    Homer, Odyssey 10.521, 10.536, 11.29, 11.49 (νεκύων ἀμενηνὰ κάρηνα), Perseus Digital Library; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX

  15. The garland briefer than a girl's l. 28

    The last line is a paradox. The laurel is 'unwithered', preserved by the athlete's death, yet it is 'briefer than a girl's' garland. One reading: the laurel, a wreath that lasts only briefly, is compared with a girl's garland of flowers, proverbially short-lived; death has kept it 'unwithered' while its natural life is briefer still. Another reading takes 'a girl's' to refer to a girl's bloom or beauty, suggesting that the athlete's glory, like his life, was as brief as youthful beauty proverbially is. A third emphasises the feminine comparison, which slightly diminishes the heroic athlete. The closure is deliberately ambiguous and ironic rather than consoling.

    Interesting Literature, 'A Short Analysis of A. E. Housman's To an Athlete Dying Young'; Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), text and commentary on ASL XIX
