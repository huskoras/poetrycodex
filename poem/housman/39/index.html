Love

XL

by A. E. Housman 1859–1936

From A Shropshire Lad

Love

Into my heart an air that kills
From yon far country blows:
What are those blue remembered hills,
What spires, what farms are those?

That is the land of lost content,
I see it shining plain,
The happy highways where I went
And cannot come again.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A Shropshire Lad XL: date and place l. 1

    Like the rest of A Shropshire Lad, the poem bears only a Roman numeral. The Housman Society commentary, and the Wikipedia article citing Burnett's edition, date it to 1886, which would make it a decade older than the bulk of the sequence (mostly written in 1895). Housman grew up in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, and knew Shropshire chiefly as the hills on his western horizon; his Shropshire is half imaginary, a landscape of memory rather than of local knowledge. The poem is the purest expression of that idea: the land is seen only from afar and only in recollection.

    Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005); The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants); Bromsgrove District Council, Housman Trail guide

  2. an air that kills l. 1

    The paradox of a killing air is the poem's first shock. 'Air' most obviously means a wind blowing 'From yon far country'; but it also means a tune, and the Housman Society asks students whether the second sense makes the poem a comment on poetry itself, a song that hurts. The killing may be figurative (heartbreak) and is often read as such; Hoagwood stresses that the memories here are destructive rather than consoling. Burnett notes close verbal parallels in Gray's Eton College ode, where gales from the 'happy hills' of childhood seem to bring 'a second spring'; Housman reverses Gray's momentary refreshment into pain.

    The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants); Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad

  3. yon far country l. 2

    'Yon', short for 'yonder', points to something distant but visible; the Housman Society marks it as regional. Its old-fashioned ring belongs to the ballad diction of the whole sequence. 'Far country' is a biblical phrase: in the parable of the prodigal son the younger son 'took his journey into a far country' (Luke 15:13, KJV). If the echo is heard, Housman inverts it: here the far country is the lost home, and there is no return. The present tense 'blows' makes the memory an event happening now.

    The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Luke 15:13 (KJV)

  4. blue remembered hills l. 3

    Hills seen at a distance appear blue through the haze of the atmosphere, so 'blue' is first a literal visual fact. 'Remembered' transfers the act of memory onto the hills themselves, which exist now only as recollected objects. The Housman Society records the rejected drafts, 'faint bewildered' and 'dissolving', which show Housman moving from vagueness to a precise, plain epithet. The phrase gave Dennis Potter the title of his television play Blue Remembered Hills (1979), about childhood, among many other borrowings. Housman's childhood view from Bromsgrove of the Shropshire hills on the western skyline is the usual biographical gloss.

    The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants); Bromsgrove District Council, Housman Trail guide; Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005)

  5. Questions and answer; ballad metre l. 4; st. 1-2

    The first stanza consists of questions ('What are those ... What spires, what farms'), as though the speaker sees a landscape and asks what it is; the second answers, 'That is the land of lost content'. The form is ballad or common metre, alternating four- and three-stress lines, but, as Hoagwood points out, unlike most ballads it rhymes the first and third lines as well as the second and fourth (kills/hills, blows/those). Drafts tried 'towns', 'fields' and 'paths' before Housman settled on 'spires' and 'farms', church and farmstead, the two poles of a rural parish.

    Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005); The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants)

  6. the land of lost content l. 5

    'Content' here is the noun meaning contentment, happiness, stressed 'con-TENT'. The answer reveals that the hills are not simply a place but a state of being that has been lost: childhood, or innocence, or an unreflecting happiness. The phrase has come to stand for Housman's Shropshire as a whole. Critics read the poem's contrast as one between the innocent country youth the speaker was and the urban maturity he now endures (Gardner, Leggett), and its tone as bitter and despairing rather than merely wistful (Hoagwood). Burnett also lists Keats's 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' and Christina Rossetti among verbal analogues.

    Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  7. shining plain l. 6

    The primary sense of 'plain' is adverbial: the speaker sees the land clearly, 'shining' as if lit by the sun of memory. The clarity is the cruelty, since it shows exactly what cannot be reached. Some readers also hear the noun 'plain', a level country, though the landscape of the poem is one of hills, so this is at most a ghostly pun. The line answers the haziness of 'blue' in l. 3: in stanza 1 the hills are distant and uncertain; in stanza 2, recognised, they shine.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants)

  8. happy highways l. 7

    The alliteration makes the line the most musical in the poem. 'Highways' is grander than the 'footpaths' and 'wayside' of the drafts recorded by the Housman Society; the roads become the open ways of youth. The adjective 'happy' is transferred from walker to road, as 'remembered' was from speaker to hills, so that the landscape carries the emotions the speaker has lost. The phrase 'where I went' is simple past: those journeys are finished.

    The Housman Society, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (study notes with manuscript variants); Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005)

  9. And cannot come again l. 8

    The ending is flat and absolute. The speaker 'cannot come again' to the happy highways, which recalls 'Twenty will not come again' in Loveliest of trees (ASL II) and the many poems of the sequence about time that does not return. Burnett notes a close parallel with Ophelia's song in Hamlet (4.5), 'And will he not come again?', which mourns her dead father; if the echo is intended, the speaker mourns himself. Harold Bloom, protesting the poem's critical neglect, professed himself 'puzzled how any lover of poetry could fail to respond' to it (as quoted in the Wikipedia article).

    Wikipedia, 'Into my heart an air that kills' (summarising Burnett 1997, Hoagwood 1995, Leggett 1978, Bloom 2005); Archie Burnett, ed., The Poems of A. E. Housman (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), commentary on A Shropshire Lad
