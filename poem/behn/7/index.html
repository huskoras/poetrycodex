Love

Love Arm’d

by Aphra Behn 1640–1689

From Poems upon Several Occasions (1684)

Love

Love in Fantastique Triumph satt,
Whilst Bleeding Hearts a round him flow’d,
For whom Fresh paines he did Create,
And strange Tryanick power he show’d;
From thy Bright Eyes he took his fire,
Which round about, in sport he hurl’d;
But ‘twas from mine he took desire,
Enough to undo the Amorous World.

From me he took his sighs and tears,
From thee his Pride and Crueltie;
From me his Languishments and Feares,
And every Killing Dart from thee;
Thus thou and I, the God have arm’d,
And sett him up a Deity;
But my poor Heart alone is harm’d,
Whilst thine the Victor is, and free.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Abdelazer and 'Love Arm'd' l. 1

    Abdelazer, or The Moor's Revenge, Behn's only tragedy, was first acted by the Duke's Company at Dorset Garden on 3 July 1676, with Thomas Betterton as the Moor; it adapts the older play Lust's Dominion. The play opens in 'A Rich Chamber' where Abdelazer sits sullen while 'still Musick' plays: this song. The Queen of Spain, who loves him, then enters, so that the song's tale of a lover tortured by a proud, cruel beloved frames their relationship. Behn reprinted the lyric in 1684 as 'Love Arm'd', the text and spelling the archive follows. Purcell wrote music for a 1695 revival of the play.

    Poem of the Week (potw.org), 'Love in fantastic triumph sat', notes on the 1677 and 1684 texts; Wikipedia, 'Abdelazer' (premiere, source, Purcell's 1695 music); Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994); Montague Summers, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, 6 vols (London, 1915), vol. 2 (Abdelazer)

  2. Fantastique l. 1

    In the seventeenth century 'fantastic' meant existing only in the imagination, unreal, and also fanciful, capricious, extravagantly strange (OED, fantastic adj.). Both senses matter: Love's triumph is a cruel and whimsical show, and also a thing of fancy, a deity the lovers have imagined into power, as the second stanza reveals ('And sett him up a Deity'). The French spelling '-ique' was common at the time.

    OED, 'fantastic, adj.'; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  3. Love in Triumph l. 1

    A Roman triumph was the victory parade of a general, with captives led in chains. Ovid pictures Cupid riding in such a triumph, with captive young men and women and 'Blanditiae' and 'Error' in his train (Amores 1.2.19-52), and Petrarch's Triumph of Love (Triumphus Cupidinis) made the procession a Renaissance commonplace, often painted. Behn's Love 'sat' in triumph with 'Bleeding Hearts' about him: the beloved and lover supply the god's weapons, and the lover is the captive.

    Ovid, Amores 1.2.19-52 (Cupid's triumph); Petrarch, Triumphus Cupidinis (Trionfi); Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  4. Bleeding Hearts a round him l. 2

    The bleeding heart pierced by Cupid's arrow was a stock emblem of love-suffering. The hearts 'flow'd' around the god like a stream or a crowd of suppliants, an image at once pretty and violent. The 1684 spelling 'a round' for 'around' is preserved. The opening thus presents Love's power as a court of suffering subjects, which the song will trace back to its origin in two particular lovers.

    Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994); OED, 'bleeding, adj.'

  5. Fresh paines l. 3

    Comparison of the two early printings shows that the only verbal difference is in this line: Abdelazer (1677) reads 'pays', and Poems upon Several Occasions (1684) has 'paines'; it is an open question whether the later reading is a correction. Love creates 'fresh pains' for the bleeding hearts, renewing their suffering; the line sets up the ensuing account of Love's 'Tryanick power'. Spelling and punctuation also vary between the two texts.

    Poem of the Week (potw.org), 'Love in fantastic triumph sat', notes on the 1677 and 1684 texts; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  6. Tryanick power l. 4

    'Tyrannic' means tyrannical, despotic (OED, tyrannic adj.); the archive's spelling 'Tryanick' is a variant or misprint for 'Tyrannick'. The tyranny of Love over his subjects was a commonplace of court song, and in Abdelazer, a play of usurpation and political murder, the word gains political resonance. The 'strange' power is Love's ability to make his subjects love their own suffering.

    OED, 'tyrannic, adj.'; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  7. From thy Bright Eyes he took his fire l. 5

    In Petrarchan convention love enters through the eyes, and the beloved's eyes shoot fire or darts. Behn divides the roles exactly: from 'thy' eyes Love takes fire, from 'mine' desire. The beloved supplies the brilliance that inflames others, the speaker the longing that makes the god dangerous. 'In sport' stresses the god's childish cruelty, as Cupid is traditionally a wanton boy.

    Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994); Ovid, Amores 1.2.19-52 (Cupid's triumph)

  8. Enough to undo the Amorous World l. 8

    'Undo' in its strong older sense means to ruin or destroy, often of a person brought to grief by love (OED, undo v.). The hyperbole makes the speaker's desire the force that turns Love's fire into a world-wide catastrophe. The line runs to ten syllables, two more than the other even lines of the stanza, so the stanza ends on a heavier beat. The first stanza closes on this image of universal ruin before the second explains how it came about.

    OED, 'undo, v.'; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  9. Languishments l. 11

    'Languishment' means the condition of languishing, pining or wasting with love (OED, languishment n.). It belongs to the vocabulary of love-sickness in Restoration songs and heroic drama. The second stanza sets out the exchange: from the speaker come sighs, tears, languishments and fears; from the beloved, pride, cruelty and 'every Killing Dart'. The balance is rhetorical, but the burden is unequal.

    OED, 'languishment, n.'; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  10. every Killing Dart l. 12

    Ovid tells how Cupid shot Apollo with a golden arrow that kindles love and Daphne with a leaden one that repels it (Metamorphoses 1.468-71). Behn gives every 'Killing' dart to the beloved: his or her cruelty is what makes love fatal. 'Killing' was also hyperbolic Restoration slang for overwhelmingly attractive, so the word has a double edge. The beloved kills by beauty and by disdain alike.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.468-71; OED, 'killing, adj.'; Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  11. sett him up a Deity l. 14

    The second stanza's argument reveals what the first had concealed: Love is armed only with what 'thou and I' have given him. To 'set up' a god is to make an idol, a phrase with biblical overtones of idolatry (e.g. Judges 18:30-31). The song thus offers a quietly sceptical psychology of desire, close to libertine thinking in Behn's circle, in which the deity of love is human feeling raised into an external power.

    Judges 18:30-31 (KJV); Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)

  12. my poor Heart alone is harm'd l. 15

    The closing couplet turns the symmetrical exchange of the stanza into an injustice. Readers have debated whose voice this is. In the play, its placing before the Queen's entrance to the indifferent Abdelazer suggests a woman's unrequited passion for a man; reprinted as a lyric, the speaker's gender is unmarked, and many modern readers, following Behn's reputation as the first Englishwoman to live by her pen, read it as a woman's comment on the inequalities of love. Others stress its conventionality as a Restoration song. The poem's force lies in the plain final contrast.

    Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994); Poem of the Week (potw.org), 'Love in fantastic triumph sat', notes on the 1677 and 1684 texts; Wikipedia, 'Abdelazer' (premiere, source, Purcell's 1695 music)

  13. Whilst thine the Victor is, and free l. 16

    The last line completes the triumph image. In a Roman triumph the victor rides free and the captives walk in chains; so here the beloved's heart is the 'Victor', 'free' from love, while the speaker's heart alone is wounded and enslaved. The ending returns to the opening's 'Triumph' and makes clear that the god's victory is really the beloved's. The god is a figurehead; the beloved holds the real power.

    Ovid, Amores 1.2.19-52 (Cupid's triumph); Janet Todd, ed., The Works of Aphra Behn, vol. 1: Poetry (London: Pickering, 1992), and The Poems of Aphra Behn: A Selection (1994)
