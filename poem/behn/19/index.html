Love

The Disappointment

by Aphra Behn 1640–1689

From Poems upon Several Occasions (1684)

Love

One day the Amorous Lysander
By an impatient Passion sway’d,
Surpriz’d fair Cloris, that lov’d Maid,
Who could defend her self no longer.
All things did with his Love conspire;
The gilded Planet of the Day,
In his gay Chariot drawn by Fire,
Was now descending to the Sea,
And left no Light to guide the World,
But what from Cloris Brighter Eyes was hurld.

In a lone Thicket made for Love,
Silent as yielding Maids Consent,
She with a Charming Languishment,
Permits his Force, yet gently strove;
Her Hands his Bosom softly meet,
But not to put him back design’d,
Rather to draw ‘em on inclin’d:
Whilst he lay trembling at her Feet,
Resistance ‘tis in vain to show;
She wants the pow’r to say--Ah! What d’ye do?

Her Bright Eyes sweet, and yet severe,
Where Love and Shame confus’dly strive,
Fresh Vigor to Lysander give;
And breathing faintly in his Ear,
She cry’d--Cease, Cease--your vain Desire,
Or I’ll call out--What would you do?
My Dearer Honour ev’n to You
I cannot, must not give--Retire,
Or take this Life, whose chiefest part
I gave you with the Conquest of my Heart.

But he as much unus’d to Fear,
As he was capable of Love,
The blessed minutes to improve,
Kisses her Mouth, her Neck, her Hair;
Each Touch her new Desire Alarms,
His burning trembling Hand he prest
Upon her swelling Snowy Brest,
While she lay panting in his Arms.
All her Unguarded Beauties lie
The Spoils and Trophies of the Enemy.

And now without Respect or Fear,
He seeks the Object of his Vows,
(His Love no Modesty allows)
By swift degrees advancing--where
His daring Hand that Altar seiz’d,
Where Gods of Love do sacrifice:
That Awful Throne, that Paradice
Where Rage is calm’d, and Anger pleas’d;
That Fountain where Delight still flows,
And gives the Universal World Repose.

Her Balmy Lips encount’ring his,
Their Bodies, as their Souls, are joyn’d;
Where both in Transports Unconfin’d
Extend themselves upon the Moss.
Cloris half dead and breathless lay;
Her soft Eyes cast a Humid Light,
Such as divides the Day and Night;
Or falling Stars, whose Fires decay:
And now no signs of Life she shows,
But what in short-breath’d Sighs returns and goes.

He saw how at her Length she lay;
He saw her rising Bosom bare;
Her loose thin Robes, through which appear
A Shape design’d for Love and Play;
Abandon’d by her Pride and Shame.
She does her softest Joys dispence,
Off’ring her Virgin-Innocence
A Victim to Loves Sacred Flame;
While the o’er-Ravish’d Shepherd lies
Unable to perform the Sacrifice.

Ready to taste a thousand Joys,
The too transported hapless Swain
Found the vast Pleasure turn’d to Pain;
Pleasure which too much Love destroys
The willing Garments by he laid,
And Heaven all open’d to his view.
Mad to possess, himself he threw
On the Defenceless Lovely Maid.
But Oh what envying God conspires
To snatch his Power, yet leave him the Desire!

Nature’s Support, (without whose Aid
She can no Humane Being give)
It self now wants the Art to live;
Faintness its slack’ned Nerves invade:
In vain th’ inraged Youth essay’d
To call its fleeting Vigor back,
No motion ‘twill from Motion take;
Excess of Love his Love betray’d:
In vain he Toils, in vain Commands
The Insensible fell weeping in his Hand.

In this so Amorous Cruel Strife,
Where Love and Fate were too severe,
The poor Lysander in despair
Renounc’d his Reason with his Life:
Now all the brisk and active Fire
That should the Nobler Part inflame,
Serv’d to increase his Rage and Shame,
And left no Spark for New Desire:
Not all her Naked Charms cou’d move
Or calm that Rage that had debauch’d his Love.

Cloris returning from the Trance
Which Love and soft Desire had bred,
Her timerous Hand she gently laid
(Or guided by Design or Chance)
Upon that Fabulous Priapus;
That Potent God, as Poets feign;
But never did young Shepherdess,
Gath’ring of Fern upon the Plain,
More nimbly draw her Fingers back,
Finding beneath the verdant Leaves a Snake:

Than Cloris her fair Hand withdrew,
Finding that God of her Desires
Disarm’d of all his Awful Fires,
And Cold as Flow’rs bath’d in the Morning Dew.
Who can the Nymph’s Confusion guess?
The Blood forsook the hinder Place,
And strew’d with Blushes all her Face,
Which both Disdain and Shame exprest:
And from Lysander’s Arms she fled,
Leaving him fainting on the Gloomy Bed.

Like Lightning through the Grove she hies,
Or Daphne from the Delphick God,
No Print upon the grassey Road
She leaves, t’ instruct Pursuing Eyes.
The Wind that wanton’d in her Hair,
And with her Ruffled Garments plaid,
Discover’d in the Flying Maid
All that the Gods e’er made, if Fair.
So Venus, when her Love was slain,
With Fear and Haste flew o’er the Fatal Plain.

The Nymph’s Resentments none but I
Can well Imagine or Condole:
But none can guess Lysander’s Soul,
But those who sway’d his Destiny.
His silent Griefs swell up to Storms,
And not one God his Fury spares;
He curs’d his Birth, his Fate, his Stars;
But more the Shepherdess’s Charms,
Whose soft bewitching Influence
Had Damn’d him to the Hell of Impotence.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Publication and genre l. 1

    The 'imperfect enjoyment' was a Restoration genre about male impotence at the moment of seduction, going back to Ovid's Amores 3.7 and represented by Rochester's 'The Imperfect Enjoyment', printed in the same 1680 volume. Behn adapted part of the French poem 'L'occasion perdue recouverte', attributed to Jean Benech de Cantenac. Her version is distinguished by attention to the woman's experience: Cloris's confusion and disappointment, rather than the man's humiliation alone, become the subject. Jessica Munns (in Aphra Behn Studies, ed. Janet Todd, 1996) notes how unusual its focus on female sexual experience was for its time.

    'The Disappointment (Aphra Behn)', Wikipedia (publication, Cantenac source, critical readings by Munns and others); The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'; The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester, ed. Harold Love (Oxford University Press, 1999), notes on 'The Imperfect Enjoyment' and the 1680 Poems on Several Occasions; Ovid, Amores 3.7, The Latin Library

  2. Lysander and Cloris ll. 1-3

    Lysander (from Greek) and Cloris (Chloris, a Greek goddess of flowers) are stock names from pastoral poetry and romance. The pastoral setting (thicket, moss, shepherdess gathering fern) creates a distance that allows erotic frankness, a strategy Behn uses in many poems. Lysander 'surprises' Cloris, i.e. comes upon her unexpectedly or takes her unawares; the verb sets up the poem's uneasy blend of seduction and force.

    The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'surprise'

  3. Stanza form st. 1

    Each stanza closes with a couplet whose final line is lengthened (for example 'But what from Cloris Brighter Eyes was hurld'), giving a falling cadence. The brisk tetrameter suits narrative and erotic suspense, and the lengthened last lines allow ironic summaries. The scheme is maintained throughout the fourteen stanzas, and the regularity of the form contrasts comically with the irregularity of the event it narrates.

    The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  4. The sun's chariot ll. 6-8

    In classical myth the sun-god drives a fiery chariot across the sky and descends into Ocean at evening. The elevated diction ('gilded Planet', 'gay Chariot drawn by Fire') provides the setting, and when only 'Cloris Brighter Eyes' give light the hyperbole flatters the heroine in the Petrarchan way. The Apollo allusion anticipates the later comparison of Cloris to Daphne fleeing 'the Delphick God'.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (Apollo and Daphne), The Latin Library; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  5. Consent and force ll. 12-14

    Restoration erotic verse often presents a woman's resistance as a sign of modesty that conceals desire, and the lines follow that convention: 'Silent as yielding Maids Consent', her hands meet his bosom 'not to put him back'. Her spoken protest in the third stanza ('Cease, Cease') complicates this, and the poem invites debate about whether Behn reproduces the male fantasy of the willing victim or exposes its contradictions by entering the woman's perspective. Some commentators note how honour and shame are treated as forces acting on Cloris from outside.

    'The Disappointment (Aphra Behn)', Wikipedia (publication, Cantenac source, critical readings by Munns and others); The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  6. Love against shame l. 22

    Shame and honour were the terms in which a woman's sexual reputation was discussed. Cloris pleads that her 'Dearer Honour' she 'cannot, must not give' and offers her life instead, the conventional heroic choice. The poem later notes that she is 'Abandon'd by her Pride and Shame' (st. 7), suggesting desire's victory, and finally shame returns in her blushes at the discovery of Lysander's failure.

    The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'; 'The Disappointment (Aphra Behn)', Wikipedia (publication, Cantenac source, critical readings by Munns and others)

  7. Religious metaphors for the body ll. 45-50

    The blazon was the poetic catalogue of a woman's body; here the most intimate part receives sacred and royal titles ('Altar', 'Awful Throne', 'Paradice'). 'Awful' means awe-inspiring. The religious diction of sacrifice is developed in the following stanzas, where Lysander lies 'Unable to perform the Sacrifice'. The metaphors combine mock-solemnity with a genuinely elevated idea of sexual pleasure as giving 'the Universal World Repose'.

    The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'awful'

  8. The failed sacrifice l. 70

    Cloris offers her 'Virgin-Innocence' as 'A Victim to Loves Sacred Flame', the language of ritual sacrifice, but the priest fails. 'O'er-Ravish'd' means carried away by excessive rapture, the irony being that excess of desire produces incapacity. The paradox 'Pleasure which too much Love destroys' (st. 8) is the genre's standard explanation, found in Ovid and Rochester. Behn's word 'Ravish'd' also glances back at the force Lysander used, so the vocabulary of violence returns as the vocabulary of his failure.

    Ovid, Amores 3.7, The Latin Library; The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester, ed. Harold Love (Oxford University Press, 1999), notes on 'The Imperfect Enjoyment' and the 1680 Poems on Several Occasions; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  9. Periphrasis for the penis ll. 81-90

    The stanza describes the failure in elevated, scientific and military language: 'slack'ned Nerves', 'fleeting Vigor', 'Motion' (a philosophical term borrowed from contemporary mechanical philosophy). 'Essay'd' means tried. The ironic line 'Excess of Love his Love betray'd' summarises the paradox, and 'The Insensible fell weeping in his Hand' makes the failure explicit in a deliberately grotesque image. Here 'Insensible' is a noun: the organ that has lost all feeling.

    The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'essay'

  10. Priapus l. 105

    Priapus was worshipped as a guardian of gardens and flocks and was proverbial for sexual potency; Latin Priapea poems are spoken by him. Behn's 'Fabulous', meaning legendary but also mythical and false, mocks the gap between the 'Potent God, as Poets feign' and the reality Cloris discovers. The comic irony is that the classical god of virility here represents failure.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fabulous'; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  11. The snake in the grass ll. 107-110

    Virgil's shepherd warns the boys gathering flowers and strawberries that 'a cold snake lies hidden in the grass' (Eclogues 3.92-93). Behn's simile turns a pastoral commonplace into a sexual joke: Cloris draws back her hand as quickly as a girl gathering fern would from a snake, but because the 'God of her Desires' is 'Cold as Flow'rs bath'd in the Morning Dew'. The comparison combines comedy with the poem's sympathy for Cloris's shock.

    Virgil, Eclogues 3.92-93, The Latin Library; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  12. Daphne and Apollo l. 122

    In Metamorphoses 1, the nymph Daphne flees Apollo, the god of Delphi, and is turned into a laurel. The allusion is ironic: Daphne flees a pursuing god's desire, whereas Cloris flees the absence of it. The wind that reveals her body as she runs ('Discover'd in the Flying Maid') comes directly from Ovid's description of Daphne's flight, where the breeze bares her limbs.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (Apollo and Daphne), The Latin Library; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  13. Venus and Adonis ll. 129-130

    In Metamorphoses 10, Venus, hearing the groans of Adonis gored by a boar, hurries back to find him dead. The comparison makes Lysander's failure a kind of death and lends Cloris's flight the dignity of mythological grief, with mock-heroic exaggeration. 'Love' here means the lover. Shakespeare's Venus and Adonis was a popular treatment of the same story. The Venus simile ends the narrative of Cloris; the final stanza turns to the narrator and to Lysander.

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.519-739 (Venus and Adonis), The Latin Library; The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  14. The narrator's voice ll. 131-34

    Only in the last stanza does the narrator reveal a sympathy with Cloris that critics read as the voice of a woman poet. Male poets in the genre (Ovid, Rochester) concentrate on the man's shame; Behn claims special insight into the woman's 'Resentments', while Lysander's soul, she says, only the powers who 'sway'd his Destiny' can guess. Munns and others argue that the poem's real subject is the woman's disappointment, which the title can name.

    'The Disappointment (Aphra Behn)', Wikipedia (publication, Cantenac source, critical readings by Munns and others); The Works of Aphra Behn, ed. Janet Todd, vol. 1: Poetry (Pickering & Chatto, 1992), notes to 'The Disappointment'

  15. The final blame l. 140

    The male speakers of 'imperfect enjoyment' poems typically blame the woman's beauty or their own excess for their failure. Lysander's rage at 'the Shepherdess's Charms' is the standard move, but after the narrator's declared sympathy for Cloris it reads as ironic: the man transfers his shame to the woman. 'Influence' keeps its astrological sense of a power flowing from the stars, so her charms are compared to malignant stars. The religious vocabulary ends in 'Hell'.

    'The Disappointment (Aphra Behn)', Wikipedia (publication, Cantenac source, critical readings by Munns and others); The Works of John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester, ed. Harold Love (Oxford University Press, 1999), notes on 'The Imperfect Enjoyment' and the 1680 Poems on Several Occasions; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'influence'
