Time & Brevity

The Ballad of Dead Ladies

by François Villon 1431–after 1463

Translated by Dante Gabriel Rossetti (1870) · From A Victorian Anthology, 1837–1895 (1895)

Time & Brevity

Tell me now in what hidden way is
Lady Flora the lovely Roman?
Where's Hipparchia, and where is Thais,
Neither of them the fairer woman?
Where is Echo, beheld of no man,
Only heard on river and mere—
She whose beauty was more than human? . . .
But where are the snows of yester-year?

Where's Héloise, the learned nun,
For whose sake Abeillard, I ween,
Lost manhood and put priesthood on?
(From Love he won such dule and teen!)
And where, I pray you, is the Queen
Who will'd that Buridan should steer
Sew'd in a sack's mouth down the Seine? . . .
But where are the snows of yester-year?

White Queen Blanche, like a queen of lilies,
With a voice like any mermaiden—
Bertha Broadfoot, Beatrice, Alice,
And Ennengarde the lady of Maine—
And that good Joan whom English men
At Rouen doom'd and burn'd her there—
Mother of God, where are they then? . . .
But where are the snows of yester-year?

Nay, never ask this week, fair lord,
Where they are gone, nor yet this year,
Save with thus much for an overword—
But where are the snows of yester-year?

Study notes by the editors

  1. The ballade and Rossetti's version l. 1

    The poem stands in François Villon's Testament (1461), a mock-will interspersed with ballades, where it is followed by the companion Ballade des seigneurs du temps jadis. Villon headed it simply 'Ballade'; the familiar title 'des dames du temps jadis' was added by Clément Marot in his edition of 1533. Rossetti, who translated three Villon poems, called this 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies'; the Rossetti Archive notes it was first printed in the Penkill proofs of August 1869. The archive's text comes from a Victorian anthology of 1895. Villon's first line is 'Dictes moy ou, n'en quel pays', 'Tell me where, in what land'; Rossetti's 'hidden way' is his own.

    Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  2. ubi sunt l. 1

    The topos takes its name from Latin texts such as Baruch 3:16 in the Vulgate ('Ubi sunt principes gentium?', 'Where are the princes of the nations?') and Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy (2 m. 7, 'Where now are the bones of faithful Fabricius?'). St Paul's 'Where is the wise? where is the scribe?' (1 Corinthians 1:20) belongs to the same rhetoric. Medieval preachers and poets used it to teach the vanity of earthly glory. Villon's twist is to ask it of beautiful women, legendary and historical, and to answer only with an image of melted snow.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1 Corinthians 1:20; Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes

  3. Flora l. 2

    Medieval readers knew from Lactantius (Divine Institutes 1.20) the story of Flora, a rich courtesan who left her fortune to the Roman people, in whose honour the Floralia were supposedly founded; Juvenal (Satire 2.49) also names a woman called Flora. Some annotators link the name to the goddess of flowers instead. The courtesan reading fits the company she keeps in the stanza, Thaïs and the misidentified 'Archipiades'. Rossetti's 'Lady Flora' gives her a courtly title.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  4. Hipparchia (Villon's Archipiades) l. 3

    Boethius (Consolation 3, prose 8) cites the beautiful body of Alcibiades as an example of beauty that would look foul if seen through; medieval readers took 'Alcibiades' for a woman, and Villon's 'Archipiades' reflects that error. Rossetti replaced the name with Hipparchia, the Greek Cynic philosopher (fl. c. 300 BC) who married Crates of Thebes and adopted his way of life (Diogenes Laertius 6.96-98). P. F. Baum excused the substitution ('he cannot of course be blamed'), though it changes the line's sense, since Hipparchia was famous for philosophy rather than beauty.

    Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937); 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary

  5. Thais l. 3

    The Athenian Thaïs accompanied Alexander's army and, according to Plutarch (Alexander 38), urged the burning of Persepolis; Terence's comedy Eunuchus has a courtesan Thaïs, and Dante places a flatterer Thaïs in Inferno 18. The medieval Lives of the Fathers told of St Thaïs, a courtesan of Egypt converted to penitence. Editors note the ambiguity; the pairing with Flora suggests a famous beauty and courtesan. Villon calls her the 'cousine germaine' of Archipiades.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes

  6. Neither of them the fairer woman l. 4

    Villon's playful genealogy makes the courtesan Thaïs a cousin of the misunderstood 'Archipiades'. Rossetti dropped it, perhaps because the joke depends on the Alcibiades error he had already removed, and wrote instead that neither was fairer than the other. Baum regarded the omission of 'cousin german' as less excusable than the change of name. The change is a small example of how Rossetti's ballade smooths Villon's learned wit into romantic melancholy.

    Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary

  7. Echo ll. 5-7

    Villon's Echo speaks 'quant bruyt on maine / Dessus riviere ou sus estan' (when a noise is made over river or pool), and had beauty 'trop plus qu'humaine', far more than human. In Ovid (Met. 3.356-401) Echo, punished by Juno, can only repeat the last words of others; rejected by Narcissus, her body dwindles until only bones and voice remain. She is thus the perfect figure for the ballade: beauty that has vanished, leaving sound. Rossetti adds 'beheld of no man', drawing out the paradox of a famous beauty whom no one can see, and renders 'estan' (pond) by 'mere', a lake or pool (OED, mere, n.1).

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.356-401 (Latin text, The Latin Library); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'mere, n.1'

  8. the refrain and 'yester-year' l. 8

    'Antan' (from Latin ante annum, 'before this year') means last year, or time gone by. Rossetti is generally credited with coining yester-year, on the model of yesterday, to render it; Baum thought it 'too poetical for antan'. The refrain answers every question with an image: the women are gone as last year's snow is gone, beautiful, natural and irrecoverable. The ballade form requires the refrain to end each stanza and the envoi, and Rossetti keeps it in all four places.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'yesteryear, n.'; Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary

  9. Héloïse l. 9

    'Sage' in Villon means learned or wise; Héloïse was famous for her Latin learning and her letters. Her love affair with her teacher Peter Abelard (1079-1142), the secret marriage, and the castration of Abelard by her uncle's men in 1117 are told in Abelard's Historia calamitatum. Afterwards both entered religion: Héloïse at Argenteuil, later as abbess of the Paraclete; Abelard as a monk at Saint-Denis. Villon's 'Pour qui chastré fut et puis moyne' (for whom he was castrated and then a monk) is exact. Rossetti's archaic 'I ween' (I suppose) is a rhyme-filler.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  10. put priesthood on l. 11

    Abelard was already a cleric and teacher when he met Héloïse; what followed his castration was profession as a monk at the royal abbey of Saint-Denis. Rossetti's antithesis 'Lost manhood and put priesthood on' is memorable and probably chosen for its balance and internal rhyme. It also slightly obscures the role of Héloïse, whose own entry into religion was, in her letters, made out of obedience to Abelard.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  11. dule and teen l. 12

    'Dule' (also dool, dole) comes from Old French doel, dol, Latin dolus or dolor, and survived in Scots and northern English; 'teen' is Old English teona, injury or grief, used by Chaucer and Spenser and by Shakespeare ('to think o' th' teen that I have turn'd you to', The Tempest 1.2). Rossetti's alliterating, archaic pair gives the line a ballad flavour. Villon's 'Pour son amour eut ceste essoyne' uses essoyne, a legal word for an excuse for absence, here meaning hardship or affliction (Rychner and Henry).

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dool | dule, n.'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'teen, n.1'; Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary

  12. the queen and Buridan ll. 13-15

    Jean Buridan (c. 1300-c. 1361) was a famous Paris philosopher, rector of the university. The legend of the Tour de Nesle, where a queen took young men as lovers and had them drowned, was attached to various queens, most often to Marguerite of Burgundy, wife of Louis X, disgraced for adultery in 1314; Jeanne of Navarre has also been named. Villon's lines, 'Qui commanda que Buriden / Fust geté en ung sac en Saine', are among the earliest and best-known mentions of Buridan in the story. Rossetti's 'steer... down the Seine' adds grim irony.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  13. Queen Blanche l. 17

    Her identity is uncertain. Blanche of Castile (1188-1252), mother of St Louis, is the usual suggestion, but some editors think of a heroine of romance or song; the epithet 'blanche comme lis' plays on her name (blanche, white). Rossetti makes the pun explicit with 'White Queen Blanche'. 'Seraine' is the siren, the singing sea-creature; Rossetti's 'mermaiden' follows the medieval tendency to conflate sirens and mermaids.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  14. Bertha Broadfoot l. 19

    The epithet comes from the epic tradition, notably Adenet le Roi's thirteenth-century Berte aus grans piés, in which she is supplanted on her wedding night by an impostor and identified at last by her feet. She stands here among legendary and epic heroines rather than strictly historical ones. Rossetti's 'Broadfoot' is a neat English epithet. Like the names around it, hers is given without explanation, as if Villon's readers would know the stories from romance and song.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  15. Beatrice and Alice l. 19

    Beatrice and Aelis appear as heroines in the epic cycle of the Lorrains (Hervis de Metz), and editors generally favour such epic figures, consistent with Berthe in the same line. Popular reference works sometimes link 'Bietris' to Dante's Beatrice Portinari or 'Aliz' to Adelaide, queen of France, but the evidence is thin. The uncertainty is part of the poem's effect: even the names of the famous dead blur.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  16. Ermengarde of Maine l. 20

    Eremburga, heiress of the county of Maine, married Fulk V of Anjou, carrying Maine to the house of Anjou; she 'held' (tint) Maine in her own right. Rossetti's printed texts, including the 1887 Complete Poetical Works, read 'Ermengarde', and the archive's 'Ennengarde' (from its 1895 anthology source) is best taken as a typographical error. Her inclusion, a real but now obscure noblewoman, is typical of Villon's mixture of the legendary and the historical.

    D. G. Rossetti, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies', text from The Complete Poetical Works, ed. W. M. Rossetti (Boston, 1887), Representative Poetry Online; Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  17. Joan of Arc ll. 21-22

    Joan's death was only thirty years old when Villon wrote, and her condemnation had been annulled by the retrial of 1456, so her place among the 'ladies of time past' brings the list into living memory. Villon's epithet 'la bonne Lorraine' (the good woman of Lorraine) and his blunt naming of the English as her executioners carry political feeling. Rossetti keeps the substance ('whom English men / At Rouen doom'd and burn'd her there'), with an ungrammatical 'her' that reproduces the spoken, ballad-like syntax.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes

  18. Mother of God l. 23

    The appeal to the Virgin closes the catalogue of women by turning to the one woman of whom the question need not be asked, since she is in heaven. Rossetti's 'Mother of God' replaces Villon's 'sovereign Virgin'. The address gives the ubi sunt question a devotional colour without answering it: the refrain follows at once. The Virgin's presence also anticipates the Ballade pour prier Nostre Dame that Villon wrote for his mother, later in the Testament.

    Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; Barbara N. Sargent-Baur, trans., François Villon: Complete Poems (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1994), Testament ll. 329-56 and notes

  19. the envoi ll. 25-28

    The envoi (French 'sending') was traditionally addressed to the 'prince' of a poetic confraternity or to a patron. Villon tells him not to ask, this week or this year, where the ladies are, without being brought back to this refrain. Rossetti's 'fair lord' replaces 'Prince', and his third line, 'Save with thus much for an overword', is freer than Villon's 'Que ce reffrain ne vous remaine'; Baum found the change less excusable than others. The ballade's three eight-line stanzas and envoi share the same rhymes throughout, a constraint Rossetti approximates.

    Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937); Jean Rychner and Albert Henry, eds., Le Testament Villon, 2 vols (Geneva: Droz, 1974), ll. 329-56 and commentary; 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis', Wikipedia (summary of identifications and of Marot's 1533 title)

  20. overword l. 27

    The OED records 'overword' (Scots owerword) in the sense of the refrain of a song, and of a phrase constantly repeated. Rossetti's choice is a deliberate archaism, matching 'dule and teen' and 'I ween', and it makes the envoi self-referential: the only answer to the question is the refrain itself. The word is rare in English verse, and its Scots colouring sets Rossetti's version in the tradition of the border ballads. It replaces Villon's plain 'reffrain'.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'overword, n.'; Rossetti Archive, 'The Ballad of Dead Ladies (Francois Villon, 1450)', commentary quoting P. F. Baum's note in his edition of Poems, Ballads and Sonnets (1937)
