Death & Dying

Death's Summons

by Thomas Nashe 1567–1601?

From Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age, ed. A. H. Bullen (London: Nimmo, 1889)

Death & Dying

Adieu; farewell earth's bliss,
This world uncertain is:
Fond are life's lustful joys,
Death proves them all but toys.
None from his darts can fly:
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Rich men, trust not in wealth,
Gold cannot buy you health;
Physic himself must fade;
All things to end are made;
The plague full swift goes by;
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Beauty is but a flower,
Which wrinkles will devour:
Brightness falls from the air;
Queens have died young and fair;
Dust hath closed Helen's eye;
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Strength stoops unto the grave:
Worms feed on Hector brave;
Swords may not fight with fate:
Earth still holds ope her gate.
Come, come, the bells do cry;
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Wit with his wantonness,
Tasteth death's bitterness.
Hell's executioner
Hath no ears for to hear
What vain art can reply;
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Haste therefore each degree
To welcome destiny:
Heaven is our heritage,
Earth but a player's stage.
Mount we unto the sky;
I am sick, I must die.

Lord have mercy on us!

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song and its play l. 1

    The song comes from Thomas Nashe's 'Summer's Last Will and Testament', a pageant-like comedy in which the dying season Summer makes his will, with commentary by Henry VIII's jester Will Summers. References to plague, a royal progress and drought point to 1592, when London theatres were closed by plague; it was probably performed in the autumn at Croydon, the residence of Archbishop John Whitgift, partly by his household. It was printed in 1600. The title 'A Litany in Time of Plague' is a later editorial one; the archive files it as 'Death's Summons'. Taken out of the play, the lyric became one of the most famous Elizabethan poems on death.

    'Summer's Last Will and Testament', Wikipedia (date, Croydon, plague, printing 1600); R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament; A. H. Bullen (ed.), Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age (1889)

  2. 'Lord have mercy on us!' refrain

    The refrain echoes the responses of the Prayer Book Litany ('have mercy upon us') and the ancient Kyrie eleison ('Lord, have mercy'), which is why editors named the song a litany. In plague time the words had a grim second use: houses shut up with infection were marked with a red cross and the words 'Lord have mercy upon us', a practice recorded in plague orders and later remembered by Defoe in 'A Journal of the Plague Year'. Each stanza thus ends like a prayer said by the whole community and like an inscription on a sealed door.

    The Book of Common Prayer (1559/1662), The Litany; Daniel Defoe, A Journal of the Plague Year (1722) (Project Gutenberg); 'Summer's Last Will and Testament', Wikipedia (date, Croydon, plague, printing 1600)

  3. Metre and second refrain l. 6 and each stanza

    Each stanza has six lines of three stresses, rhyming in couplets (aabbcc), followed by the refrain. The heavy, end-stopped lines sound like a tolling bell or a funeral procession, and the brevity of the line allows no ornament. The fixed sixth line, 'I am sick, I must die', is individual and present-tense, while 'Lord have mercy on us!' is plural and liturgical, so the poem moves in every stanza from personal sentence to communal prayer. The structure suits song: the play text calls for the lyric to be sung.

    R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament; A. H. Bullen (ed.), Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age (1889)

  4. Fond; lustful l. 3

    In Elizabethan English 'fond' commonly meant foolish or doting (OED 'fond', adj.), and 'lustful' could mean pleasant or full of delight as well as lascivious. So the line says that life's pleasures are foolish, and Death proves them mere 'toys' (trifles). The opening stanza states the theme of the contemptus mundi tradition (contempt of the world): this world is uncertain and its joys vanish.

    Oxford English Dictionary; R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament

  5. Death's darts l. 5

    In medieval and Renaissance iconography Death often appears as a skeleton carrying a dart or arrow, with which he strikes his victims. Plague in particular was imagined as arrows shot from heaven, as in Psalm 91:5-6 ('the arrow that flieth by day ... the pestilence that walketh in darkness'). 'Fly' here means flee. The image makes Death a hunter or warrior and the human race his quarry, which fits the poem's theme of universal mortality.

    The Bible (KJV), Psalm 91:5-6; Oxford English Dictionary

  6. Physic l. 9

    'Physic' (medicine, the healing art) is personified as 'himself', so the line can mean both medicine and the doctor who practises it. Elizabethan physicians could do little against plague, and many fled London or died. The stanza addresses the rich, warning that gold (used in some cordials as a supposed remedy) cannot buy health, an idea from the Gospel warning against trusting in riches (Mark 10:24; 1 Timothy 6:17).

    Oxford English Dictionary; The Bible (KJV), 1 Timothy 6:17; R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament

  7. The plague of 1592-93 l. 11

    The outbreak of 1592-93 was one of the worst plagues of Elizabethan London; the theatres were closed for much of the period, which is why Nashe's entertainment was played in a private household outside the city. 'Full swift goes by' captures how quickly the disease killed, often within days. The line is the only one that names the plague directly, anchoring the conventional meditation on death in a specific crisis.

    'Summer's Last Will and Testament', Wikipedia (date, Croydon, plague, printing 1600); R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament

  8. Beauty as a flower l. 13

    The comparison of beauty to a flower that fades derives from Isaiah 40:6-8 ('All flesh is grass ... The grass withereth, the flower fadeth'), repeated in 1 Peter 1:24, and from the carpe diem tradition of Latin and Renaissance lyric. 'Which wrinkles will devour' turns the flower into prey of age. The stanza moves from beauty in general to the deaths of famous queens and of Helen herself.

    The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 40:6-8; The Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 1:24

  9. 'Brightness falls from the air' l. 15

    This is the most discussed line in Nashe. McKerrow, his great editor, suspected that the true reading might be 'hayre' (hair), a more obvious sense, while admitting that it would be far inferior poetically; with 'hair', the line would describe the fading of golden hair in age, matching 'wrinkles' and 'Helen's eye'. Read as 'air', it suggests the light dying from the sky at dusk, falling stars, or even the fall of angels, a mysterious image. William Empson cited it in 'Seven Types of Ambiguity' (1930), and Harry Morris (1959) argued that the line is less difficult than McKerrow and Empson implied. Most editors keep 'air'.

    Harry Morris, 'Nashe's "Brightnesse falls from the ayre"', Renaissance News (1959); R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament; William Empson, Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930)

  10. Ubi sunt l. 16

    The line belongs to the medieval 'ubi sunt' tradition ('where are they now?'), which asks where the great and beautiful of the past have gone, as in Villon's 'Ballade des dames du temps jadis' ('where are the snows of yesteryear?'). The line names no queens; it would have been a daring line in a performance before Elizabeth's archbishop, but it is general rather than pointed. Its plain monosyllables give it unusual pathos.

    R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament; A. H. Bullen (ed.), Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age (1889)

  11. Helen l. 17

    Helen, wife of Menelaus, whose abduction by Paris caused the Trojan War in Homer's 'Iliad', was the classical type of supreme beauty. 'Dust' is both the grave and the substance of mortal man ('dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return', Genesis 3:19). Marlowe's Faustus, from roughly the same years, was asking whether Helen's was 'the face that launched a thousand ships'; Nashe instead closes her eye in death.

    The Bible (KJV), Genesis 3:19; Homer, Iliad, Book 3 (Helen on the walls)

  12. Hector l. 20

    Hector, son of Priam and the greatest Trojan warrior, killed by Akhilleus in Iliad 22, stands for strength and courage, as Helen stands for beauty. The worms that feed on his body echo the biblical image of corruption in the grave ('though after my skin worms destroy this body', Job 19:26; 'the worm shall feed sweetly on him', Job 24:20). 'Brave' means valiant and also splendid. 'Swords may not fight with fate' makes the point explicit.

    The Bible (KJV), Job 24:20; Homer, Iliad, Book 22

  13. Earth's open gate l. 22

    'Still' has its common Elizabethan sense 'always, continually', and 'ope' is 'open'. The earth's gate is the grave, imagined as a door that never closes, an image that in plague time recalls the great pits dug to receive the dead. The next line, 'Come, come, the bells do cry', makes the earth's invitation audible. The image is stark and unconsoling: the gate leads down into earth, not up into heaven, until the last stanza reverses the direction.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Daniel Defoe, A Journal of the Plague Year (1722) (Project Gutenberg)

  14. The bells l. 23

    Church bells were rung for the dying (the passing bell) and at funerals; during epidemics the tolling could be nearly continuous. Here the bells 'cry' 'Come, come', as if summoning the listener to the grave. The image links to the open gate of the previous line. Donne's later 'for whom the bell tolls' (Devotions upon Emergent Occasions, 1624) draws on the same custom.

    Oxford English Dictionary; John Donne, Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624), Meditation 17

  15. Wit l. 25

    'Wit' in the 1590s meant intellect, ingenuity and verbal cleverness, the quality for which Nashe himself, a brilliant satirist and pamphleteer, was famous. 'Wantonness' here means playfulness, frivolity and unrestrained high spirits, not only lust. Even this lively faculty must 'taste death's bitterness'. The stanza suggests a self-judgment by a writer whose art was eloquence. The word also recalls the university-trained 'University Wits' among whom Nashe is usually placed.

    Oxford English Dictionary; R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament

  16. Hell's executioner ll. 27-30

    'Hell's executioner' is Death, who carries out the sentence that leads to judgment, or the devil himself. He 'Hath no ears for to hear / What vain art can reply': the arts of rhetoric and argument, the lawyer's or the poet's eloquence, cannot plead against him. The stanza joins Dunbar's lament for the learned and the long tradition in which no art or skill escapes Death.

    R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament; A. H. Bullen (ed.), Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age (1889)

  17. degree l. 31

    'Degree' is social rank, as in Ulysses' speech on 'degree' in Shakespeare's 'Troilus and Cressida'. The last stanza turns from lament to exhortation: everyone, of every rank, should welcome destiny, because heaven is our inheritance. The tone of the final stanza is more confident than the earlier ones. 'Destiny' here means the appointed end, death, and, through it, the life to come. Welcoming it, rather than fleeing, is the attitude recommended by the 'art of dying'.

    Oxford English Dictionary; R. B. McKerrow (ed.), The Works of Thomas Nashe, vol. 3 (1905), Summer's Last Will and Testament

  18. Heaven our heritage l. 33

    The legal language of inheritance suits a play in which Summer is making his will. Christians inherit not earthly goods but heaven, as in 1 Peter 1:4, 'an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for you'. The contrast with the fading flowers and vain riches of earlier stanzas is explicit. The word 'heritage' would also have reminded the original audience of the will-making action of the play.

    The Bible (KJV), 1 Peter 1:4; 'Summer's Last Will and Testament', Wikipedia (date, Croydon, plague, printing 1600)

  19. The world as a stage l. 34

    The idea that the world is a theatre and human life a role ('theatrum mundi') goes back to classical writers and was a Renaissance commonplace; Shakespeare's 'All the world's a stage' in 'As You Like It' is the best-known version. Sung within a play, the line becomes self-referential: the actors on Nashe's stage are reminded that their real life is also a performance that ends. 'Mount we unto the sky' then imagines the soul's ascent.

    William Shakespeare, As You Like It, 2.7 (Folger Shakespeare); 'Summer's Last Will and Testament', Wikipedia (date, Croydon, plague, printing 1600)
