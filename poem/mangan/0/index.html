Dark Rosaleen (From the Irish)

by James Clarence Mangan 1803–1849

From A Book of Irish Verse, ed. W. B. Yeats (1900)

History & Politics

O my Dark Rosaleen,
Do not sigh, do not weep!
The priests are on the ocean green.
They march along the deep.
There's wine from the royal Pope,
Upon the ocean green;
And Spanish ale shall give you hope,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My own Rosaleen!
Shall glad your heart, shall give you hope,
Shall give you health, and help, and hope,
My Dark Rosaleen!

Over hills, and through dales,
Have I roamed for your sake;
All yesterday I sailed with sails
On river and on lake,
The Erne, at its highest flood,
I dashed across unseen,
For there was lightning in my blood,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My own Rosaleen!
O there was lightning in my blood,
Red lightning lightened through my blood,
My Dark Rosaleen!

All day long in unrest
To and fro do I move,
The very heart within my breast
Is wasted for you, Love!
The heart in my bosom faints
To think of you, my queen!
My life of life, my saint of saints,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My own Rosaleen!
To hear your sweet and sad complaints,
My life, my love, my saint of saints,
My Dark Rosaleen!

Woe and pain, pain and woe,
Are my lot night and noon;
To see your bright face clouded so,
Like to the mournful moon.
But yet will I rear your throne
Again in golden sheen:
'Tis you shall reign, shall reign alone,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My own Rosaleen!
'Tis you shall have the golden throne,
'Tis you shall reign, and reign alone,
My Dark Rosaleen!

Over dews, over sands,
Will I fly for your weal:
Your holy, delicate white hands
Shall girdle me with steel.
At home, in your emerald bowers,
From morning's dawn till e'en,
You'll pray for me, my flower of flowers,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My fond Rosaleen!
You'll think of me through daylight's hours,
My virgin flower, my flower of flowers,
My Dark Rosaleen!

I could scale the blue air,
I could plough the high hills,
O, I could kneel all night in prayer,
To heal your many ills.
And one beamy smile from you
Would float like light between
My toils and me, my own, my true,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My fond Rosaleen!
Would give me life and soul anew,
A second life, a soul anew,
My Dark Rosaleen!

O! the Erne shall run red
With redundance of blood,
The earth shall rock beneath our tread,
And flames wrap hill and wood,
And gun-peal, and slogan cry,
Wake many a glen serene,
Ere you shall fade, ere you shall die,
My Dark Rosaleen!
My own Rosaleen!
The Judgment Hour must first be nigh
Ere you can fade, ere you can die,
My Dark Rosaleen!

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, source and first printing l. 1

    James Clarence Mangan (1803-1849) published the poem in The Nation, the paper of the Young Ireland movement, with a headnote saying the original was written in the reign of Elizabeth by a poet of Hugh the Red O'Donnell, chief of Tir Chonaill, as an allegorical address to Ireland. D. J. O'Donoghue notes that Mangan worked from an unrhymed version given by Samuel Ferguson in his 1834 articles on Hardiman's Irish Minstrelsy, and that the Irish original was probably a love song. 'Rosaleen' is Mangan's diminutive for Roisin, and 'Dark' renders 'dubh', black.

    James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001); Cartlann, 'My Dark Rosaleen' (text from The Nation, 30 May 1846, with Mangan's headnote); 'Roisin Dubh (song)', Wikipedia (origins, Red Hugh O'Donnell tradition, lyrics referring to the Pope, Rome and Spanish wine)

  2. Rosaleen as Ireland l. 8

    Irish poetry has a long tradition of figuring the land or its sovereignty as a woman, developed in the eighteenth-century aisling (vision poem), in which Ireland appears as a sorrowing beauty awaiting deliverance. Mangan's headnote makes the political reading explicit, and the poem's love language (sighs, weeping, a throne to be restored) works on both levels at once. The Irish song is sometimes said to be named after Roisin, daughter of Hugh O'Neill and wife of Red Hugh O'Donnell, though scholars doubt the early date.

    Cartlann, 'My Dark Rosaleen' (text from The Nation, 30 May 1846, with Mangan's headnote); 'Roisin Dubh (song)', Wikipedia (origins, Red Hugh O'Donnell tradition, lyrics referring to the Pope, Rome and Spanish wine)

  3. The priests are on the ocean green l. 3

    In the Nine Years War (1594-1603) the Irish lords O'Neill and O'Donnell sought help from Spain and the Papacy, and a Spanish force landed at Kinsale in 1601, where the Irish and Spanish were defeated. The priests marching 'along the deep' carry the promise of that Catholic alliance. 'Ocean green' fuses sea and national colour. Mangan's headnote dates the original to Elizabeth's reign, so the priests belong to the age of the Counter-Reformation, when Irish clergy trained on the Continent returned secretly by sea.

    'Roisin Dubh (song)', Wikipedia (origins, Red Hugh O'Donnell tradition, lyrics referring to the Pope, Rome and Spanish wine); 'Nine Years' War (Ireland)', Wikipedia

  4. wine from the royal Pope ... Spanish ale l. 5

    Mangan's headnote singles out the 'Roman wine' and 'Spanish ale' of the first stanza as figurative and leaves their meaning to 'the intelligent reader'. In the Irish song the lover tells Roisin she has a pardon from the Pope in Rome and Spanish wine, which has been read both literally (a Church dispensation, imported wine) and as a political code for foreign Catholic help. Mangan heightens the political sense: wine from the 'royal Pope' and 'Spanish ale' that 'shall give you hope'. 'Royal' points to the Pope's temporal power as a sovereign.

    'Roisin Dubh (song)', Wikipedia (origins, Red Hugh O'Donnell tradition, lyrics referring to the Pope, Rome and Spanish wine); Cartlann, 'My Dark Rosaleen' (text from The Nation, 30 May 1846, with Mangan's headnote)

  5. Stanza and refrain l. 9

    The stanza alternates long and short lines, then closes with 'My Dark Rosaleen! / My own Rosaleen!' followed by three lines that restate the central image with mounting repetition ('Shall glad your heart, shall give you hope, / Shall give you health, and help, and hope'). The effect is hypnotic, like a litany or a chant. The variations of the refrain ('My fond Rosaleen!') track the speaker's changing feeling. The style owes something to Irish song but is Mangan's own.

    James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001); 'James Clarence Mangan', Wikipedia

  6. The Erne l. 17

    The Erne flows through Upper and Lower Lough Erne to the sea at Ballyshannon, on the southern edge of O'Donnell country. The speaker has sailed lake and river and dashed across the flooding Erne 'unseen', a feat of daring that suits the voice of a guerrilla chief in the Nine Years War. The river returns in the last stanza, where it will 'run red / With redundance of blood'.

    'Roisin Dubh (song)', Wikipedia (origins, Red Hugh O'Donnell tradition, lyrics referring to the Pope, Rome and Spanish wine); 'River Erne', Wikipedia

  7. lightning in my blood l. 19

    The image is one of Mangan's most striking: passion for Rosaleen courses through the speaker like lightning. The coda doubles it into 'Red lightning lightened through my blood', where the tautology (lightning lightened) and the colour red make love already a foreboding of battle. The violence of the image prepares for the apocalyptic close. The speaker's sleepless roaming in the next stanza ('All day long in unrest / To and fro do I move') extends this fevered energy.

    James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  8. My life of life, my saint of saints l. 31

    The construction 'X of Xs' is the biblical superlative, as in the 'most holy' place, the 'holy of holies' of the Tabernacle (Exodus 26:33), or the 'song of songs' (Song of Solomon 1:1). Applied to Rosaleen it makes her both lover and holy object of devotion, mixing erotic and religious language in the manner of the Song of Songs itself.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Exodus 26:33; Song of Solomon 1:1; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  9. your bright face clouded so, / Like to the mournful moon l. 40

    The simile of the clouded moon gives Rosaleen the pale, suffering beauty of the aisling woman. The speaker's 'Woe and pain, pain and woe' (a chiasmus) and 'night and noon' frame a grief that never lifts. The next lines turn from lament to promise. The moon is a traditional emblem of female beauty and changeability, but here its clouding suggests eclipse rather than inconstancy: Ireland's brightness is obscured, not lost. The image of a clouded face also echoes the 'sigh' and 'weep' of the opening stanza, keeping the grief of the beloved in view.

    James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  10. rear your throne / Again in golden sheen l. 41

    'Rear' means raise or build up. The golden throne and the promise that she 'shall reign, shall reign alone' make the love-vow a political programme: Ireland ruling alone, without foreign masters. Mangan's headnote described the song as Hugh's resolve to raise Ireland again to the glory she had before the coming of the 'Saxon and Norman spoilers'. The repetition 'shall reign, shall reign alone' insists on sole sovereignty.

    Cartlann, 'My Dark Rosaleen' (text from The Nation, 30 May 1846, with Mangan's headnote); James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  11. weal l. 50

    'Weal' is an archaic word for prosperity or welfare, surviving in 'commonweal' and 'weal and woe'. The archaism helps give the poem a heroic, antique colouring appropriate to an Elizabethan-era speaker. Here it rhymes with 'steel' two lines later, so the speaker's service to her welfare is at once tied to the weapons of war. The phrase 'Over dews, over sands' that opens the stanza continues the speaker's vow to travel any distance, by land or shore.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'weal'; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  12. Shall girdle me with steel l. 52

    To gird or girdle someone is to fasten a belt round them, especially a sword-belt; 'girded with strength unto the battle' is biblical (Psalm 18:39). The 'holy, delicate white hands' that arm the warrior make Rosaleen both a chivalric lady and a sacred figure blessing a crusade. The hands are 'holy' as well as 'delicate', which keeps the poem's double register of devotion and love. The girding is a kind of consecration before battle.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Psalm 18:39; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'girdle'

  13. emerald bowers l. 53

    Green was by the 1840s the national colour, and 'Emerald Isle', popularised in the 1790s, was a common name for Ireland. Rosaleen at home in 'emerald bowers' praying for the absent warrior combines the landscape with the image of a lady in her garden awaiting her knight. The stanza turns from the speaker's travel to the beloved's waiting at home, from 'morning's dawn till e'en' (evening), praying for him. Her prayer answers his vow, so the stanza pairs active and contemplative service.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'emerald'; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  14. My virgin flower, my flower of flowers l. 59

    The rose has long been a symbol of the Virgin Mary, invoked in the Litany of Loreto as 'Mystical Rose' (Rosa mystica). Calling Rosaleen 'virgin flower' and 'flower of flowers' (another Hebraic superlative) gives the national figure a devotional aura familiar to a Catholic readership. Yeats later drew on the same fusion of rose, woman and Ireland in The Rose (1893).

    'Litany of Loreto', Wikipedia (invocation 'Mystical Rose'); James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  15. beamy l. 65

    'Beamy' is a poetic adjective meaning emitting beams, radiant. The smile that would 'float like light between / My toils and me' is the poem's tenderest image, a moment of rest before the final stanza's violence. The preceding hyperboles ('I could scale the blue air, / I could plough the high hills') are typical of Irish love-song. Its light pairs with the 'golden sheen' of the throne.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'beamy'; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  16. redundance of blood l. 74

    'Redundance' here keeps its Latin sense of overflowing, a superabundance (redundare, to flow back, overflow). The final stanza turns to apocalyptic war: the river running red, the earth rocking, hills and woods in flames, gunfire and battle-cries. The vision of national resurrection through bloodshed was taken up by later nationalist writers, and modern readers often note how the poem's romance becomes a summons to sacrifice.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'redundance'; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  17. slogan l. 77

    In its original sense a slogan was the rallying cry of a Highland or Irish host, often the clan name shouted in battle. Mangan uses it correctly, beside 'gun-peal', for the noise of a rising waking the quiet glens. The modern advertising sense came much later. The word thus belongs to the same Gaelic world of war-cries as the poem's speaker, and its pairing with modern 'gun-peal' blends the sixteenth century with the imagined rising to come. The 'glen serene' woken by it marks the end of peace.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'slogan'; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  18. The Judgment Hour l. 82

    The 'Judgment Hour' is the Last Judgement of Christian belief, the end of history (compare Revelation 14:7, 'the hour of his judgment is come'). The closing claim is that the nation will survive until the end of time. The rhyme 'nigh / die' and the repetition of 'Ere you can fade, ere you can die' end the poem on defiance rather than lament.

    The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Revelation 14:7; James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)

  19. Reception and influence l. 10

    Mangan, an impoverished Dublin clerk and translator who died in the cholera epidemic of 1849, was celebrated after his death as a national poet. Yeats placed himself in the line of 'Davis, Mangan, Ferguson' in 'To Ireland in the Coming Times' and included 'Dark Rosaleen' in his anthology A Book of Irish Verse (1900), the source of the archive's text. 'Glad' as a verb (to make glad) is archaic.

    'James Clarence Mangan', Wikipedia; W. B. Yeats, The Collected Works in Verse and Prose, vol. I: Poems Lyrical and Narrative (Stratford-on-Avon: Shakespeare Head Press, 1908), text of the poem (Project Gutenberg); James Clarence Mangan, 'Dark Rosaleen', ed. D. J. O'Donoghue, The Poems of James Clarence Mangan, with O'Donoghue's note (CELT, University College Cork, doc. E840000-001)
