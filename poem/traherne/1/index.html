Religion

Wonder

by Thomas Traherne 1636?–1674

From Poetical Works, ed. B. Dobell (1906)

Religion

How like an Angel came I down!
How bright are all things here!
When first among His works I did appear
O how their Glory me did crown!
The world resembled His Eternity,
In which my soul did walk;
And every thing that I did see
Did with me talk.

The skies in their magnificence,
The lively, lovely air;
Oh how divine, how soft, how sweet, how fair!
The stars did entertain my sense,
And all the works of God, so bright and pure,
So rich and great did seem,
As if they ever must endure
In my esteem.

A native health and innocence
Within my bones did grow,
And while my God did all his Glories show,
I felt a vigour in my sense
That was all Spirit. I within did flow
With seas of life, like wine;
I nothing in the world did know
But 'twas divine.

Harsh ragged objects were concealed,
Oppressions, tears and cries,
Sins, griefs, complaints, dissensions, weeping eyes
Were hid, and only things revealed
Which heavenly Spirits and the Angels prize.
The state of Innocence
And bliss, not trades and poverties,
Did fill my sense.

The streets were paved with golden stones,
The boys and girls were mine,
Oh how did all their lovely faces shine!
The sons of men were holy ones,
In joy and beauty they appeared to me,
And every thing which here I found,
While like an angel I did see,
Adorned the ground.

Rich diamond and pearl and gold
In every place was seen;
Rare splendours, yellow, blue, red, white and green,
Mine eyes did everywhere behold.
Great Wonders clothed with glory did appear,
Amazement was my bliss,
That and my wealth was everywhere;
No joy to this!

Cursed and devised proprieties,
With envy, avarice
And fraud, those fiends that spoil even Paradise,
Flew from the splendour of mine eyes,
And so did hedges, ditches, limits, bounds,
I dreamed not aught of those,
But wandered over all men's grounds,
And found repose.

Proprieties themselves were mine
And hedges ornaments,
Walls, boxes, coffers, and their rich contents
Did not divide my joys, but all combine.
Clothes, ribbons, jewels, laces, I esteemed
My joys by others worn:
For me they all to wear them seemed
When I was born.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its opening l. 1

    'Wonder' is the second poem in the Dobell Folio, Traherne's autograph collection, which was found, together with the manuscript of the Centuries, by William T. Brooke in 1896-7 and whose author was identified by Bertram Dobell, who published the poems in 1903; a revised version also exists in the Poems of Felicity copied by his brother Philip (British Library MS Burney 392). The opening exclamation sets the theme of the sequence: the infant soul arrives from God and sees the world as heaven. Traherne does not clearly teach the soul's pre-existence; 'came I down' may be a figure for birth as a gift from above. Readers inevitably compare Wordsworth's Immortality Ode, though Traherne's poems were unknown to him.

    Bertram Dobell, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Traherne (London, 1903; 2nd ed. 1906), from the Dobell Folio (Bodleian MS Eng. poet. c. 42); Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts (CELM), introduction to Thomas Traherne; Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)

  2. Stanza st. 1

    Each stanza combines an enclosed quatrain (abba) and an alternating one (cdcd), with lines ranging from pentameter to dimeter. The indentation of the manuscript and Dobell's text shows the shape: long lines of exclamation alternate with short ones, and every stanza closes on a brief line ('Did with me talk', 'In my esteem', 'But 'twas divine') that sums up the stanza's wonder. The form owes something to Herbert's stanza-making, though Traherne's diction is plainer and more repetitive, seeking the effect of rapture rather than wit.

    Bertram Dobell, ed., The Poetical Works of Thomas Traherne (London, 1903; 2nd ed. 1906), from the Dobell Folio (Bodleian MS Eng. poet. c. 42); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958); A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969)

  3. their Glory me did crown l. 4

    The inverted word order ('their Glory me did crown') is typical of the poem's simple, incantatory style. The thought comes from Psalm 8:5, 'thou ... hast crowned him with glory and honour', in a psalm about man's dominion over the works of God's hands. For Traherne this is no metaphor of rank: every person is meant to be heir and king of the whole world, and the child knows this naturally. The Centuries develop the same idea at length.

    Psalm 8:3-6 (KJV); Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969)

  4. The world resembled His Eternity l. 5

    Traherne repeatedly claims that the child sees things as eternal. In the Third Century he writes that in infancy 'all things abided eternally as they were in their proper places' and that 'Eternity was manifest in the Light of the Day' (III.3). The poem condenses that prose: the world 'resembled His Eternity, / In which my soul did walk', and creatures 'Did with me talk', as in Eden before the Fall. The 'talk' of things recalls Psalm 19:1-3, where the heavens declare God's glory without speech.

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; Psalm 19:1-3 (KJV); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)

  5. lively, lovely air l. 10

    The near-pun of 'lively' and 'lovely' is typical of the poem's sound-play, which works through repetition and accumulation ('how divine, how soft, how sweet, how fair!'). 'Lively' carried the stronger sense 'living, life-giving' (OED, lively adj.). In l. 12 the stars 'entertain my sense', which means both amuse and hold, occupy, receive as a host (OED, entertain v.). The senses are not dulled by habit but feasted, as a guest is entertained.

    OED, 'lively, adj.'; 'entertain, v.'; Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  6. 'As if they ever must endure' ll. 15-16

    Here the poem's double perspective shows. The infant sees all things as everlasting; the adult narrator says only that they 'did seem' so. Critics have debated whether Traherne's poetry of infancy is nostalgic, regretting a lost vision, or programmatic, teaching that the vision can be regained by 'retrieving' a childlike way of seeing. The Centuries favour the second view: he tells how he had to 'unlearn' the 'dirty devices of this world' to become 'as it were a little child again' (III.3).

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)

  7. A native health and innocence l. 17

    'Native' means inborn, natural (OED, native adj.). Traherne insists that the child is born healthy and innocent, its corruption coming afterwards from custom and from the 'dirty devices' of adults. In the Centuries he says 'I was a little stranger, which at my entrance into the world was saluted and surrounded with innumerable joys' (III.2), and blames his later 'Apostasy' on the influence of others (III.3-8). Some critics have called this Pelagian, at odds with the Church of England's teaching on original sin; others argue that Traherne is describing the child's experience, not denying doctrine, and that he elsewhere affirms the Fall.

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); OED, 'native, adj.'

  8. seas of life, like wine ll. 21-2

    The paradox of a vigour 'in my sense / That was all Spirit' locates the spiritual in the bodily senses. The speaker 'did flow / With seas of life, like wine': inner fullness is both oceanic and exhilarating. Compare the famous sentence of the First Century: 'You never enjoy the world aright, till the Sea itself floweth in your veins' (I.29). Wine also suggests the joy of the Eucharist and the 'new wine' of the Spirit.

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Dobell (1908), First Century 29; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  9. Harsh ragged objects were concealed st. 4

    The stanza lists what the infant did not see: oppression, tears, sins, complaints, dissensions. The point is not that these did not exist but that they were 'hid', so that only heavenly things were 'revealed'. The vocabulary of hiding and revealing is biblical (Matthew 11:25: 'thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes'). 'Trades and poverties' adds a social note, picking up the economic theme of the last stanzas.

    Matthew 11:25 (KJV); A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  10. The state of Innocence l. 30

    Theologians distinguished man's 'state of innocence' (status innocentiae) in Eden from the states of sin and grace (OED, innocence n.; state n.). By using the technical phrase Traherne claims that each infant recapitulates Adam's unfallen condition, an idea he states directly in the Centuries: 'I seemed as one brought into the Estate of Innocence' (III.2). 'Innocence' is also the title of a later poem in the Folio, two places after 'Wonder'.

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; OED, 'innocence, n.'; H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)

  11. streets paved with golden stones l. 33

    In Revelation the heavenly city has gates of pearl and a street of 'pure gold, as it were transparent glass' (21:21); its walls are garnished with precious stones (21:19). Traherne sees ordinary streets with these eyes. The Third Century describes the same vision in prose that the poem echoes: 'The dust and stones of the street were as precious as gold', and 'The streets were mine, the temple was mine' (III.3).

    Revelation 21:18-21 (KJV); Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  12. The boys and girls were mine l. 34

    A central Traherne paradox: the child possesses all things by enjoying them. The prose parallel is in Centuries III.3: 'Boys and girls tumbling in the street, and playing, were moving jewels'; 'the people were mine', and indeed 'all the World was mine'. Ownership here is a mode of love and wonder, opposite to the 'Cursed and devised proprieties' that later divide the world.

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)

  13. Rich diamond and pearl and gold st. 6

    Precious stones and the run of colours 'yellow, blue, red, white and green' make the child's world like the jewelled foundations of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:19-20). Traherne's critics note how insistently he uses the language of riches, treasure and wealth, but always to redirect it: true wealth is the world itself freely enjoyed. Hence 'my wealth was everywhere' at the stanza's close.

    Revelation 21:19-20 (KJV); A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  14. Amazement was my bliss l. 46

    'Amazement' is overwhelming wonder (OED, amazement n.). Plato and Aristotle held that philosophy begins in wonder (Theaetetus 155d; Metaphysics 982b12); for Traherne wonder is not a starting point to be left behind but the goal, a form of beatitude. The poem's title names this state. In the following lines 'No joy to this!' means 'no joy compares with this', 'to' being used in the sense 'in comparison with' (OED, to prep.).

    Plato, Theaetetus 155d; Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.2, 982b12; OED, 'amazement, n.'; 'to, prep.'; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969)

  15. Cursed and devised proprieties l. 49

    In the seventeenth century 'propriety' commonly meant ownership or a piece of property (OED, propriety n.), a sense central to the period's political debates over the rights of property. Traherne calls such proprieties 'cursed' and 'devised': they are human contrivances, not part of God's creation, and they bring envy, avarice and fraud. Dobell, editing the Centuries, notes that Traherne uses the word where we would now say 'properties'; in III.3 he recalls that as a child he 'knew no churlish proprieties, nor bounds, nor divisions'. In the poem they simply vanish, and the last stanza reverses the idea: 'Proprieties themselves were mine'.

    OED, 'propriety, n.'; Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'

  16. hedges, ditches, limits, bounds l. 53

    Hedges and ditches were the means by which open fields and commons were enclosed into private holdings, a contentious process throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries that provoked riots and, in 1649, the Diggers' attempt to cultivate common land. Traherne, a clergyman in Herefordshire and later in Teddington, does not argue a political case, but the list carries social weight: the child 'wandered over all men's grounds' as if there were no trespass. Critics differ on how far to read the poem as social critique rather than spiritual allegory.

    A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); Jan Ross, ed., The Works of Thomas Traherne, vol. VI (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer), 'Wonder'; OED, 'enclosure, n.'

  17. My joys by others worn ll. 61-2

    The poem ends by redeeming possession rather than rejecting it. Hedges become 'ornaments'; walls and coffers 'Did not divide my joys'; and the finery of others ('Clothes, ribbons, jewels, laces') seems to be worn for the child's pleasure. Enjoyment, not ownership, is what makes things 'mine', so that envy has no place. The thought appears in the Centuries as the doctrine that each soul is 'the sole heir of the whole world', and that all others are 'sole heirs as well as you' (I.29).

    Thomas Traherne, Centuries of Meditations, ed. Bertram Dobell (London, 1908), Third Century, sections 1-3; A. L. Clements, The Mystical Poetry of Thomas Traherne (Harvard, 1969); H. M. Margoliouth, ed., Thomas Traherne: Centuries, Poems, and Thanksgivings, 2 vols (Oxford, 1958)
