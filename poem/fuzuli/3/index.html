Love

A Stately Cypress Yesterday

by Fuzuli c. 1483–1556

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

LoveNature

Stately Cypress yesterday her shade threw o'er my head;
Her form was heart-ensnaring, heart-delighting her light tread;
When speaking, sudden opened she her smiling rubies red,
There a pistachio I beheld that drops of candy shed.
"This casket can it be a mouth? Ah! deign!" I said; said she:
"Nay, nay, 'tis balm to cure thy hidden smart: aye, truly thine!"

Down o'er her crescents she had pressed the turban she did wear,
By which, from many broken hearts, sighs raised she of despair;
She loosed her tresses — hid within the cloud her moon so fair,
And o'er her visage I beheld the curls of her black hair.
"Those curling locks, say, are they then a chain?" I said; said she:
"That round my cheek, a noose to take thy heart; aye, truly thine!"

The taper bright, her cheek, illumined day's lamp in the sky;
The rose's branch was bent before her figure, cypress-high;
She, cypress-like, her foot set down upon the fount, my eye,
But many a thorn did pierce her foot, she suffered pain thereby.
"What thorn unto the roseleaf-foot gives pain?" I said; said she:
"The lash of thy wet eye doth it impart; aye, truly thine!"

Promenading, to the garden did that jasmine-cheeked one go;
With many a bright adornment in the early springtide's glow;
The hyacinths their musky locks did o'er the roses throw;
That Picture had tattooed her lovely feet rose-red to show.
"The tulip's hue whence doth the dog-rose gain?" I said; said she:
"From blood of thine shed 'neath my glance's dart; aye, truly thine!"

To earth within her ward my tears in torrents rolled apace;
The accents of her ruby lip my soul crazed by their grace;
My heart was taken in the snare her musky locks did trace,
That very moment when my eyes fell on her curls and face.
"Doth Scorpio the bright Moon's House contain?" I said; said she:
"Fear! threatening this Conjunction dread, thy part; aye, truly thine!"

Her hair with ambergris perfumed was waving o'er her cheek,
On many grieving, passioned souls it cruel woe did wreak;
Her graceful form and many charms my wildered heart made weak;
The eye beheld her figure fair, then heart and soul did seek.
"Ah! what bright thing this cypress of the plain?" I said; said she:
"'Tis that which thy fixed gaze beholds apart; aye, truly thine!"

When their veil her tulip and dog-rose had let down yesterday,
The morning breeze tore off that screen which o'er these flow'rets lay;
Came forth that Envy of the sun in garden fair to stray,
Like lustrous pearls the dew-drops shone, a bright and glistening spray.
"Pearls, say, are these, aye pearls from 'Aden's main?" I said; said she;
"Tears, these, of poor Fuzûlî, sad of heart; aye, truly thine!"

Codex Note

A musaddas, a six-line stanza form, in which the beloved's cypress-like figure, her smile and her hair are described, and each answer of the beloved ends with the refrain 'aye, truly thine!'

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: Fuzûlî's müseddes of question and answer st. 1

    Fuzûlî (Mehmed bin Süleyman, d. 1556) lived in Ottoman and Safavid Iraq (Karbala, Hilla, Najaf, Baghdad) and wrote in Azerbaijani Turkish, Persian and Arabic; he is the great lyric poet of love and suffering in the Turkish tradition. Gibb's heading gives the first line as 'Dün sâye saldı başıma bir serv-i ser-bülend' ('yesterday a lofty cypress cast its shade over my head'). In a müseddes each stanza has six lines: the first four rhyme together and the last two form a couplet that carries a refrain. Here the closing couplet is always a short dialogue, 'I said ... said she', the question-and-answer device (sual ü cevap) found in many divan poems. Gibb's note 136 says that Ziya Beg (Ziya Pasha) wrote a parody of this müseddes in his anthology Harâbât.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Fuzûlî, Müseddes, pp. 58-60, and notes 136-147, p. 248; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Fuzûlî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; TDV contributor, 'Müseddes', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  2. The beloved as cypress l. 1

    In Persian and Turkish poetry the beloved's stature is always a cypress (serv): tall, slender, upright and moving gracefully, 'cypress-high' as stanza 3 puts it. Cypresses were planted beside running water in Eastern gardens, which Gibb notes elsewhere, and the poem plays on that in stanza 3 when the cypress sets her foot in the lover's tearful eye. To have someone's shade fall on one's head means to enjoy their protection or favour, a phrase used of kings, so the opening already treats the beloved as a sovereign. The 'cypress of the plain' in the last stanza but one repeats the figure.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 58-60; note 253, p. 262 (cypresses by streams); Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  3. Pistachio and candy: the mouth and tongue l. 4

    Gibb notes that a poet sometimes compares the mistress's tongue to a small pistachio nut; a half-open pistachio shell also suggests a tiny smiling mouth, and the beloved's mouth must be as small as possible. The 'candy' is her sweet speech. Gibb adds that the word used, qand, is the source of English 'candy', and the OED traces 'candy' through French and Arabic to Persian qand, sugar. Her 'smiling rubies red' are her lips, rubies being the standard figure for lips.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 137, p. 248; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'candy'

  4. The mouth as casket, the kiss as cure l. 5

    A small jewelled box (hokka) is a usual figure for the tiny mouth, which contains the 'pearls' of the teeth and the 'rubies' of the lips. The lover's question is half a plea ('Ah! deign!'), since a mouth is for kissing. Her answer is the wit of the stanza: what looks like a mere casket is the medicine (the kiss, or her healing words) for his secret pain. In divan poetry the beloved's lip is often said to heal like the breath of Jesus, which revives the dead. The pattern of question and teasing answer is repeated in every stanza.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 58-59; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  5. The refrain: 'aye, truly thine' end of every stanza

    The refrain gives the poem its irony. What the beloved hands the lover as 'truly thine' moves from a promise (balm for his pain, st. 1) to a trap (a noose for his heart, st. 2), to his own tears and blood (sts. 3-4), to a threat (the Scorpio conjunction, st. 5), and finally to his own image of her and his own tears (sts. 6-7). The lover's questions take each feature of her beauty in turn, which is a recognized catalogue form, and the answers turn each into an instrument of his suffering. Gibb kept the original's six-line form and its final dialogue couplet. The archive text prints the sixth line of the first stanza ending 'aye, truly thine!'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 58-60; TDV contributor, 'Müseddes', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  6. Eyebrows as crescents l. 7

    Gibb's note: a lady's crescents are her eyebrows. The arched brow is compared to the new moon, and also to a bow, which is why glances are arrows in stanza 4. The face is then the moon itself, and in the next lines the loosened black hair hides it like a cloud (Gibb's note 139). The picture is of a beloved in a turban, pressing it down at a rakish angle, which in the poetry of the period is a sign of coquetry.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 138-139, p. 248; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  7. Curls as chain and noose l. 12

    The beloved's curls (zülf) are among the most worked images of the tradition. They are a chain, because madmen were chained and the lover is mad (the type is Mecnun, crazed for Leylâ, on whom Fuzûlî wrote his greatest romance); a noose or lasso (kemend), used by hunters and warriors to snare their prey; a snare or net for the heart-bird; and dark as night around the moon-face. The question 'are they then a chain?' suggests the first meaning, and the answer supplies the second. Stanza 5 returns to the 'snare her musky locks did trace'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 58-59; TDV contributor, 'Leylâ vü Mecnûn', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  8. The cypress's foot in the lover's eye ll. 15-18

    Gibb's note 140 explains the conceit in steps. A cypress grows by water and so 'sets its foot' in a spring or fountain. 'On my head and eye' is a Turkish phrase for 'willingly, at your command'. Conquerors set their feet on the necks of the vanquished. So the cypress-like beauty may set her foot on the head, or in the streaming eye (the fountain), of her conquered lover; but if she treads in his eye, his lashes, like thorns, pierce her tender foot. Her answer blames 'the lash of thy wet eye'. The lover's tears are a fountain, and even his devotion hurts her.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 140, p. 248

  9. Hyacinth curls over the roses l. 21

    The hyacinth (sünbül), dark and fragrant, is the standard figure for curled hair, and musk, the dark perfume from the musk-deer of Central Asia, is the figure for its scent. Gibb's note says only that her hair hung over her cheeks. The garden and the beloved are described at once: the flowers are her features, so that the walk through the spring garden is also a survey of her face.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 141, p. 248; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  10. 'That Picture' and the reddened feet l. 22

    Gibb capitalizes 'Picture', which probably renders the Persian nigâr, literally a painted image and by extension an idol of beauty or a beloved. He explains the line by the custom of tattooing the feet, hands or face, citing Lane's Modern Egyptians; readers may also think of henna, the red-brown dye with which feet and hands were stained in Ottoman lands. His note 143 asks how her white feet (the white dog-rose) have become red (the tulip), which implies staining or tattooing. The answer, in the refrain couplet, is that they are red with the lover's blood, shed by her glance.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 142-143, p. 248

  11. The glance as arrow l. 24

    The eyebrow is a bow, the eyelashes and glances are arrows, and the beloved is a cruel archer or warrior who sheds her lovers' blood. The answer is the darkest so far: the rosy colour that seems to be cosmetic or natural is the lover's own blood. Gibb's notes elsewhere explain that 'to shed tears of blood' and 'drink one's own blood' mean to suffer intense grief. The violence is conventional, but the dialogue form makes the beloved herself say it with apparent satisfaction.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 77, p. 239; note 143, p. 248; Walter G. Andrews, Poetry's Voice, Society's Song: Ottoman Lyric Poetry (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1985)

  12. The Moon in Scorpio ll. 29-30

    Gibb's note: her curls twisting over her face are compared to a scorpion's claws, so her face encircled by her hair is the Moon in the sign of Scorpio, a conjunction regarded as menacing by astrologers. In traditional astrology Scorpio is the sign of the Moon's 'fall', where it is weakest. The scorpion-curl (zülf-i akreb) is a common divan image; the sting is the hair's cruelty. Her answer turns the omen on the lover: fear, for this dread conjunction threatens you.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 144, p. 248

  13. Pearls of Aden: the signature stanza ll. 41-42

    Gibb's notes say that the pearls of Aden and the Persian Gulf were highly esteemed, and explain a technical term for a class of pearls in the original. The 'tulip and dog-rose' whose veil the morning breeze tears away are her red cheeks and white face, and the 'dew-drops' are, by Gibb's note 147, drops of perspiration, since moisture on the face was praised and compared to dew. The final answer brings the poet in by name, as the convention requires: the pearls are his tears. Fuzûlî's pen-name can mean both 'meddlesome, superfluous' and 'excellent, virtuous'; in the preface to his Persian Divan he says he chose it after earlier pen-names turned out to be shared by others.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), notes 145-147, p. 248; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Fuzûlî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi
