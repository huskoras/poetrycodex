Love

Yield Not the Soul to Pang of Love

by Fuzuli c. 1483–1556

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

LoveThe Mind

Yield not the soul to pang of Love, for Love's the soul's fierce glow;
That Love's the torment of the soul doth all the wide world know.

Seek not for gain from fancy wild of pang of Love at all;
For all that comes from fancy wild of Love's pang is grief's throe.

Each curving eyebrow is a blood-stained sabre thee to slay;
Each dusky curl, a deadly venomed snake to work thee woe.

Lovely, indeed, the forms of moon-like maidens are to see —
Lovely to see, but ah! the end doth bitter anguish show.

From this I know full well that torment dire in love abides,
That all who lovers are, engrossed with sighs, rove to and fro.

Call not to mind the pupils of the black-eyed damsels bright,
With thought: 'I'm man,' be not deceived, 'tis blood they drink, I trow.

E'en if Fuzûlî should declare: 'In fair ones there is troth;'
Be not deceived: 'A poet's words are falsehoods all men know.'

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A ghazel warning the reader that love's pangs bring only grief, and ending with the warning not to be deceived by the beloved's or the poet's own professions of faith in fair ones.

Study notes by the editors

  1. A ghazel from Leyli and Mejnun couplet 1

    Fuzuli (c. 1480-1556), born in Iraq near Karbala and writing in Azerbaijani Turkish, Persian and Arabic, is the great lyric poet of classical Turkish literature. His Leyli and Mejnun, written soon after the Ottoman conquest of Baghdad (1534), retells the Arab legend of the mad lover Qays, called Mejnun; Gibb translates passages from it elsewhere in this archive (for example 'Mejnun's Gazel'), but this ghazel is an independent lyric, not one of the romance's interpolated poems. The Turkish first line means 'Do not give your soul to love's grief, for love is the calamity of the soul.' Gibb's notes 155 and 156 explain two of its allusions, and his note 154 outlines the romance of Leyli and Mejnun. The ghazel is widely known in Turkey today, often under the title 'Aska sevdalanma'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; E. J. W. Gibb, A History of Ottoman Poetry, vol. III (London: Luzac, 1904), chapter on Fuzuli; Fuzuli, Leyla and Mejnun, trans. Sofi Huri, with a history of the poem by Alessio Bombaci (London: Allen and Unwin, 1970); Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Fuzuli'

  2. 'Love's the soul's fierce glow' couplet 1

    The Turkish line says, 'Do not give your soul to love's grief, for love is the calamity (afet) of the soul'; the second line answers, 'That love is the calamity of the soul is famous throughout the world.' Gibb, needing a rhyme in '-ow' for every couplet, renders the first afet as 'the soul's fierce glow' and the second as 'the torment of the soul', which introduces an image of fire not in the original, though burning is everywhere in Fuzuli's love poetry. The repetition of the key phrase from line to line, a figure called reddu'l-acuz 'ala's-sadr in Ottoman rhetoric, is preserved only in part.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Fuzuli, 'Can verme gam-i aska...', modern Turkish text reproduced as 'Aska Sevdalanma'

  3. Monorhyme and the ghazel form couplet 1

    A ghazel is a short lyric of five to fifteen couplets on a single rhyme, which both lines of the first couplet share; Fuzuli's poem rhymes on -an followed by the repeated ending (redif) dir, 'is' (jandir, jihandir, yalandir). Gibb's title page announces translations 'in the original forms', and here he keeps one English rhyme through all fourteen lines, with fillers like 'I trow' and 'rove to and fro'. The effect is an incantatory monotony close to the original's, at some cost to natural English. Each couplet is an argument from experience against love, and the last couplet overturns the argument.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  4. 'fancy wild' couplet 2

    In Ottoman love-poetry sevda means passionate, melancholy love; the word comes from Arabic sawda', 'black', the black bile whose excess was thought to cause melancholy and madness. Gibb's 'fancy wild of pang of Love' looks like a literal rendering of such a phrase, 'the passion of love's grief', but the Turkish of this couplet is not printed by Gibb, so the equivalence is a probability rather than a certainty. The couplet's point is economic: there is no profit (gain) in love, only grief. The same humoral word underlies Rumi's 'sweet madness' in the Masnavi prologue.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997); Oxford English Dictionary, 'fancy, n.'

  5. Eyebrow as sabre couplet 3

    In the Persianate lyric each feature of the beloved has a fixed emblem: the arched eyebrow is a scimitar or a bow, the glance an arrow, the curl a snake or a chain, the mole a grain of bait. Fuzuli turns the stock catalogue into a warning: these are not ornaments but weapons. The 'blood-stained' sabre is the lover's blood. Gibb's Fuzuli selections are full of the same code, for instance 'Bows of poplar green, thy painted brows; thy glances shafts provide' in another poem, and his notes explain the indigo-painted eyebrows of Eastern ladies.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  6. Curl as snake couplet 3

    The dark lock of hair is constantly compared to a snake in Persian and Turkish poetry, because of its coils and its blackness, and because, like the serpent guarding a treasure, it guards the beloved's face. Fuzuli's line pairs it with the sabre-eyebrow in strict parallel ('Each curving eyebrow ... / Each dusky curl'), a figure of balanced correspondence much prized by Ottoman rhetoricians. The serpent returns in Fuzuli's Mejnun ghazel in this archive, where hope's thread becomes 'the serpent's train'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Walter G. Andrews, Najaat Black and Mehmet Kalpakli, eds., Ottoman Lyric Poetry: An Anthology (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1997)

  7. 'moon-like maidens' couplet 4

    The moon is the commonest figure for the beloved's face in Persian and Turkish poetry, for its pale radiance and its distance. Gibb's 'Lovely, indeed ... / Lovely to see, but ah!' reproduces a repetition across the couplet and turns on a contrast between sight and outcome. Leyli's own name, as Gibb explains in his notes, means 'nocturnal', and in his summary she is 'a damsel bright as the moon', so the moon-faced maidens of the ghazel point forward to the heroine whose love destroys Mejnun.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156

  8. The wandering lovers couplet 5

    Within the romance the line points directly at Mejnun, who, as Gibb's summary says, rushes into the desert with matted hair and bare breast, making the rocks echo with Leyli's name, and finally dies on her grave. The couplet presents his madness as evidence: since all lovers rove sighing, love must be torment. The ghazel's warning voice is thus ironic within the story, which celebrates the lover who ignores such advice. Gibb's version keeps the reasoning 'From this I know full well', the argumentative style of many of Fuzuli's ghazels.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Fuzuli, Leyla and Mejnun, trans. Sofi Huri, with a history of the poem by Alessio Bombaci (London: Allen and Unwin, 1970)

  9. Pupils and 'I'm man': an untranslatable pun couplet 6

    Gibb's note 155 explains that the Persian merdum, 'pupil of the eye' (properly merdumek, 'manikin', the tiny image of ourselves seen reflected in another's pupil), sounds like merd-im, 'I am a man, a hero'. The lover must not think, 'I am a man, I can look into those black eyes safely', for the pupils, the 'little men' of the eyes, drink blood: the blood, Gibb adds, of their lovers. The couplet depends entirely on this play of words, and Gibb's 'With thought: "I'm man,"' is an attempt to keep a trace of it.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156

  10. Eyes that drink blood couplet 6

    The bloodthirsty beloved is a commonplace of the Persianate lyric: eyes are assassins, eyelashes daggers, and the lover's heart turns to blood. Fuzuli gives it a twist through the merdum pun, so that the little men in the eyes drink the blood of grown men who thought themselves heroes. 'I trow', 'I believe', is an archaic filler supplied by Gibb for the rhyme (OED, trow v.). 'Black-eyed damsels' may also remind readers of the dark-eyed houris of the Qur'anic Paradise (hur 'in), though Gibb does not say so.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Oxford English Dictionary, 'trow, v.'

  11. 'troth' and the signature couplet 7

    'Troth' is an old form of 'truth' in the sense of loyalty or plighted faith (OED, troth n.), as in 'plight one's troth'; it renders Turkish vefa, fidelity, the virtue that beauties are proverbially said to lack. The modern Turkish text of the couplet, 'Gel derse Fuzuli ki guzellerde vefa var / Aldanma ki sair sozu elbette yalandir', means: 'If Fuzuli says, Come, there is fidelity in beauties, do not be deceived, for a poet's word is surely a lie.' The signature (mahlas) is used here not for self-praise but to undercut the speaker himself.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Oxford English Dictionary, 'troth, n.'; Fuzuli, 'Can verme gam-i aska...', modern Turkish text

  12. 'A poet's words are falsehoods' couplet 7

    Gibb's note 156 says only that there is a proverb to this effect. Arabic criticism had a famous maxim, 'the best poetry is the most lying', and the Qur'an itself speaks of poets who 'say what they do not do' (26:224-226). Fuzuli's closing line is a paradox of the liar type: if a poet's words are lies, then the poet's warning against love is also untrustworthy, and so perhaps is the claim that beauties are faithless. Placed in a romance celebrating Mejnun's absolute fidelity, the ghazel leaves the reader unsure whether to trust the warning or the love it warns against.

    E. J. W. Gibb, trans., Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trubner, 1882), Fuzuli selections (pp. 54-66), and notes 154-156; Qur'an 26:224-226
