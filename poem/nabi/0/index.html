Alas! nor Dew nor Smiling Rose Within This

by Nâbî d. 1712

Translated by E. J. W. Gibb (1882) · From Ottoman Poems (1882)

Religion

Alas! nor dew nor smiling rose within this mead is mine;
Within this market-place nor trade nor coin for need is mine;
Nor more nor less; nor power nor strength for act or deed is mine;
Nor might nor eminence; nor balm the cure to speed is mine.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Being 's the bounty of the Lord; and Life, the gift Divine;
The Breath, the present of His Love; and Speech His Grace's sign:
The Body is the pile of God; the Soul, His Breath benign;
The Powers thereof, His Glory's trust; the Senses, His design.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

No work, no business of my own within this mart have I;
All Being is of Him alone — no life apart have I;
No choice of entering this world, or hence of start have I;
To cry: "I am! I am!" in truth, no power of heart have I
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

The Earth the carpet is of Power; the Sphere, the tent of Might;
The Stars, both fixed and wandering, are Glory's lamps of light:
The World 's the issue of the grace of Mercy's treasures bright:
With Forms of beings is the page of Wisdom's volume dight.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Being is but a loan to us, and Life in trust we hold:
In slaves a claim to Power 's pretension arrogant and bold;
The servant's part is by submission and obedience told;
Should He: "My slave" address to me, 'twere favours manifold.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

I'm poor and empty-handed, but grace free is of the Lord;
Nonentity 's my attribute: to Be is of the Lord;
For Being or Non-being's rise, decree is of the Lord;
The surging of the Seen and Unseen's sea is of the Lord.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Of gifts from table of His Bounty is my daily bread;
My breath is from the Breath of God's benignant Mercy fed;
My portion from the favours of Almighty Power is shed;
And my provision is from Providence's kitchen spread.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

I cannot, unallotted, take my share from wet or dry;
From land or from the ocean, from earth or from the sky:
The silver or the gold will come, by Providence laid by;
I cannot grasp aught other than my fortune doth supply.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Creation's Pen the lines of billows of events hath traced;
Th' illumined scroll of the Two Worlds, Creation's Pencil graced;
Their garments upon earth and sky, Creation's woof hath placed;
Men's forms are pictures in Creation's great Shâh-Nâma traced.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

I cannot make the morning eve, or the dark night the day;
I cannot turn the air to fire, or dust to water's spray;
I cannot bid the Sphere stand still, or mountain-region stray;
I cannot Autumn turn by will of mine to lovely May.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

From out of Nothingness His mighty Power made me appear;
Whilst in the womb I lay, saw He to all I need for here;
With kindnesses concealed and manifest did He me rear;
With me He drew a curtain o'er Distinction's beauty dear.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

God's Revelation is Discernment's Eye, if 't oped remain;
The picturings of worlds are all things changing aye amain;
The showing of the Hidden Treasure is this raging main,
This work, this business of the Lord, this Majesty made plain.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Now void, now full, are Possibility's store-houses vast;
This glass-lined world's the mirror where Lights Twain their phases cast;
The blinded thing — in scattering strange fruits its hours are past;
Ruined hath this old Vineyard been by autumn's sullen blast.
O that I knew what here I am, that which indeed is mine!

Codex Note

Mukhammes.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem: Nâbî's muhammes on what is 'mine' st. 1

    Nâbî (Yusuf, born at Ruha, now Şanlıurfa, in 1642, died in Istanbul in 1712) is the leading representative of the 'hikemî' (wisdom, didactic) style in Ottoman poetry, which put thought before ornament; his Hayriyye, a book of advice to his son, was widely read. Gibb's heading gives the opening line in transliteration as 'Bu gülistânda benim içün ne gül ne şebnem var' ('in this rose-garden there is for me neither rose nor dew'). The poem has thirteen stanzas, each of four rhyming lines and a fixed refrain. The theme is the Sufi doctrine that all being belongs to God and that the self owns nothing, not even its existence.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), Nâbî, Mukhammes, pp. 113-115, and notes 228-229, p. 259; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Nâbî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)

  2. The refrain l. 5 of every stanza

    In a muhammes the fifth line of each stanza may be a fixed refrain, as here. The refrain is a question rather than a statement, and the four lines before it change their approach: first a list of things the poet lacks (st. 1), then a list of what God gives (st. 2), then the world as God's handiwork (st. 4), then the self as borrowed and dependent (sts. 5-8), then the poet's powerlessness (st. 10), then the metaphysics of creation (sts. 11-13). The steady return of the question gives the poem its humility, a characteristic note of Nâbî's reflective poetry.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), pp. 113-115; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Nâbî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)

  3. The world as garden and bazaar l. 2

    Two of the commonest figures for the world are joined: the garden (gülistan, 'mead'), whose roses fade, and the bazaar, where everyone trades for gain. The poet has no share in either; 'nor more nor less' means he has neither surplus nor lack of his own. The theme of the stanza is poverty (fakr) in the Sufi sense: to possess nothing and to know it is the beginning of wisdom. The third stanza returns to the 'mart'.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), p. 113; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  4. Being as God's gift ll. 6-9

    The second stanza lists the parts of the human person and assigns each to God. Behind it lies the Sufi doctrine of the unity of being (vahdet-i vücûd), associated with Ibn Arabi, according to which only God truly exists and creatures have a borrowed existence. Nâbî does not argue the doctrine but draws its moral: claims of ownership and of power are pretension. 'The Body is the pile of God' uses 'pile' in its old sense of a large building or edifice (OED, s.v. 'pile', n.).

    TDV contributor, 'Vahdet-i Vücûd', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'pile'; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), p. 113

  5. The breath of God l. 8

    The Qur'an says of the creation of Adam that God fashioned him and 'breathed into him of My spirit' (15:29; 38:72). Sufi writers made this breath the link between the human soul and God. Stanza 7 returns to it: 'My breath is from the Breath of God's benignant Mercy fed', where the 'Breath of the Merciful' (nefes-i Rahmân) was a technical term in Ibn Arabi's school for the creative outpouring of being.

    The Qur'an, 15:29; The Qur'an, 38:72; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  6. Saying 'I' l. 14

    Saying 'I' was, for Sufi moralists, the root of pride: Iblis refused to bow to Adam with the words 'I am better than he' (Qur'an 7:12). The famous cry of al-Hallaj, 'I am the Truth', stands at the opposite pole, where the self is annihilated in God. Nâbî's poem is not ecstatic; it takes the sober line that a servant has 'no power of heart' to claim existence at all. The theme returns in stanza 6: 'Nonentity 's my attribute: to Be is of the Lord'.

    The Qur'an, 7:12; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  7. Life held in trust l. 21

    Emanet is something entrusted to a person for safe keeping and to be returned. The Qur'an (33:72) says that God offered 'the trust' to the heavens, the earth and the mountains, and they refused it, but man bore it. Sufi and moral writers read the trust as life, reason, the soul or religious responsibility. Stanza 2 already calls the powers of the body 'His Glory's trust'. Nâbî's point is ethical: what is held in trust cannot be claimed as property, so 'a claim to Power' is arrogance.

    The Qur'an, 33:72; TDV contributor, 'Emanet', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  8. 'My servant' l. 24

    In Islamic piety servanthood ('ubûdiyyet) is the summit of human dignity, not a humiliation. The Qur'an calls the Prophet 'His servant' at the most exalted moment of his life, the Night Journey (17:1), and God addresses believers as 'My servants' (39:53). Gibb's 'slave' renders kul, the usual Turkish word for God's servant (and also for the sultan's servants). The stanza moves from the arrogance of claiming power to the grace of being claimed.

    The Qur'an, 17:1; The Qur'an, 39:53; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), p. 114

  9. Being and non-being; the Seen and the Unseen ll. 27-29

    Theology distinguished the visible world (âlem-i şehâdet) from the unseen world (âlem-i gayb); the Qur'an calls God 'Knower of the unseen and the seen' (59:22). The 'sea' of both whose waves surge is God's creative activity. The pair 'Being or Non-being' (vücûd and adem) is the language of the unity-of-being school, in which created things are in themselves non-existent and receive existence from God at every moment.

    The Qur'an, 59:22; TDV contributor, 'Vahdet-i Vücûd', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  10. Daily bread and allotted portion ll. 31-39

    Stanzas 7 and 8 develop the doctrine of rızk, divinely allotted provision: the Qur'an says that there is no creature on earth whose provision is not upon God (11:6). Nâbî sets it out in homely terms, a bounteous table and a kitchen, and then as a principle: 'I cannot, unallotted, take my share from wet or dry'. The 'wet or dry', 'land or ocean', 'silver or gold' pairs (merisms) sweep in everything. Nâbî's reflective verse is often proverbial in this way, and many of his lines circulated as sayings.

    The Qur'an, 11:6; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Nâbî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)

  11. The Pen and the picture-book of creation ll. 41-44

    The Qur'an swears 'by the Pen' (68:1) and speaks of God 'who taught by the pen' (96:4); in tradition the first thing God created was the Pen, which wrote all that would be on the Preserved Tablet. Nâbî makes the world a manuscript: illuminated scroll, woven garment and, finally, a Shâhnâma, Firdawsī's Persian epic, of which manuscripts were richly illustrated. Gibb's note explains that copies of the Shâhnâma were usually decorated with miniatures showing the course of the story, so men are figures in a picture painted by another hand.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 228, p. 259; The Qur'an, 68:1; The Qur'an, 96:4

  12. Created in the womb ll. 51-53

    The Qur'an describes human creation 'in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in three darknesses' (39:6), and the stages of the embryo (23:12-14). Nâbî's stanza follows the life from nothingness ('adem) to birth and upbringing. The last line, 'With me He drew a curtain o'er Distinction's beauty dear', is obscure in Gibb's English; it seems to say that God veiled from the poet the beauty of the divine order or of his own rank, but the sense is uncertain and should be checked against the Turkish.

    The Qur'an, 39:6; The Qur'an, 23:12 -14; E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), p. 115

  13. The Hidden Treasure l. 58

    A saying attributed to God, much loved by the Sufis, runs: 'I was a hidden treasure; I wished to be known, so I created creation in order to be known.' Ibn Arabi and his followers took the 'hidden treasure' to be the divine essence, and the created world as its self-disclosure. Hadith scholars doubted its authenticity, but in mystical poetry it is everywhere. For Nâbî the 'raging main' of changing appearances is that disclosure, 'this Majesty made plain'.

    TDV contributor, 'Kenz-i Mahfî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi; Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1975)

  14. The world as a mirror; the store-houses of possibility ll. 61-62

    'Possibility's store-houses' renders a philosophical term: the mümkinât are contingent beings, things that may or may not exist, as opposed to God, the Necessary Being. They come into being and pass away, 'now void, now full'. Gibb's note: the 'Lights Twain' are the Sun and Moon, and the world is a mirror in which the influences of the planets are reflected. In Sufi metaphysics the world is also the mirror of the divine names.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), note 229, p. 259; TDV contributor, 'Vahdet-i Vücûd', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi

  15. The old vineyard in autumn l. 64

    The poem began in a rose-garden where the poet had neither rose nor dew; it ends in a vineyard ruined by autumn. The garden and its seasons are the traditional figure for the transience of the world (spring for youth and prosperity, autumn for decay and death). The preceding line, 'The blinded thing — in scattering strange fruits its hours are past', is hard; it may refer to the world (or time) as a blind sower. The final refrain then asks its question once more, now against the ruin of everything that seemed to belong to us.

    E. J. W. Gibb, Ottoman Poems Translated into English Verse in the Original Forms (London: Trübner, 1882), p. 115; Abdülkadir Karahan, 'Nâbî', TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, (2006)
