Religion

I Sing of a Maiden

by Anonymous (Middle English) c. 13th–16th century

From Sloane MS 2593

Religion

I sing of a maiden
That is makeles,
King of all kinges
To her sone sche ches.

He cam also stille
There his moder was,
As dew in Aprille
That falleth on the grass.

He cam also stille
To his moderes bour,
As dew in Aprille
That falleth on the flour.

He cam also stille
There his moder lay,
As dew in Aprille
That falleth on the spray.

Moder and maiden
Was never non but sche;
Well may swich a lady
Godes moder be.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A carol of the Annunciation from the early fifteenth century: Christ comes to Mary ‘as dew in Aprille / That falleth on the grass’. The word ‘makeles’ means both matchless and mateless. Text from E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick, Early English Lyrics (1907).

Study notes by the editors

  1. The poem and its manuscript l. 1

    The lyric survives only in British Library Sloane MS 2593, a small fifteenth-century collection of carols and songs (the Library dates it c. 1400; others say c. 1430) that may have belonged to a professional singer. W. W. Greg pointed out that it reworks lines from a thirteenth-century poem in a Trinity College, Cambridge manuscript closely enough to be dependent; Alan Fletcher found its closing lines quoted in a fifteenth-century sermon, which suggests it was well known. Its subject is the Incarnation: Christ's silent coming into Mary's womb and the paradox of a virgin mother. The opening 'I sing of' places it in the tradition of sung praise of the Virgin.

    British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907); Rosemary Woolf, The English Religious Lyric in the Middle Ages (Oxford, 1968)

  2. makeles l. 2

    'Makeles' is built on Middle English 'make', a mate or equal (Old English 'gemaca'). It therefore means both 'matchless, peerless' and 'mateless, without a spouse', so in one word it praises Mary as beyond compare and declares her virginal. Many readers also hear in it the sense 'spotless', echoing Latin 'immaculata' (without stain, 'macula'), though that sense rests on a sound-likeness rather than etymology. The MED records 'makeles' in both principal senses. The pun introduces the poem's theme: Mary is unique precisely because she is both mother and maiden.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles); Oxford English Dictionary

  3. King of all kings l. 3

    'King of kings' is a title of God and of Christ in the New Testament: Revelation 19:16 ('KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS') and 1 Timothy 6:15. Placing the highest title beside the humble 'maiden' sets up the poem's courtly and theological contrast: the greatest king becomes a child of a girl. The word order of the stanza ('King of all kinges / To her sone sche ches') puts the king first and Mary's choice last, so the line can be read as an act of royal favour and of divine humility at once.

    The Bible (KJV), Revelation 19:16; The Bible (KJV), 1 Timothy 6:15

  4. 'To her sone sche ches' l. 4

    'Ches' is the past tense of 'chesen', to choose. The plain sense is 'she chose the King of all kings to be her son', which gives Mary an active part in the Incarnation, recalling her consent at the Annunciation: 'be it unto me according to thy word' (Luke 1:38). Theology usually stresses that God chose Mary, and some readers take the line as ambiguous on purpose. Either way, the courtly vocabulary of choosing a lord or lover is turned to sacred use. The line's quiet assertion of the maiden's will is one reason modern critics find the poem so humane.

    The Bible (KJV), Luke 1:38; Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles)

  5. also stille l. 5

    'Also' here means 'as' (it is the full form from which 'as' was reduced), so 'He cam also stille ... As dew' means 'He came as silently as dew'. 'Stille' means quiet, silent, without commotion, and perhaps also secret. The idea that the Word came into the world in silence echoes Wisdom 18:14-15 ('while all things were in quiet silence ... thine Almighty word leaped down from heaven'), sung as the introit 'Dum medium silentium' in the Christmas season. Laura Saetveit Miles stresses Mary's own stillness as a sign of her virginity, while Derek Pearsall emphasises the ease and delicacy of the dew images.

    The Bible (KJV), Wisdom of Solomon 18:14-15 (Apocrypha); British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles); Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan)

  6. There l. 6

    In Middle English 'there' frequently serves as a relative adverb, 'where'. So 'He cam also stille / There his moder was' is 'He came as quietly to where his mother was'. The same construction returns in stanza 4 ('There his moder lay'). The calling of Mary 'his moder' even before the coming of the child is a deliberate paradox: in the poem's eternal perspective, Christ chooses his own mother and comes to her as her son.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)

  7. Dew in April ll. 7-8

    Dew that falls without being seen or heard and leaves the ground unharmed was a favourite medieval figure for Christ's entry into Mary's womb without breaking her virginity. Behind it lie Gideon's fleece, wet with dew while the ground was dry (Judges 6:36-40), long read as a type of the Virgin; Psalm 72:6, 'He shall come down like rain upon the mown grass' (Latin Vulgate 'sicut pluvia in vellus', like rain upon a fleece); and the Advent text 'Drop down, ye heavens, from above' (Isaiah 45:8, 'Rorate caeli'). Derek Pearsall connects the image to Psalm 72. 'Aprille' links the Annunciation (25 March) to spring.

    The Bible (KJV), Judges 6:36-40; The Bible (KJV), Psalm 72:6; The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 45:8; British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles)

  8. Grass, flower, spray: a progression? ll. 8, 12, 16

    The dew falls first on 'the grass', then on 'the flour' (flower), then on 'the spray' (a twig or branch), while Christ comes to the place where his mother 'was', then to her 'bour', then to where she 'lay'. Several critics have read the sequence as a progression, from conception through pregnancy to birth, or as rising from ground level to the branch. Derek Pearsall argues against treating the images as stages, finding in them rather a cumulative sense of ease and grace. Students should note that both readings depend on the poem's incremental repetition, where a single word changes in each stanza.

    British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)

  9. Incremental repetition sts 2-4

    The three central stanzas repeat 'He cam also stille ... As dew in Aprille / That falleth on the ...', varying only the place (where his mother was, her bower, where she lay) and the thing touched by dew (grass, flower, spray). This technique, called incremental repetition, is common in ballads and carols and suits a sung text. The repetition itself enacts the poem's meaning: the coming of Christ is quiet, gentle and repeated like dew, with no dramatic event. The short lines of two or three stresses, rhyming abcb, keep the movement light.

    E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907); British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles)

  10. bour l. 10

    'Bour' (bower) is Middle English for an inner room, especially a lady's private chamber, a word at home in romance and courtly lyric. Its use here makes Mary a noble lady visited by a royal lover, a common idiom in devotional poetry to the Virgin. Medieval writers also called Mary's womb the chamber or bridal chamber in which God was joined to human nature (an image derived from Psalm 19:5, the bridegroom coming out of his chamber, read in the liturgy of the Nativity). The word carries both the literal and the figurative sense.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); The Bible (KJV), Psalm 19:5

  11. flour (flower) l. 12

    'Flour' is the Middle English spelling of 'flower'. The dew-touched flower sits naturally in the sequence of grass and spray, but it also recalls one of the commonest symbols of the Virgin and of Christ: the prophecy of a 'rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch [or flower] shall grow out of his roots' (Isaiah 11:1), read as a prophecy of Mary (the rod, Latin 'virga', punning on 'virgo') and Christ (the flower). Medieval carols such as 'There is no rose of such virtue' make similar use of floral images for the Incarnation.

    The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 11:1; Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan)

  12. lay l. 14

    The change from 'was' (stanza 2) to 'bour' (stanza 3) to 'lay' (stanza 4) is often read as moving towards childbirth, since 'to lie' was the usual term for a woman's confinement ('lying-in'). Those who see a progression in the poem find the Nativity here; others, like Pearsall, regard the variation as musical rather than narrative. The line shows how much meaning the poem obtains from tiny changes within its repeated frame.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles)

  13. spray l. 16

    'Spray' means a slender shoot or twig with its leaves or blossoms (MED, OED). After grass and flower, the dew now falls on the branch. Readers who connect the images with Isaiah 11:1 hear the 'rod' or branch of Jesse, the type of Mary, and so see the dew settling where it belongs. Others note simply that 'spray' completes the rhyme with 'lay' and finishes the series with the highest and most delicate part of the plant.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Oxford English Dictionary; The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 11:1

  14. Mother and maiden ll. 17-20

    The last stanza states the paradox the poem has shown: 'Moder and maiden / Was never non but sche', no one except Mary was ever both mother and virgin. The doctrine rests on Isaiah 7:14 ('a virgin shall conceive') as cited in Matthew 1:23. 'Godes moder' translates the title Theotokos (God-bearer), affirmed at the Council of Ephesus (431). The final lines move from narrative to praise: 'Well may swich a lady / Godes moder be' (such a lady is truly fit to be God's mother). The conclusion returns to the 'makeles' of line 2: she is without equal.

    The Bible (KJV), Isaiah 7:14; The Bible (KJV), Matthew 1:23; British Library, Sloane MS 2593; see 'I syng of a mayden', Wikipedia (Greg, Pearsall, Miles)
