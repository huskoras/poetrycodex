Nature

Sumer Is Icumen In

by Anonymous (Middle English) c. 13th–16th century

From Reading Abbey manuscript (British Library, Harley 978)

Nature

Sumer is icumen in,
Lhude sing cuccu!
Groweth sed, and bloweth med,
And springth the wude nu--
Sing cuccu!

Awe bleteth after lomb,
Lhouth after calve cu;
Bulluc sterteth, bucke verteth,
Murie sing cuccu!

Cuccu, cuccu, well singes thu, cuccu:
Ne swike thu naver nu;
Sing cuccu, nu, sing cuccu,
Sing cuccu, sing cuccu, nu!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

The ‘Cuckoo Song’, copied at Reading Abbey in the thirteenth century with music for a round in four voices over a two-voice ground. Only the English words are given here. Text from Quiller-Couch’s Oxford Book of English Verse (1900).

Study notes by the editors

  1. The Cuckoo Song and its manuscript l. 1

    The 'Cuckoo Song' is preserved, with its music, on folio 11v of British Library Harley MS 978, a miscellany linked to Reading Abbey (or its dependency at Leominster). The music page is usually dated to the 1260s, though earlier and later dates have been argued (Manfred Bukofzer argued in 1944 for a later date; most scholars now accept the mid-century dating). It is a rota, a round in which up to four singers enter in turn, over a two-voice ground called a pes. It is among the earliest surviving English secular lyrics with music and is remarkable as a six-part composition. A Latin devotional text, 'Perspice Christicola', is written under the same music.

    'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999); DIAMM, GB-Lbl Harley 978 (manuscript record)

  2. Sumer ... icumen l. 1

    In Middle English 'sumer' could mean the warm season generally, including what we call spring; the song is in fact about spring (cuckoo, lambs, calves, budding woods). 'Icumen' is the past participle of 'cumen', to come, with the prefix 'i-' descended from Old English 'ge-' (as in German 'gekommen'); so 'Sumer is icumen in' means 'summer has come in'. Using 'is' as the auxiliary with a verb of motion is normal in Middle English. Ezra Pound's well-known parody, 'Ancient Music', turns the line into a curse on winter.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  3. Lhude sing cuccu l. 2

    'Lhude' (loudly, from Old English 'hlude') keeps an old spelling for the aspirated 'hl-'. 'Sing' is imperative or subjunctive: 'sing, cuckoo' or 'let the cuckoo sing'. The cuckoo's call was the classic sign of spring's arrival in English lore. Yet the bird also had disreputable associations, as a nest-parasite and a symbol of cuckoldry, and Roscow has argued that it is an odd choice for a reverdie (a spring song) unless irony is intended. Most readers take the song's delight at face value, but the debate is worth knowing.

    'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999); Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Oxford English Dictionary

  4. Groweth sed, and bloweth med l. 3

    The verb comes first in each half-line, a lively word order typical of the song. 'Sed' is seed; 'bloweth' is from Old English 'blowan', to blossom or flower (distinct from 'blawan', to blow), and 'med' is a meadow (modern 'mead'). The internal rhyme ('sed' / 'med') and the paired verbs give a sense of everything bursting into growth at once. The scene widens from the crops to the meadow to the wood in the next line.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Oxford English Dictionary; 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  5. springth the wude nu l. 4

    'Springth' (springs) means comes into leaf or shoots up; 'wude' is wood. 'Nu' (now) is a key word in the song, repeated in the last stanza, insisting on the present moment of spring. The present tense throughout ('groweth', 'bloweth', 'bleteth') gives the song a festive immediacy, as if the singers were watching nature wake up around them. The verb 'springen' is the same one that gives the season of spring its name, though the noun in that sense is later.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  6. Awe bleteth after lomb l. 6

    'Awe' is ewe, a female sheep (Old English 'eowu'), and 'lomb' is lamb. The ewe calls after her young, as in the next line the cow lows after her calf. The pair moves the song from plants to animals and from growth to birth and maternal care, and the alliteration and strong verbs make the stanza sound like a list of farmyard events. The word order (subject, verb, 'after' + object) is mirrored in the following line.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  7. Lhouth after calve cu l. 7

    'Lhouth' is the verb 'to low' (Old English 'hlowan'), written with the archaic 'lh-' seen in 'Lhude'. The word order is inverted for rhyme ('cu' rhyming with 'cuccu' and 'nu'): 'the cow lows after her calf'. Together with the preceding line it presents the mothers of the farm calling to their newborn young, a homely image of spring fertility. The parallel syntax of the two lines, verb in the middle, makes them sound like a rhythmic chant suited to a round.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  8. Bulluc sterteth l. 8

    'Bulluc' is a young bull or bullock; 'sterteth' (modern 'starts') means leaps, springs or capers. The young male animals are full of energy, the counterpart of the mothers in the previous two lines. The two short clauses of the line ('Bulluc sterteth, bucke verteth') are balanced with rhyme and parallel structure, making the second verb, a famous crux, all the more conspicuous.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  9. verteth l. 8

    The best-known crux in the song. Most editors have taken 'verteth' as a southern form (with 'v' for 'f') of the verb 'farten', to break wind, giving a frank farmyard image of animal high spirits; Ericson (1938) and Moore (1951) defended this reading. Hans Platzer (1995) argued that the traditional reading is less secure than the number of editors supporting it suggests and proposed a borrowing from Latin 'vertere', to turn, so 'the buck capers about'. 'Bucke' may be a male deer or a he-goat. Students should give both readings; the earthy one remains the more common in anthologies.

    'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999); Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Oxford English Dictionary

  10. Murie l. 9

    'Murie' is the southern Middle English form of 'merry' (Old English 'myrge'), which covered pleasant, delightful and agreeable as well as cheerful. It closes the second stanza as 'Lhude' closed the first line, and the cuckoo's song becomes the merry centre of the whole season. The adverb sets the tone of the whole piece: this is a song of pleasure in the season, sung by a group, and its insistent repetitions in performance multiply the merriment the word names.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Oxford English Dictionary

  11. A reverdie l. 10

    In praising the cuckoo ('well singes thu', you sing well), the song addresses the bird directly, a common move in spring poems. It is usually classed as a reverdie, a genre from Old French, a celebration of the return of green and of birdsong; it often leads into love, but here the focus stays entirely on nature and the farm. Placed in a monastic manuscript with a Latin religious text under the same music, the song shows how sacred and secular repertories were shared. Roscow has questioned whether a cuckoo song fits the genre without irony.

    'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999); Roscow, 'What is Sumer is Icumen In?' (1999), as summarised in Wikipedia

  12. Ne swike thu naver nu l. 11

    'Swike' is from Old English 'swican', to cease, desist or fail. With the double negative typical of Middle English ('Ne ... naver'), the line means 'never cease now'. It is a wish for spring to last for ever, and in a round, where the singers can repeat endlessly, the request is fulfilled by the form itself. The imperative addressed to the bird also works as a wish addressed to the season itself.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999)

  13. Rota and pes ll. 12-13

    The final lines preserve the text of the pes, the repeated foundation sung by two lower voices: 'Sing cuccu nu, sing cuccu'. Over it, up to four singers enter in turn with the melody, producing a six-part texture; Latin instructions in the manuscript explain how to perform it. Repetition of 'cuccu' and 'nu' in the text thus reflects musical function as much as poetic choice. The Latin text 'Perspice Christicola', on Christ's sacrifice, was written under the same music, and a later scribe altered some notes so that the melody fits both texts.

    'Sumer is icumen in', Wikipedia (Harley 978, rota, Platzer 1995, Roscow 1999); DIAMM, GB-Lbl Harley 978 (manuscript record); 'Sumer is icumen in / Perspice Christicola: silencing the cuckoo' (Early Music Muse)
