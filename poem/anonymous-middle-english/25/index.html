Love

Western Wind

by Anonymous (Middle English) c. 13th–16th century

From Royal Appendix MS 58

LoveNature

Western wind, when will thou blow,
The small rain down can rain?
Christ, if my love were in my arms
And I in my bed again!

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

Four lines found in a music manuscript of the early sixteenth century (British Museum, Royal Appendix 58) and among the most famous English lyrics of longing; its tune later served for several Mass settings. The 1907 editors modernised its spelling. Text from E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick, Early English Lyrics (1907).

Study notes by the editors

  1. The song and its manuscript l. 1

    The lyric survives with its melody in a partbook of about 1530 now in the British Library (Royal Appendix MS 58), a collection of songs from the court circle of Henry VIII. The words may be older than the copy, but nothing certain is known of their author or occasion. The tune had an unusual afterlife: John Taverner, Christopher Tye and John Sheppard each used it as the cantus firmus (the borrowed melody) of a Mass, so a secular song of longing became the skeleton of sacred polyphony. The archive prints the modernised spelling of Chambers and Sidgwick (1907); the manuscript reads 'Westron wynde'. Nineteenth-century editors such as William Chappell (1859) altered the text, partly to soften the oath in line 3.

    'Westron Wynde', Wikipedia (manuscript, Mass settings, Frey 1976); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)

  2. The west wind and spring l. 1

    For an English reader steeped in the classical tradition, the west wind is Zephyrus, the gentle wind whose breath announces spring and new growth. Chaucer opens the General Prologue with the same pairing of April showers and 'Zephirus' bringing the crops to life. The speaker's question ('when will thou blow') therefore implies a present season of cold, waiting and separation, and asks when the season of renewal (and of reunion) will come. Read this way, lines 1-2 are not idle weather but a calendar of desire: the lover can only be reunited when the weather turns. 'Will thou' (for 'wilt thou') is the plain second-person form of the early Tudor manuscript.

    Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue, ll. 1-6 (Harvard Chaucer site); Oxford English Dictionary

  3. small rain l. 2

    In Middle and early modern English 'small' could describe rain made of fine drops, a drizzle or soft shower (OED, MED, 'small', sense of fine or slender in texture). The phrase points again to spring weather, the fertile shower that comes with the west wind, and contrasts with the harsh weather the speaker is presumably enduring. Some readers hear a further suggestion of tears, or of a gentle release after long tension; these are interpretations rather than glosses. The concrete, almost homely phrase is part of what makes the lyric so memorable: the larger feelings of the poem are never stated, only implied through weather and bed.

    Oxford English Dictionary; Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); Charles Frey, 'Interpreting "Western Wind"' (1976), as listed in Wikipedia's bibliography

  4. 'can rain': the syntax of line 2 l. 2

    Line 2 is grammatically awkward to a modern reader. 'Can' is best taken as a late form of the Middle English auxiliary 'gan' (from 'ginnen', to begin), which by the fifteenth century was often used simply to form a past or periphrastic verb, 'did rain'; here, after the question, the sense is something like 'so that the small rain may rain down'. Editors also debate whether the two lines form one question ('When will you blow, so the rain can fall?') or two linked ones, and whether the rain is wished for or is what the wind should blow away. William Chappell, believing the wind drove the rain off, inserted 'doth' to make his preferred sense. The archive keeps the question mark after line 2.

    Middle English Dictionary (Middle English Compendium, University of Michigan); 'Westron Wynde', Wikipedia (manuscript, Mass settings, Frey 1976)

  5. 'Christ': oath or prayer? l. 3

    The single word 'Christ' turns the lyric from weather to passion. It may be heard as an exclamation of longing or frustration, a strong oath by the standards of the time, or as a genuine invocation, a prayer that the lover be restored. Chappell's 1859 text replaced it with 'Oh' (and added an opening 'O'), a change that may have been meant to make the poem a safer reading for women and children, a sign that later readers felt the oath's force. Readers who allegorise the poem (the 'love' as Christ or the soul's desire for God) lean on this word; most modern critics prefer a secular reading in which the name is a cry rather than an address. The ambiguity is part of the lyric's power.

    'Westron Wynde', Wikipedia (manuscript, Mass settings, Frey 1976); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)

  6. The turn to desire l. 3

    After two lines about the weather the poem turns abruptly to the body: 'if my love were in my arms'. 'If' here introduces an exclamatory wish ('O that...'), and 'were' is subjunctive, marking the wish as contrary to fact. We are never told why the lovers are apart (travel, war, the sea, death) and the poem's brevity forces the reader to supply the story. The gender of the speaker is not fixed either, though most readers assume a man away from home. The connection between the halves is left implicit: the spring the speaker longs for in lines 1-2 is the reunion longed for in lines 3-4.

    E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907); 'Westron Wynde', Wikipedia (manuscript, Mass settings, Frey 1976)

  7. 'in my bed again' l. 4

    'And I in my bed again' completes the wish with a return to one's own place. 'Again' suggests the speaker is away (travelling, at sea, on campaign, or in exile) rather than simply lonely. The frankness of the line is notable: the bed is a place of love, of rest and safety, and possibly (in a darker reading) of the grave, though that reading is a minority one. Some interpreters have tried to make the whole lyric spiritual, the soul longing for rest; Charles Frey's 1976 essay is a well-known discussion of how differently the four lines have been read. Most readers take the poem as a direct secular lament.

    Charles Frey, 'Interpreting "Western Wind"' (1976), as listed in Wikipedia's bibliography; E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)

  8. Form: a two-part quatrain ll. 1-4

    The quatrain alternates longer and shorter lines (roughly four and three stresses), like a ballad stanza, but rhymes only lines 2 and 4 ('rain' / 'again'). It is built in two halves: a question addressed to the wind, and an exclamation of personal longing. Nothing connects them grammatically, so the reader must supply the link (the parataxis that has made the poem a favourite example of compression). It was written to be sung, so the metre was shaped by the tune; its rhythm in the archive's modernised spelling is approximate. Its survival as a melody used in three Tudor Masses shows how widely the song was known.

    'Westron Wynde', Wikipedia (manuscript, Mass settings, Frey 1976); E. K. Chambers and F. Sidgwick (eds), Early English Lyrics (1907)
