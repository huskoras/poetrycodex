To Celia

by Ben Jonson 1572-1637

Religion

DRINK to me only with thine eyes,
And I will pledge with mine;
Or leave a kiss but in the cup,
And I'll not look for wine.
The thirst that from the soul doth rise
Doth ask a drink divine:
But might I of Jove's nectar sup,
I would not change for thine.

I sent thee late a rosy wreath,
Not so much honouring thee,
As giving it a hope that there
It could not withered be.
But thou thereon didst only breathe,
And sent'st it back to me:
Since when it grows, and smells, I swear,
Not of itself, but thee.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Song: To Celia title; l. 1

    The poem appeared as 'Song. To Celia' (The Forest 9) in the folio Workes of 1616. 'Celia' (from Latin caelum, heaven) is a stock name for a mistress in Latin and Renaissance love poetry; Jonson also uses it in Volpone's seduction song 'Come, my Celia' (Forest 5). The poem is less a confession than a display of craft: two eight-line stanzas building a single compliment, that the lady is more divine than wine and more fragrant than roses. Its fame grew in the eighteenth century, when it was set to a tune of uncertain origin (often attributed, without proof, to various composers; John Wall Callcott is believed by some to have composed it as a glee around 1790); in that form it became a parlour and drinking song.

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; 'Drink to Me Only with Thine Eyes', Wikipedia (summary of the reception, Cumberland 1791, Symonds 1884, and the tune's history)

  2. Philostratus' love letters ll. 1-16

    Richard Cumberland pointed out in The Observer in 1791, and J. A. Symonds demonstrated in 1884, that the poem stitches together phrases from several of Philostratus' love letters: one asks the beloved to drink only with her eyes, or to put her lips to the cup and fill it with kisses; others describe a rose wreath sent not to honour the beloved but so that the roses might not wither, and ask her to send it back smelling of herself. The letters are numbered differently in different editions (RPO cites them as XXIV, XXX and XXXI). Hoeniger calls the poem little more than a skilled cento; George Burke Johnston preferred to describe it as a synthesis of scattered passages. Either way, Jonson's art lies in turning loose Greek prose into tight English song, a typical example of his classical 'imitation'.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; Philostratus, Love Letters (Epistles), in Alciphron, Aelian and Philostratus: The Letters, trans. A. R. Benner and F. H. Fobes (Loeb Classical Library, 1949); George Burke Johnston, ed., Poems of Ben Jonson (Muses' Library; London: Routledge), note to The Forest 9; 'Drink to Me Only with Thine Eyes', Wikipedia (summary of the reception, Cumberland 1791, Symonds 1884, and the tune's history)

  3. pledge l. 2

    In the drinking ritual of the period one person drank a health and the other 'pledged' it by drinking in return (OED, pledge, v., sense of drinking in response to a toast). The conceit makes glances into wine: if Celia drinks to him with her eyes, he will answer with his. The image belongs to the Petrarchan tradition in which love enters through the eyes, but here it is made convivial and social rather than painful. The next couplet offers an alternative: a kiss left on the rim of the cup will be enough, and he will not need wine at all.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; Philostratus, Love Letters (Epistles), in Alciphron, Aelian and Philostratus: The Letters, trans. A. R. Benner and F. H. Fobes (Loeb Classical Library, 1949)

  4. a kiss in the cup l. 3

    This is the second half of the Philostratean letter Jonson is following, in which the lover asks the beloved to fill the cup with kisses instead of wine. 'But' here means 'only': she need do no more than leave a kiss. The line also recalls the ancient practice of lovers drinking from the side of the cup the other has touched (a motif in Ovid, Amores 1.4.31-32, and the Greek Anthology). The point is that her slightest gesture surpasses the real drink.

    Philostratus, Love Letters (Epistles), in Alciphron, Aelian and Philostratus: The Letters, trans. A. R. Benner and F. H. Fobes (Loeb Classical Library, 1949); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; George Burke Johnston, ed., Poems of Ben Jonson (Muses' Library; London: Routledge), note to The Forest 9

  5. thirst of the soul ll. 5-6

    The second quatrain turns from the cup to the soul. A thirst that rises from the soul cannot be satisfied by wine; it asks for something divine. The phrasing borrows the Platonic and Christian language of spiritual thirst (compare Psalm 42:2, 'My soul thirsteth for God'), but uses it as gallant compliment: the divine drink is the lady herself. The tension between the sacred vocabulary and the secular wooing is typical of Renaissance love lyric and gives the stanza its lift before the final hyperbole.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, 1611; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  6. Jove's nectar l. 7

    In Greek myth the gods drink nectar and eat ambrosia, which keep them immortal (Homer, Iliad 1.598, Odyssey 5.93). 'Might I of Jove's nectar sup' means 'even if I could drink Jupiter's nectar'. The 1616 folio prints 'Ioves', and because the capital I and J were not yet distinguished in print, later texts sometimes misread the word as 'Love's', which changes the sense; the archive's 'Jove's' is the correct reading. The stanza ends on the classic hyperbole of the lover who would refuse immortality for his mistress.

    'Drink to Me Only with Thine Eyes', Wikipedia (summary of the reception, Cumberland 1791, Symonds 1884, and the tune's history); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13

  7. change for l. 8

    The idiom is the older sense of 'change' as 'exchange' (OED, change, v., to give or take in exchange). The line is grammatically compressed: 'I would not change [thine] for [it]' or 'I would not change [it] for thine'. Readers have noticed that the words can be read either way; most editors take it that he would not exchange her cup even for Jove's nectar, which is the natural sense of the climax. The neat monosyllables give the stanza a firm close.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; George Burke Johnston, ed., Poems of Ben Jonson (Muses' Library; London: Routledge), note to The Forest 9

  8. the rosy wreath l. 9

    The second stanza takes up another Philostratean letter, in which the lover sends roses so that they might not wither when touched by the beloved. The wreath (a chaplet of roses, worn at feasts in the classical world) is a carpe diem emblem of fading beauty, but Jonson reverses its usual moral: instead of reminding the lady that she will fade like the roses, he imagines her as the source of the roses' immortality. 'Not so much honouring thee' is a witty, almost impudent, qualification: the compliment is greater for pretending to be smaller.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; Philostratus, Love Letters (Epistles), in Alciphron, Aelian and Philostratus: The Letters, trans. A. R. Benner and F. H. Fobes (Loeb Classical Library, 1949); The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  9. her breath ll. 13-16

    The lady's response looks like a rejection (she returns the gift), but the poem turns it into a miracle: her breath has entered the roses, which now grow and give off her scent rather than their own. The closing 'Not of itself, but thee' answers 'Not so much honouring thee' in l. 10, completing a chiasmus across the stanza. In Philostratus the lover asks for the roses back for this very reason. Some readers find a quiet melancholy under the wit: the lover keeps only a token of a lady who has refused him.

    Philostratus, Love Letters (Epistles), in Alciphron, Aelian and Philostratus: The Letters, trans. A. R. Benner and F. H. Fobes (Loeb Classical Library, 1949); Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  10. metre and rhyme whole poem

    Each stanza alternates eight- and six-syllable lines, the 'common measure' of hymns and ballads, which made it easy to set to music. Only the even lines rhyme across the whole octave (mine/wine/divine/thine; thee/be/me/thee), while the odd lines rhyme in pairs across the quatrains (eyes/rise, cup/sup; wreath/breathe, there/swear): RPO gives the scheme as abcbabcb. The repeated long -ine rhyme of the first stanza is itself musical, linking the lover's 'mine' to the lady's 'thine' through 'wine' and 'divine'. The indentation of the even lines in the archive text reflects this alternation.

    Representative Poetry Online (Univ. of Toronto), 'Song: To Celia', ed. F. D. Hoeniger, notes to ll. 1, 8, 9, 13; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem
