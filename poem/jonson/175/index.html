Nature

To Penshurst

by Ben Jonson 1572-1637

From The Forest (1616)

Nature

Thou art not, Penshurst, built to envious show
Of touch or marble; nor canst boast a row
Of polish'd pillars, or a roof of gold:
Thou hast no lantern, whereof tales are told;
Or stair, or courts; but stand'st an ancient pile,
And these grudg'd at, art reverenced the while.
Thou joy'st in better marks, of soil, of air,
Of wood, of water; therein thou art fair.
Thou hast thy walks for health, as well as sport:
Thy mount, to which thy Dryads do resort,
Where Pan and Bacchus their high feasts have made,
Beneath the broad beech, and the chestnut shade;
That taller tree, which of a nut was set,
At his great birth, where all the Muses met.
There, in the writhed bark, are cut the names
Of many a sylvan, taken with his flames;
And thence the ruddy satyrs oft provoke
The lighter fauns, to reach thy lady's oak.
Thy copse, too, named of Gamage, thou hast there,
That never fails to serve thee season'd deer,
When thou wouldst feast, or exercise thy friends.
The lower land, that to the river bends,
Thy sheep, thy bullocks, kine, and calves do feed;
The middle grounds thy mares and horses breed.
Each bank doth yield thee conies; and the tops
Fertile of wood, Ashore and Sydney's copp's,
To crown thy open table, doth provide
The purpled pheasant, with the speckled side:
The painted partridge lies in ev'ry field,
And for thy mess is willing to be kill'd.
And if the high-swoln Medway fail thy dish,
Thou hast thy ponds, that pay thee tribute fish,
Fat aged carps that run into thy net,
And pikes, now weary their own kind to eat,
As loth the second draught or cast to stay,
Officiously at first themselves betray.
Bright eels that emulate them, and leap on land,
Before the fisher, or into his hand.
Then hath thy orchard fruit, thy garden flowers,
Fresh as the air, and new as are the hours.
The early cherry, with the later plum,
Fig, grape, and quince, each in his time doth come:
The blushing apricot, and woolly peach
Hang on thy walls, that every child may reach.
And though thy walls be of the country stone,
They're rear'd with no man's ruin, no man's groan;
There's none, that dwell about them, wish them down;
But all come in, the farmer and the clown;
And no one empty-handed, to salute
Thy lord and lady, though they have no suit.
Some bring a capon, some a rural cake,
Some nuts, some apples; some that think they make
The better cheeses, bring them; or else send
By their ripe daughters, whom they would commend
This way to husbands; and whose baskets bear
An emblem of themselves in plum, or pear.
But what can this (more than express their love)
Add to thy free provisions, far above
The need of such? whose liberal board doth flow,
With all that hospitality doth know!
Where comes no guest, but is allow'd to eat,
Without his fear, and of thy lord's own meat:
Where the same beer and bread, and self-same wine,
That is his lordship's, shall be also mine.
And I not fain to sit (as some this day,
At great men's tables) and yet dine away.
Here no man tells my cups; nor standing by,
A waiter, doth my gluttony envy:
But gives me what I call, and lets me eat,
He knows, below, he shall find plenty of meat;
Thy tables hoard not up for the next day,
Nor, when I take my lodging, need I pray
For fire, or lights, or livery; all is there;
As if thou then wert mine, or I reign'd here:
There's nothing I can wish, for which I stay.
That found king James, when hunting late, this way,
With his brave son, the prince; they saw thy fires
Shine bright on every hearth, as the desires
Of thy Penates had been set on flame,
To entertain them; or the country came,
With all their zeal, to warm their welcome here.
What (great, I will not say, but) sudden chear
Didst thou then make 'em! and what praise was heap'd
On thy good lady, then! who therein reap'd
The just reward of her high huswifry;
To have her linen, plate, and all things nigh,
When she was far; and not a room, but drest,
As if it had expected such a guest!
These, Penshurst, are thy praise, and yet not all.
Thy lady's noble, fruitful, chaste withal.
His children thy great lord may call his own;'
A fortune, in this age, but rarely known.
They are, and have been taught religion; thence
Their gentler spirits have suck'd innocence.
Each morn, and even, they are taught to pray,
With the whole household, and may, every day,
Read in their virtuous parents' noble parts,
The mysteries of manners, arms, and arts.
Now, Penshurst, they that will proportion thee
With other edifices, when they see
Those proud ambitious heaps, and nothing else,
May say, their lords have built, but thy lord dwells.

Codex Note

The Forest, no. 2.

Study notes by the editors

  1. To Penshurst: occasion and genre title; l. 1

    Penshurst Place, near Tonbridge in Kent, was the seat of Robert Sidney (1563-1626), younger brother of Sir Philip Sidney, created Viscount Lisle in 1605 and Earl of Leicester in 1618, and of his wife Barbara Gamage. Because Prince Henry (d. November 1612) appears as alive (ll. 76-77), the poem is usually dated c. 1611-12. It was printed as the second poem of The Forest in the 1616 folio. Addressing the house itself, Jonson adapts Martial's praise of working estates (esp. 3.58) and Horace's rural ideal to create a genre later imitated by Carew, Herrick and Marvell. G. R. Hibbard's classic essay defined the genre; Alastair Fowler later collected its examples.

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; G. R. Hibbard, 'The Country House Poem of the Seventeenth Century', Journal of the Warburg and Courtauld Institutes 19 (1956); Alastair Fowler, ed., The Country House Poem: A Cabinet of Seventeenth-Century Estate Poems and Related Items (Edinburgh UP, 1994); Martial, Epigrams, Book 3 (esp. 3.58 on Faustinus' villa at Baiae, and 3.60 on unequal dinners), Latin text

  2. envious show l. 1

    The poem opens by negation, defining Penshurst by what it lacks. 'Envious show' means display designed to excite envy (and perhaps display that itself envies rivals). Contemporary readers would think of huge new houses such as Theobalds, Burghley or Robert Cecil's Hatfield (built 1607-11), monuments of courtly ambition. Penshurst, a medieval house extended by the Sidneys, stands for continuity and use rather than conspicuous expense. The contrast returns in the last lines, where other lords' 'proud ambitious heaps' are opposed to Penshurst. Don Wayne reads the whole poem as a defence of a traditional ideal of community against new forms of wealth.

    Don E. Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; 'Penshurst Place', Wikipedia (history of the house, the Sidney family and the Sidney oak)

  3. touch l. 2

    Touchstone was originally the dark stone on which gold was tested by rubbing (OED, touch, n., sense of 'touchstone'), and by extension any costly black stone used in architecture and tombs. Along with marble, polished pillars and gilded roofs, it signifies imported, expensive splendour. The list echoes Horace, Odes 2.18.1-5 ('Non ebur neque aureum / mea renidet in domo lacunar'), where the poet's modest house has no ivory or gilded ceiling and no marble columns. Jonson transfers the Horatian topos of modest sufficiency from the poet to a great aristocratic house.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  4. lantern l. 4

    In architecture a lantern is a structure on top of a dome or roof, with windows, admitting light (OED, lantern, n., architectural sense). Elizabethan and Jacobean prodigy houses were known for such features, and for grand staircases and courts, all of which Penshurst is said to lack. The line is read as a general jab at showy modern houses; no particular target is certain. 'Pile' in l. 5 simply means a large building, with no pejorative sense; 'ancient' stresses its age and lineage.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  5. these grudg'd at l. 6

    The syntax is compressed. The most common reading takes 'these' as the lantern, stairs and courts just listed: such things are grudged at (envied or resented), whereas Penshurst is reverenced 'the while' (all the while, meanwhile). An alternative takes 'these grudg'd at' as an absolute phrase meaning 'although these [splendours] are begrudged you', i.e. though you are denied them, you are revered. Either way the point is moral: respect follows not from display but from what the house is and does.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  6. Dryads, Pan and Bacchus ll. 10-12

    The estate's 'mount' (a raised viewing mound in the park) is imagined as a sacred grove. Dryads are tree nymphs; Pan, the Arcadian god of shepherds; Bacchus, god of wine and revelry. The 'broad beech' recalls the opening of Virgil's first Eclogue ('patulae ... sub tegmine fagi'), signalling that Penshurst is a living Arcadia. This mythological colouring flatters the Sidneys, whose most famous member wrote the Arcadia, and makes the estate a place where classical pastoral and English country life meet. Raymond Williams later criticised exactly this kind of mythologising for hiding real rural labour.

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Raymond Williams, The Country and the City (London: Chatto & Windus, 1973), the discussion of the country-house poem; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  7. the Sidney oak ll. 13-14

    Philip Sidney (1554-86), poet, courtier and soldier, mortally wounded at Zutphen, was born at Penshurst on 30 November 1554, and an oak (the 'nut' is an acorn) was said to have been planted to mark it. The tree traditionally identified with it, Sidney's Oak, stood until it died in 2016, though its real age was probably far greater than the legend allows. 'Where all the Muses met' turns his birth into a mythic nativity attended by the goddesses of poetry, a compliment to the author of Astrophil and Stella, the Arcadia and the Defence of Poesy. The house is thus praised through its literary lineage, and Jonson, a poet, implicitly places himself in that line.

    'Penshurst Place', Wikipedia (history of the house, the Sidney family and the Sidney oak); The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  8. names cut in the bark ll. 15-16

    Carving a beloved's name on a tree is a commonplace of pastoral (Virgil, Eclogue 10.53-54; Ovid, Heroides 5.21-24; it recurs in As You Like It). Here the carvers are 'sylvans', forest beings or rustic folk, 'taken with his flames' - caught by the ardours of Sidney's love verse, or by the passion his memory inspires. 'Writhed' means twisted, describing the gnarled bark of an old oak. The two lines make the tree a living monument to Sidney's poetry.

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11

  9. satyrs, fauns and the lady's oak ll. 17-18

    Satyrs and fauns are goat-legged woodland spirits of Greek and Roman myth, here sporting like country youths. 'Provoke' means challenge; 'lighter' means nimbler. 'Thy lady's oak' is by tradition 'My Lady's Oak', under which Barbara Gamage (Lady Sidney) was taken in labour; this is local tradition rather than documented fact. The point is that every feature of the park carries family memory, and the mythology is domestic and affectionate.

    'Penshurst Place', Wikipedia (history of the house, the Sidney family and the Sidney oak); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  10. Gamage's copse ll. 19-21

    Barbara Gamage (c. 1563-1621), of Coity Castle in Glamorgan, married Robert Sidney in September 1584; as a wealthy only child and heiress she had attracted many suitors, and the match was arranged largely for political and financial reasons, though it proved a happy one. Tradition says she liked to feed the deer in the shade of this copse. Naming the copse after her inscribes the lady into the landscape, preparing for her praise later in the poem (ll. 84-88, 90). 'Season'd deer' are deer killed in their proper season, ready for the table. Hunting and venison were aristocratic privileges, and 'exercise thy friends' refers to hunting as entertainment for guests.

    'Penshurst Place', Wikipedia (history of the house, the Sidney family and the Sidney oak); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  11. conies; Ashore and Sydney's copp's ll. 25-26

    'Coney' was the normal word for an adult rabbit (OED, coney, n.). The 'tops / Fertile of wood' are the wooded uplands of the estate, two of which Jonson names: Ashour wood and Sidney's copse, real places in the Penshurst park. 'Copp's' is a contraction of 'copses' (coppices: woods regularly cut for timber and underwood). The precise local names give the panegyric documentary weight, as if Jonson were walking the estate. Note the singular verb 'doth' with the plural subject 'tops', a common early modern licence.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11

  12. willing game ll. 27-38

    Birds 'lie' in every field and are 'willing to be kill'd'; fish 'pay thee tribute', pikes betray themselves 'officiously' (dutifully, eagerly), eels leap into the fisherman's hand. The motif comes from Martial 10.30, where the fish at Apollinaris' villa swim up to their master, and more widely from golden-age myth (Hesiod, Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.89-112), in which the earth gives without labour. Critics divide over its tone: some find gentle comic hyperbole, others (Raymond Williams, followed by many materialist readers) see a revealing ideological move by which the work and violence that supply the lord's table are made to vanish.

    Martial, Epigrams 10.30 (Apollinaris' villa at Formiae, where the fish swim to their master), Latin text; Raymond Williams, The Country and the City (London: Chatto & Windus, 1973), the discussion of the country-house poem; Don E. Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984); The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  13. the Medway l. 31

    The Medway rises in Sussex and flows through Kent to the Thames estuary; the Eden joins it near Penshurst. 'High-swoln' (swollen with flood) explains why the river might 'fail thy dish'. The estate's ponds then 'pay tribute', a political metaphor that makes the fish the house's loyal subjects. The house is imagined as a small kingdom whose produce comes to it as dues, an image that prepares for the king's visit later in the poem.

    'Penshurst Place', Wikipedia (history of the house, the Sidney family and the Sidney oak); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11

  14. the orchard and the Hours ll. 39-44

    The fruits arrive 'each in his time', a picture of ordered abundance rather than sudden luxury; 'his' is the normal neuter possessive. The 'hours' may glance at the Horae, Greek goddesses of the seasons who preside over growth. The apricot and peach that 'Hang on thy walls, that every child may reach' are espaliered on walls, but the phrase also makes the abundance available to everyone, even children: the poem's ideal of generosity extending down the social order. Renaissance country-house poems commonly list fruits and game this way (compare Marvell's 'The Garden').

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Alastair Fowler, ed., The Country House Poem: A Cabinet of Seventeenth-Century Estate Poems and Related Items (Edinburgh UP, 1994); The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  15. built with no man's ruin ll. 45-48

    The line distinguishes Penshurst from houses raised on enclosure, rack-renting and eviction, which were contemporary grievances. 'Country stone' is local stone, not imported marble. 'Clown' (l. 48) means a peasant or rustic, without the modern comic sense (OED). Williams, in a famous critique, argues that the poem's moral contrast depends on erasing the labour that built and fed all such houses. Defenders reply that Jonson is setting an ideal standard against which actual landlords, including perhaps the Sidneys, could be measured. The debate has shaped later readings of the whole genre.

    Raymond Williams, The Country and the City (London: Chatto & Windus, 1973), the discussion of the country-house poem; Don E. Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984); Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; G. R. Hibbard, 'The Country House Poem of the Seventeenth Century', Journal of the Warburg and Courtauld Institutes 19 (1956)

  16. the tenants' gifts ll. 49-56

    In Martial 3.58 the rustic visitor to Faustinus' farm does not come empty-handed ('nec venit inanis rusticus salutator'), bringing honeycomb, cheese and other farm produce, while the grown daughters of honest farmers offer their mothers' gifts in woven baskets. Jonson adapts this closely: 'no one empty-handed', 'ripe daughters', whose baskets of plums or pears are 'an emblem of themselves' (they are ripe for marriage, and the fruits carry erotic suggestion). 'Salute' means to greet formally; 'though they have no suit' means they ask no favour in return, stressing that the gifts are freely given love, not rent or bribery.

    Martial, Epigrams, Book 3 (esp. 3.58 on Faustinus' villa at Baiae, and 3.60 on unequal dinners), Latin text; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  17. Martial and the working estate ll. 57-58

    Martial 3.58, addressed to Bassus, contrasts Faustinus' productive villa at Baiae with merely ornamental country places (idle myrtles, a 'widowed' plane tree, clipped box) that yield nothing for the table. Jonson adapts this into the poem's central contrast between a house that produces and gives and houses that merely display. 'Free provisions' means generous, liberal supplies; 'liberal board' (l. 59) means a generous table. The rhetorical question makes the tenants' gifts purely expressive: they 'express their love' but cannot add anything to such plenty.

    Martial, Epigrams, Book 3 (esp. 3.58 on Faustinus' villa at Baiae, and 3.60 on unequal dinners), Latin text; G. R. Hibbard, 'The Country House Poem of the Seventeenth Century', Journal of the Warburg and Courtauld Institutes 19 (1956); Alastair Fowler, ed., The Country House Poem: A Cabinet of Seventeenth-Century Estate Poems and Related Items (Edinburgh UP, 1994)

  18. equal fare for guests ll. 61-64

    Jonson's speaker, a poet-guest, now enters the poem as 'mine'. Roman satire had attacked patrons who served guests worse food than themselves: Martial 3.60, addressed to Pontice, asks why, now that the dole of food (sportula) is no longer given, he is not served the same dinner as his host, and Juvenal's Satire 5 describes the client Trebius' humiliations at Virro's table. 'Allow'd to eat, / Without his fear' means he may eat without anxiety. 'Of thy lord's own meat' means from the same dishes. The social egalitarianism is real but limited; Wayne notes how carefully the poet places himself within the household order.

    Martial, Epigrams, Book 3 (esp. 3.58 on Faustinus' villa at Baiae, and 3.60 on unequal dinners), Latin text; Juvenal, Satire 5 (the patron Virro's humiliating dinner for his client), Latin text; Don E. Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  19. dine away; fain ll. 65-66

    'Fain' here means constrained, obliged (OED, fain, a., sense of being compelled). The phrase 'sit ... and yet dine away' is variously glossed: the lesser guest at a grand table is seated, yet does not really dine (he is given poorer food than his host), or is in effect sent away to dine elsewhere. Jonson had personal grievances on this score: in the Conversations recorded by Drummond (1619), seated at the lower end of Lord Salisbury's table, he complains that he does not dine with his host, since he gets none of his lord's own dishes. The parenthesis 'as some this day' points the satire at contemporary hosts.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest

  20. tells ll. 67-70

    'Tell' in the sense 'count' survives in 'bank teller' and 'all told' (OED, tell, v.). The waiter does not 'envy' (begrudge) the poet's gluttony, because he knows there is plenty left 'below', in the servants' hall, where the same food will reach him. Jonson, well known for his size and appetite, makes a self-mocking joke about his own 'gluttony'. The passage again shows how the house's plenty runs through every level of the household.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  21. livery ll. 71-74

    The older sense of livery is anything 'delivered', especially rations or provisions given to servants or guests (OED, livery, n., sense of allowance of provisions). The guest at Penshurst does not have to beg servants for fire, candles or a night's allowance: 'all is there'. The extravagant conclusion, 'As if thou then wert mine, or I reign'd here', makes the poet momentarily the lord of the house, a fantasy that anticipates the royal visit that follows: in this house every guest is treated as a king, and the king himself is entertained.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11

  22. King James and Prince Henry ll. 76-78

    James I (reigned 1603-25) was a passionate hunter, and his heir Henry, Prince of Wales (1594-1612), was the hope of the Protestant party with which the Sidneys were associated. 'This way' means near here; 'brave' means splendid. Henry's death on 6 November 1612 provides the usual terminus for the poem's date. The episode is the climax of the hospitality theme: Penshurst, which entertains every rustic and poet generously, proves equal to an unannounced royal visit.

Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984)

  23. Penates ll. 78-79

    In Roman religion the Penates guarded the household and its provisions (penus, food store); with the Lares they were worshipped at the hearth, and Aeneas carries Troy's Penates to Italy in the Aeneid. Jonson's conceit gives the house a soul: the fires on every hearth are the household gods' own desires burning to entertain the king. 'Sudden chear' (l. 82) means improvised entertainment and food ('cheer' in the sense of fare); the parenthesis 'great, I will not say, but' modestly claims that the welcome was not lavish, only immediate.

    The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds., Ben Jonson, vol. 8: The Poems; The Prose Works (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1947), The Forest, with commentary in vol. 11; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword

  24. huswifry ll. 84-88

    'Huswifry' (housewifery) is the skilled oversight of a great household (OED). Lady Sidney had arranged that her linen and silver plate were at hand and every room dressed even while she was 'far' (absent). The praise turns domestic competence into a noble virtue, as 'high' suggests. It also has a political edge: a well-governed household was a type of the well-governed state, and the lady's preparedness for a royal guest proves the house's order.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow

  25. legitimate children ll. 90-92

    The lines are a sharp satirical aside. Jacobean court culture was notorious for adultery scandals, and Roman satire offered precedents, as in Martial's and Juvenal's jokes about husbands and other men's children. Barbara Gamage bore Robert Sidney a large family. The stray closing apostrophe after 'own;' in the archive text is a transcription slip, not part of the poem. The praise of chastity also prepares the final picture of a pious household.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow; Juvenal, Satire 5 (the patron Virro's humiliating dinner for his client), Latin text

  26. mysteries ll. 93-98

    'Mystery' in the sense of a craft or trade (from medieval Latin misterium, ministerium) survives in 'mystery plays' (OED, mystery, n.2), though the word also keeps its religious sense. The Sidney children are taught to pray morning and evening with the whole household, an image of Protestant domestic piety, and they read virtue in their parents' 'noble parts' (qualities) as in a book. Manners, arms and arts are the three accomplishments of the Renaissance gentleman, embodied supremely by Philip Sidney.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online), s.v. the headword; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, Oxford UP, 1985), notes to The Forest; The Norton Anthology of English Literature, vol. B: The Sixteenth Century / The Early Seventeenth Century, footnotes to the poem

  27. built / dwells ll. 99-102

    'Proportion' means compare, measure against. The 'proud ambitious heaps' return to the prodigy houses of the opening, bringing the poem full circle. The final contrast between building and dwelling carries moral weight: to 'dwell' implies residence, hospitality, continuity and responsibility to the community, whereas 'built' implies only expense. The poem is written in heroic couplets, and the epigrammatic final line shows Jonson's Martial-trained skill at closure. Critics from Hibbard to Wayne take this line as the key to the country-house ideal.

Wayne, Penshurst: The Semiotics of Place and the Poetics of History (London: Methuen; Madison: Univ. of Wisconsin Press, 1984); The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (Cambridge UP, 2012), The Forest, ed. Colin Burrow
