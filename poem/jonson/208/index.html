To the Memory of my Beloved, Master William Shakspeare, and what he hath left us

by Ben Jonson 1572-1637

From Underwoods (1640)

Arts & Sciences

To draw no envy, Shakspeare, on thy name,
Am I thus ample to thy book and fame;
While I confess thy writings to be such,
As neither man, nor Muse, can praise too much.
'Tis true, and all men's suffrage. But these ways
Were not the paths I meant unto thy praise;
For silliest ignorance on these may light,
Which, when it sounds at best, but echoes right;
Or blind affection, which doth ne'er advance
The truth, but gropes, and urgeth all by chance;
Or crafty malice might pretend this praise,
And think to ruin, where it seem'd to raise.
These are, as some infamous bawd, or whore,
Should praise a matron; what could hurt her more?
But thou art proof against them, and, indeed,
Above the ill fortune of them, or the need.
I therefore will begin: Soul of the age!
The applause! delight! the wonder of our stage!
My Shakspeare rise! I will not lodge thee by
Chaucer, or Spenser, or bid Beaumont lie
A little further off, to make thee room:
Thou art a monument without a tomb,
And art alive still, while thy book doth live
And we have wits to read, and praise to give.
That I not mix thee so, my brain excuses,
I mean with great, but disproportion'd Muses:
For if I thought my judgment were of years,
I should commit thee surely with thy peers,
And tell how far thou didst our Lily outshine,
Or sporting Kyd, or Marlow's mighty line.
And though thou hadst small Latin and less Greek,
From thence to honour thee, I will not seek
For names: but call forth thund'ring Eschylus,
Euripides, and Sophocles to us,
Pacuvius, Accius, him of Cordoua dead,
To live again, to hear thy buskin tread,
And shake a stage: or when thy socks were on,
Leave thee alone for the comparison,
Of all, that insolent Greece, or haughty Rome
Sent forth, or since did from their ashes come.
Triumph, my Britain, thou hast one to show,
To whom all scenes of Europe homage owe.
He was not of an age, but for all time!
And all the Muses still were in their prime,
When, like Apollo, he came forth to warm
Our ears, or like a Mercury to charm!
Nature herself was proud of his designs,
And joy'd to wear the dressing of his lines!
Which were so richly spun, and woven so fit,
As, since, she will vouchsafe no other wit.
The merry Greek, tart Aristophanes,
Neat Terence, witty Plautus, now not please;
But antiquated and deserted lie,
As they were not of nature's family.
Yet must I not give nature all; thy art,
My gentle Shakspeare, must enjoy a part.
For though the poet's matter nature be,
His art doth give the fashion: and, that he
Who casts to write a living line, must sweat,
(Such as thine are) and strike the second heat
Upon the Muses anvil; turn the same,
And himself with it, that he thinks to frame;
Or for the laurel, he may gain a scorn;
For a good poet's made, as well as born.
And such wert thou! Look how the father's face
Lives in his issue, even so the race
Of Shakspeare's mind and manners brightly shines
In his well torned, and true filed lines:
In each of which he seems to shake a lance,
As brandish'd at the eyes of ignorance.
Sweet Swan of Avon! what a sight it were
To see thee in our water yet appear,
And make those flights upon the banks of Thames,
That so did take Eliza, and our James!
But stay, I see thee in the hemisphere
Advanced, and made a constellation there!
Shine forth, thou Star of poets, and with rage,
Or influence, chide, or cheer the drooping stage,
Which, since thy flight from hence, hath mourn'd like night,
And despairs day, but for thy volume's light.

Codex Note

Underwoods, Miscellaneous Poems, no. 12.

Study notes by the editors

  1. The occasion: the First Folio, 1623 l. 1

    The poem was one of the prefatory verses in the 1623 Folio collected by John Heminges and Henry Condell, and was reprinted in Jonson's 1640 Workes. It is a formal encomium in heroic couplets that defines Shakespeare's place in literary history: above his contemporaries, equal to the ancients, a poet 'for all time'. The opening lines are defensive: Jonson's lavish praise ('thus ample') is not meant to draw envy on Shakespeare's name. Jonson elsewhere said that Shakespeare 'wanted art' (according to Drummond's notes of their conversations in 1618-19) and, in Timber, that he wished Shakespeare had blotted a thousand lines, so readers have weighed this public praise against his private reservations.

    Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies (the First Folio, 1623), prefatory verses; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ian Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford UP, 2011)

  2. ample l. 2

    'Ample' means liberal, abundant. Jonson is 'thus ample', generous both to the book and to the fame of its author. The opening couplet's logic is that he does not praise in order to make others envious, and then that Shakespeare's writings are beyond the reach of excessive praise: neither 'man, nor Muse, can praise too much'. The defensive opening is a classical convention of the encomium, which often begins by disclaiming flattery so that the praise will be believed.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'ample'; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  3. suffrage l. 5

    'Suffrage' means vote or approving judgement (Latin suffragium). Jonson concedes that Shakespeare's greatness is universally agreed, but says that general opinion is not the path his praise will take. Lines 7-14 then list three bad ways of praising: 'silliest ignorance', which only echoes; 'blind affection', which gropes; and 'crafty malice', which praises in order to ruin, like a bawd praising a respectable matron.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'suffrage'; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  4. infamous bawd l. 13

    The simile completes the argument of lines 7-12. A matron, a respectable married woman, would be harmed rather than helped if a notorious bawd or whore praised her. Jonson insists that Shakespeare is 'proof against' such ill-founded praise, armoured like a shield 'of proof', and above both its bad fortune and the need for it. The bluntness of the comparison is typical of Jonson, who liked to put moral judgements in coarse, concrete images.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  5. Soul of the age! l. 17

    Having cleared away bad praise, Jonson begins: 'I therefore will begin.' The exclamations 'Soul of the age! / The applause! delight! the wonder of our stage!' make Shakespeare the vital principle of his time and the object of the theatre's applause. The phrase 'Soul of the age' is set against line 43, 'He was not of an age, but for all time', which has been read as a deliberate correction or expansion.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  6. I will not lodge thee by Chaucer, or Spenser ll. 19-21

    Chaucer, Spenser and Francis Beaumont (died 1616, the same year as Shakespeare) were buried in Westminster Abbey. William Basse's elegy on Shakespeare, circulating in manuscript, had begun by asking 'Renowned Spenser' to lie a little nearer Chaucer, and Beaumont nearer Spenser, to make room for Shakespeare. Jonson declines: Shakespeare needs no grave among the poets, because 'Thou art a monument without a tomb', alive while his book lives. The idea echoes Horace's 'exegi monumentum aere perennius' (Odes 3.30).

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; William Basse, 'On Mr. Wm. Shakespeare' (c. 1616-23), printed in Shakespeare's Poems (1640); Horace, Odes 3.30

  7. a monument without a tomb l. 22

    The paradox draws on the classical boast that poetry is more lasting than bronze or marble (Horace, Odes 3.30; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.871-79), which Shakespeare himself had used in Sonnet 55. The Folio, as a book, is the monument, and readers keep the poet 'alive still' by reading and praising. The following lines tie his survival to the existence of 'wits to read, and praise to give'.

    Horace, Odes 3.30; Shakespeare, Sonnet 55 ('Not marble, nor the gilded monuments'); Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  8. disproportion'd Muses l. 26

    Jonson explains why he will not 'mix' Shakespeare with Chaucer, Spenser and Beaumont: they are 'great, but disproportion'd Muses', poets of real stature but out of proportion to him. The judgement places Shakespeare above the established English canon of the time, a striking claim in 1623. The word 'Muses' here stands for poets, by metonymy. Shakespeare is thus a monument by himself, not one tomb among others, and the poem's comparisons will be with the ancients rather than with English predecessors.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  9. my judgment were of years l. 27

    The lines are often glossed: if I thought my judgement must be confined to periods of years, I would 'commit' you, match or bring you into comparison, with your peers of the same generation, and show how far you outshone them. Others take 'of years' to mean mature, seasoned. Either way, Jonson then does make the comparison with Lyly, Kyd and Marlowe briefly before moving to the ancients. 'Commit' had the sense of setting in contest, as in matching cocks or champions.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'commit'

  10. Lily, Kyd, Marlowe ll. 29-30

    John Lyly (c. 1554-1606) wrote courtly prose comedies and the euphuistic romance Euphues; Thomas Kyd (1558-94) wrote The Spanish Tragedy; Christopher Marlowe (1564-93) wrote Tamburlaine and Doctor Faustus. 'Sporting Kyd' probably plays on the name (kid, young goat) as well as on play; 'Marlow's mighty line' became the standard phrase for Marlowe's powerful blank verse. Jonson's tribute to Marlowe is generous and has shaped critical opinion ever since.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  11. small Latin and less Greek l. 31

    Taken as a statement, the line says that Shakespeare had little Latin and less Greek, and it fed the eighteenth-century myth of an untutored natural genius. Some scholars argue that 'though thou hadst' is conditional ('even if you had had'), so that Jonson is not reporting a fact but saying he will compare Shakespeare with the ancients regardless. Measured against Jonson's own learning, a grammar-school education of the time gave a good command of Latin, and modern study of Shakespeare's sources supports a more learned Shakespeare than the phrase suggests.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; T. W. Baldwin, William Shakspere's Small Latine & Lesse Greeke, 2 vols (Urbana, 1944)

  12. Aeschylus, Euripides, Sophocles ... Pacuvius, Accius ll. 33-35

    Jonson calls up the great tragedians to hear Shakespeare: the Athenian trio Aeschylus (called 'thund'ring' for his grandeur), Euripides and Sophocles; the Roman Republican tragedians Marcus Pacuvius (c. 220-130 BC) and Lucius Accius (170-c. 86 BC), whose works survive only in fragments; and Seneca the Younger, born at Corduba (Córdoba) in Spain, whose closet tragedies were the main classical model for Elizabethan tragedy. The list proves Jonson's own learning while it honours Shakespeare.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Seneca, Tragedies (born at Corduba, Spain)

  13. buskin ... socks ll. 36-37

    Greek and Roman tragic actors wore the cothurnus, a thick-soled boot, rendered in English as 'buskin'; comic actors wore the soccus, a low slipper, the 'sock'. The terms were standard metonymies for the two genres (Milton's 'Jonson's learned sock' in L'Allegro uses the same convention). Jonson praises Shakespeare's tragedy against the ancients, then his comedy 'when thy socks were on', leaving him 'alone for the comparison' with all Greece and Rome.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'buskin'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'sock'; Milton, L'Allegro 132; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  14. insolent Greece, or haughty Rome l. 39

    Editors trace the phrase to the preface of the elder Seneca's Controversiae, which says that Rome had in Cicero a genius to set against 'insolent Greece'. Jonson reused the same tag in Timber for Francis Bacon. 'Insolent' means proud, arrogant in its superiority. The point is that Shakespeare equals or surpasses everything the classical world 'sent forth', or everything that has risen since 'from their ashes', the classical revival of the Renaissance.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Seneca the Elder, Controversiae 1, preface 6; Ben Jonson, Timber, or Discoveries (1641), on Bacon

  15. Triumph, my Britain ll. 41-42

    Under James I, who styled himself King of Great Britain, 'Britain' was a newly charged name for the united realms of England and Scotland. Jonson, who wrote masques celebrating that union, calls on Britain to triumph because it possesses a dramatist to whom 'all scenes of Europe homage owe'. The claim of national literary supremacy was bold in 1623, when Italian, Spanish and French drama were widely admired.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ian Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford UP, 2011)

  16. not of an age, but for all time l. 43

    The line corrects and enlarges 'Soul of the age' (l. 17): Shakespeare is both the spirit of his period and beyond it. It is one of the founding statements of Shakespeare's universality and has been quoted ever since, sometimes against Jonson's other remarks about his want of art. Modern historicist criticism has often argued the opposite, that Shakespeare must be read as 'of an age', which makes the line a useful starting point for debate.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  17. Apollo ... Mercury ll. 45-46

    Apollo, the sun god and leader of the Muses, warms; Mercury (Hermes), messenger of the gods, patron of eloquence and inventor of the lyre, charms, with a hint of the magic of his caduceus, which charms men to sleep. The Muses being 'still in their prime' when Shakespeare appeared suggests that poetry was at its height. The pairing of warming and charming combines power and grace.

    Homeric Hymn to Hermes (invention of the lyre); Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  18. nature and art ll. 55-64

    This passage is the critical core of the poem. Jonson grants that nature is the poet's matter but says art gives it 'fashion', form. The debate whether poems come from nature or art goes back to Horace, Ars Poetica 408-11, who concluded that both are needed. 'A good poet's made, as well as born' answers the proverb 'poeta nascitur, non fit' (a poet is born, not made). The insistence reads partly as Jonson's own credo, and partly as a correction of the view, his own elsewhere, that Shakespeare 'wanted art'. 'Gentle' was a common epithet for Shakespeare and means noble, courteous.

    Horace, Ars Poetica 408-11 (nature or art) and 441 ('male tornatos incudi reddere versus'); Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Ben Jonson, Timber, or Discoveries (1641), 'De Shakespeare nostrati'

  19. the Muses anvil ll. 60-61

    A smith heats iron and strikes it, then heats it a second time to work it further. Jonson's poet who 'casts to write a living line' must sweat and 'strike the second heat' on the Muses' anvil, turning the line and himself with it. The image adapts Horace, Ars Poetica 441, who advises sending badly turned verses back to the anvil ('male tornatos incudi reddere versus'). It contradicts the Folio's own claim, in Heminges and Condell's preface, that Shakespeare scarcely blotted a line, and Jonson's retort in Timber that he wished he had blotted a thousand.

    Horace, Ars Poetica 408-11 (nature or art) and 441 ('male tornatos incudi reddere versus'); Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, Timber, or Discoveries (1641)

  20. well torned, and true filed l. 68

    'Torned' is a spelling of 'turned', shaped on a lathe; 'filed' smoothed with a file. Both are images of craft, continuing the anvil passage, and both derive from Horace's metaphors for polishing verse (Ars Poetica 291 on the 'labor limae', the work of the file, and 441 on turned verses). The preceding lines compare Shakespeare's works to children who bear the father's face, so that his mind and manners shine in his 'race', his offspring.

    Horace, Ars Poetica 408-11 (nature or art) and 441 ('male tornatos incudi reddere versus'); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'turn'; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem

  21. shake a lance l. 69

    The image puns on the poet's name, a 'shake-spear', brandishing a lance 'at the eyes of ignorance'. The coat of arms granted to Shakespeare's father in 1596 showed a spear, and the pun was current in the period. The military image also links back to Jonson's opening attack on ignorant praise: Shakespeare's lines themselves fight his enemies. The swordsman's or jouster's lance also fits Jonson's view of satire and criticism as a kind of combat.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  22. Sweet Swan of Avon l. 71

    The swan was sacred to Apollo and was thought to sing beautifully before death; poets were traditionally called swans, as Horace calls Pindar 'the Dircaean swan' (Odes 4.2.25). The River Avon runs through Stratford-upon-Avon, Shakespeare's birthplace. Jonson imagines the swan making 'flights upon the banks of Thames', performances in London, that so pleased Elizabeth and James. The epithet 'Swan of Avon' has since become one of the commonest names for Shakespeare.

    Horace, Odes 4.2.25; Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11

  23. Eliza, and our James l. 74

    'Eliza' is the poetic name for Elizabeth I. Shakespeare's company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men, played often at Elizabeth's court, and in 1603 James took them under his patronage as the King's Men. 'Take' means delight, captivate. Jonson thus records Shakespeare's royal success under two reigns. Many of the plays were performed at Whitehall and other royal palaces during the Christmas seasons, and court performance was the highest mark of success for a professional company. The swan's 'flights' are thus his plays at court.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ian Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford UP, 2011)

  24. made a constellation l. 76

    Classical myth often turned heroes into stars (catasterism), and the swan already has its constellation, Cygnus. Jonson sees Shakespeare 'in the hemisphere / Advanced' and calls on him as 'Star of poets' to influence the stage from heaven. 'Rage' is poetic inspiration, furor; 'influence' is the astrological power of stars over earthly events. The theatre, 'drooping' and mourning 'like night' since his death, has only the light of his 'volume', the Folio itself, a fitting close for a poem printed at the front of that book.

    Ben Jonson, ed. Ian Donaldson, The Oxford Authors (Oxford UP, 1985), notes to this poem; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson (Oxford, 1925-52), vol. 8 (poems) and commentary in vol. 11; Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.843-51 (Julius Caesar's soul made a star)
