On my First Son

by Ben Jonson 1572-1637

Love

FAREWELL, thou child of my right hand, and joy;
My sin was too much hope of thee, loved boy;
Seven years thou wert lent to me, and I thee pay,
Exacted by thy fate, on the just day.
Oh! could I lose all father, now! for why,
Will man lament the state he should envy?
To have so soon 'scaped world's, and flesh's rage,
And, if no other misery, yet age!
Rest in soft peace, and, asked, say here doth lie
Ben Jonson his best piece of poetry;
For whose sake, henceforth, all his vows be such,
As what he loves may never like too much.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Occasion and form l. 1

    Jonson's eldest son Benjamin died in the London plague of 1603, according to tradition on his seventh birthday. Jonson was then staying at Sir Robert Cotton's house in Huntingdonshire with William Camden; he later told William Drummond (Conversations, 1619) that he had seen a vision of the boy with a bloody cross on his forehead before news of the death came. The poem was printed as Epigram 45 in the 1616 folio. It is twelve lines of heroic couplets, combining farewell (the Latin 'vale' of epitaphs), self-reproach, consolation and the inscription of a tomb.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Epigrams: On my First Son', ed. F. D. Hoeniger (text of the 1616 Workes); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds, Ben Jonson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52): Epigrams in vol. 8, commentary in vol. 11; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011)

  2. child of my right hand l. 1

    Rachel, dying in childbirth, named her son Benoni, 'son of my sorrow', but Jacob called him Benjamin, 'son of the right hand' (Genesis 35:18). Renaissance commentators also glossed the name as fortunate or dexterous, as the RPO editors note. The right hand is the place of favour and strength, so the phrase means both 'my Benjamin' and 'my dearest, my support'. The biblical context also carries the pairing of birth and grief that the poem enacts. The boy shared his father's name, which links the grief to Jonson's sense of himself.

    KJV, Genesis 35:16-18; Representative Poetry Online, 'Epigrams: On my First Son', ed. F. D. Hoeniger (text of the 1616 Workes); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011)

  3. too much hope l. 2

    Christian moralists warned against setting one's heart on worldly goods, including children, more than on God. Jonson's confession treats his hope in the boy as the sin that God has punished, or at least as a wrong attachment. The line prepares the final couplet, which resolves to love without liking 'too much'. Compare the ending of Martial's epitaph for the boy Glaucias (6.29): 'whatever you love, wish that it may not please you too much'.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); Martial, Epigrams 6.29 (Latin Library); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds, Ben Jonson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52): Epigrams in vol. 8, commentary in vol. 11

  4. lent ... pay ... Exacted ... just day ll. 3-4

    Life as a loan to be returned is a classical and Christian commonplace (Lucretius 3.971; compare Job 1:21, 'the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away'). Jonson develops it in legal-financial terms: the child was 'lent', the father now 'pays' him back, 'Exacted' (demanded) by fate 'on the just day', the precise date of repayment. If the tradition that Benjamin died on his birthday is correct, 'just' means exact as well as rightful. The bookkeeping language, as the RPO note on 'lent' suggests, turns the gift of heaven into a debt and gives the grief an austere dignity.

    Representative Poetry Online, 'Epigrams: On my First Son', ed. F. D. Hoeniger (text of the 1616 Workes); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); KJV, Job 1:21

  5. lose all father l. 5

    'Father' is used as an abstract noun: fatherhood, paternal feeling. The wish is impossible, and the exclamatory 'Oh!' registers its failure. The following question offers the standard consolation: why should a man lament a state (death, freedom from life's miseries) that he should rather envy? Critics differ over whether the consolation holds. Some read the poem as achieving Stoic composure, others as dramatizing the gap between what reason says and what the father feels.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds, Ben Jonson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52): Epigrams in vol. 8, commentary in vol. 11

  6. world's, and flesh's rage ll. 7-8

    The Book of Common Prayer's baptism service asks the child to renounce 'the devil and all his works, the vain pomp and glory of the world... and the carnal desires of the flesh'. To have escaped the world and the flesh so young is a consolation drawn from that triad. 'And, if no other misery, yet age!' adds the miseries of old age, a classical topic. The clipped ''scaped' and the heavy pauses roughen the couplet.

    Book of Common Prayer (1559/1604), Publick Baptisme; Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011)

  7. Rest in soft peace ll. 9-10

    Christian and Roman epitaphs supply the wording: requiescat in pace, and the Roman wish that the earth lie lightly on the dead. The child is then imagined answering a passer-by who asks who lies here, the classical convention of the speaking tomb. 'Soft' contrasts with the hard 'rage' of world and flesh and with the debt-language of ll. 3-4. The imagined dialogue lets the child speak the next line, so that the poem becomes both the father's farewell and the son's own inscription.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds, Ben Jonson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52): Epigrams in vol. 8, commentary in vol. 11

  8. best piece of poetry l. 10

    'Poetry' derives from Greek poiein, to make; Jonson often called the poet a 'maker'. The son is the best thing he has made, better than any poem; the line is also the son's own epitaph. 'Ben Jonson his' is the 'his-genitive', common in the period. The word 'piece' suggests both a work of art and a coin, continuing the money imagery of the loan. The poem thus claims that the epigram itself is a lesser making than the child it commemorates.

    Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, eds, Ben Jonson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52): Epigrams in vol. 8, commentary in vol. 11; Oxford English Dictionary, 'poetry', n.; 'piece', n.

  9. loves ... like too much ll. 11-12

    The couplet vows that what he loves may never 'like' too much. In early modern English 'like' could mean 'please' (it likes me), so the line can mean 'may never please me too much', exactly rendering Martial 6.29.8, 'quidquid ames, cupias non placuisse nimis'. It can also mean that he will never love too much. The distinction between loving and liking has been read as a resolve to temper affection, or as a protective emotional distance learned in pain. Critics such as Donaldson note how much feeling the restraint conceals.

    Martial, Epigrams 6.29 (Latin Library); Ian Donaldson, ed., Ben Jonson (Oxford Authors, 1985), notes to Epigrams 45; and Donaldson, Ben Jonson: A Life (Oxford, 2011); Oxford English Dictionary, 'like', v.1 (to please)
