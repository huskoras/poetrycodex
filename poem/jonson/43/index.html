Love

Simplex Munditiis

by Ben Jonson 1572-1637

From Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age, ed. A. H. Bullen (London: Nimmo, 1889)

Love

Still to be neat, still to be drest,
As you were going to a feast;
Still to be powdered, still perfumed:
Lady, it is to be presumed,
Though art's hid causes are not found,
All is not sweet, all is not sound.

Give me a look, give me a face,
That makes simplicity a grace;
Robes loosely flowing, hair as free:
Such sweet neglect more taketh me
Than all the adulteries of art;
They strike mine eyes, but not my heart.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Simplex munditiis: the song from Epicoene l. 1

    The song comes from the first scene of Epicoene, or The Silent Woman, first acted in 1609 and printed in the 1616 folio. Clerimont, a young gallant, has his boy sing it while the talk turns to Lady Haughty and the arts of the toilette; his friend Truewit at once argues the opposite case, in praise of women's dressing and art. Taken out of the play, as Bullen and anthologists printed it, it becomes a general lyric preferring natural beauty to cosmetics. Editors note that it adapts a Latin poem beginning 'Semper munditias, semper, Basilissa, decoras', attributed to the French Neo-Latin poet Jean Bonnefons, though the attribution is not certain.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (2012), Epicoene, ed. David Bevington; Representative Poetry Online, note on 'Still to be neat' (University of Toronto); Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age, ed. A. H. Bullen (1889), the text used here

  2. Simplex munditiis title

    The title is not Jonson's but was supplied in anthologies. It quotes Horace, Odes 1.5, line 5, where Pyrrha binds her golden hair 'simplex munditiis', plain in her neatness or elegant without fuss (Milton translated it 'plain in thy neatness'). The phrase sums up the ideal of the song's second stanza: a careful simplicity that seems natural. The irony is that Jonson's lyric is itself a highly worked imitation of Latin models.

    Horace, Odes 1.5.5; Milton, translation of Horace, Odes 1.5 ('Plain in thy neatness'); Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age, ed. A. H. Bullen (1889), the text used here

  3. Still ... neat l. 1

    The repeated 'Still' (always) makes the stanza a catalogue of constant grooming: always neat, always dressed, always powdered, always perfumed, as if for a feast. 'Neat' carried the sense of elegant, spruce and clean, sometimes with a suggestion of over-careful trimness. The anaphora mimics the tedious repetition of the toilet that the speaker mocks. The phrase 'As you were going to a feast' implies that the lady is always dressed for show, never simply herself, and it sets up the suspicion of the following lines.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'still'; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'neat'; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary

  4. powdered ... perfumed l. 3

    Powder (for face and hair) and perfume were standard elements of fashionable toilette and frequent targets of satire and moralising, which accused them of hiding blemishes and bad smells. The suspicion is spelled out in lines 4-6: the speaker presumes that, although the 'hid causes' of all this art are not discovered, all is not sweet or sound underneath. The rhyme 'perfumed / presumed' turns the scent into evidence, as if the perfume itself gave the game away.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (2012), Epicoene, ed. David Bevington

  5. art's hid causes l. 5

    The line means that the reasons concealed behind the art cannot be seen, but they can be presumed: so much effort suggests something to hide. 'Sweet' and 'sound' in line 6 are words of smell and health, implying odour and disease, perhaps the pox. The satiric logic, common in Jacobean anti-cosmetic writing, treats artifice as evidence of a defect. In the play, the debate continues with Truewit's defence of art, so the song is one side of an argument.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (2012), Epicoene, ed. David Bevington; Representative Poetry Online, note on 'Still to be neat' (University of Toronto)

  6. simplicity a grace l. 8

    The second stanza turns from satire to positive taste. 'Grace' here is attractiveness, beauty of manner, with an echo of the Graces. Simplicity is made into a grace, an adornment in its own right; the phrase translates Horace's 'simplex munditiis' into an aesthetic principle. Loosely flowing robes and free hair are the visible signs of that ideal. The paradox is that such simplicity is itself an effect of art, so the song does not reject art altogether but prefers an art that conceals itself.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; Horace, Odes 1.5

  7. sweet neglect l. 10

    'Sweet neglect' is the oxymoron at the centre of the poem: an artful negligence that pleases more than finish. Renaissance courtiers knew the idea as sprezzatura, the studied ease recommended in Castiglione's Courtier. Herrick's 'Delight in Disorder' in Hesperides (1648) takes up the same preference ('A sweet disorder in the dress') and is usually read as a reply to or variation on Jonson's song. 'Taketh' means captivates, delights.

    Representative Poetry Online, note on 'Still to be neat' (University of Toronto); Baldassare Castiglione, The Courtier, trans. Thomas Hoby (1561), book 1 on sprezzatura; Robert Herrick, 'Delight in Disorder', Hesperides (1648)

  8. adulteries of art l. 11

    'Adulteries' here means adulterations, the corruptions or debasements produced by mixing in foreign substances. The word also keeps the sense of marital infidelity, so that cosmetics become a kind of unfaithfulness to nature. The final line sums up the contrast: these arts 'strike mine eyes, but not my heart', they catch attention without moving affection. The couplet's eye/heart distinction is a commonplace of love poetry.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'adultery'; Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Ben Jonson, ed. David Bevington, Martin Butler and Ian Donaldson (2012), Epicoene, ed. David Bevington

  9. Form ll. 1-12

    Each stanza has three couplets of four-stress lines. The first stanza is built on the anaphora of 'Still', the second on the imperative 'Give me'. The balance of attack and preference matches the poem's argument, and the clipped couplets give it the sound of an epigram set to music. As a stage song it had to be clear and memorable, and the simple pattern served that end.

    Ben Jonson, ed. C. H. Herford, Percy and Evelyn Simpson, 11 vols (Oxford, 1925-52), Epicoene 1.1 and commentary; Lyrics from the Dramatists of the Elizabethan Age, ed. A. H. Bullen (1889), the text used here
