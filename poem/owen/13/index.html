Nature

Exposure

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

NatureDeath & Dying

I
Our brains ache, in the merciless iced east winds that knife us . . .
Wearied we keep awake because the night is silent . . .
Low drooping flares confuse our memory of the salient . . .
Worried by silence, sentries whisper, curious, nervous,
But nothing happens.

Watching, we hear the mad gusts tugging on the wire.
Like twitching agonies of men among its brambles.
Northward incessantly, the flickering gunnery rumbles,
Far off, like a dull rumour of some other war.
What are we doing here?

The poignant misery of dawn begins to grow . . .
We only know war lasts, rain soaks, and clouds sag stormy.
Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army
Attacks once more in ranks on shivering ranks of gray,
But nothing happens.

Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence.
Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow,
With sidelong flowing flakes that flock, pause and renew,
We watch them wandering up and down the wind's nonchalance,
But nothing happens.
II
Pale flakes with lingering stealth come feeling for our faces--
We cringe in holes, back on forgotten dreams, and stare, snow-dazed,
Deep into grassier ditches. So we drowse, sun-dozed,
Littered with blossoms trickling where the blackbird fusses.
Is it that we are dying?

Slowly our ghosts drag home: glimpsing the sunk fires glozed
With crusted dark-red jewels; crickets jingle there;
For hours the innocent mice rejoice: the house is theirs;
Shutters and doors all closed: on us the doors are closed--
We turn back to our dying.

Since we believe not otherwise can kind fires burn;
Nor ever suns smile true on child, or field, or fruit.
For God's invincible spring our love is made afraid;
Therefore, not loath, we lie out here; therefore were born,
For love of God seems dying.

To-night, His frost will fasten on this mud and us,
Shrivelling many hands and puckering foreheads crisp.
The burying-party, picks and shovels in their shaking grasp,
Pause over half-known faces. All their eyes are ice,
But nothing happens.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A winter night in the trenches in which nothing happens but the cold, the wind and the slow dying. 'But nothing happens' recurs at the end of the eight-line stanzas.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Exposure: title and occasion l. 1

    In January 1917 Owen's battalion held front-line positions near Beaumont Hamel and Serre on the Somme in one of the coldest winters on record. On 4 February he wrote to his mother that his platoon had no dug-outs and had to lie in the snow 'under the deadly wind', and that one man froze to death. The Oxford archive says the poem was begun at Scarborough in December 1917, revised there in early 1918 and completed in France in September 1918; one draft page carries a puzzling date ('Feb. 1916'), which may be a slip for 1917 or 1918. 'Exposure' means both lying unprotected in the open and dying of cold. The enemy in the poem is the weather, not the Germans.

    Wilfred Owen, Collected Letters, ed. Harold Owen and John Bell (Oxford UP, 1967), letter of 4 February 1917 (no. 482); First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), Owen's letter to Susan Owen, 4 February 1917; Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  2. merciless iced east winds that knife us l. 1

    The verb 'knife' turns the wind into an assailant with a blade, and 'merciless' gives it a will. Throughout the poem the weather behaves like an army (dawn 'attacks', the air is 'deadly'), while actual combat is distant ('some other war'). The long, heavy lines, with their slow stresses and trailing ellipses, enact the exhausted waiting of the sentries. Owen also uses the first person plural throughout, so the experience is collective.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  3. Pararhyme l. 2

    Owen's innovation is pararhyme (also called consonantal or half rhyme): words whose consonants match but whose stressed vowels differ, as in silent/salient, wire/war, brambles/rumbles, grow/gray, faces/fusses, burn/born, us/ice. Each five-line stanza rhymes ABBA on these partial chimes, followed by a short refrain line. The ear expects a full rhyme and gets a dissonance, and the effect suits a poem of frustration and unresolved suspense. Edmund Blunden's memoir in his 1931 edition of the poems drew early attention to these assonances, and Owen is now regarded as the technique's chief pioneer in English.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); The Poems of Wilfred Owen, ed. Edmund Blunden (Chatto & Windus, 1931), memoir; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  4. the salient l. 3

    In military usage a salient is a bulge in the front line projecting toward the enemy, and therefore vulnerable to fire from several sides; the Ypres Salient was the most notorious of the war. 'Low drooping flares' are star-shells fired to light up no man's land; as they fall they distort the familiar shapes, so the sentries' 'memory of the salient' is confused. The word pararhymes with 'silent' in the previous line.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'salient, n.'; Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  5. But nothing happens l. 5

    Each stanza ends with a short, indented line. 'But nothing happens' recurs four times (stanzas 1, 3, 4 and 8), and the other refrains vary it with questions and statements ('What are we doing here?', 'Is it that we are dying?', 'We turn back to our dying.', 'For love of God seems dying.'). The refrain makes boredom and futility the poem's subject. In military terms the night is uneventful, yet men are dying of cold. The last 'But nothing happens' is the bitterest: by then the burial party is at work.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  6. twitching agonies of men among its brambles l. 7

    The 'wire' is the barbed-wire entanglement in front of the trench, and its 'brambles' are the coils of barbed wire, a grim pastoral metaphor. The gusts tugging at it make it twitch like the bodies of men dying on it, a sight familiar from failed attacks. The simile introduces dying men into a stanza where, officially, nothing happens. The full stop after 'wire' (where a comma might be expected) is in the text here.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  7. What are we doing here? l. 10

    Hearing the guns rumble 'northward' like 'a dull rumour of some other war' (the distant fighting elsewhere on the front), the soldiers question their own presence. The colloquial phrasing is a soldier's grumble and also a profound question about the war's meaning. Stanza 7 offers a strained, ambiguous answer: they lie out here because otherwise the fires of home could not burn.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  8. Dawn ... her melancholy army l. 13

    Dawn was the usual hour for attacks ('stand-to' at first light). Here Dawn herself is the attacker, massing grey clouds in ranks as an enemy masses troops. The personification inverts the classical rosy-fingered Dawn of Homer into a 'melancholy army'. Again nothing happens in human terms. The line also echoes the opening's 'east winds': the east was where the German lines lay.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Homer, Odyssey 2.1 and passim ('rosy-fingered Dawn')

  9. Less deadly than the air l. 17

    The bullets streaking the silence are real danger, yet the air 'that shudders black with snow' is deadlier. Owen's letter of 4 February 1917 used the same adjective for the 'deadly wind' under which his platoon lay. The snowflakes are rendered with soft sibilants and liquids (sidelong, flowing, flakes, flock), and their lazy beauty is set against their lethal effect. 'Nonchalance' gives the wind an indifference that matches the God of the later stanzas.

    Wilfred Owen, Collected Letters, ed. Harold Owen and John Bell (Oxford UP, 1967), letter of 4 February 1917 (no. 482); First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), Owen's letter to Susan Owen, 4 February 1917; Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  10. Pale flakes with lingering stealth (textual) l. 21

    This archive follows the first edition, edited by Siegfried Sassoon (1920), which prints 'lingering stealth'. Later editions read 'fingering stealth', which fits the touch of 'come feeling for our faces': the snowflakes are like fingers groping in the dark, uncanny and stealthy. The text here is therefore mixed: in stanza 7 it prints 'Nor ever suns', the corrected reading of later editions, where Sassoon's 1920 text has 'Now ever suns'. Owen's manuscripts survive in several drafts, and Stallworthy's edition records their readings.

    Poems by Wilfred Owen, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (Chatto & Windus, 1920; reprinted 1921); Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  11. snow-dazed ... sun-dozed l. 22

    The compound pair snow-dazed / sun-dozed is itself a pararhyme and a hinge. The freezing men, crouching in their holes, drift ('drowse') into memory or hallucination of English spring: grassy ditches, blossom, a blackbird fussing. The dream is gentle and pastoral, and its drowsiness is also the drowsiness of hypothermia, which is why the refrain asks 'Is it that we are dying?'

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  12. Slowly our ghosts drag home l. 26

    The dying men's spirits 'drag' home, an exhausted verb. They see the fire, crickets and mice, a cosy domestic interior in which the house now belongs to the mice: the household has gone on without them. The shutters and doors are closed, and the repeated 'closed' turns literal into figurative: home is shut against those who have been sent away to die. The refrain 'We turn back to our dying' accepts the exclusion.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  13. glozed l. 26

    'Gloze' in standard English means to gloss, explain away or flatter, and it is related to 'gloss'. Owen seems to use it for the sheen of the sunken fire: embers that glow under a glaze of ash, 'crusted dark-red jewels'. Editors note that it is a rare, possibly coined usage. The sound also prepares the pararhyme with 'closed' at the end of the stanza.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'gloze, v.'; Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  14. Since we believe not otherwise can kind fires burn l. 31

    The syntax is compressed. The men believe that only through their sacrifice can the 'kind fires' of home burn, and suns shine on child, field and fruit. So they lie out 'not loath' (not unwilling), as if born for it. 'For God's invincible spring our love is made afraid' is the crux. It may mean that the men's love fears for the renewal of the world in spring that they are defending, or that they fear spring itself, which will come whether they live or not. The refrain 'For love of God seems dying' can mean that God's love for them, or their love of God, is failing. Critics differ on whether the stanza expresses genuine conviction or bitter irony at patriotic religion.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002); Jon Stallworthy, Wilfred Owen: A Biography (Oxford UP / Chatto & Windus, 1974)

  15. His frost l. 36

    With 'His frost', the God of 'God's invincible spring' becomes the agent of death. The frost will 'fasten' on mud and men alike, shrivelling hands and puckering foreheads. Owen had once been a lay assistant to an evangelical vicar, at Dunsden (1911-13), and lost much of his faith there. The poem's theology is one of indifference or hostility, echoing the 'nonchalance' of the wind.

    Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002); Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983)

  16. The burying-party ... half-known faces l. 38

    A burying-party was a work detail assigned to collect and bury the dead. The cold makes them shake, and they 'pause over half-known faces', the faces of comrades half recognisable, or distorted by frost. 'All their eyes are ice' refers ambiguously to the dead (frozen eyes) or to the burial party (numbed beyond feeling), and the pararhyme us/ice closes the poem in the sound. Then the refrain returns, 'But nothing happens', at its most ironic: men are dead and the official record notes no action.

    Wilfred Owen, The Complete Poems and Fragments, ed. Jon Stallworthy (Chatto & Windus / Hogarth Press / Oxford UP, 1983); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)
