The Mind

Insensibility

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

The MindHistory & Politics

I
Happy are men who yet before they are killed
Can let their veins run cold.
Whom no compassion fleers
Or makes their feet
Sore on the alleys cobbled with their brothers.
The front line withers,
But they are troops who fade, not flowers
For poets' tearful fooling:
Men, gaps for filling
Losses who might have fought
Longer; but no one bothers.
II
And some cease feeling
Even themselves or for themselves.
Dullness best solves
The tease and doubt of shelling,
And Chance's strange arithmetic
Comes simpler than the reckoning of their shilling.
They keep no check on Armies' decimation.
III
Happy are these who lose imagination:
They have enough to carry with ammunition.
Their spirit drags no pack.
Their old wounds save with cold can not more ache.
Having seen all things red,
Their eyes are rid
Of the hurt of the colour of blood for ever.
And terror's first constriction over,
Their hearts remain small drawn.
Their senses in some scorching cautery of battle
Now long since ironed,
Can laugh among the dying, unconcerned.
IV
Happy the soldier home, with not a notion
How somewhere, every dawn, some men attack,
And many sighs are drained.
Happy the lad whose mind was never trained:
His days are worth forgetting more than not.
He sings along the march
Which we march taciturn, because of dusk,
The long, forlorn, relentless trend
From larger day to huger night.
V
We wise, who with a thought besmirch
Blood over all our soul,
How should we see our task
But through his blunt and lashless eyes?
Alive, he is not vital overmuch;
Dying, not mortal overmuch;
Nor sad, nor proud,
Nor curious at all.
He cannot tell
Old men's placidity from his.
VI
But cursed are dullards whom no cannon stuns,
That they should be as stones.
Wretched are they, and mean
With paucity that never was simplicity.
By choice they made themselves immune
To pity and whatever mourns in man
Before the last sea and the hapless stars;
Whatever mourns when many leave these shores;
Whatever shares
The eternal reciprocity of tears.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A six-part poem on the dulling of feeling in war. Owen praises the soldier who has lost imagination, and ends by cursing the 'dullards' who chose by choice to be immune to pity.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Happy are men ... (Wordsworth's 'Happy Warrior') st. I, l. 1

    The opening inverts Wordsworth's 'Character of the Happy Warrior' (1807), which begins 'Who is the happy Warrior? Who is he / That every man in arms should wish to be?' and portrays a soldier of virtue, reason and compassion. Owen's 'happy' men are those whose feelings have been deadened, so that they can face death without pity or fear. The repeated formula 'Happy are ... Happy the ...' also recalls the Beatitudes of Matthew 5 ('Blessed are ...'), which the poem's last stanza turns to curses. The poem was drafted between late 1917 and early 1918 (Scarborough is usually named) and possibly revised at Ripon in spring 1918, before Owen returned to France; it was published after his death in Poems (1920), edited by Siegfried Sassoon.

    William Wordsworth, 'Character of the Happy Warrior' (1807), Wikisource; Crossref-it, 'Wilfred Owen, selected poems: Insensibility' (background and synopsis); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), Wikisource transcription

  2. Pararhyme and irregular form st. I, ll. 1-2

    Owen rhymes by pararhyme or consonantal rhyme: words with the same consonant frame and different vowels, such as killed/cold, fleers/flowers, fooling/filling, red/rid, stuns/stones. The effect is of a rhyme that fails, an echo with a dying fall, suited to a poem about the failure of feeling. The poem is in six irregular sections of varying line length, and its lines range from two stresses to six. The idiom 'let their veins run cold', usually used of fear, here means the deliberate chilling of emotion.

    Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise')

  3. fleers st. I, l. 3

    To 'fleer' is to laugh scornfully, sneer, or mock (OED). Owen uses it transitively and unusually: compassion does not mock these men or harass them into pain. The archaic word, found in Shakespeare, gives the line a strange, intense sound. Simcox glosses it as 'mocks'. The sense is that compassion, which in a normal person would torment them on seeing the dead, has no hold on them.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox)

  4. alleys cobbled with their brothers st. I, l. 5

    The 'alleys' are the narrow passages of the trench system, and they are 'cobbled', paved, with the bodies of the dead. Simcox notes that Owen used a similar image in a remark to his sister Mary in March 1918, saying that Beaumont Hamel was 'cobbled' with skulls. 'Brothers' means fellow soldiers but also makes the dead kin. The hideous literalness is offset by the euphemism of 'sore', and the line shows what the 'happy' men no longer feel.

    Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  5. troops who fade, not flowers / For poets' tearful fooling st. I, ll. 7-8

    Owen rejects a stock figure of elegy and war verse, the young dead as flowers cut down, which ran from classical epigram to wartime poems. Men 'fade' in the real sense, wasting and dying, not in the poetic sense: they are 'not flowers / For poets' tearful fooling'. 'Fooling' is sharp: the sentimental poet is playing games with grief. The line is part of Owen's critique of the poetic tradition, consistent with his draft preface, 'Above all I am not concerned with Poetry.'

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox); Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), Wikisource transcription

  6. Men, gaps for filling st. I, l. 9

    The men are reduced to 'gaps for filling' and 'Losses', the vocabulary of staff reports, in which casualties leave holes in the line to be filled by drafts of reinforcements. The flat statement 'but no one bothers' ends the stanza with a shrug. Owen exposes the bureaucratic indifference that matches the numbness of the soldiers themselves. The broken syntax across the line ends ('Losses who might have fought / Longer') mimics the abrupt cutting off of those lives.

    Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise'); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  7. Chance's strange arithmetic st. II, l. 5

    The soldier's life depends on 'Chance's strange arithmetic', the random sums by which shells kill some and spare others. Paradoxically, this is 'simpler than the reckoning of their shilling'. The shilling is the soldier's pay; a British private earned about a shilling a day. It also recalls 'taking the King's shilling', the old phrase for enlistment. The numbness makes the life-and-death calculation easier than the trivial financial one.

    Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise'); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  8. decimation st. II, l. 7

    From Latin decimare, to take a tenth, 'decimation' first meant the Roman military punishment of killing every tenth man of a mutinous unit. By extension it came to mean heavy losses. Owen restores something of the precise sense: the armies are being reduced by calculated proportion. The 'happy' soldiers 'keep no check' on it, do not count the dead. The word therefore links the soldiers' numbness to the army's own statistical way of thinking about death, which the poem has already mocked in 'gaps for filling'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  9. lose imagination st. III, l. 1

    For the Romantics, imagination was the source of moral sympathy. Shelley's Defence of Poetry argues that a man, to be greatly good, 'must imagine intensely and comprehensively', putting himself in another's place, and the poem's title and argument can be read against Shelley's account of sympathetic imagination. Owen reverses the idea: soldiers who 'lose imagination' are happy because they no longer picture their own deaths or others' suffering. 'They have enough to carry with ammunition' turns the loss into a practical lightening of the load.

    P. B. Shelley, A Defence of Poetry (written 1821, publ. 1840), Project Gutenberg; Crossref-it, 'Wilfred Owen, selected poems: Insensibility' (background and synopsis); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  10. Having seen all things red st. III, l. 5

    Having 'seen all things red', in blood, they are now 'rid' of the hurt of that colour 'for ever'. The pararhyme red/rid enacts the release. Seeing red is also an idiom for rage; Owen's soldiers have passed beyond rage into insensibility. The line 'Their old wounds save with cold can not more ache' (as printed in the 1920 text) says that their old wounds ache only with cold, no longer with feeling.

    Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox); Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), Wikisource transcription

  11. scorching cautery of battle st. III, l. 10

    Cautery is the medical practice of burning tissue with a hot iron or caustic to stop bleeding or destroy infection; the cauterised area loses sensation. Battle is the 'scorching cautery' that has burned away their senses. 'Ironed' (l. 11) continues the image: their senses have been seared with a hot iron, and also flattened, pressed smooth. The result: they 'Can laugh among the dying, unconcerned'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise'); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  12. Happy the soldier home st. IV, l. 1

    Section IV considers the soldier who has returned home, 'with not a notion' of the daily attacks still going on. Owen himself had been invalided home in 1917 and treated for shell shock at Craiglockhart, so this stanza has autobiographical force. The soldier at home and 'the lad whose mind was never trained' are both happy in their ignorance; the 'we' who 'march taciturn' are not.

    Crossref-it, 'Wilfred Owen, selected poems: Insensibility' (background and synopsis); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  13. the lad whose mind was never trained st. IV, l. 4

    The 'lad whose mind was never trained' is the uneducated soldier, who 'sings along the march' while 'we', the educated and reflective, march 'taciturn', silent, 'because of dusk'. The class divide between officers and men is implied. 'His days are worth forgetting more than not' is a bleak judgment of a life without reflection. The march leads 'From larger day to huger night', a line of elemental grandeur suggesting the movement from life to death.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Crossref-it, 'Wilfred Owen, selected poems: Insensibility' (background and synopsis)

  14. We wise st. V, l. 1

    The pronoun shifts to 'we'. Those who are 'wise' are tormented by thought, which 'besmirches' (stains, soils) their souls with blood. Who are 'we'? Stallworthy reads 'we poets'; Douglas Kerr proposes 'we officers', 'we shellshocked', or even 'we cowards'. The question matters because it determines whether Owen identifies himself with the sensitive or criticises them. 'How should we see our task / But through his blunt and lashless eyes?' suggests the wise must learn from, or at least try to see through the eyes of, the insensible soldier.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Douglas Kerr, Wilfred Owen's Voices: Language and Community (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993); Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise')

  15. not vital overmuch st. V, l. 5

    'Vital' means full of life; 'overmuch' means excessively. The soldier is neither too alive nor too mortal when dying: he is a kind of living dead, and death means little to him. The lines 'Nor sad, nor proud, / Nor curious at all' list the feelings he lacks. 'He cannot tell / Old men's placidity from his' shows him already aged in feeling.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Wilfred Owen Association, 'Insensibility' (critique by Kenneth Simcox)

  16. cursed are dullards st. VI, l. 1

    The final section reverses the structure: the formula 'Happy are' gives way to 'cursed are', as the Beatitudes of Matthew 5 and Luke 6 ('Blessed are') are followed in Luke by woes ('Woe unto you'). The 'dullards' are civilians at home who, though no cannon has stunned them, have chosen insensibility: 'By choice they made themselves immune / To pity'. Their insensibility is not forced like the soldiers' but willed, and therefore culpable. 'That they should be as stones' is the curse: may they be as unfeeling as stones.

    Luke 6:20-26 (KJV); Crossref-it, 'Wilfred Owen, selected poems: Insensibility' (background and synopsis); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  17. paucity that never was simplicity st. VI, l. 4

    'Paucity', littleness or scarcity, describes the poverty of the dullards' feeling. It 'never was simplicity': unlike the uneducated soldier of section IV, whose simplicity is innocent, theirs is a deliberate meanness. 'Mean' has its old sense of base and petty. The line distinguishes the two kinds of insensibility on which the poem turns. The shared ending of 'paucity' and 'simplicity' makes the distinction audible: the words sound alike but are set against each other, just as two kinds of numbness look alike but differ morally.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'

  18. the eternal reciprocity of tears st. VI, ll. 6-10

    The poem ends with the noblest phrase in it: 'The eternal reciprocity of tears', the mutual exchange of grief binding human beings. The dullards have made themselves immune to 'whatever mourns in man / Before the last sea and the hapless stars', a phrase of cosmic sadness. Owen's draft preface declares that 'the Poetry is in the pity', and the close is a statement of that faith. Readers may compare Virgil's 'sunt lacrimae rerum' ('there are tears of things', Aeneid 1.462). The final line, in its length and abstraction, is set against the clipped lines before it.

    Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), Wikisource transcription; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Insensibility'; Poetry Lovers' Page, 'Insensibility: literary analysis' (summarising Stallworthy and Kerr on 'We wise')
