Death & Dying

Anthem for Doomed Youth

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

Death & DyingReligion

What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?
Only the monstrous anger of the guns.
Only the stuttering rifles' rapid rattle
Can patter out their hasty orisons.
No mockeries for them; no prayers nor bells,
Nor any voice of mourning save the choirs,--
The shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells;
And bugles calling for them from sad shires.

What candles may be held to speed them all?
Not in the hands of boys, but in their eyes
Shall shine the holy glimmers of goodbyes.
The pallor of girls' brows shall be their pall;
Their flowers the tenderness of patient minds,
And each slow dusk a drawing-down of blinds.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A sonnet that replaces the church's passing-bells and choirs with the rifles' rapid rattle and the 'shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells'. The mourning that remains is the girls' pallor and 'a drawing-down of blinds'.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, occasion and passing-bells title; l. 1

    Owen wrote the sonnet in September-October 1917 while being treated for shell-shock at Craiglockhart, Edinburgh, where he met Siegfried Sassoon. Several drafts survive; one carries Sassoon's pencilled suggestions, including the change of 'Dead' to 'Doomed' in the title. 'Anthem' is an ironic word (a choral hymn of praise, and also a national song), and 'Doomed' turns an elegy into a protest about the young still to be killed. A passing-bell was the church bell tolled to announce a death, so the opening question asks what funeral rites these soldiers get. Stallworthy suggested Owen was answering the Prefatory Note of the anthology Poems of To-Day, which praised poetry sounding 'the passing-bell of Death'; Owen is said to have owned a copy. The echo is probable, not proven.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), draft of 'Anthem for Doomed Youth' with annotations by Siegfried Sassoon; Academy of American Poets, 'Draft of Wilfred Owen's "Anthem for Doomed Youth"' (British Library / Owen Literary Estate manuscript image); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Poems of To-Day: An Anthology (London: Sidgwick & Jackson for the English Association, 1915), Prefatory Note, Project Gutenberg text; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history)

  2. who die as cattle l. 1

    The simile denies the dead the human dignity that funeral ritual is meant to confer. Cattle are killed in numbers, without names and without mourning, and the comparison exposes the industrial scale of Western Front casualties. The line also inverts the sacrificial language common in 1914-17 patriotic verse and sermons: the young men are not offered up like the lamb of sacrifice but simply butchered. Muir notes that Owen's reading of the Prefatory Note in Poems of To-Day, which says of one young poet that he 'has gone singing to lay down his life', helped set off the poem; 'cattle' is its bitter reply. Compare the 'sacrifice' imagery of Owen's 'The Parable of the Old Man and the Young'.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Poems of To-Day: An Anthology (London: Sidgwick & Jackson for the English Association, 1915), Prefatory Note, Project Gutenberg text; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  3. the monstrous anger of the guns l. 2

    The guns are personified as an inhuman, enraged power, and their roar replaces the tolling bell. Muir, following Stallworthy, notes that Owen took the line from an earlier unfinished poem, where it read 'The monstrous anger of our taciturn guns'; he dropped 'taciturn', which was hypermetrical and unsuitable for a barrage. 'Monstrous' keeps its older sense of unnatural or portentous (Lat. monstrum, a portent or prodigy) as well as 'huge'. The repeated 'Only' (ll. 2-3) answers the opening question with grim economy: there are no rites except the weapons themselves.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  4. stuttering rifles' rapid rattle l. 3

    The heavy alliteration on r and t and the clipped rhythm make the line sound like the weapon it describes, one of the most frequently cited examples of onomatopoeia in war poetry. 'Stuttering' personifies the rifles as speakers who cannot form words, so their noise is a parody of the spoken prayers of a funeral service; some readers also hear an allusion to the speech impediments of shell-shocked patients, which Owen saw at Craiglockhart, though that reading is critical inference. The line feeds into 'patter out', which can mean to recite prayers rapidly and mechanically (from paternoster) as well as the sound of rain or bullets.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history)

  5. patter out their hasty orisons l. 4

    'Orison' (from Old French oreison, Latin oratio, 'speech, prayer') is an archaic, literary word for a prayer, familiar from Hamlet's 'Nymph, in thy orisons / Be all my sins remembered' (3.1). 'Patter' originally meant to repeat the Paternoster rapidly and mechanically, hence to gabble. Owen thus makes the rifle fire into a hurried, perfunctory requiem said over men who die too quickly and in too great numbers for any real service. The deliberately antique, liturgical diction clashes with the modern weapons, a tension that runs through the octave.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); William Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1, Folger Shakespeare Library text

  6. No mockeries for them l. 5

    The line can be read two ways. On one reading, conventional Christian funerals would be 'mockeries' because the faith they express, and the Church that blessed the war, ring false beside such deaths; Owen thus refuses them. On another, the phrase is bitterly literal: the dead do not even receive the forms of mourning, however inadequate. Critics have taken the poem as both a consolatory elegy and an anti-religious protest, and the tension between these readings is part of its power. Owen had been a lay assistant to an evangelical vicar at Dunsden (1911-13) and grew critical of institutional religion, which supports the sharper reading.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history); Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text

  7. the shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells l. 7

    Shells in flight made a rising whine or shriek that soldiers commonly described as wailing or screaming. Owen turns them into a demented choir, sustaining the funeral liturgy conceit (bells, orisons, choir, candles, pall, flowers). 'Demented' (Lat. de + mens, 'out of one's mind') also glances at the shell-shocked men among whom Owen wrote the poem. Muir notes that the 'wailing of . . . shells' already appears in the related draft fragments Owen was working on. The repetition 'choirs,-- / The shrill, demented choirs' enacts the swelling sound.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  8. bugles calling for them from sad shires l. 8

    The last line of the octave shifts from the front to home. Bugles sound at military funerals (the Last Post), but also call men to service, and the line holds both senses: the shires that sent the battalions now grieve for them. 'Shires' were English counties, and many British regiments were county-based, so the loss falls on whole districts. Muir notes that the image grew from Owen's earlier fragments, one with the line 'Bugles that sadden all the evening air'. The phrase is the only element in the octave that admits human mourning, and it prepares for the domestic sestet.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  9. Sonnet form and volta l. 9

    The poem is a sonnet rhyming ababcdcd effe gg: an English (Shakespearean) octave and a closing couplet, but with the Petrarchan division into octave and sestet, each opened by a question. The volta at l. 9 moves from the deafening sounds of the front to the silence and stillness of grieving families. Each sestet image takes an element of a church funeral (candles carried round the coffin, the pall, flowers, and the drawn blinds of a house of mourning) and relocates it in human feeling. Using the sonnet, a form of love and devotion, to protest against war was itself pointed; Owen admired Keats's sonnets.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history)

  10. candles . . . in their eyes ll. 9-11

    At a Christian funeral, candles were carried and set round the coffin, often by choirboys or acolytes, to 'speed' the soul. Owen keeps the boys but takes away their candles: the only light is the glint of tears in their eyes as they say goodbye, 'the holy glimmers of goodbyes'. The 'boys' may be younger brothers and schoolfellows of the dead, and perhaps also fellow soldiers barely older than boys. The sacramental adjective 'holy' moves from the Church to private grief, one of the poem's main strategies.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history)

  11. The pallor of girls' brows shall be their pall l. 12

    A pall (Lat. pallium, 'cloak') is the heavy cloth spread over a coffin, often black or purple; for soldiers it would usually be a flag. Owen links it by sound to 'pallor' (Lat. pallor, 'paleness'), though the two words are etymologically unrelated, so that the white faces of sweethearts, sisters and mothers become the only funeral covering these men receive. The bodies, buried where they fell or never found, have no coffin to cover; the mourning is displaced onto the living.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  12. the tenderness of patient minds l. 13

    Flowers on a grave are replaced by the 'tenderness' of those who wait and grieve. 'Patient' keeps its root sense of suffering and enduring (Lat. pati, 'to suffer'), and perhaps also recalls the hospital patients among whom Owen wrote. The surviving drafts show Sassoon's hand in the phrase. The line is one of the few in the poem in which grief is given positive, almost consoling expression, though critics differ on whether the sestet offers real consolation or only a quieter form of loss.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), draft of 'Anthem for Doomed Youth' with annotations by Siegfried Sassoon (Complete Poems and Fragments no. 96); 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  13. each slow dusk a drawing-down of blinds l. 14

    In Victorian and Edwardian Britain, a household in mourning drew its blinds or curtains, especially on the day of a funeral, as a public sign of a death. Owen fuses this custom with nightfall: every 'slow dusk' is like the closing of a coffin lid or of eyes, and the mourning becomes daily and endless. The falling cadence and the run of long vowels slow the line almost to a halt. Some readers hear an echo of Binyon's 'For the Fallen' ('At the going down of the sun'), but Muir thinks the debt far from certain.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; 'Anthem for Doomed Youth', Wikipedia (summarising Stallworthy and the manuscript history)
