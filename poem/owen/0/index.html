The Mind

Strange Meeting

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

The MindDeath & Dying

It seemed that out of the battle I escaped
Down some profound dull tunnel, long since scooped
Through granites which Titanic wars had groined.
Yet also there encumbered sleepers groaned,
Too fast in thought or death to be bestirred.
Then, as I probed them, one sprang up, and stared
With piteous recognition in fixed eyes,
Lifting distressful hands as if to bless.
And by his smile, I knew that sullen hall;
With a thousand fears that vision's face was grained;
Yet no blood reached there from the upper ground,
And no guns thumped, or down the flues made moan.
"Strange, friend," I said, "Here is no cause to mourn."
"None," said the other, "Save the undone years,
The hopelessness. Whatever hope is yours,
Was my life also; I went hunting wild
After the wildest beauty in the world,
Which lies not calm in eyes, or braided hair,
But mocks the steady running of the hour,
And if it grieves, grieves richlier than here.
For by my glee might many men have laughed,
And of my weeping something has been left,
Which must die now. I mean the truth untold,
The pity of war, the pity war distilled.
Now men will go content with what we spoiled.
Or, discontent, boil bloody, and be spilled.
They will be swift with swiftness of the tigress,
None will break ranks, though nations trek from progress.
Courage was mine, and I had mystery;
Wisdom was mine, and I had mastery;
To miss the march of this retreating world
Into vain citadels that are not walled.
Then, when much blood had clogged their chariot-wheels
I would go up and wash them from sweet wells,
Even with truths that lie too deep for taint.
I would have poured my spirit without stint
But not through wounds; not on the cess of war.
Foreheads of men have bled where no wounds were.
I am the enemy you killed, my friend.
I knew you in this dark; for so you frowned
Yesterday through me as you jabbed and killed.
I parried; but my hands were loath and cold.
Let us sleep now . . ."

(This poem was found among the author's papers.
It ends on this strange note.)

*Another Version*

Earth's wheels run oiled with blood. Forget we that.
Let us lie down and dig ourselves in thought.
Beauty is yours and you have mastery,
Wisdom is mine, and I have mystery.
We two will stay behind and keep our troth.
Let us forego men's minds that are brute's natures,
Let us not sup the blood which some say nurtures,
Be we not swift with swiftness of the tigress.
Let us break ranks from those who trek from progress.
Miss we the march of this retreating world
Into old citadels that are not walled.
Let us lie out and hold the open truth.
Then when their blood hath clogged the chariot wheels
We will go up and wash them from deep wells.
What though we sink from men as pitchers falling
Many shall raise us up to be their filling
Even from wells we sunk too deep for war
And filled by brows that bled where no wounds were.

*Alternative line--*

Even as One who bled where no wounds were.

Codex Note

A soldier escapes into a dim tunnel in hell and meets a dead man who turns out to be the enemy he killed. The poem is built on pararhyme (hell/hall, groined/groaned), which Owen helped make a mainstay of modern verse.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Title, date and publication title; l. 1

    The poem belongs to the first half of 1918, probably written at Scarborough or Ripon (the Wilfred Owen Association says spring or early summer 1918; the dating is approximate). Owen was killed on 4 November 1918, a week before the Armistice. The poem appeared in the Sitwells' anthology Wheels (1919) and then in Poems (1920), edited by Sassoon, who had worked on the selection with Edith Sitwell; Sassoon's Introduction names it as the finest example of Owen's experiments in assonance and dissonance. Dennis Welland identified the title's source in Shelley's The Revolt of Islam, Canto V, where enemy soldiers seem 'like some brothers on a journey wide / Gone forth, whom now strange meeting did befall / In a strange land'. In summer 1918 Owen's draft contents list for his planned volume described the poem's 'motive' as 'Foolishness of War', according to Kerr.

    First World War Poetry Digital Archive (Oxford), manuscript of 'Strange Meeting'; Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Dennis Welland, Wilfred Owen: A Critical Study (London: Chatto & Windus, 1960; rev. 1978), as reported by Muir; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; P. B. Shelley, The Revolt of Islam, Canto V, st. 13 (University of Pennsylvania text)

  2. Down some profound dull tunnel l. 2

    The descent recalls the epic journey to the underworld (Odysseus in Odyssey 11, Aeneas in Aeneid 6, Dante in the Inferno, which Owen owned in Cary's translation). Muir thinks the general influence of the Inferno, a landscape of hell where the newly dead converse, matters more than any verbal echoes. John Middleton Murry, reviewing the poem in 1919, stressed its debt to Keats's Hyperion poems; and the Wilfred Owen Association suggests Sassoon's 'The Rear-Guard', about a soldier groping through an underground tunnel among the dead, as a probable prompt. The deep dug-outs and mine tunnels of the Western Front give the setting its literal basis. 'Profound' keeps its Latin sense of 'deep' (profundus).

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Wilfred Owen Association, 'Strange Meeting'; Dante Alighieri, The Vision of Hell (Inferno), tr. H. F. Cary, Project Gutenberg

  3. Titanic wars l. 3

    In Greek myth the Titanomachy was the ten-year war in which Zeus and the Olympians overthrew the Titans, who were then imprisoned in Tartarus, deep beneath the earth (Hesiod, Theogony 617-735). Owen's tunnel runs through granite hollowed by those primeval conflicts, setting modern war in a mythic, geological perspective of endless violence; Keats's Hyperion, which Owen loved, describes the fallen Titans in their cavernous den. The phrase was one Yeats singled out as a cliche when dismissing Owen, according to Kerr, and opinions on its effectiveness remain divided.

    Hesiod, Theogony 617-735 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White), Perseus Digital Library; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36

  4. groined l. 3

    In architecture a groin is the edge formed by the intersection of two vaults, and to groin is to build with such vaults (OED). The granite tunnel is thus imagined as a natural crypt, appropriate to a place of the dead, and the word also carries a faint bodily suggestion. 'Groined' is the first half of the poem's first pararhyme pair with 'groaned' in the next line: the same consonant frame with a changed vowel (see 'Pararhyme'). Muir lists 'grained/groined' and 'escaped/scooped' as examples of Owen's strict practice, in which both the opening and closing consonants are matched.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36

  5. Pararhyme ll. 1-4 and throughout

    The poem is written in pentameter couplets, but each pair is linked by pararhyme (also called consonant or half-rhyme): hall/Hell in Stallworthy's text, groined/groaned, mourn/moan, years/yours, killed/cold. The vowel usually falls in pitch from the first word to the second, producing an effect of dissonance and failed harmony suited to a dream of the dead. Sassoon's Introduction to Poems (1920) singled the poem out as the finest example of Owen's experiments in assonance and dissonance. Sources proposed include the French poet Jules Romains (suggested by Welland via Laurent Tailhade), Owen's early reading in J. A. Symonds on Shelley (Stallworthy), and, as Muir argued, a passage in Marlowe's Jew of Malta; Kerr is sceptical of Muir's Marlowe theory. John Middleton Murry's 1919 review first analysed the device's effect.

    Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  6. encumbered sleepers ll. 4-5

    'Encumbered' means burdened or hampered, perhaps by full kit, perhaps by wounds or by death itself. The ambiguity of 'Too fast in thought or death' keeps the sleepers on the line between life and death; 'fast' means firmly held, as in 'fast asleep'. The image draws on real dug-outs crammed with sleeping or wounded men, and on epic underworlds full of shades. 'Bestirred', an archaism, keeps the slightly antique, visionary diction that Owen uses throughout.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  7. piteous recognition in fixed eyes l. 7

    The 'fixed eyes' are those of a dead man, but they also recall the uncanny gazes of literary visions: 'Piteous' introduces the poem's key word pity (see 'The pity of war'). The gesture 'Lifting distressful hands as if to bless' is ambiguous: a plea, a priestly blessing, or the hands-up of surrender. The recognition is completed only in l. 39, when the stranger reveals that he is the enemy the speaker killed.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  8. And by his smile, I knew that sullen hall l. 9

    The text shown here follows the first printed versions (Wheels 1919 and Poems 1920). Owen's manuscripts are untidy and unfinal, and the now-standard text in Stallworthy's critical edition reads 'With a thousand pains that vision's face was grained; / . . . By his dead smile I knew we stood in Hell', the version now most often printed; Muir quotes the 'sullen hall' and 'Hell' lines together as a pararhyme pair. 'Sullen hall' suggests a gloomy hall of the dead, perhaps recalling Valhalla or the halls of Hades, whereas 'Hell' names the place outright and pararhymes with 'hall'. Students should be aware that line wording varies between editions.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text

  9. grained l. 10

    To be 'grained' is to have marks running through the substance, like the grain of wood or stone, or to be dyed deep (OED, 'ingrained'). The dead man's face carries the lines of a thousand fears, written into it rather than on its surface. The word belongs to the pararhyme pair groined/grained in Muir's list of Owen's 'sandwiching' rhymes, where initial and final consonants are both repeated. Stallworthy's text has 'pains' for 'fears'.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  10. the undone years l. 14

    The speaker says there is no cause to mourn, since no shells reach this place; the dead man replies that there is one: 'the undone years'. 'Undone' means both not done (unlived) and ruined. The phrase introduces the lament for unfulfilled promise that dominates the middle section: the dead man's 'hope' was the same as the speaker's, suggesting from the start that the two are mirror images.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36

  11. hunting wild / After the wildest beauty ll. 16-20

    The beauty sought is not that of 'eyes, or braided hair', the stock subjects of love poetry, but something that 'mocks the steady running of the hour', a Romantic ideal of beauty beyond time, reminiscent of Keats and Shelley, whom Owen idolised. Muir argues that the stranger is a German poet and 'Owen's doppelganger', speaking Owen's own words. Welland also read him as the poet's alter ego; Kerr notes that Dominic Hibberd later questioned the 'double' reading, and the poem itself does not settle it. Muir hears a native echo for the pararhymes 'wild/world' in Marlowe.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Dennis Welland, Wilfred Owen: A Critical Study (London: Chatto & Windus, 1960; rev. 1978), as reported by Muir

  12. The pity of war l. 24

    Owen's draft Preface to his intended collection, printed in Poems (1920), states that his subject is war and 'the pity of War', and that 'The Poetry is in the pity'; it adds that 'All a poet can do to-day is to warn'. The dead poet in the tunnel laments that his insight into that pity will now die untold. 'Distilled' suggests something extracted and concentrated, as in a still, like an essence or perhaps a poison, so war is both the occasion of pity and the process that refines it. Muir connects the passage directly with the Preface.

    Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36

  13. Now men will go content with what we spoiled ll. 25-28

    With the truth 'untold', men will either be content with a spoiled world or, discontented, 'boil bloody, and be spilled', fight again and die. Muir calls this prophecy surprisingly little discussed and reads it as foreseeing post-war militarism and the dictatorships and wars of the next twenty years; Kerr finds the middle section's prophetic language largely incoherent, which he blames partly on the demands of pararhyme. The 'swiftness of the tigress' signals cruelty: Muir points out that in Owen's fragment 'Earth's wheels' (printed here as 'Another Version') it is better to 'break ranks' than trek from progress. Stallworthy compares David's lament for Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1), which Muir thinks too eulogistic a parallel.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Bible (KJV), 2 Samuel 1:17-27

  14. Courage was mine . . . mastery ll. 29-30

    The parallel syntax ('Courage was mine, and I had mystery; / Wisdom was mine, and I had mastery') is incantatory, and 'mystery/mastery' is an especially pure pararhyme. In the fragment printed as 'Another Version', the qualities are shared out ('Beauty is yours and you have mastery, / Wisdom is mine, and I have mystery'), which supports readings of the two speakers as complementary halves of one self. 'Mystery' may carry its older sense of a craft or trade, as well as of a hidden truth.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online)

  15. vain citadels that are not walled l. 32

    A citadel is a fortress commanding a city. These are 'vain' (empty, useless, and conceited) and 'not walled', offering no real protection, so the 'march' of the retreating world ends in illusion. The image ironically inverts the language of military advance. Stallworthy's text reads 'To miss the march of this retreating world / Into vain citadels that are not walled'; the 'Another Version' has 'old citadels'. Muir lists 'world/walled' among the pararhymes he links to Marlowe.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  16. much blood had clogged their chariot-wheels ll. 33-35

    The war-chariot is the emblem of ancient heroic warfare; in Iliad 20 the axle and rails of Akhilleus' chariot are spattered with blood thrown up by the wheels as he slaughters the Trojans. The Bible gives a parallel in the Egyptian chariots whose wheels the Lord took off in the Red Sea (Exodus 14:25). Owen imagines that, after the war, the poet would have gone up to cleanse the wheels with water 'from sweet wells', a prophetic and almost sacramental act of healing through truth. 'Truths that lie too deep for taint' echoes Wordsworth's 'Thoughts that do often lie too deep for tears' (the last line of the Intimations Ode).

    Homer, Iliad 20.498-503 (tr. A. T. Murray), Perseus Digital Library; Bible (KJV), Exodus 14:25; William Wordsworth, 'Ode: Intimations of Immortality'; Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36

  17. the cess of war l. 37

    The dead man would have poured out his spirit freely, but not through wounds and 'not on the cess of war'. 'Cess' is recorded as a short form of cesspool (OED), which fits the mud and sewage of the trenches; it also has the older sense of a tax or assessment, so the war becomes a levy paid in lives. The Irish phrase 'bad cess to . . .' (bad luck to) may add a curse-like undertone. The line contrasts a voluntary, Christ-like outpouring of the spirit with the meaningless spilling of blood.

    Oxford English Dictionary (OED Online); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  18. Foreheads of men have bled where no wounds were l. 38

    The image of bleeding without wounds alludes to Christ's agony in the Garden of Gethsemane, where his sweat became 'as it were great drops of blood' (Luke 22:44), and perhaps to the crown of thorns. The archive's 'Alternative line', 'Even as One who bled where no wounds were', makes the reference to Christ explicit with its capitalised 'One'. Owen often compared the infantryman with Christ, and the line implies inner suffering, the mental wounds of shell-shock and grief, which he had seen at Craiglockhart. Critics have read the line as the climax of the poem's prophetic, quasi-religious register, which Kerr traces in the poem's reception from Murry and Blunden onwards.

    Bible (KJV), Luke 22:44; Bible (KJV), Matthew 27:29; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  19. I am the enemy you killed, my friend l. 39

    The climactic line joins the opposites 'enemy' and 'friend' (the speaker's own greeting in l. 13: 'Strange, friend'). The stranger recognises the speaker by his frown as he 'jabbed and killed', implying bayonet fighting, and says he parried, but with hands 'loath and cold'. Readings divide: Welland and Muir see the enemy as the poet's double or alter ego, so that killing him is a kind of self-destruction; Hibberd, as Kerr reports, doubted this and preferred a more literal reading of two soldiers reconciled in death. Shelley's Revolt of Islam, the title's source, also depicts enemies reconciled as brothers.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Dennis Welland, Wilfred Owen: A Critical Study (London: Chatto & Windus, 1960; rev. 1978), as reported by Muir; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  20. Let us sleep now . . . l. 43

    The poem stops on a short line of reconciliation and rest. Stallworthy asked whether Owen thought it finished. Muir argues it was meant to look like a fragment, on the model of Keats's Hyperion, which breaks off in mid-sentence; Kerr accepts that Owen probably saw the poem as incomplete, but suggests that the break may be deliberate and that the unfinished quality shows throughout its texture. The parenthesis that follows ('This poem was found among the author's papers. It ends on this strange note.') is an editorial note printed in Poems (1920), not Owen's text.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Douglas Kerr, '"Strange Meeting" Again', Connotations 3.2 (1993/94), 173-85; Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem

  21. Another Version after l. 43

    The archive's text adds a passage headed 'Another Version'. Stallworthy prints it as a separate fragment ('Earth's wheels run oiled with blood'), written between November 1917 and March 1918, and parts of it reappear in ll. 29-35 of the finished poem. In it the two figures resolve to break ranks and 'hold the open truth'; Muir uses it to show that the tigress stands for cruelty. Its image of men as pitchers sunk into deep wells and filled with truth develops the 'sweet wells' of the main poem, and its alternative last line points directly to Christ.

    Kenneth Muir, 'Connotations of "Strange Meeting"', Connotations 3.1 (1993), 26-36; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments, 2 vols (London: Chatto & Windus / Hogarth / OUP, 1983), notes to the poem; Wilfred Owen, Poems, ed. Siegfried Sassoon (London: Chatto & Windus, 1920), with Owen's Preface and Sassoon's Introduction, Project Gutenberg text
