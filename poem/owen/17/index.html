Death & Dying

Futility

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

Death & DyingNature

Move him into the sun--
Gently its touch awoke him once,
At home, whispering of fields unsown.
Always it woke him, even in France,
Until this morning and this snow.
If anything might rouse him now
The kind old sun will know.

Think how it wakes the seeds--
Woke, once, the clays of a cold star.
Are limbs so dear-achieved, are sides
Full-nerved,--still warm,--too hard to stir?
Was it for this the clay grew tall?
--O what made fatuous sunbeams toil
To break earth's sleep at all?

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A man has died and the speaker asks the sun to wake him as it once did at home. The bitter second stanza asks why sunbeams ever toiled to wake the clay at all. Britten set it in his War Requiem.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Futility: date and publication l. 1

    Owen wrote the poem while at the Northern Command Depot at Ripon, probably in May 1918 (Scarborough is possible), between his recovery from shell-shock and his return to France, where he was killed on 4 November 1918. It appeared in The Nation on 15 June 1918, with 'Hospital Barge', and was later included in Poems (1920), edited by Siegfried Sassoon and Edith Sitwell. In his draft table of contents Owen listed it under the motive 'Grief'. The opening command, spoken presumably to fellow soldiers, assumes a man not yet known to be dead, or one whom the speaker cannot yet accept is dead. Benjamin Britten set the poem in the Lacrimosa of his War Requiem (1962).

    Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  2. the sun's touch l. 2

    The first stanza personifies the sun as a kindly presence ('Gently', 'whispering', 'The kind old sun') that has always woken the man, in home and at the front. The tenderness depends on the poem's pastoral memory. The faith in the sun's power is a kind of desperate hope, and the stanza ends on a line of quiet trust ('The kind old sun will know') that the second stanza will savagely question.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website

  3. fields unsown l. 3

    The phrase identifies the dead man as a countryman, woken by the sun to sow his fields. The fields are 'unsown' because the work was waiting, and now will never be done: the phrase carries the waste of the man's life and labour. The Wilfred Owen Association commentary notes that an earlier version read 'half-sown'. The image also anticipates the second stanza's 'seeds', which the sun can wake though it cannot wake the man.

    Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'

  4. this morning and this snow l. 5

    The snowy morning sets the scene in winter at the front. Owen had experienced extreme cold in the trenches in January 1917, when men froze to death on sentry duty, and his poem 'Exposure' records it. Whether the man died of wounds or of cold is left open; the snow emphasises the coldness of the body, which the sun's warmth can no longer reach. The demonstrative 'this ... this' places us at the moment of discovery.

    Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002); Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'

  5. Pararhyme and stanza st. 1-2

    Pararhyme, Owen's characteristic device, keeps the consonants of a rhyme but changes the vowel: sun/sown (l. 1/3), once/France (2/4), seeds/sides, star/stir, tall/toil. The effect is of an expectation unfulfilled, a harmony that fails, apt in a poem about a life that does not resume. Each stanza ends on a full or near-full rhyme (snow/now/know; all), giving a closing chime. The two stanzas of seven lines make fourteen, a broken sonnet, and the shift from statements (st. 1) to questions (st. 2) acts as a volta.

    Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; D. S. R. Welland, 'Half-Rhyme in Wilfred Owen: Its Derivation and Use', Review of English Studies n.s. 1 (1950)

  6. the sun wakes the seeds l. 8

    The imperative 'Think' turns the speaker from the dead man to the cosmic scale. The sun wakes seeds each year; the man, like a seed, has been buried, but will not germinate. The image echoes the biblical promise of resurrection in the figure of the seed (John 12:24; 1 Corinthians 15:36, 'that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die'), only to deny it. Owen had been brought up an evangelical Christian and knew such texts well.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 12:24; 1 Corinthians 15:36-38; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'

  7. the clays of a cold star l. 9

    The line compresses the history of life on earth: the sun once warmed 'the clays of a cold star', bringing living things out of inert matter. 'Clay' is also the biblical stuff of man, formed by God 'of the dust of the ground' (Genesis 2:7), and a common figure for the mortal body ('thou hast made me as the clay', Job 10:9). The poem's science is broadly evolutionary, its language scriptural; the sun takes the place of the Creator. Critics have read the passage as a post-Darwinian lament in which the long process of creation ends in pointless death.

    The Holy Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, Genesis 2:7; Job 10:9; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website

  8. dear-achieved l. 10

    'Dear' here means costly, as in 'dearly bought'. The compound 'dear-achieved' presents the human body as an achievement won over ages of evolution, and also as dear in the sense of beloved. The line's broken rhythm, interrupted by dashes ('Full-nerved,--still warm,--too hard to stir?'), mimics a person feeling a body that is still warm but will not move. The question is rhetorical: if the sun could make life from clay, why can it not stir a body so recently alive?

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'dear, adj.'; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'

  9. Was it for this l. 12

    The clay 'grew tall': the dust of Genesis rose into upright humanity. The question 'Was it for this' is an old formula of grievance, and readers have heard in it the opening of Wordsworth's 1799 Prelude, which begins 'Was it for this'; Wordsworth asks whether his formative childhood among nature was meant for his present idleness, and Owen asks the same of the whole of natural history. The echo, if intended, turns the Romantic faith in nature's nurture into bitterness. Some critics see the line as the poem's emotional crisis, from which the final question follows.

    William Wordsworth, The Prelude (1799), Part 1, l. 1; Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website

  10. fatuous l. 13

    'Fatuous' (Latin fatuus, foolish, silly) is the poem's harshest word, sharply opposed to the 'kind old sun' of l. 7. The sunbeams 'toil' like labourers, and their work is condemned as stupidly pointless. Simcox notes that the line can be stressed in two ways, on 'what' or on 'made', which shifts the question between the sun's folly and the power that set it to work. The question is directed beyond the sun to whatever, God or nature, set the process going.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. 'fatuous, adj.'; Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website

  11. earth's sleep l. 14

    The last line reverses the poem's opening wish. At first the speaker hoped the sun would wake the dead man; now he wonders why it ever woke the earth from its lifeless 'sleep'. Death is figured as sleep throughout (the man will not wake), and the final question extends that figure to the whole planet. The poem's title, 'Futility', names the conclusion the questions imply, though the poem itself ends with a question rather than a statement.

    Jon Stallworthy, ed., Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (Chatto & Windus/Hogarth, 1983), notes to 'Futility'; Kenneth Simcox, commentary on 'Futility', Wilfred Owen Association website; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)
