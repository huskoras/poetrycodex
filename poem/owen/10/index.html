Dulce et Decorum est

by Wilfred Owen 1893–1918

From Poems (1920)

History & PoliticsDeath & Dying

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,
Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,
Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,
And towards our distant rest began to trudge.
Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame, all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots
Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.

Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!--An ecstasy of fumbling
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,
But someone still was yelling out and stumbling
And flound'ring like a man in fire or lime.--
Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

In all my dreams before my helpless sight
He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.

If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace
Behind the wagon that we flung him in,
And watch the white eyes writhing in his face,
His hanging face, like a devil's sick of sin,
If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood
Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs
Bitter as the cud
Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues,--
My friend, you would not tell with such high zest
To children ardent for some desperate glory,
The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est
Pro patria mori.

✦ ✦ ✦

Codex Note

A gas attack in which one man cannot get his helmet on in time, and the speaker is haunted by his drowning. It ends by calling the Latin tag 'Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori' the old Lie. The best known of Owen's poems.

Study notes by the editors

  1. Composition and title title; l. 1

    Owen, treated for shell shock at Craiglockhart War Hospital near Edinburgh from June 1917, sent his mother a draft in October 1917 (the earliest manuscript is dated 8 October), calling it a 'gas poem' and glossing the Latin tag with bitter irony ('Sweet! and decorous!'). He revised the poem at Scarborough and Ripon in 1918. It was published after his death (4 November 1918) in Poems (1920), edited by Siegfried Sassoon with Edith Sitwell. The title, taken from Horace, sets up the bitter ending, where the Latin is called 'the old Lie'.

    Harold Owen and John Bell (eds), Wilfred Owen: Collected Letters (London: OUP, 1967), letter to Susan Owen, October 1917; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; 'Dulce et Decorum Est', Imperial War Museums, Lives of the First World War

  2. like old beggars under sacks l. 1

    The opening similes invert the recruiting-poster image of upright young soldiers: bowed under heavy packs, exhausted men look like old beggars and 'hags', ugly old women, knock-kneed and coughing. The degrading comparisons deny the soldiers heroic dignity and even youth. 'Cursed through sludge' gives the trench mud a sound of its own. The first stanza moves slowly and heavily, matching the trudge away from the line.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  3. haunting flares l. 3

    Flares (star shells, Very lights) were fired at night to light up no man's land and expose movement. The men turn their backs on them as they leave the front line for 'distant rest', a relief camp behind the lines. 'Haunting' looks forward to the dreams that haunt the speaker in the poem's third part. The image of men turning away from the light to trudge into darkness also suggests a wider exhaustion of morale by 1917.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (New York: OUP, 1975)

  4. blood-shod l. 6

    'Blood-shod' is Owen's coinage on the model of 'well-shod' or 'iron-shod': cut and bleeding feet serve as the only shoes. The heavy pauses in ll. 5-6 ('Men marched asleep.', 'All went lame; all blind;') break the line as the men's marching breaks down. The near-total loss of the senses (lame, blind, deaf) prepares for the sudden alarm of the next stanza.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. shod, adj.

  5. the hoots of gas-shells ll. 7-8

    Gas shells burst with less noise than high explosive, so their soft fall was easily missed by exhausted men. 'Hoots' suggests their whistling flight, and perhaps the hooting of an owl or of a jeering crowd. The archive follows the 1920 text. Stallworthy's apparatus records an earlier manuscript reading, 'Of tired, outstripped Five-Nines that dropped behind' (Five-Nines being German 5.9-inch shells). The short, metrically irregular line 8 itself seems to fall away.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  6. Gas! GAS! l. 9

    The repeated cry, in capitals, and the broken syntax mark the shift into urgent present. Gas attacks were among the most feared weapons of the Western Front: chlorine from 1915, then phosgene and, from July 1917, mustard gas. The symptoms Owen describes, choking and frothing from the lungs, fit the lung-damaging agents chlorine and phosgene, which filled the lungs with fluid and so 'drowned' the victim on dry land.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Imperial War Museums, 'What you need to know about chemical warfare in the First World War'

  7. An ecstasy of fumbling l. 9

    'Ecstasy' comes from Greek ekstasis, standing outside oneself; its older English senses included any frenzy or stupor, not only rapture. Owen exploits the clash with the usual meaning of joy: the men are beside themselves with terror as they fumble at their masks. The 'clumsy helmets' of the next line are the gas masks or hoods themselves. The single word, set against 'fumbling', compresses the chaos of a gas alarm into one oxymoron.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. ecstasy, n.; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem

  8. clumsy helmets l. 10

    Early British anti-gas hoods were bags of chemically treated flannel with eyepieces; by 1917 troops also carried the small box respirator, with a face-piece, goggle eyepieces and a filter canister. The adjective 'clumsy' conveys both the equipment and the fumbling hands. The eyepieces are the 'misty panes' through which the speaker watches the man who failed to get his on.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Imperial War Museums, 'What you need to know about chemical warfare in the First World War'

  9. fire or lime l. 12

    Quicklime (calcium oxide) reacts violently with moisture and burns skin; the soldier writhes as if burning. Lime was also used on corpses, so the image hints at burial. 'Flound'ring' (floundering, struggling clumsily, as in water or mud) prepares for the drowning image of the next lines. The double dash before and after the stanza's central scene marks interruption and breathlessness, as if the speaker were choking too.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. lime, n.1; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem

  10. Dim through the misty panes l. 13

    The speaker sees through the misted windows of his own mask, and the gas cloud tints everything green. The scene becomes an underwater world in which another man drowns, a terrible inversion, since he is on land. 'Thick green light' renders the greenish-yellow haze of chlorine gas. 'Dim' and 'misty' blur the speaker's vision, so that the dying man is seen as a shape, not a face, until the dreams bring the face close.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  11. drowning: the repeated rhyme ll. 14, 16

    Line 14 ends the second stanza on 'drowning', and a separate two-line unit (ll. 15-16) ends on the same word. The identical rhyme stresses the obsessive return of the memory, recurring 'In all my dreams'. The isolated couplet stands outside the stanza pattern; readers take it as the point where the poem shifts from narrative to traumatic recurrence, from what happened to what keeps happening.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  12. In all my dreams l. 15

    At Craiglockhart, under Dr Arthur Brock, Owen was encouraged to confront his experience by activity and writing. Recurrent nightmares were a classic symptom of shell shock, and Owen's letters mention his war dreams. The shift to the present tense, 'He plunges at me', makes the past continually present. The speaker's 'helpless sight' mirrors the dying man's helplessness. Owen edited the hospital magazine, The Hydra, and met Siegfried Sassoon there in August 1917, who encouraged him to write directly of the front.

    Harold Owen and John Bell (eds), Wilfred Owen: Collected Letters (London: OUP, 1967); Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  13. guttering l. 16

    A candle 'gutters' when its flame flickers and the melted wax runs down in channels, just before it goes out. Owen uses the word for a life going out in spasms; its sound also suggests liquid in the throat, anticipating 'gargling'. The three present participles, 'guttering, choking, drowning', compress the agony into a single line. 'Plunges at me' makes the dying man seem to attack the dreamer, as if accusing the survivor.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. gutter, v.; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem

  14. the wagon that we flung him in l. 18

    Casualties were often carried off on wagons or limbers. 'Flung' shows the haste and brutality of war. The address to 'you' begins here: the reader, and specifically the civilian propagandist, is asked to walk behind the cart as in a grotesque funeral procession. The final section (ll. 17-28) is one long sentence of conditional clauses ('If ... If ...') building to the accusation.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Jon Silkin, Out of Battle: The Poetry of the Great War (London: OUP, 1972)

  15. like a devil's sick of sin l. 20

    The simile is deliberately excessive: even a devil, who should relish sin, would be sickened by this. Critics have found it either grotesquely powerful or strained. It moves the poem into a register of religious horror, preparing for 'innocent tongues' and for 'the old Lie', whose capital may recall the devil as 'a liar, and the father of it' (John 8:44).

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; The Bible, Authorized (King James) Version, John 8:44

  16. Bitter as the cud l. 23

    Cud is partly digested food returned from a ruminant's stomach to be chewed again. The simile matches the blood gargling up from the lungs, and implies something sour brought up repeatedly. The short line 23 breaks the pentameter, a broken line for a broken body. 'Incurable sores on innocent tongues' extends the image of disease and, by implication, of the lie that infects the young.

    Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. cud, n.; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem

  17. My friend: Jessie Pope l. 25

    One draft was headed 'To Jessie Pope', another 'To a certain Poetess': Pope wrote jingoistic verses such as 'Who's for the game?' for the Daily Mail, collected in Jessie Pope's War Poems (1915). Owen removed the dedication, widening the address to every civilian who tells 'children ardent for some desperate glory' that war is glorious. 'My friend' is bitterly ironic courtesy; 'high zest' mimics the enthusiasm of the propagandist.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Jessie Pope, 'Who's for the game?' (1915), Poetry by Heart; Dominic Hibberd, Wilfred Owen: A New Biography (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 2002)

  18. The old Lie l. 27

    The capital L gives 'Lie' the force of a proper name, an institution handed down from antiquity. Owen's charge falls on the schoolmasters, writers and politicians who taught boys the classical ideal of glorious death. Fussell places the poem within the war's wider exposure of the 'high diction' of honour and sacrifice. The spacing after 'Lie:' sets the Latin apart as a quotation on trial.

    Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem; Paul Fussell, The Great War and Modern Memory (New York: OUP, 1975)

  19. Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori ll. 27-28

    The line comes from Horace's ode urging Roman youth to harden themselves for war (Odes 3.2.13). In Britain it was a common schoolroom tag. Owen glossed it sarcastically for his mother as 'Sweet! and decorous!'. Horace's next line adds that death pursues even the man who flees; Owen's soldier dies not gloriously but in agony. The final half-line, 'Pro patria mori', ends the poem on 'mori', to die.

    Horace, Odes 3.2.13-14; Harold Owen and John Bell (eds), Wilfred Owen: Collected Letters (London: OUP, 1967), letter of October 1917; Jon Stallworthy (ed.), Wilfred Owen: The Complete Poems and Fragments (London: Chatto & Windus/Hogarth Press/OUP, 1983), notes and variants to this poem
