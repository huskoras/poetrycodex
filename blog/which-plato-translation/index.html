Which Translation of Plato Should You Read?

The Poetry Codex editors · 2026-10-21

In brief: the Poetry Codex holds two families of Plato translation. Benjamin Jowett's covers most of the dialogues and is the most graceful to read. The Loeb translations by H. N. Fowler, W. R. M. Lamb and R. G. Bury are closer to the Greek and better for study. Read Jowett for pleasure and the Loeb versions when the exact words matter.

The two families

Benjamin Jowett (1817–1893), Master of Balliol College and Regius Professor of Greek at Oxford, published The Dialogues of Plato in 1871, and revised it twice. The site uses his third edition of 1892. For more than half a century it was the English Plato, and its prose was admired as English writing in its own right. Its weakness is the other side of that strength: Jowett makes Plato sound like a cultivated Victorian, and where the Greek is blunt or awkward he sometimes smooths it or explains it as he goes.

The Loeb Classical Library, which began publishing in 1912, prints the Greek on the left-hand page and an English translation on the right. Its translators were writing for readers who might glance across at the original, so they stay close to it. Three of them are on the site:

  • Harold North Fowler, who translated the dialogues of Socrates' trial and death in 1914 and several later dialogues in the 1920s.
  • W. R. M. Lamb, who translated the Meno, Protagoras, Gorgias and Symposium in 1924–25.
  • R. G. Bury, who translated the Laws (1926) and the Letters (1929).

Paul Shorey's Loeb Republic (1930–35) is not on the site, so for the Republic the version here is Jowett's. (Study guide)

One opening, two ways: the Apology

The Apology is Socrates' speech in his own defence at his trial in 399 BC. He begins by answering his accusers, who had told the jury to beware of him as a skilled speaker. The Greek phrase is deinos legein, "clever at speaking", even "dangerously clever at speaking".

Jowett (1892):

How you, O Athenians, have been affected by my accusers, I cannot tell; but I know that they almost made me forget who I was

Fowler (1914):

How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know; but I, for my part, almost forgot my own identity

The two begin almost word for word alike. The difference comes a sentence later. Jowett has the accusers warn the jury against "the force of my eloquence". Fowler keeps the plainer sense of the Greek: the jury should not be deceived by Socrates "because I was a clever speaker". Jowett's phrase is finer English, but it loses the edge of the charge. Socrates was accused of being a clever arguer who could make the weaker case look the stronger, and Fowler's "clever speaker" keeps that accusation in view. In the first sentence, Fowler's "men of Athens" is also closer to the Greek andres Athenaioi than Jowett's "O Athenians". (Study guide)

Two more openings

The Symposium. The dialogue opens with Apollodorus telling a friend how someone hailed him in the street. The Greek is a joking, mock-formal greeting. Jowett makes it solemn: "Apollodorus, O thou Phalerian man, halt!" Lamb (1925) makes it colloquial: "Hullo, Phalerian! I say, Apollodorus, wait a moment." Lamb catches the tone of a man calling to a friend, though his English now sounds like a novel of the 1920s. (Study guide)

The Laws. Plato's last and longest dialogue begins with a question to two old men from Crete and Sparta. Jowett asks "is a God or some man supposed to be the author of your laws?" Bury (1926) asks "To whom do you ascribe the authorship of your legal arrangements, Strangers?" Here the Loeb version is the stiffer one. Bury is careful and exact, but Jowett is easier to read through twelve books.

What the site holds

Jowett is on the site for the Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, Phaedo, Republic, Symposium, Phaedrus, Meno, Protagoras, Gorgias, Theaetetus, Timaeus, Laws and eleven more, including the Ion, on poets and inspiration.

The Loeb translators are on the site for these dialogues:

  • Fowler: Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Phaedo, Phaedrus, Sophist, Statesman, Parmenides and Hippias Major.
  • Lamb: Meno, Protagoras, Gorgias, Symposium, Alcibiades II and Epinomis.
  • Bury: Laws and Letters.

Several of these, the Alcibiades II, the Epinomis, the Hippias Major and most of the Letters, are works whose authorship by Plato has long been doubted.

Where to start

The best first reading is the four dialogues of Socrates' last days, in this order: the Euthyphro (outside the court, before the trial), the Apology (the trial), the Crito (in prison) and the Phaedo (the day of his death). Fowler translated all four, and you can read them in either version. Then read the Symposium, and then the Republic.

Which should you read?

  • Reading Plato for the first time: Jowett, for the Republic and for the flow of the dialogues as conversation.
  • Studying a dialogue, or reading it beside the Greek: the Loeb version where the site has one. Fowler for the trial and death of Socrates, Lamb for the Meno, Gorgias and Symposium, Bury for the Laws.
  • Comparing: read the Apology or the Symposium in both, a page at a time. It is a good way to see where a translation is interpreting rather than translating.

All of these translations are in the public domain. The site does not include Jowett's long introductions or the translators' notes, so the study guides are the place to turn for background.
